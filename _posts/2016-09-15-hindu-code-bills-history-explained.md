The Hindu Code Bill is the most instructive failure in Indian legislative history, because it did not fail on its merits as its opponents and supporters argued them, and it did not fail permanently. A single comprehensive code, drafted by a committee of lawyers, revised by the Law Minister of a new republic, debated intermittently for four years and supported by the Prime Minister, was abandoned in September 1951 with only a fragment of it considered. Its principal pilot resigned from the government days later. Within five years of that abandonment, substantially the same body of law stood on the statute book, enacted by the same political party in the same Parliament building, divided into four separate Acts. The content had survived the journey almost intact. The vehicle had not. Explaining that difference, between a code that could not pass and four statutes that could, is the work of this article, and it yields the single most portable lesson the Indian legislative record offers about how large reforms are actually carried: a reform presented as one instrument gives every interest it touches a single target and a single occasion for coalition, while the same reform presented in pieces forces each interest to fight on ground it may not hold.

The stakes were not technical. Hindu personal law governed the marriage, divorce, adoption, guardianship, maintenance and inheritance of the largest community in the country, and it governed them unequally by sex and by caste in ways that the Constitution, adopted while the bill was pending, appeared to repudiate in its equality clauses. The code proposed to make monogamy compulsory, to give a wife a judicial route out of a marriage for the first time in the law applicable to most Hindus, to place a daughter beside a son in the line of inheritance, to convert the limited estate of a widow into property she owned outright, and to let a woman adopt a child in her own right. Every one of those propositions had an organised constituency for it and an organised constituency against it, and several had both inside the governing party. The passage history is therefore a record of two contests running at once: a contest over the content of family law, and a contest over who was entitled to decide that content, an unelected provisional legislature, a President who doubted his own obligation to assent, or the electorate that had not yet voted in a general election. The second contest decided the timing of the first.

## The Law the Code Proposed to Replace

The law administered as Hindu law in the middle of the twentieth century was not a code and had never been one. It was a layered accumulation of Sanskrit texts as interpreted by commentators, customs proved in litigation, and decisions of the High Courts and the Privy Council, organised into schools whose rules differed by region. In the greater part of the country the Mitakshara school governed, and under it a son acquired by birth an interest in the joint family property of his father and ancestors, taking by survivorship rather than by inheritance, so that the property of the family was held by its male members as a coparcenary into which daughters were never admitted as members. In Bengal and Assam the Dayabhaga school governed, under which a son took only on his father's death, but the devolution of property remained structured around male heirs, religious efficacy and the widow's limited estate. A Hindu woman who inherited from a male relative held, in the general run of cases, not ownership but a life interest: she could enjoy the property and its income, she could alienate it only for legal necessity or benefit of the estate, and on her death it passed not to her own heirs but to the next heir of the man from whom she had taken it. Marriage under the shastric law was a sacrament rather than a contract, indissoluble in principle, so that for most Hindus no court could dissolve a marriage however cruel or deserted it had become, although particular communities recognised divorce by custom and a few provincial legislatures had begun to legislate, Bombay enacting a Hindu divorce statute in 1947. Polygamy was lawful for a Hindu husband. Adoption was a religious act performed by a man for the spiritual benefit of his soul and his ancestors, and a woman could adopt only under authority derived from her husband. Caste restrictions governed the validity of marriages and adoptions, so that the law fastened the caste order into the most intimate transactions of life.

Reform of this body of law had proceeded, for a century, in fragments. The nineteenth century produced statutes against sati, for widow remarriage and against child marriage, each a single practice attacked in isolation. The early twentieth century added statutes on the age of marriage and on the disqualification of converts. The fragment that matters for the code's story came in 1937, when the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act, moved in the central legislature by G. V. Deshmukh, gave a Hindu widow an interest in her deceased husband's property, including a share in joint family property, though it left her with the limited estate and generated difficult questions about her position in the coparcenary she had come to share in but could not be a member of. The 1937 Act demonstrated both the appetite for legislative reform of Hindu law and the incoherence of reforming it one section at a time: a statute that gave the widow a share without saying what kind of estate she held in it, or how it interacted with the survivorship rule, exported its unanswered questions to the courts. The working of the 1937 Act in the courts exposed the defects that made a committee inevitable. The Act had to be construed against a body of law it did not displace, so its central gift to the widow, a share in her husband's property including an interest in the joint family estate, arrived wrapped in questions the statute had not answered: whether her interest could be converted into a defined share by partition, how it interacted with the rights of the coparceners among whom she then held, and what precisely she could alienate during her lifetime. Two amending measures followed within a few years to relieve particular hardships the Act had produced, and each fresh amendment demonstrated the same structural truth, that a reform drafted as an exception to an uncodified system inherits the system's incoherence and adds its own. By the end of the nineteen thirties the case for examining Hindu law as a whole, rather than amending it hardship by hardship, had been made by the litigation itself. It was the inadequacy of that Act, and the litigation it produced, that brought the Government of India to appoint a committee in 1941 to examine the working of the 1937 Act and the state of Hindu law more generally. The committee route, rather than a government bill, was the chosen instrument, and the choice shaped everything that followed, because it meant the code would arrive in the legislature as the product of an expert body that had toured the country taking evidence, carrying a claim to authority that was professional rather than electoral, and vulnerable to precisely the objection that it lacked a popular mandate.

The reform constituency outside the legislature was organised and had been for decades. The All India Women's Conference, founded in 1927, had made the codification of Hindu law and the raising of women's legal status a standing demand, and its members included women who would sit in the Constituent Assembly and argue for the code on its floor. Within the nationalist movement there were two distinguishable cases for codification, and they did not always pull together. One was the equality case, made by the women's organisations and by reformers in the tradition of the nineteenth century social reform movements, that the disabilities of Hindu women under the shastric law were indefensible in a republic that promised equality before the law. The other was the uniformity case, made by nation builders across the political spectrum, that a body of law divided by school, region, caste and custom was an obstacle to the consolidation of a single Indian citizenry, and that a code would do for Hindus what codes had done for crime and contract: replace a bewildering plurality with one ascertainable text. A third case, held by B. R. Ambedkar with a force the others did not match, treated the reform of Hindu personal law as inseparable from the annihilation of caste, because the law of marriage and adoption was the legal machinery by which caste reproduced itself. These three cases shared a bill but not a theory, and the differences among them would matter when the bill came under attack and its defenders had to decide which ground to hold.

## The Provincial and Princely Precedents

The code did not arrive in a vacuum, and its drafting committee worked in the knowledge that pieces of its programme were already law somewhere in India. Under the Government of India Act, 1935, marriage and divorce fell within the provincial legislative field, and the provinces had used the competence unevenly. Bombay, the province with the strongest tradition of social reform legislation, enacted a Hindu divorce statute in 1947, giving Hindus there a statutory route out of marriage that the code proposed to generalise, and the existence of a working provincial divorce law was deployed by the code's supporters as a standing answer to the prediction that divorce would dissolve Hindu society: the institution had been available in India's most commercial province without the sky falling. The central legislature itself had kept legislating in fragments around the code's edges. The Hindu Marriage Disabilities Removal Act, 1946 removed legal disabilities that had attached to marriages between Hindus of different castes and subcastes in certain circumstances, a direct ancestor of the code's proposal to strike caste out of the law of marriage altogether. Even more telling, while the comprehensive bill waited for time in the Constituent Assembly, Parliament found time in 1949 to enact the Hindu Marriages Validity Act, which declared marriages between Hindus, Sikhs, Jains and their castes and subcastes to be valid notwithstanding the parties' difference of caste. A single clause of the code's marriage part, detached and enacted alone, passed without convulsion in the very years when the whole could not be moved. The episode is a small prophecy of the code's eventual fate, delivered in advance: the legislature of the late nineteen forties was already capable of enacting the code's components and already incapable of enacting the code, and the difference lay not in the content of the propositions but in the size of the instrument that carried them. The princely states supplied further precedents of a different kind, since several of the larger states had maintained their own reforming law departments and had legislated on widow remarriage, marriage age and women's property within their territories, so that the notion of a Hindu ruler or a Hindu legislature remaking Hindu family law by enactment was not the novelty the bill's opponents sometimes claimed. What had never been attempted was the whole field at once, for the whole country, in one text. That was the code's distinction and its vulnerability.

## The Rau Committee and the Drafting of a Code

The Hindu Law Committee appointed in January 1941 was chaired by B. N. Rau, a judge of the Calcutta High Court who would later serve as Constitutional Adviser to the Constituent Assembly and as a judge of the International Court of Justice, and its original members included Dwarka Nath Mitter and R. V. V. Joshi. The committee's first report, submitted in 1941, accompanied two draft bills, one on intestate succession and one on marriage, and recommended that Hindu law be codified in stages, beginning with succession and proceeding to marriage and then to the remaining subjects, so that an agreed and uniform code would grow by instalments rather than descend complete. The two bills were considered by committees of the legislature, and a joint committee recommended that the Hindu Law Committee be revived and entrusted with preparing a complete code. The Government of India accepted that recommendation, and by a resolution of 20 January 1944 the committee was reconstituted with Rau again as chairman and Mitter, J. R. Gharpure and T. R. Venkatarama Sastri as members. The revived committee prepared a draft code dealing with succession, maintenance, marriage and divorce, minority and guardianship, and adoption. The draft, published for public comment, was circulated in twelve regional languages, and the committee toured the country examining witnesses, hearing the bar associations, the women's organisations, the religious bodies and the provincial governments, in one of the largest consultations ever conducted on an Indian legislative proposal. The exercise matters to the passage history because it fixed the code's character: this was to be the considered product of professional inquiry and public evidence, and its supporters would always argue that no bill had been more thoroughly examined before introduction, while its opponents would reply that consultation with organised bodies was no substitute for the verdict of the electorate.

The committee's final report, submitted in 1947, went far beyond consolidation. It recommended the abolition of the joint family property system itself, so that the coparcenary and the right by birth would disappear and property would devolve by succession for sons and daughters alike. It recommended that a daughter succeed simultaneously with a son to her father's estate. It recommended the removal of caste as a bar to marriage, the assimilation of civil and sacramental marriage, monogamy, and the introduction of divorce. Read as a whole, the draft code was not a restatement of Hindu law with modifications but a replacement of its organising principles: survivorship out, succession in; sacrament supplemented by contract; the limited estate abolished; caste struck out of the law of marriage and adoption. The government of the day intended the code to be enacted promptly, and a commencement at the start of 1948 was contemplated, but the transfer of power and the making of the Constitution consumed the legislature's time, and the code passed into the hands of the new republic's first Law Minister. The committee had, however, already supplied the two facts that determined the code's parliamentary fate. It had made the code comprehensive, so that every contested question in Hindu family law stood or fell together in one vote. And it had made the code radical at its economic centre, because the abolition of the coparcenary touched the property arrangements of the propertied classes who sat in and around the legislature, in a way that monogamy and divorce, for all the religious feeling they excited, did not.

The consultation deserves a closer look, because it was the committee's answer in advance to the question of legitimacy, and its limits prefigure the bill's defeat. The committee took evidence across the country from the organised interests the code would bind: the bar, whose members were divided between those who wanted an ascertainable text and those whose practices were built on the profitable uncertainty of the uncodified law; the women's organisations, which supported codification and pressed the committee to go further on property than it initially proposed; the religious and caste bodies, which ranged from qualified acceptance of consolidation to outright rejection of the state's competence in sacramental matters; and the provincial governments, several of which were legislating on the same subjects themselves. The draft that emerged was therefore not the product of a closed drafting room. It had been exposed, clause by clause, to the country it would govern, in twelve languages, years before it reached a legislature. Reformers drew from this the conclusion that the code was the most democratically prepared measure of its generation. The bill's opponents drew the opposite conclusion, and the dispute between the two conclusions is instructive: a consultation hears from organisations, and organisations are not the electorate. The orthodox bodies that mobilised against the code after 1948 were, in part, interests the committee had heard and not satisfied, and their campaign against the bill consisted precisely in denying that being heard was the same as being represented. Between those two positions there was no procedural compromise available, because they were not disagreements about the bill but about the source of legislative authority itself, which is why the argument was eventually settled not by a better committee but by a general election.

### When was the Hindu Code Bill first introduced in the legislature?

The Hindu Code Bill was introduced on 11 April 1947, a date its pilot B. R. Ambedkar himself put on record in his resignation statement four years later, when he observed that the bill, after a life of four years, had been killed. It entered the central legislature before independence, and the Constituent Assembly inherited it.

Before the code could be introduced in revised form, the Law Ministry worked over the Rau draft in 1948 and made alterations its authors described as small, softening some edges while keeping the architecture. The revised bill was arranged in eight parts. The first defined who was to count as a Hindu for the purposes of the code and swept caste away as a legal classification. The second dealt with marriage, the third with adoption, the fourth with minority and guardianship, the fifth with joint family property, which retained a joint family system in a remodelled unitary form while allocating property to women in ways the traditional law had never done, the sixth with women's property, and the seventh and eighth with succession and maintenance. Two features of this text deserve emphasis because they recur throughout the story. The definition of the governed class was drawn negatively and expansively: the code was to apply to Sikhs, Buddhists and Jains as well as to Hindus of every caste and sect, and in substance to any person who was not a Muslim, a Christian, a Parsi or a Jew. And the bill carried its reforms as a single indivisible proposal, so that a member who favoured divorce but feared the daughter's share, or favoured the daughter's share but abhorred divorce, was offered no way to record the distinction.

## The Bill as Drafted: Eight Parts and One Wager

The anatomy of the 1948 text repays attention, because the wager of comprehensiveness is visible in its table of contents. Part One settled the governed class and the conceptual foundations: it defined the persons to whom the code applied by the negative method the Acts retain, and it provided that caste, in the eyes of this law, would not determine capacity, so that the code began by legislating against the institution its opponents accused it of attacking only incidentally. Part Two, on marriage, made monogamy the rule, removed caste and prohibited relationship barriers in their shastric form, treated marriage as both sacrament and civil act so that a marriage could be solemnised by the rites of either party or registered as a civil union, and introduced judicial separation and divorce on grounds that included cruelty, desertion, incurable disease and the taking of a concubine. Part Three, on adoption, discarded the requirement that an adoption serve the spiritual ends of the adopter, removed caste as a condition of a valid adoption, and treated the adopted child's status as a civil fact. Part Four dealt with minority and guardianship, naming guardians and ordering their authority over the minor's person and property. Part Five, on joint family property, was the economic heart: the bill retained a joint family system in a remodelled and unitary form for all Hindus, displacing the regional schools, and within it allocated shares to women on a footing the traditional law withheld, while Part Six, on women's property, gathered the stridhan and the inherited estate into a regime of absolute ownership. Parts Seven and Eight completed the structure with succession, in which the widow and the daughter were placed in the first line of heirs taking with the son, and maintenance, in which the obligations of support among spouses, parents, children and dependants were stated as enforceable rights rather than moral duties. Two details show how far the drafting reached beyond the enacted Acts. The bill treated the property given to a daughter at her marriage as trust property held for her, claimable by her on attaining eighteen, so that neither her husband nor his family could waste it, a dowry provision aimed at converting a pernicious custom into a protected settlement, which found no place in the four Acts. And the bill's succession scheme, taken with the Rau draft behind it, pointed toward the end of the right by birth as the organising principle of Hindu property, the proposition on which the whole economic settlement turned and on which the enacted law would retreat furthest. The wager of the eight parts was that their combination was a strength: that marriage reform would carry property reform, that the women's organisations would mobilise for the whole, that a member impressed by any part would swallow the rest. The parliamentary history is the record of that wager being lost, clause by clause, to opponents who needed to win only the arguments about the parts their constituents feared.

## The Select Committee's Moderation

The Select Committee of 1948 is the understudied hinge of the whole history, because it was the first place where the draft met the arithmetic of a legislature and was trimmed accordingly. Ambedkar chaired it, and the committee he chaired returned a bill importantly different from the Rau text he had inherited. The Rau draft had proposed to abolish the joint family property system as such; the bill as reported retained a joint family system, remodelled into a single unitary form for all Hindus in place of the regional schools, with shares for women built into its devolution rather than substituted for it. The moderation was deliberate and it was structural, not cosmetic: abolition would have required the immediate reconstitution of every Hindu family's property holding in the country, while remodelling allowed the institution to continue under new rules of membership and succession. The government's own later documents testify to the committee's lasting influence. When the Hindu Succession Bill was introduced in 1954, its Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded that the original draft provisions on intestate succession in the Rau Committee's bill had undergone substantial changes in the hands of the Select Committee of 1948, and that the 1954 bill followed, to a large extent, the scheme the Select Committee had adopted. The line of descent matters. The enacted succession law of 1956 is not the Rau draft diluted at random by a decade of politics; it is, in its architecture, the Select Committee's bill of 1948, carried through the 1951 wreck and reintroduced as an instalment, which means the committee stage of the failed code did the drafting work the successful Acts inherited. There is a lesson in that for the reading of legislative failure generally. The comprehensive bill failed as a bill, but its committee proceedings were not wasted motion: they produced the moderated text, the tested arguments and the rehearsed majorities on which the instalments later drew, so that the four years of the code's parliamentary life functioned, in retrospect, as the longest committee stage in Indian legislative history, at the end of which the measure was reported, in effect, as four bills.

## The Bill in the Constituent Assembly: Committee, Consideration, Delay

Ambedkar moved that the bill be referred to a Select Committee of the Assembly, and the committee appointed was chaired by himself as Law Minister and numbered among its members Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, one of the leading lawyers of the Constitution's drafting, Bakshi Tek Chand, M. Ananthasayanam Ayyangar, P. S. Deshmukh, and three women members, G. Durgabai, Renuka Ray and Ammu Swaminathan, an overlap between the bill's parliamentary handlers and the organised reform constituency that its opponents noticed and used. The Select Committee made important changes to the bill, and on 31 August 1948 Ambedkar moved that the bill as reported by the committee be taken into consideration. Discussion, intermittent and crowded out by the Constitution's own making and by the emergencies of a partitioned country, ran through 1948 and 1949, and the Assembly adopted the motion for consideration on 19 December 1949. Then the bill entered the phase that kills more Indian legislation than any vote: the phase of no time. Through 1950 no time was allotted to it at all. In February 1951 it was taken up for three days and clause by clause consideration began, reaching only the opening provisions before the session's other business reclaimed the floor. In the arithmetic of the House, a bill of this size required weeks of sustained consideration; what it received, across four years, was days, separated by long silences in which the opposition outside the House organised and the government's attention moved elsewhere. Ambedkar pressed the Prime Minister repeatedly to allot time and to begin the debate at the start of the session rather than at its fag end, a complaint he had already made when moving the reference to the Select Committee, when he protested that so important a measure had come up for discussion with the session nearly over.

Delay suited almost everyone except the bill's authors. It suited the opponents inside the Congress Party, who could avoid the embarrassment of voting against their own government's flagship reform while ensuring it never reached a vote. It suited a government facing the refugee crisis, a war in Kashmir, the integration of the princely states, food scarcity and the drafting of a republic's first electoral rolls. It suited, in the short term, the Prime Minister, who supported the bill in principle and repeatedly said so, but who governed through a party in which the bill's enemies held senior office, including the presidency of the republic itself. What delay did not suit was the bill's legal position. A measure that is debated clause by clause over years, in fragments, accumulates amendments, concessions and expectations without ever acquiring the protection of enactment, and each resumption reopens questions the previous session had appeared to settle. The Hindu Code Bill was, by 1951, the most discussed and least decided major bill in the legislature's history, and its supporters outside the House, particularly the women's organisations that had campaigned for it since the Rau Committee's tour, watched a consultation they had won argument by argument produce no law.

Two episodes inside the long delay deserve record because they show the bill's enemies and its friends learning the same lesson at different speeds. The first is the fate of the consideration stage itself. When a bill is taken into consideration clause by clause in fragments across separated sessions, every clause becomes a fresh battlefield and no battlefield is ever finally won, because the text as a whole is never put. The opponents of the code understood this perfectly and used the procedure as their principal weapon, filling the allotted fragments with speeches whose object was the consumption of time rather than the persuasion of the House, secure in the knowledge that the government would not apply the closure to its own members on a measure it had declined to whip. The second episode is Nehru's intervention as the February 1951 sittings petered out, when he promised the bill's supporters that he would carry the question to the country and argue it in plain terms on its merits before the electorate. The promise was an admission that the argument could not be won in the provisional Parliament, and it relocated the bill's fate from the legislature to the election a year before the truncation forced the same conclusion on Ambedkar. A passage history should mark the symmetry: by the middle of 1951 both the bill's Prime Minister and its Law Minister knew the code would not pass the House that had discussed it for four years, and differed only on what should be salvaged from the wreck and on who should bear the blame for the wreckage.

## The Case for the Code, Stated at Its Strongest

The government's case began with codification itself. Ambedkar, introducing the bill, argued in substance that the rules of Hindu law were scattered across innumerable decisions of the High Courts and the Privy Council, forming what he described as a bewildering accumulation that the ordinary person could not ascertain and that generated constant litigation, and that a legislature which had codified the criminal law, the law of contract and the law of evidence for all Indians could not plead incapacity when it came to the law that governed the family life of the majority community. The codification argument was deliberately unglamorous, and it was the hardest for opponents to answer, because it conceded nothing to ideology: whatever the content of Hindu law was to be, there was no defence for its being unknowable. Onto that foundation the reform case proper was built. Its first storey was equality between the sexes. The bill gave the widow and the daughter the same share as the son in the property of a man dying intestate, converted the woman's limited estate into absolute ownership that she could dispose of as she pleased, gave a wife an enforceable claim to maintenance when she lived separately for recognised causes such as cruelty or a loathsome disease, made monogamy mandatory, and gave either spouse access to divorce on defined grounds. Its second storey was the attack on caste as a legal institution. The bill removed caste and subcaste as conditions of a valid marriage or a valid adoption, so that the law would cease to police the boundaries that the caste order required for its reproduction, and Ambedkar, who regarded endogamy as the essence of caste, understood this as the bill's deepest cut, deeper than any property provision. Its third storey was uniformity: one law for all Hindus in place of the schools and the local customs, completing for the majority community the consolidation that the national movement had promised and that the Directive Principle favouring a uniform civil code, placed in the Constitution during the same years, held out as an aspiration for the whole country.

The personnel of the case mattered as much as its arguments. Nehru lent the bill the authority of the Prime Ministership and of the national movement's social programme, and treated it publicly as a test of whether the new state would govern by its proclaimed principles. Within the Assembly, members such as G. Durgabai and Renuka Ray, who had served on the Select Committee, and women members generally, argued the bill from the floor with a command of its detail that embarrassed the suggestion, made outside, that the measure was a ministerial eccentricity. Hansa Mehta, who had represented India in the drafting of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, the Minister of Health, were among the prominent women of the Congress who stood with the reform. The All India Women's Conference kept the demand organised between sessions, passing resolutions, lobbying members and answering the orthodox campaign in the press. Against the objection that Hindu women themselves did not want the bill, the reformers could point to the only organised expression of Hindu women's opinion in existence, which supported codification, and against the objection that the measure was foreign, they could point to its drafting history, a committee of Indian lawyers applying Indian materials, and to the uncomfortable fact that the law it proposed to replace had been administered for a century and a half by British judges construing Sanskrit texts through translators. The reform case, at its strongest, was that the disabilities of Hindu women were not an inheritance the republic had chosen but a residue it had neglected to clear, and that a Parliament which had just enacted adult franchise for women owed them a family law consistent with the vote it had given them.

The constitutional limb of the reform case deserves separate statement, because it was constructed before the bill's troubles began and it disarmed in advance the objection that the state was incompetent to touch religious personal law. When the Constituent Assembly debated the fundamental rights, the question whether personal law counted as religion beyond legislative reach had been squarely raised, and K. M. Munshi, among the Assembly's leading lawyers, had rejected the proposition that inheritance and succession could be immunised as religion, arguing that the Constitution's own scheme permitted the state to legislate on secular activities associated with religious practice where social reform was the object, and that a contrary view would entrench precisely the discriminations the Assembly was assembled to abolish. Ambedkar, in the same debates, had warned that religion in India covered, in its inherited conception, the whole of life from birth to death, and that unless the legislature's power to reform the social system was preserved, the political democracy the Constitution created would sit on a social order that contradicted it at every point. The Hindu Code Bill was the first great test of that preserved power, and its supporters knew it: the bill's fate would decide whether the Assembly's carefully retained competence was a reality or a form of words. That is one reason the bill's abandonment stung its authors as more than a legislative defeat. It suggested that a power the Constitution had expressly kept could not in practice be used at scale against an organised religious objection, and the suggestion hung over every later exercise of the same power.

## The Case Against, Stated at Its Strongest

The opposition made three arguments, of increasing institutional seriousness, and the article's discipline requires that they be stated as their makers stated them, by ground rather than by characterisation. The first was religious. Hindu marriage, on this view, is a sanskara, a sacrament that constitutes the relationship it solemnises, and a sacrament cannot be dissolved by a court or conditioned by a statute without ceasing to be what it is; divorce and monogamy enforced by law were therefore not reforms of Hindu marriage but its replacement by a contractual institution wearing its name. The same objection ran to adoption, a religious act directed to the spiritual welfare of the adopter, which the code secularised into a child welfare transaction, and to the law of succession, where the order of heirs rested on the doctrine of spiritual benefit to the deceased. Lakshmi Kanta Maitra, speaking in the Assembly, added a codification objection of real juristic weight: the shastras themselves had left a wide field to established custom, custom had been the law's stabilising and adaptive force, and to force Hindu law into a statutory mould was to make it rigid and inflexible in a way its own tradition had never been, importing a character foreign to it. Outside the House, Swami Karpatri Maharaj, who founded the Ram Rajya Parishad in 1948, carried the religious objection into organised politics and challenged Ambedkar to public disputation on the scriptures, while the Hindu Mahasabha, whose leaders included N. C. Chatterjee, campaigned against the bill as an assault on the Hindu social order by a state that had no mandate to remake it.

The second argument was about the family and property, and it was the one with material interests behind it. The code's property provisions, above all the draft's abolition of the coparcenary and the daughter's simultaneous share, were said to threaten the disintegration of the joint family, the institution through which most Hindu property was held, most Hindu businesses were organised and most Hindu old age was provided for. Rajendra Prasad, in private correspondence with Vallabhbhai Patel, who shared reservations about the bill, put the conservative fear in a sentence that Guha's history of the period preserves in paraphrase: the code's new concepts were foreign to Hindu law and were capable of dividing every family. The argument deserves to be stated fairly, because it identified something true about the draft: the abolition of the right by birth was a revolution in the law of property for the majority community, proposed without the land records, the registration machinery or the credit arrangements that such a revolution would disturb, and the men who made the argument included many who had accepted, or even supported, reform of marriage while drawing the line at the coparcenary. The third argument was constitutional and political, and it was the strongest of the three. The Constituent Assembly and the provisional Parliament that succeeded it had been elected in 1946 on the restricted franchise of the Government of India Act 1935, a franchise that excluded the great majority of adults, including almost all the women whose law was being rewritten. For such a body to enact permanent social legislation of this magnitude, opponents argued inside the House and out, was to exceed the moral authority of a caretaker: Lakshmi Kanta Maitra told the Assembly that the legislature was, in his submission, not competent to deal with the matter, and whatever shelter was taken in legal form, there was no moral justification for passing the code. A connected political objection held that codifying the law of the majority community alone, while the personal laws of the minorities were left untouched in the name of the very religious freedom invoked against interference, was selective reform whose timing, in the immediate aftermath of Partition, burdened one community with the costs of a modernity the state did not propose to impose on others. That objection was made by orthodox Hindus who resented the singling out, and it has been made since by critics of the codification from entirely different positions, which is one measure of its durability.

The opposition was not external to the government; it was lodged inside it. The Congress Party contained the bill's most consequential enemies, among them the President of the republic, Rajendra Prasad, who had presided over the Constituent Assembly and made his views known from that chair as well, and the party's own president, Pattabhi Sitaramayya, while Patel, until his death in December 1950, was at best unenthusiastic. No party whip was issued for the bill's consideration, leaving Congress members free to consume its time in prolonged speeches, which they did. The government, in short, was attempting to carry the most contested social legislation of the century with a divided cabinet's party, against an organised religious campaign, under a President who doubted the measure and his own duty to assent to it, in a legislature whose electoral mandate its opponents plausibly denied. That is the configuration the comprehensive form of the bill had to survive, and a single instrument maximised every element of it: each clause's enemies could borrow the others' strength, the religious objector and the property objector and the mandate objector voting the same way for different reasons, while the bill's supporters were required to win every argument at once.

The reformers' replies to the three objections were also made at the time and belong in any balanced record. To the sacramental objection they replied that the bill regulated the civil consequences of marriage, its validity, its dissolution and the rights that flow from it, and left the rite itself to the parties, a distinction the enacted marriage Act preserved by allowing a marriage to be solemnised in accordance with the customary rites of either party, saptapadi included where the custom required it, so that the state claimed jurisdiction over the legal relation and disclaimed jurisdiction over the sacrament. To the property objection they replied that the joint family was not the idyll of the speeches: it was already being dissolved in practice by partition deeds, by urbanisation and by the individual property holding of the salaried classes, and the 1937 Act had already breached the principle that a woman could hold no interest in it, so the code was completing a movement the law had begun rather than initiating one. To the selectivity objection they replied that reform follows demand, that the demand for codification had arisen within Hindu society and its organisations over decades, and that a state which waited until every community's reformers were equally organised before reforming any community's law would reform nothing at all, an answer the minorities' own representatives in the Assembly substantially accepted by not opposing the bill's passage over their co-religionists' exclusion from it. None of these replies is conclusive, and the objections they answered have all been heard since in later debates, which is the mark of a genuine legislative controversy rather than a misunderstanding. The point for the passage history is narrower: the replies were good enough to carry each subject separately by 1955, and had never been the obstacle. The obstacle was the combination.

## The Government's Divided House

Between the case for the code and the case against it stood the institution that had to choose, and its internal geometry explains the outcome better than either case does. The Prime Minister supported the bill and said so in public and in correspondence, but his support had a tempo problem. In his letters to Ambedkar during 1951, quoted in the published correspondence and recounted in the standard histories, Nehru counselled patience: the opposition inside and outside the House was real, the Cabinet had decided the bill should be taken up at the beginning of September, and things should be taken easy in the interval. Each element of that advice was reasonable, and its cumulative effect was fatal, because the interval was the resource the opposition needed and the one thing the bill could not replenish. The Home Minister and the strongman of the party organisation, Vallabhbhai Patel, had reservations about the measure and about its timing which he communicated within the government; the President of the republic was against it; the president of the Congress Party was against it; and the Congress members of the legislature were under no whip in the matter, so that the government's nominal majority was never converted into a working one for this bill. A government in that condition has three options. It can make the measure a question of confidence and dare its own side to bring it down, the course Ambedkar in substance demanded. It can abandon the measure openly and pay the price in reputation once, the course the opposition would have accepted. Or it can postpone, session by session, paying the price in instalments while hoping the configuration changes, which is the course the government took for four years. Postponement had a constituency logic in a party preparing for the first general election, in which the Congress organisation in the provinces depended on local notables, landholders and religious figures among whom the code was unpopular, and in which Nehru himself was determined not to hand the orthodox parties a unifying grievance. The reformers' rejoinder, made then and repeated by historians since, is that the party overestimated the code's electoral cost and underestimated its own leader's appeal, since Nehru carried the election overwhelmingly while campaigning as a moderniser. Both judgments can be true together, and the passage record supports both: the election of 1951 and 1952 was won with the code unpassed, and the code was passed by the Parliament the election produced, which suggests the constraint all along was not the electorate but the provisional Parliament's composition and the men who led it.

## September 1951: The Truncation, the President's Note, the Resignation

The end came in a fortnight, and its sequence should be kept precise because each step altered the legal position of the next. On 15 September 1951 Rajendra Prasad wrote to Nehru, enclosing a note on the Hindu Code Bill. The note asserted two things of the first constitutional importance. Prasad recorded his view that the measure lacked the support of the Hindu community at large and was being pressed by what he characterised as a small minority, and he reserved to himself the right to examine the bill on its merits when it came to him for assent after passage, signalling that he did not regard his assent as a formality to be performed on advice. Nehru replied the same day in terms that have since been quoted in a judgment of the Supreme Court delivered in the Tamil Nadu Governor case, when the Court recounted this episode as the republic's first contest over the assent power: the President, Nehru wrote, had no power or authority to go against the will of Parliament in regard to a bill well considered and passed by it, and the whole conception of constitutional government was against any such authority. The government referred the question to the first Attorney General, M. C. Setalvad, who opined that the President's position was analogous to that of the British monarch and that he was a constitutional figurehead expected to act on the advice of his Council of Ministers. Prasad accepted the position, with a reluctance his later writings record, and the assent crisis was laid to rest without ever being tested, since the bill whose assent he had reserved never reached his desk. The episode left the series a permanent marker: the first assertion of an independent presidential discretion over assent in the republic's history was made over this bill, and it was made by a President whose objection was in part the very mandate objection the bill's opponents had been pressing in debate.

The parliamentary end ran parallel. Nehru, hoping to salvage the portion of the code that commanded the widest assent, proposed to proceed with the code in parts and to take up the marriage and divorce provisions first, as a truncated measure that might pass before the provisional Parliament dissolved. Ambedkar, who had argued for the code as a whole and who had spent four years watching comprehensiveness used as the reason for postponement, accepted the truncation as the only remaining route. Clause by clause consideration of the marriage and divorce part began on 17 September 1951. It did not survive the month. Members hostile to the measure used the clause stage to move amendments that deformed the provisions they could not defeat outright, attendance and momentum drained away, and accounts of the session agree that only four clauses had been disposed of when the government abandoned the part as hopeless. On 25 September 1951 the truncated bill was buried with the rest. The comprehensive code had died the death its structure invited, killed not by a division it lost but by a procedure it could not complete, and the truncation intended to save it had arrived four years too late to borrow the technique that would later save its contents.

Prasad's intervention of September 1951 was the second time he had placed himself between the code and enactment, and the continuity matters for judging the mandate objection's pedigree. As President of the Constituent Assembly he had already cautioned Nehru, in the period when the bill was first before the Assembly, against introducing basic changes in the personal law of a community on the strength of the progressive ideas of what he called a microscopic minority of its members, language preserved in the published accounts of the correspondence. The objection was of a piece across both interventions: not that the legislature lacked the legal competence, which as a lawyer Prasad did not seriously deny, but that a measure reconstructing the family law of the majority community required the demonstrated consent of that community expressed through a general election, and that until such consent existed the measure should wait. Stated in that form, the objection had a principled core that survived the bill, and it is the same objection, in modern dress, that attends every later proposal to legislate a uniform civil code in advance of the communities' consent. The reformers' answer was equally principled and equally durable: that a Parliament's authority to legislate for social reform does not wax and wane with the anticipated popularity of the reform, and that the women whose rights the bill created were themselves part of the community whose consent was being invoked against them by men who had never consulted them. The election of 1951 and 1952 did not decide between these two theories of consent. It merely made the question academic for a generation, by supplying the form of mandate Prasad had demanded, after which Nehru's government proceeded, with Prasad in the presidency assenting to each of the four Acts, in a closing of the constitutional circle that neither man commented on in public.

### How much of the Hindu Code Bill had Parliament passed by September 1951?

None of it had been enacted. Four years after introduction, the whole code had been considered only in fragments, the motion for consideration had been carried in December 1949, and when the truncated marriage and divorce part was abandoned in September 1951, only four of its clauses had been disposed of.

Ambedkar tendered his resignation from the cabinet on 27 September 1951. The statement in which he explained the decision is dated 10 October 1951, and it is a document the passage history must handle with care, because popular memory has simplified it into a resignation over a single bill, which the statement itself declines to be. It dealt with several matters: his differences with the government over the treatment of the Scheduled Castes, whose interests he held had been neglected by the administration he served; his disagreements over foreign policy; and the condition of his health and position within a cabinet in which, as he put it in the statement's broader complaints, he had never been given the portfolios or the standing his office implied. But the statement is explicit about which matter finally decided him, and the sentence in which he turned to it is the hinge of the whole episode: he would deal, he wrote, with the matter which had finally led him to decide to resign, and that matter was the treatment accorded to the Hindu Code. He recorded that the bill had been introduced on 11 April 1947 and that after a life of four years it had been killed, describing it in a phrase whose exact wording varies slightly between published accounts as having died unwept and unsung. He called the code, in the statement's characterisation, the greatest social reform measure undertaken by the legislature, and he framed the government's abandonment of sex and class inequality, the soul, as he argued, of the social order the code addressed, while it legislated on economic questions, as building a palace upon a dung heap; the metaphors have been quoted so often, in so many variants, that a careful account paraphrases them, as this one does, rather than pretend to a verbatim text. The resignation's legislative significance is separate from its personal one. It removed from the government the one minister for whom the code was a first charge rather than an inheritance, and it converted the bill's failure from a parliamentary misfortune into a public indictment of the governing party's sincerity, an indictment the party then had to answer at the first general election and in the Parliament that election produced. There the matter would have rested, as the most famous failed bill of the founding period, had the government not drawn from the failure the correct operational conclusion.

## The Campaign Outside the House

The parliamentary story cannot be separated from the campaign that ran alongside it, because the campaign supplied the pressure to which the government's tempo was a response. On the orthodox side the mobilisation was formidable and inventive. The Hindu Mahasabha made opposition to the code a central plank, framing it as the thin end of a state project to dissolve Hindu society's legal personality, and Swami Karpatri Maharaj gave the campaign a religious command structure, touring, preaching and organising through the Ram Rajya Parishad, the party he founded in 1948 substantially for this fight. The orthodox campaign's demands escalated beyond amendment. Its leaders demanded that the bill be withdrawn altogether, that it be submitted to a referendum of Hindu opinion, and that no legislature act on it until a Parliament elected on adult franchise had spoken, demands pitched deliberately so that the government could concede time without conceding the principle, which is what it repeatedly did. The campaign also worked the constituency level, where it mattered most: orthodox opinion in the districts was organised through religious networks that Congress candidates would face at the coming election, and the message delivered to the party's provincial bosses was that the code would be paid for in seats. On the reform side, the counter mobilisation was led by the women's organisations, whose campaign was older than the bill itself. The All India Women's Conference and its provincial branches passed resolutions, sent deputations, lobbied members between sessions and maintained a press campaign arguing the bill's clauses in detail, and women members of the Assembly carried the floor fight. The asymmetry between the two campaigns is itself a finding of the passage history. The orthodox campaign could threaten the governing party with electoral consequences in the present tense. The reform campaign could only promise a constituency of gratitude in the future, composed substantially of women who, until the election of 1951 and 1952, had no votes with which to make the promise good. A government managing its survival will listen to the first kind of campaign over the second every time, and it did, until the franchise itself was widened and the arithmetic changed.

## The Aftermath for the Bill's Pilot

Ambedkar's resignation did not end his connection with the code's contents, and the dates at the end of his life give the passage history a closing symmetry that no novelist would risk. He entered the Rajya Sabha in 1952 and sat in Parliament through the years in which Pataskar carried the four bills, a former Law Minister watching his draft enacted in instalments by the government he had left, with the coparcenary concession he had not been willing to make made in his presence. In October 1956 he led his followers into Buddhism at Nagpur, an act continuous with the argument of his whole career, that the legal reform of Hinduism from within had been tried and had reached its limit. He died on 6 December 1956. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, the last of the four instalments and the statute that completed the code's coverage of its subjects, received the President's assent on 21 December 1956, a fortnight later. The code he had piloted as a single bill thus became complete law within weeks of his death, in a form he had not chosen, by a method he had resisted and then accepted in despair, and the judgment of the passage record is that the form, more than any man's advocacy, was the variable that decided the outcome.

## The Election as the Answer to the Mandate Objection

The general election of 1951 and 1952, the first conducted on universal adult franchise, was not fought on the Hindu Code, and no honest account should pretend the electorate delivered a specific verdict on divorce or the daughter's share. What the election did was more mechanical and, for the bill's prospects, more decisive. It dissolved the provisional Parliament whose restricted franchise had been the opposition's best argument, and replaced it with a Lok Sabha every member of which had been elected by the women the code proposed to benefit as well as by the men it proposed to restrain. The mandate objection, which had been unanswerable as a matter of political morality in 1951, whatever its weakness as a matter of strict law, simply ceased to be available: a member who wished to oppose the succession bill in 1954 had to oppose it on its merits, in a House that owed its seats to the very electorate in whose name postponement had been demanded. Nehru, who had promised the bill's supporters during the 1951 debates that he would carry the argument to the country in plain terms on the merits, treated the Congress victory as removing the procedural excuse. The Congress manifesto and the government's programme for the new Parliament included the reform of Hindu law, and Nehru's majority, returned with a commanding position in the Lok Sabha, gave the government the numbers it had formally possessed, but could not discipline, in the provisional Parliament. The change in personnel at the Law Ministry mattered as well, though in a direction a simple heroic account would not predict. The bills of 1955 and 1956 were piloted not by a reformer of Ambedkar's stamp but by H. V. Pataskar, a senior Congress lawyer from Bombay who had spoken for codification in the Assembly debates of 1948 on the ground, as he then put it, that the country was to be a single secular state rather than a confessional one, and who was sympathetic to aspects of the Hindu consolidation the code represented. Pataskar was a manager of legislation rather than its prophet, and the settlement he carried reflects that: the four Acts bear the impress of Ambedkar's draft in their architecture and of Nehru's and Pataskar's parliamentary arithmetic in their concessions.

### Did the first general election change the fate of the Hindu Code?

Yes, mechanically rather than rhetorically. The election replaced the provisional Parliament elected on a restricted franchise, against which the mandate objection had real force, with a Lok Sabha elected by universal adult franchise, so the code's successor bills could no longer be postponed in the electorate's name.

## Unbundling: Four Bills, Four Arguments, Four Majorities

The government's central decision after the election was the decision this article exists to record. The code would not be reintroduced as a code. Its subjects would be severed and introduced as separate bills, marriage first, then succession, minority and guardianship, and adoptions and maintenance, each to be debated, amended and carried on its own support. The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the succession bill described it, in the government's own words, as the third instalment of the Hindu Code, a phrase that concedes the whole strategy: the code had been converted into a serial, and its unity survived only as a label on the instalments. The effects of severance were immediate and structural. The opposition could no longer pool itself. A member moved by the religious objection to divorce had no occasion to vote against the guardianship bill, which excited no religious feeling at all. A member defending the coparcenary could concede monogamy, which cost his constituents nothing, and concentrate his fire on the one bill that touched his interests. The women's organisations could campaign for each instalment on ground of their choosing. And the government could sequence: marriage, where the reform case was most popular and the property interests least engaged, went first and established the principle that Parliament would legislate on Hindu personal law at all; the harder economic bill followed once the principle was a precedent rather than a proposition.

The succession bill shows what severance did and did not do to the content. When the Law Ministry presented the Hindu Succession Bill to Parliament in May 1954, it had already retreated from the Rau draft at the decisive point: the bill excluded the Mitakshara coparcenary from its purview, and Pataskar defended the exclusion in Parliament on the express ground that to make daughters coparceners would render the joint family unviable. The parliamentary Joint Committee that examined the bill pushed back, and the account of that committee's work given by Narendra Subramanian in his study of personal law and gendered citizenship records the nature of the bargain: the committee, whose members included the communist legislators Renu Chakravartty and Parvathi Krishnan pressing the claims of the natal family, applied the bill to Mitakshara joint property so far as devolution on death was concerned, gave daughters a share in the father's interest when succession opened, and raised the daughter's share in the father's separate property from one half of the son's share, which is where the ministry's bill had left it, to equality with the son. The episode deserves its weight in the history, because it shows a parliamentary committee functioning as the reform's second engine: the ministry had priced the bill for passage by surrendering the coparcenary, the committee judged that the surrender had been overpaid on the separate property question, where conservative attention was weakest, and recovered equality there, while accepting the surrender where resistance was organised. Committee stages are usually where Indian bills go to be delayed. In this passage they were where one bill went to be improved, and the contrast with the comprehensive code's experience, debated for years without a committee ever being allowed to settle its text into a defensible shape, is part of the unbundling dividend. Membership of the coparcenary itself remained confined to male kin, who alone could demand partition as an entitlement. This is the introduced and enacted gap in its purest form, and it ran in the opposite direction from the usual story of dilution: the ministry's bill was more conservative than the draft code, the committee pulled it partway back toward the draft, and the enacted Act stopped deliberately short of the draft's central economic proposition, the abolition of the right by birth. What severance purchased was passage; what it cost was the coparcenary, which the government surrendered before the bill was even introduced, as the price of detaching the succession question from the siege the comprehensive code had endured.

### What was dropped from the Hindu Code between the draft and the four Acts?

Three large things. The abolition of the coparcenary and the right by birth, the draft's economic centre, was surrendered before the succession bill was introduced. The dowry provision treating a bride's marriage gifts as trust property in her hands disappeared. And caste ceased to be abolished as a general legal classification, surviving instead through the customs the Acts preserved.

## The Four Passages, One by One

The severed bills began reaching Parliament from 1952 onward, and the order of their arrival repeated the order of the code's own parts, marriage at the head, which was a decision about politics disguised as a decision about drafting. Marriage reform had the longest public pedigree, the clearest organisational support and the least property at stake, and a government testing whether the new Parliament would legislate on personal law at all had every reason to ask that question first on the subject where the answer was likeliest to be yes. Each bill then travelled the ordinary road the code had been denied the time to travel: introduction, a general discussion in which the old arguments were rehearsed in their subject specific forms, reference in the succession bill's case to a joint committee of both Houses, clause by clause consideration in which amendments could be priced and traded within the four corners of one subject, and passage by the two Houses in turn. The contrast with 1948 to 1951 is not that the arguments had changed; the debates of 1955 and 1956 contain the sacramental objection, the family disintegration objection and the selectivity objection in recognisably their earlier forms. The contrast is that each argument had to be won afresh against the specific bill it addressed, in a House elected to decide such questions, with a government prepared to use its majority. Procedure, mandate and severance, the three variables the comprehensive bill had lacked, were all three supplied, and the result followed in nineteen months.

The marriage bill came first, and its passage shows severance working as designed and dilution working as usual, both at once. Detached from the property questions, the bill's contested ground narrowed to monogamy and divorce, on which public argument had been running for a generation and on which the women's organisations could campaign without frightening the propertied. Yet even here the enacted text stopped short of the draft in a way the passage record makes visible. As Narendra Subramanian's account of the parliamentary handling records, the Act as passed in 1955 treated the matrimonial offences unevenly: divorce was available at once where a spouse had converted to a religion originating outside South Asia, while cruelty, desertion and adultery grounded only judicial separation, with divorce following after a further interval, a hierarchy of grounds that survived until the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act, 1976 placed divorce on an even footing. The asymmetry is a fingerprint of the legislative bargaining of 1955. Conversion threatened the community's boundary and could therefore dissolve a marriage instantly; cruelty threatened only the wife, and was made to wait. The succession bill, debated through 1955 and enacted in 1956, has been described in the section on unbundling above through its committee bargain, and it remains the clearest case in the whole episode of a bill whose decisive amendment occurred before introduction, in the Law Ministry's decision to spare the coparcenary. The guardianship bill was the quietest of the four, precisely as the strategy predicted: a subject on which the religious objection had no purchase and the property objection only a marginal one, it passed with a fraction of the attention the marriage and succession bills consumed, and its main doctrinal choice, to supplement the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 rather than displace it, was a lawyer's solution that excited no one's constituency. The adoptions and maintenance bill, last of the series, combined the code's most sacramental subject, adoption, with its most material, maintenance, and carried both in the slipstream of three enactments that had established the principle. By December 1956 the legislature that had been unable to consider four clauses of a truncated code in September 1951 had enacted the code's entire substance in four statutes in nineteen months, not because its members had been persuaded of anything new, but because the questions had been put to it one at a time, in an order that asked the easy questions first, by a House whose mandate no one could discount. The orthodox campaign of 1948 to 1951 reassembled against the instalments, but it reassembled in pieces, and its fragmentation is the empirical core of this article's thesis. Against the marriage bill it could deploy the sacramental argument at full strength, and did, but the sacramental argument had nothing to say to the guardianship bill, and the organisations built to fight a code found themselves fighting a queue of dissimilar measures, each requiring its own campaign, its own literature and its own parliamentary watch, against a government that needed to win only the argument in front of it. The property interest, conversely, concentrated on the succession bill and largely let the marriage bill go, having calculated, correctly, that monogamy and divorce did not touch its holdings. No instalment faced the united front the code had faced, because no instalment gave the front a common object. That is the whole mechanism, visible in the lobbying record of 1955 and 1956 as plainly as in the division lobbies.

The sequence of enactment is itself part of the record a student of this history should carry away. The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, Act 25 of 1955, received the President's assent on 18 May 1955 and came into force the same day. The Hindu Succession Act, 1956, Act 30 of 1956, received assent on 17 June 1956. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, Act 32 of 1956, received assent on 25 August 1956. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, Act 78 of 1956, received assent on 21 December 1956, closing the series nineteen months after it had opened and five years after the single bill that contained all four subjects had been abandoned. Each Act carried the common definition of its governed class, applying to a person who is a Hindu by religion in any of its forms or developments, expressly including a Virashaiva, a Lingayat and a follower of the Brahmo, Prarthana or Arya Samaj, to a person who is a Buddhist, a Jain or a Sikh by religion, and residually to any other person domiciled in the territory who is not a Muslim, a Christian, a Parsi or a Jew by religion, unless it is proved that he would not have been governed by Hindu law had the Act not been passed. The definition was inherited from the bill's first part, and it survived severance untouched, because it was the one element every instalment needed identically and the one whose contestation could be postponed to the courts.

### Who piloted the four Hindu code Acts through Parliament?

H. V. Pataskar, the Union Minister of Law in 1955 and 1956, a Bombay Congress lawyer who had spoken for codification in the debates of 1948. The bills bear Ambedkar's architecture, but their passage, their sequencing and their concessions were the work of a minister managing a majority rather than drafting a manifesto.

## What the Four Acts Did, and What They Pointedly Did Not Do

The substance carried across can be stated shortly, since this article owns the passage and not the provisions, whose detailed treatment belongs to the statute articles of the cluster. The marriage Act, whose own passage and provisions are treated in [the statute article on the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955](/2016/10/01/hindu-marriage-act-1955-explained/), made a subsisting prior marriage a disqualification and a ground of nullity, so that monogamy became a condition of a valid Hindu marriage rather than a moral exhortation, and it created judicial separation and divorce on specified grounds, breaking the sacramental indissolubility for the population the Act governed. The succession Act placed the daughter in Class I of its Schedule, taking simultaneously with the son, the widow and the mother in the separate property of a male dying intestate, and by its fourteenth section it provided that property possessed by a female Hindu is held by her as full owner and not as limited owner, extinguishing the limited estate at a stroke for property within its reach. The guardianship Act named the natural guardians of a Hindu minor and the order among them, supplementing rather than replacing the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890. The adoptions and maintenance Act gave a Hindu woman capacity to take a child in adoption in her own right where she was unmarried, widowed or divorced, and placed the obligations of maintenance, of a wife, of widowed daughters in law, of children and aged parents, and of dependants, on a statutory footing with defined enforcement. The general map of the field these Acts inhabit, and of the personal laws of the other communities that were not codified in this episode, is drawn in [the guide to personal laws in India](/2016/09/01/personal-laws-india-complete-guide/), to which the reader is referred for the frame within which this history sits.

What the Acts did not do is as much a part of the passage record as what they did, because the omissions were decisions taken during passage, not oversights. The Mitakshara coparcenary survived, with the son's right by birth and the rule of survivorship intact, so that the largest single category of Hindu family wealth continued to devolve outside the law of succession the Act had just enacted, and a daughter was an heir to her father's separate property while remaining a stranger to his coparcenary interest except through the notional partition the Act devised for computing a deceased coparcener's share. Agricultural land was substantially left to the state tenancy and land reform laws, which in several states restricted the devolution the Act proclaimed. Testamentary freedom was left untouched, so a Hindu could will away the separate property the Act made heritable by daughters, a freedom the reformers accepted as the price of the intestacy rules. The gap at the centre, the coparcenary, stood for half a century as the standing refutation of the claim that 1956 had completed the reform, until Parliament returned to it in the amendment whose passage and effect are treated in [the article on the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/). The guardianship Act's choices repay a paragraph of their own, because they show the instalment method producing a settlement no comprehensive debate would have reached so quietly. The Act named the natural guardians of a Hindu minor in a statutory order, the father first and after him the mother for a boy and for an unmarried girl, with the custody of a child below the age of five ordinarily with the mother, and it declared in its thirteenth section that the welfare of the minor is the paramount consideration in any appointment or declaration of guardianship by a court. The order it enacted was the traditional one, and reformers noticed that a Parliament enacting the father's priority in 1956 was codifying a hierarchy the equality clauses of the Constitution pointed against, a tension the courts were left to work out in the custody litigation of later decades. But the welfare principle it enacted alongside the hierarchy was the code's own, and in practice it became the lever by which courts moderated the hierarchy the same statute had just affirmed. The pairing is characteristic of the instalments: a conservative rule and a reforming principle in adjacent sections, each the price of the other, the whole carried because no single clause had to bear the weight of the code's reputation. The comprehensive bill had proposed to settle guardianship as one part among eight and would have forced the House to vote the father's position in the shadow of the divorce and coparcenary fights; the instalment let the question be argued as a question about children, which is what it was.

The maintenance scheme of the fourth Act illustrates the code's method in miniature, and it is worth a paragraph because maintenance was the subject on which the gap between the shastric duty and the legal right had been widest. Under the prior law a wife's claim to be maintained was real but remedially frail, entangled with the conditions of the joint household and with doctrines about her conduct and residence that made the right difficult to enforce against a husband determined to resist it. The Act stated the obligations as a series of enforceable claims: a wife's claim against her husband during her lifetime, a widowed daughter in law's claim against her father in law in defined circumstances, the claims of children and of aged or infirm parents, and the claims of dependants against the estate of a deceased person, with the quantum to be fixed by the court on stated considerations and the claim capable of being secured as a charge on property. The drafting technique is the codifier's characteristic one, visible across all four Acts: take a duty the tradition acknowledged in principle, sever it from the household structure that had made it unenforceable, restate it as a right held by a named person against a named person, and give a court the power to quantify and enforce it. For a reader tracing a family claim, this history explains why the law has the shape it has; the position in any individual matter turns on the Acts and their later amendments, on which specific advice should be taken rather than a general account relied on.

## The Definition That Outlived the Debate

The inclusion of Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs within statutes named for Hindus was contested from the beginning and has never been wholly settled in the minds of the communities concerned, and the honest treatment is to state the positions rather than resolve them. The legislative position was one of classification for the purposes of family law. The communities swept in were those whose family customs had historically been administered as part of, or by analogy to, Hindu law, and the Constitution itself, in the Explanation to the religious freedom article dealing with the throwing open of Hindu religious institutions, directs that the reference to Hindus be construed as including a reference to persons professing the Sikh, Jain or Buddhist religion, so the draftsmen of the code were following a constitutional usage rather than inventing one. The communities' position, pressed by Sikh bodies in particular, was and remained that a distinct religious identity is not respected by a statute that administers their marriages under another faith's name, whatever its administrative convenience, and Sikh organisations sought separate statutory recognition of the Anand Karaj marriage rite, a demand partly met when the Anand Marriage (Amendment) Act, 2012 provided for the registration of marriages solemnised by that rite, without removing Sikh marriages from the Hindu Marriage Act's substantive governance. Jain bodies pressed a different but cognate claim, for recognition of the community as a distinct religious minority, a claim that reached the Supreme Court in Bal Patil v Union of India in 2005, where the Court treated Jainism as a distinct religion and observed that its identity was not in doubt, while declining to direct the government to issue the notification the petitioners sought. The definition also carried a working jurisprudence in its Explanation, which the succession Act's text states and the other Acts repeat: a child both of whose parents are Hindus, Buddhists, Jains or Sikhs is within the class; a child one of whose parents belongs to the class and who is brought up as a member of that parent's community is within it; and a convert or reconvert to any of the four religions is within it. Those clauses, rather than the headline inclusion, are what the courts have actually applied, in the long run of cases about conversion, mixed marriages and children whose religious upbringing was contested, and they show the draftsmen solving a classification problem with a functional test of upbringing and affiliation rather than a theological one. A related judicial consequence of codification belongs to the same afterlife. In State of Bombay v Narasu Appa Mali, decided in 1952 while the code still waited, the Bombay High Court held that the personal laws, in their uncodified form, were not laws in force within the meaning of the Constitution's provision voiding laws inconsistent with fundamental rights, so that uncodified custom stood substantially outside the equality and freedom guarantees. Whatever the merits of that much debated holding, its structural implication for the codification was sharp: the moment Hindu family law was enacted as a statute it became fully answerable to the fundamental rights, which the shastric law it displaced was not. Codification therefore changed not only what the law said but the constitutional discipline to which it was subject, and several of the code's early critics understood that the statute form itself, and not merely its contents, was a transfer of power from the tradition to the Constitution.

Scholars of the codification have accordingly described the definition in two incompatible vocabularies, as legislative convenience in the service of uniformity, and as an act of absorption that folded three distinct traditions into a statutory Hinduism for reasons of state. The article records both descriptions and adopts neither, because the question whether a classification for family law purposes asserts a religious identity is one the litigation has posed in several forms without any judgment settling it at the level of principle, and a passage history has no warrant to settle what the courts have left open.

One boundary of the definition was drawn the other way, and it shows the draftsmen making a distinction the critics of absorption sometimes miss. The four Acts, following the bill, excluded members of the Scheduled Tribes from their operation unless the Central Government directed otherwise by notification, leaving tribal communities to their own customary law of marriage, succession and adoption. Subramanian's study records that the exclusion reflected the settled preference of legislators from those communities themselves, who did not want a code framed for caste Hindu society applied to tribal custom, and the exclusion was carried in the enacted Acts, the second section of the succession Act stating it expressly. The code's universality therefore had two different edges. Toward the Buddhist, Jain and Sikh communities it was inclusive against at least some of their organisations' wishes, on the juridical ground that their family law had historically been administered with Hindu law. Toward the tribal communities it was exclusive at their representatives' own instance, on the ground that their customs were distinct systems the code had no business displacing. Whether those two edges can be reconciled under one principle is a live question in the case law and the scholarship, and it is recorded here, like the larger definition question, as a position stated by each side rather than a conclusion this article adopts.

## The Two Readings of the Codification

Two readings of this episode compete in the literature, and both rest on facts the preceding sections have stated, which is why the dispute between them is a dispute about weight rather than about evidence. The reform reading holds that the codification was the young republic's clearest act of constitutional fidelity: a Parliament that had promised equality dismantled, for the majority community, a family law that subordinated women in marriage, property and adoption, and it did so against the organised resistance of religious authority and propertied interest, at genuine political cost, twice. On this reading the concessions, above all the coparcenary, mark the limits of the possible in 1956 rather than a failure of conviction, and the arc from the Rau draft through the 2005 amendment is a single reform proceeding by stages toward the draft's destination, with the daughter's coparcenary right arriving fifty years late but arriving as the draft's logic required. The critical reading, made by writers of otherwise opposed persuasions, answers that the codification legislated for one community and left the others uncodified, so that the state's modernising energy was spent where resistance was internal to its own majority while minorities were assured their personal laws were safe from the same treatment, a selectivity whose consequences run through [the continuing debate on a uniform civil code for India](/2017/01/01/uniform-civil-code-debate-india/) to this day. It adds that the preserved coparcenary was not a detail but the substance, since joint family property was where Hindu wealth was, and that a reform which gave daughters equality in the property their fathers held separately while excluding them from the property their fathers held jointly equalised the smaller estate and conserved the larger. The legal scholar Flavia Agnes, in her work on law and gender inequality, pressed the further point that codification itself is a double edged instrument for women, since a code freezes the law it reforms and subjects customary arrangements, some of which served women in particular communities, to a uniform text administered by courts they may not reach. Narendra Subramanian's study offers a fourth, cooler reading that cuts across the other three: that the drive behind the codification, for many of its parliamentary managers, was the consolidation of a Hindu community defined broadly enough to hold its sects and its neighbouring traditions together as the demographic foundation of the nation, a project in which women's rights were a real but instrumental part. A passage history cannot referee among these readings, but it can insist on the discipline this article has tried to observe: the arguments of 1948 to 1956 should be reported in the terms their makers used, the constitutional objection about the mandate should not be reclassified as orthodoxy, the religious objection should not be reclassified as a property interest in disguise, and the property objection should not be reclassified as either. Each was what it was, and the bill fell to their combination.

## The Unbundling Lesson and the Series Thesis

The namable claim of this article can be stated in its full form. Unbundle to pass. The comprehensive Hindu Code Bill failed, between 1948 and 1951, not because any single one of its propositions lacked a majority but because the single instrument pooled the enemies of all of them, while obliging its friends to defend every proposition at once and giving a hostile procedure, the indefinite withholding of time, a single throat to seize. The same content, divided into four bills between 1952 and 1956, passed, because each bill could be carried by the majority that existed for that subject, at a moment chosen for that subject, with concessions priced for that subject alone. The taxation cluster of this series identified the same mechanism at the other end of the legislative century: the comprehensive recodifications that tried to replace a working code in a single instrument fared as the Hindu Code had fared, and [the history of the Direct Taxes Code Bill](/2012/10/01/direct-taxes-code-bill-india/), a single comprehensive replacement that was introduced, examined and allowed to lapse without enactment, is the modern instance whose absence of an unbundling strategy this article's thesis predicts. The Hindu Code episode is earlier than the tax episodes by half a century and cleaner than either, because the same government, the same party and substantially the same text appear on both sides of the comparison, the only variable that changed being the number of instruments into which the reform was divided, together with the electoral mandate whose absence had served as the excuse for inaction. A legislator or a staffer reading this history for use should extract the operational rules rather than the slogan. Sequence the instalments so the popular principle precedes the expensive one, because the marriage Act's passage in 1955 was the succession Act's best argument in 1956. Price concessions inside each bill rather than across the package, so that a concession on the coparcenary does not have to be repaid on divorce. Let each bill acquire its own committee record and its own majority, so that no single hostile sitting can kill the whole reform. And recognise the cost with open eyes: unbundling converts a principled whole into a series of bargains, and the bargains are struck where the interests are strongest, which in this history meant that the most radical economic proposition in the draft was the one traded away. The comparison with the founding generation's other great comprehensive instrument sharpens the claim rather than blunting it. The Constitution itself was comprehensive, and it passed, but it was not carried as a single undifferentiated text through a single undifferentiated debate: the Constituent Assembly divided its work among subject committees whose reports were taken up, debated and settled separately, so that the most comprehensive instrument in Indian history was itself assembled by a process of internal unbundling, each part acquiring its own record and its own settlement before the whole was adopted. The Hindu Code's sponsors, sitting in the same building in the same years, attempted the opposite procedure for their instrument, one bill, one debate, one fate, and the contrast between the two procedures is the closest thing the founding period offers to a controlled experiment in legislative method. Where the subject committees of the Assembly converted a vast design into a series of winnable questions, the code's single motion for consideration converted a series of winnable questions into one vast design that could be, and was, talked to death.

Two features of the enacted Acts, often treated as technicalities, belong to the passage record because they were the points on which the code's unitary ambition was either kept or qualified. The first is territorial and customary reach. The Acts extended to the whole of India as the succession Act's own text records, the State of Jammu and Kashmir excepted, and each Act carried an overriding provision abrogating the texts, customs and usages inconsistent with it, which is what converted the code's promise of uniformity from an aspiration into a rule of decision for the courts: after 1956 a custom could survive, where the Acts saved it expressly, but it could no longer contradict the statute. The saving of custom in particular corners, the ceremonies of marriage being the conspicuous one, was itself a passage concession, the price of the sacramental objection, and it left the Acts with their characteristic double nature, uniform in their rights and plural in their forms. The second feature is the treatment of the communities at the margin, the Scheduled Tribes exclusion recorded above, which was written into the Acts at the instance of the excluded communities' own legislators and which survived every later amendment of the succession law. Both features were present in embryo in the comprehensive bill, and both survived severance unchanged, a reminder that unbundling redistributed the politics of the reform without reopening every question the draft had settled.

## The Archive of a Failure

Few failed bills leave a record as complete as this one, and the record is part of the article's payload for the reader who wants to go further. The Rau Committee's reports and its published draft state the reform in its undiluted form. The debates of the Constituent Assembly acting as legislature, and then of the provisional Parliament, preserve the arguments in the words of the members who made them, including the select committee motion with its list of members and the resignation statement of the Law Minister, which is printed in the official collection of Ambedkar's writings and speeches. The correspondence between Nehru and Rajendra Prasad, including the exchange of 15 September 1951, is preserved in the published papers of the Prime Minister and in the President's, and the opinion of Attorney General Setalvad can be traced through his published memoir and through the Supreme Court's later recounting of the episode. A reader assembling this material into a working file of the codification, the committee draft beside the enacted sections, the debate extracts beside the correspondence, will find that the file grows into a small archive of its own, and the discipline of keeping such an archive in one private place is exactly what [keeping the drafting record and one's own statute notes together in a private encrypted workspace on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) is designed to support. The archive also corrects the standard errors into which the compressed versions fall, and a reader of this history should be able to name them. The comprehensive code was never enacted, in 1951 or at any other time, and accounts that describe India as having adopted a Hindu Code in a single statute are describing an event that did not occur. The four Acts are not all of one year: the marriage Act is of 1955 and the other three of 1956, and the succession Act is not the marriage Act's twin but its successor by a year and a separate parliamentary history. The resignation was not tendered on the day the bill was dropped, nor over the bill alone: it followed two days after the abandonment of the truncated part, and its own statement ranges across the government's treatment of the Scheduled Castes and its foreign policy before arriving at the matter that finally decided its author. And the four Acts are not Ambedkar's Acts in the simple sense the slogans suggest: he had been out of the government for five years when the last of them passed, the minister who carried them was Pataskar, and the text they enacted was the Select Committee's moderation of the Rau draft as further moderated by the Law Ministry of 1954, which is a lineage with at least four authors, only one of whom is remembered. Precision on these points is not pedantry. Each error flatters a side: the enacted code flatters the state, the single cause resignation flatters the cult of its pilot, the single authorship flatters everyone who prefers heroes to committees, and the passage record, read whole, flatters nobody, which is why it is worth keeping in a file of one's own.

The archive matters because the episode is not closed. The Hindu Code is invoked in every subsequent argument about personal law reform in India, usually in compressed and sloganised form, as precedent by reformers who want a uniform code and as warning by those who resist one, and the compressed versions almost always omit the operative fact this article has tried to restore: that the code's history is not a story of a reform defeated or a reform triumphant, but of a reform whose fate was decided by the form in which it was presented to the legislature.

## What the Passage Record Predicted

Template and precedent are the two products of a passage history, and this one produced both. The precedent was personal law reform by instalment as the standing method of the Indian state. The half century after 1956 is a catalogue of the method. The imperfections of the marriage Act were corrected by instalment, as in 1976, when the divorce grounds were equalised and divorce by mutual consent introduced, rather than by any reopening of the matrimonial code as a whole. The surrendered coparcenary was recovered by instalments and from below: the state of Kerala abolished the joint family system outright by its legislation of 1975, in force from 1976, and between 1986 and 1994 the states of Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Maharashtra amended the succession law within their territories to make daughters coparceners, building the evidentiary record on which the Law Commission's report of 2000 on the property rights of women under Hindu law recommended the central change and on which the central amendment of 2005 finally acted, forty nine years after the proposition had been dropped from a bill to get the bill through. The personal laws of the other communities were handled, when they were handled at all, by the same technology of the single instalment aimed at a single practice or a single judgment, most conspicuously in the legislation of 1986 on the maintenance of divorced Muslim women and in the statute of 2019 on instant triple talaq, each a single subject statute for a single community, carried or contested on its own ground, in a legislative world whose operating rules the Hindu Code's fate had written. The template was the unbundling strategy itself, available thereafter to any government with a reform too large for one vote: sever, sequence, price the concessions separately, and let each instalment's enactment argue for the next. Governments that forgot the template and reverted to the single comprehensive instrument, as the sponsors of the direct taxes recodification did, relearned the lesson at the cost of their bills. The passage record therefore predicts, with unusual confidence for a historical claim, that Indian social legislation of any magnitude will continue to arrive in pieces, and that the appearance of a comprehensive code on any contested subject should be read as the opening of a negotiation about its division rather than as a proposal likely to be enacted whole.

## Verdict

The verdict the record supports is a double one. As legislation, the codification of 1955 and 1956 was a substantial and durable advance in the legal position of Hindu women, incomplete at its economic centre by deliberate concession, and its incompleteness structured the next fifty years of reform, which proceeded, as the codification itself had finally proceeded, in instalments. As legislative history, the episode establishes the earliest clear Indian instance of the strategy this series has tracked through the taxation cluster: comprehensiveness is a liability in a legislature, because a single instrument manufactures a single opposition out of many, and division is the technology by which large reforms are carried past it. The Hindu Code Bill is therefore misremembered when it is remembered as a failure at all. It was a first attempt that supplied the draft, the arguments, the committee record and the cautionary tale, and the four Acts of 1955 and 1956 are its second attempt, corrected in the one respect its pilot had not been free to correct: its shape.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Hindu Code Bill propose?

The bill proposed to replace the uncodified Hindu law of marriage, divorce, adoption, guardianship, joint family property, succession and maintenance with a single statutory code. It imposed monogamy, removed caste as a condition of a valid marriage or adoption, introduced judicial separation and divorce, placed a daughter beside a son in the inheritance of a father's separate property, converted a woman's limited estate into absolute ownership, treated the property given to a daughter at marriage as trust property for her, allowed a woman to adopt in her own right, and applied the whole code to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs under one definition.

### Q: Why did the comprehensive Hindu Code Bill fail?

It failed by procedure rather than by vote. Introduced in April 1947, it was debated only in fragments: no time was allotted in 1950, three days were given in February 1951, and the government's own party was never whipped behind it. Its single instrument pooled three distinct oppositions, the religious objection to divorce and secularised adoption, the property interest defending the coparcenary, and the constitutional objection that a legislature elected on a restricted franchise lacked the mandate for permanent social legislation of this scale. When the government truncated it to the marriage and divorce part in September 1951, only four clauses had been disposed of before the part was abandoned.

### Q: Why did Ambedkar resign over the Hindu Code Bill?

Ambedkar's resignation statement of 10 October 1951, following his resignation on 27 September, gives several reasons, including the government's treatment of the Scheduled Castes and his disagreements over foreign policy, and then identifies the treatment of the Hindu Code as the matter that finally decided him. He recorded that the bill had been introduced on 11 April 1947 and killed after four years of intermittent debate, described it as the greatest social reform measure undertaken by the legislature, and treated its abandonment, while the government legislated on economic questions, as proof that the equality the Constitution promised was not being carried into the social order.

### Q: How was the Hindu Code eventually enacted?

After the first general election of 1951 and 1952 returned a Parliament elected on universal adult franchise, the government abandoned the single instrument and divided the code into four separate bills, introduced from 1952 onward and piloted by Law Minister H. V. Pataskar. Each bill was debated and amended on its own subject, so the opposition could no longer combine against a single text. The marriage bill passed first, in 1955, followed by the succession, guardianship and adoptions bills in 1956. The government's own Statement of Objects and Reasons for the succession bill described it as the third instalment of the Hindu Code.

### Q: Which four statutes replaced the single Hindu Code?

The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, Act 25 of 1955, covering marriage, monogamy, judicial separation and divorce; the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, Act 30 of 1956, covering intestate succession, the Class I heirs and the conversion of the limited estate into absolute ownership; the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, Act 32 of 1956, naming the natural guardians of a Hindu minor and supplementing the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890; and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, Act 78 of 1956, covering adoption, including adoption by a woman in her own right in defined cases, and the statutory obligations of maintenance.

### Q: Who opposed the Hindu Code Bills and on what grounds?

Opposition came from orthodox religious leadership, including Swami Karpatri Maharaj and the Hindu Mahasabha under leaders such as N. C. Chatterjee, arguing that marriage and adoption are sacraments a state cannot remake; from legislators such as Lakshmi Kanta Maitra, arguing that codification would freeze a law whose strength was its customary flexibility, and that the provisional legislature was not competent, morally, to enact it; from propertied interests defending the joint family and the coparcenary against the daughter's share; and from within the government itself, where President Rajendra Prasad doubted both the measure and his obligation to assent to it, the Congress president Pattabhi Sitaramayya opposed it, and Vallabhbhai Patel held reservations.

### Q: Why does codified Hindu law apply to Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs?

Because the four Acts define their governed class functionally rather than theologically. Each Act applies to a Hindu by religion in any of its forms, expressly including Virashaivas, Lingayats and followers of the Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samajs, then to any person who is a Buddhist, Jain or Sikh by religion, and then residually to any domiciled person who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew, unless Hindu law would not have governed him. The classification followed the historical administration of these communities' family law alongside Hindu law, and it parallels the Constitution's own usage in the Explanation to the religious freedom article on temple entry. The communities' organisations have contested it as absorption rather than convenience.

### Q: What did codification actually change for Hindu women?

It changed the legal foundations of a Hindu woman's position in four ways. In marriage, it made monogamy a condition of validity and gave her access to judicial separation and divorce, where the shastric law applicable to most Hindus had recognised neither a bar on a husband's second marriage nor any dissolution. In property, it made her a Class I heir taking with her brothers in her father's separate property and converted her limited estate into absolute ownership she could dispose of, including by will. In adoption, it gave her capacity to adopt in her own right where she was unmarried, widowed or divorced. In maintenance, it converted acknowledged duties into enforceable claims. The coparcenary, however, it left male until 2005.

### Q: What was the Hindu Law Committee and who was B. N. Rau?

The Hindu Law Committee was the expert body appointed by the Government of India in January 1941 to examine the working of the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act, 1937 and the state of Hindu law, and revived in January 1944 to draft a complete code. Its chairman in both incarnations was B. N. Rau, a judge of the Calcutta High Court who later served as Constitutional Adviser to the Constituent Assembly and as a judge of the International Court of Justice. The committee toured the country taking evidence, published its draft code in twelve regional languages, and reported in 1947 with recommendations that included abolishing the joint family property system and introducing divorce.

### Q: What did the Select Committee of 1948 change in the Hindu Code Bill?

The Select Committee, chaired by Ambedkar himself and including Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, G. Durgabai, Renuka Ray and Ammu Swaminathan among its members, moderated the Rau draft at its economic centre. Where the committee of experts had proposed abolishing the joint family property system, the bill as reported retained a joint family system in a remodelled unitary form, with shares for women built into its devolution. Its influence outlasted the bill: the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the Hindu Succession Bill of 1954 recorded that the Rau draft's succession provisions had undergone substantial changes in the Select Committee's hands and that the 1954 bill followed the committee's scheme to a large extent.

### Q: In what order were the four Hindu Code statutes passed?

The Hindu Marriage Act came first, receiving the President's assent on 18 May 1955 and coming into force the same day. The Hindu Succession Act followed, with assent on 17 June 1956. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act received assent on 25 August 1956. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act closed the series, with assent on 21 December 1956, nineteen months after the first instalment and five years after the single bill containing all four subjects had been abandoned. The order was deliberate: marriage, the subject with the widest organised support and the least property at stake, was carried first, and the economically hardest bill, succession, followed once the principle of legislating was established.

### Q: How did Hindu women's organisations campaign for the Hindu Code Bill?

The All India Women's Conference, which had demanded codification since before the Rau Committee, sustained the campaign across the bill's whole life: passing resolutions at its sessions, sending deputations to ministers and members, lobbying between parliamentary sessions, and answering the orthodox campaign in the press clause by clause. Women inside the legislature carried the same case on the floor and in committee, among them G. Durgabai, Renuka Ray and Ammu Swaminathan on the Select Committee, with Hansa Mehta and Rajkumari Amrit Kaur among the prominent Congress women supporting the reform. Their organised support was the reformers' standing answer to the claim that Hindu women themselves did not want the bill.

### Q: What did Nehru argue in favour of the Hindu Code Bill?

Nehru treated the bill as a test of whether the new state would govern by its proclaimed principles, and argued that a Parliament which had given women the vote owed them a family law consistent with it. He supported codification as part of the consolidation of a single citizenry out of a legal order divided by school, region and caste. In the constitutional exchange of September 1951 he also laid down the government's doctrine on assent, writing to President Rajendra Prasad that the President had no power to go against the will of Parliament on a bill well considered and passed by it. His weakness, as the passage record shows, was tempo rather than conviction: he repeatedly counselled patience while the bill's time ran out.

### Q: What happened to the Hindu Code Bill's dowry proposal?

The bill proposed that the property given to a daughter by her parents at her marriage be treated as trust property held for her, which she could claim on reaching the age of eighteen, so that neither her husband nor his relatives would have any interest in it or opportunity to waste it. The proposal was one of the casualties of the code's long passage. It did not survive into the four Acts of 1955 and 1956, none of which contains a dowry settlement of this kind, and the subject was left to be addressed separately, and prohibitively rather than protectively, by the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961.

### Q: Why is the Hindu Code Bill linked to the law on presidential assent?

Because the republic's first assertion of an independent presidential discretion over assent was made over this bill. In his note of 15 September 1951 President Rajendra Prasad reserved the right to examine the bill on its merits before assenting, rather than acting on ministerial advice as a matter of course. Nehru replied that constitutional government excluded any such authority, and Attorney General M. C. Setalvad opined that the President's position was analogous to the British monarch's, that of a constitutional figurehead bound by advice. Prasad accepted the position and the controversy rested, to be recounted by the Supreme Court in the Tamil Nadu Governor case as the founding episode of the assent doctrine.

### Q: What lesson did later Indian codifications draw from the Hindu Code?

The lesson this series calls unbundling: a comprehensive reform presented as a single instrument pools every affected interest into one opposition and can be defeated by delay alone, while the same content divided into separate statutes lets each subject be carried by the majority that exists for it. The Hindu Code failed whole between 1948 and 1951 and passed in four instalments between 1955 and 1956 under the same political party. Later comprehensive recodifications that ignored the lesson, most conspicuously the Direct Taxes Code Bill, repeated the failure, lapsing without enactment, while successful large reforms, from the instalments that completed the succession reform in 2005 to staged tax changes, followed the Hindu Code's eventual method.

### Q: Where can the Hindu Code Bill debates and papers be read?

The primary record is unusually complete. The Rau Committee's reports and its published draft code state the reform in its original form. The debates of the Constituent Assembly acting as legislature and of the provisional Parliament preserve the arguments, including Ambedkar's motion referring the bill to a Select Committee and the membership of that committee. Ambedkar's resignation statement is printed in the official collection of his writings and speeches published by the Government of India. The Nehru and Rajendra Prasad correspondence, including the exchange of 15 September 1951, appears in their published papers, and Attorney General Setalvad's part is recounted in his memoir and in the Supreme Court's later retelling of the episode.

### Q: What was the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act 1937?

It was the piecemeal statute, moved in the central legislature by G. V. Deshmukh, from which the codification story starts. The Act gave a Hindu widow an interest in her deceased husband's property, including a share in joint family property, improving on a prior law under which she had been largely excluded from inheritance. But it left her with the limited estate rather than ownership, and it generated difficult questions about her position among the coparceners, her power to seek partition and her power to alienate, which the courts had to answer without statutory guidance. The inadequacy of reform by single hardship statutes, demonstrated by the litigation the 1937 Act produced, is what brought the Government of India to appoint the Hindu Law Committee in 1941.

### Q: Did the Hindu Code Bill propose to abolish the joint family system?

The Rau Committee's draft and report did. The committee recommended the abolition of the joint family property system, with the coparcenary and the son's right by birth replaced by succession for sons and daughters alike. The bill as revised and reported by the Select Committee of 1948 retreated from outright abolition to a remodelled, unitary joint family system with shares for women built in. The enacted Hindu Succession Act, 1956 retreated further, preserving the Mitakshara coparcenary and survivorship and admitting the daughter only as an heir to separate property and to a deceased coparcener's computed share. The draft's central economic proposition was thus the principal casualty of the passage, recovered only in part by the state amendments and the central amendment of 2005.

### Q: Why was the marriage bill taken up first among the four statutes?

Because it was the instalment likeliest to pass, and its passage would establish the principle the others needed. Marriage reform had the longest public pedigree, reaching back through the provincial statutes such as Bombay's divorce Act of 1947 and the central validating Acts of 1946 and 1949, the clearest organised support in the women's movement, and the least property at stake, so the religious objection stood alone without the coparcenary interest beside it. Carrying it in 1955 proved that the elected Parliament would legislate on Hindu personal law, converted that proposition from an argument into a precedent, and left the harder succession bill to be fought in 1956 on economic ground alone, with the mandate objection already answered by the election and by the first instalment's success.
