The two most famous names in English drama were old before the play that carries them was written. Romeo and Juliet were not coined for the Verona tragedy: they arrived with the story, handed down through an Italian novella, a French adaptation, an English poem and an English prose collection, and each name brought a buried cargo of meaning across every border. Romeo began as a common noun, a word for a pilgrim bound for Rome, before Luigi da Porto pinned it to a young Veronese of the Montecchi house. Juliet began as Giulietta, a diminutive, the little Giulia, and reached the London stage with the smallness clipped off her name by the journey. The verdict of the evidence is precise and it divides in two. The pilgrim inside Romeo is documented etymology, and the play, knowingly or not, puts the buried meaning on its feet in the feast scene, where the hero plays a pilgrim at a shrine with his own lips. The July inside Juliet is a resonance rather than a derivation, a real kinship of roots that the play warms with a midsummer calendar without ever speaking it aloud. Keeping those two grades of claim apart, documented descent on one side and suggestive echo on the other, is the whole discipline of the subject, and the names reward the discipline: read strictly, they show a playwright inheriting words the way he inherited the plot, and making the inheritance act.

A note on texts governs everything that follows. Quotations from the play follow Jill Levenson's Oxford Shakespeare edition, cited by act, scene and line; the source texts are cited from the versions gathered in Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, the collection that prints Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke and Painter in sequence and lets the name forms be compared on facing evidence. Where a claim rests on a dictionary rather than a text, the dictionary is named. The method throughout is collation: lay the forms side by side, date each one, and let the spelling testify before the interpretation begins.

## Romeo the Pilgrim: What the Name Meant Before It Was a Man

The Oxford English Dictionary derives the name Romeo from the Italian common noun romeo, a pilgrim to Rome, and that derivation is the firmest fact in the whole inquiry. Behind the Italian word stands the medieval Latin Romaeus, itself borrowed from the Greek Rhōmaios, a Roman, a subject of the eastern empire whose capital was the new Rome. In the centuries when pilgrimage organised European travel, the word narrowed from a nationality into a vocation: a romaeus was a person on the road to Rome, and the Italian romeo kept exactly that sense. The word belonged to the vocabulary of roads, shrines, badges and safe-conducts long before it belonged to a lover in Verona. Spanish preserved the same word as romero, and the family of terms around it, romería for a pilgrimage festival, romeraje for the pilgrim traffic, shows a whole culture naming itself after the Rome road.

The precision matters because medieval pilgrimage sorted its travellers by destination, and each destination had its badge and its title. The pilgrim to Jerusalem was a palmer, named for the palm branch carried home from the Holy Land as proof of the journey. The pilgrim to the shrine of St James at Compostela had the scallop shell. The pilgrim to Rome wore the vernicle, the image of Christ's face miraculously printed on the cloth of St Veronica, kept at St Peter's and copied onto the lead badges sewn to a thousand hats and scrips. A romeo, strictly, was the Rome pilgrim, and the word carried the dust of that specific road: southward over the Alps, down the Italian peninsula, to the threshold of the apostles. When the name is restored to this company, the hero of the Verona tale stops sounding like a pure invention of romance and starts sounding like a traveller, a young man defined by a journey undertaken for devotion, which is a suggestive thing to call the character who will cross a city at night, scale an orchard wall, ride to Mantua and make a final journey to a tomb.

### What did a Rome-bound pilgrim carry, and how did the badge differ from a palmer's?

A Rome pilgrim carried a staff and scrip like every long-distance traveller, but the distinguishing mark was the vernicle, the small image of the Holy Face worn as the badge of the Roman journey. The palmer's palm announced Jerusalem; the vernicle announced Rome, and the two titles sorted travellers by their shrines.

The two titles were therefore not interchangeable in strict usage, however loosely later poets blended them, and the hero's name belongs to the Roman side of the ledger: a romeo is Rome-bound, not palm-bearing, and the distinction sharpens rather than blurs the name's sense.

Against this documented descent one caution must be entered at once, because the whole article turns on it. A word's origin is a fact about the word, not automatically a fact about every bearer of it. Names shed their meanings as they pass from person to person; a child christened Romeo in Verona was no more a pilgrim than a child christened Victor was a conqueror. The etymology proves what the word meant in the language Da Porto wrote. Whether any teller or playwright activated that meaning is a separate question, to be answered from texts rather than from dictionaries, and it is answered in the feast scene below. What the dictionary settles is the raw material: when the Italian tale needed a name for its hero, the language offered one that meant a Rome-bound pilgrim, a devout traveller, a man whose identity is a journey toward a shrine. Da Porto did not coin the name. The name was waiting in the vocabulary of Italian devotion, and its availability is the first link in the chain.

It is worth pausing on how unusual the choice was. Romeo was not a stock name of Italian romance, not a Tristan or a Lancelot worn smooth by centuries of storytelling. The novella tradition before Da Porto had called its hero Mariotto, in Masuccio Salernitano's Sienese tale of Mariotto and Gianozza, a good ordinary name that means nothing in particular. Da Porto, rebuilding the tale around Verona and the two houses Dante had named, renamed the lovers outright, and the renaming is his most durable single act as an author: plots can be borrowed and reborrowed, but the names Romeo and Giulietta are the labels under which the story conquered Europe. For the wider map of that conquest, the account of [the sources of the lovers' story drawn as a family tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/) sets each retelling on its branch; the concern here is narrower, the two given names and what each one carried.

## Dante Defines the Romeo: The Pilgrim Word in Its Native Century

The pilgrim derivation does not rest on a dictionary's say-so alone, because the word's meaning is defined, in so many words, by the greatest Italian writer of the age before Da Porto. Dante, in the fortieth chapter of the Vita Nuova, pauses over a company of pilgrims passing through Florence on their way to Rome, and the pause becomes a small treatise on pilgrim vocabulary. Pilgrim, he explains, can be taken in a broad sense, for anyone travelling far from home, or in a narrow sense, for those bound for the shrine of St James in Galicia; and then the taxonomy sharpens. Those who go overseas to the Holy Land are called palmieri, palmers, because they bring back the palm. Those who go to the house of St James are called peregrini. And those who go to Rome are called romei. The passage is the locus classicus for the whole vocabulary, quoted by editors and lexicographers for six centuries, and it fixes the hero's name in its native century with an authority no later gloss can match: in the Italian of Dante, which is also the Italian of Da Porto two hundred years on, a romeo is a Rome pilgrim as exactly as a palmer is a Jerusalem pilgrim. The word the novella would make immortal was a technical term of the roads, defined by the poet whose own poem supplied the tale with its feuding houses.

The Dante passage does further work, because it shows the word alive in a literary register, not merely in the ledgers of travel. Dante's pilgrims are passing through a city that is not their goal, weeping nothing, knowing nothing of the death that fills the book they walk through, and the poet wonders at their dry eyes before turning the reflection on himself. The romei of the Vita Nuova are, already, figures of a particular pathos: travellers whose devotion aims them at a shrine beyond the horizon of the story they happen to cross. When Da Porto reached for a hero's name, the word he took had this literary aura as well as its dictionary sense, a word Dante had bent toward meditation on journeys, shrines and the sorrows of those who pass through. The naming begins to look less like a label grabbed from the street and more like an inheritance taken from the shelf, the same shelf that held the Montecchi and the Cappelletti. Da Porto's tale is built from Dante at both ends, the houses from the Purgatorio, the hero's very name glossed in the Vita Nuova, and the double debt strengthens the case that the pilgrim sense was part of the name's value to its chooser, not a fossil later readers dug out of it.

### Why does Dante bother to define the word for his readers?

Dante defines the pilgrim terms because the broad and narrow senses of pilgrim were already drifting apart in his Italian, and his book turns on the difference between travellers who pass through a sorrow and the lover who stays inside it. The definition is a poet's precision, not a pedant's taxonomy.

The taxonomy of palmieri, peregrini and romei lets him place his Rome-bound pilgrims exactly, on their road, with their goal elsewhere. The passage incidentally preserves, in amber, the exact sense the hero's name would carry into the Verona tale two centuries later.

The wider Mediterranean currency of the word confirms the definition from the other side. Spanish romero names the same traveller, and the romería, the festive pilgrimage to a local shrine, keeps the word's family alive in the Iberian calendar; the herb rosemary carries the name in Spanish folk usage through the pilgrims who gathered it on the road, a derivation the dictionaries record with due caution. French had its own forms for the Rome pilgrim, and the whole of Latin Christendom understood the classification Dante sets out, because the classification was stamped on the badges the travellers wore home. A word so placed, at the crossing of devotion, geography and literature, was not an obscure word. It was a word any Italian reader of Da Porto's generation had seen on the road and met in Dante. The burden of proof lies on the sceptic who would have the tale's first readers hear Romeo as a meaningless sound; the record shows a word whose meaning was defined by the national poet and enacted yearly on every road to Rome.

## Dante's Other Romeo: The Pilgrim Steward of the Paradiso

Dante's testimony about the name does not end with the Vita Nuova's definition, because Dante also gives the name to a man, and the man he gives it to is a pilgrim. In the sixth canto of the Paradiso, the emperor Justinian, recounting the history of the Roman eagle, pauses over Romeo of Villeneuve, a figure of Provençal history who served Raymond Berengar, count of Provence, administered the count's lands and revenues with famous honesty, married the count's four daughters to four kings, and then, slandered by the courtiers his honesty had shamed, asked leave to depart and went out from the court as he had come to it, poor. Dante's line for the manner of the man's arrival is the one the names tradition has treasured ever since: Romeo, persona umile e peregrina, Romeo, a humble and pilgrim person. The name and the condition are fused in the phrase. Whether the historical steward's parents had christened him with the pilgrim word or the road had renamed him for his travelling, the figure Dante canonised is a Romeo whose name and whose pilgrim state gloss each other, two centuries before Da Porto's hero was christened.

The bearing of the Paradiso passage on the Verona names is the kind of evidence this article has been grading throughout, and it should be graded honestly. It does not prove that Da Porto took his hero's name from Dante's steward; no document connects the novella's christening to the canto. What it proves is the air the name breathed in the literature Da Porto was formed by. The tale's author took his feuding houses from the Purgatorio, his pilgrim vocabulary stood defined in the Vita Nuova, and the one Romeo in the national poem was a pilgrim steward whose story Dante tells as a parable of humble service and ingratitude. A writer assembling a Verona tale out of Dantean materials, who needed a name for a young hero of one of Dante's houses, had the word Romeo lying in the same quarry as Montecchi and Cappelletti, already cut, already bearing its pilgrim sense in the most authoritative text in the language. The conjecture that the name came out of that quarry cannot be certified, and it is filed here as conjecture; but as conjectures go it has the rare virtue of explaining all the evidence at once, the choice of an uncommon name, its pilgrim fitness for the tale of journeys, and its pairing with houses drawn from the same poem. Skepticism about the route does not touch the destination: by whatever road, the name that reached the Verona tale was a pilgrim's word in a pilgrim's story, in a literary culture where Dante had fixed both facts on the record.

There is a further fitness in the steward's story that a Veronese teller might have savoured, and it is recorded here under the same conjecture flag. Dante's Romeo is a just man ruined by a court's ingratitude, a servant whose reward for arranging royal marriages is exile and beggary, who leaves asking only his mule and his staff, the pilgrim's equipment, and grows old begging his bread morsel by morsel. Service, marriages arranged for others, slander, departure, poverty on the road: the steward's outline is not the hero's outline, and no one should pretend the novella copies it. But the name's one great literary bearer before the tale was a man whose story turned on marriages, envy and a journey into exile, and who carried the pilgrim word as his name through all of it. Names acquire weather from their famous bearers. When Da Porto's readers met a Romeo, the name arrived with Dante's steward's weather on it, humble, travelling, ill-rewarded, and the weather suited the young Montecchio who would end his story on the road to a tomb.

## Before Romeo: Mariotto, Gianozza and the Renaming at Verona

One more control experiment is available, and it is the cleanest in the whole inquiry, because the story exists in a version where the lovers have entirely different names. Masuccio Salernitano's tale of Mariotto and Gianozza, printed in Il Novellino in 1476, tells of a secret marriage, a killing, an exile, a feigned death by a friar's powder, a tomb opened too late and a hero executed, the whole skeleton of the later tragedy, in Siena rather than Verona, with no feud between houses and no Dante behind it. The lovers' names in that telling are ordinary Italian stock. Mariotto is a familiar diminutive-tinged form of Mario; Gianozza, spelled Ganozza in some witnesses, is a Sienese girl's name of no literary ancestry. Neither name means anything to the tale. Neither is glossed, punned on or played with. They are counters, in the transmission scholars' sense, pure labels, and the tale gets on perfectly well with them, which proves that the story does not need meaningful names in order to work.

What happened next is the event this article exists to record. Da Porto took Masuccio's plot skeleton, moved it to Verona, set it among the Montecchi and Cappelletti, and renamed both lovers. The renaming is the one element of the familiar story that cannot be traced to any earlier telling, the single piece of pure authorship in the foundation layer, and its quality deserves attention. For the heroine he reached for a clan name of Rome's founding house and diminished it, producing a name that states youth, smallness and household keeping in its very grammar. For the hero he reached for the pilgrim word, producing a name that states journeying, devotion and a goal beyond the city. Whether he reached consciously for those statements is beyond proof, as established; that he reached past the ordinary name stock, twice, in a tale where his predecessor had used ordinary names, is a fact of the texts. A teller content with counters had counters ready to hand in Mariotto and Gianozza. Da Porto was not content. The names Romeo and Giulietta enter literature as a deliberate upgrade, the moment a borrowed plot was given a pair of names with roots, and the whole later tradition, Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, Painter and the play, is among other things the history of those roots being preserved, respelled and, in the heroine's case, planed away.

The comparison also disciplines the argument about the play. Shakespeare, at the end of the chain, is sometimes credited with the names as with everything else, and the credit is doubly misplaced: the names were chosen in Vicenza in the 1520s, and their meanings were chosen then too, insofar as they were ever chosen at all. The playwright's contribution to the onomastics of the tale is confined to the hybrid selection of forms, Romeo with Juliet, and to the activation of the hero's buried sense in the feast scene. That is a real contribution, and the feast scene is its proof, but it is the contribution of an heir working an estate, not of a founder. Restoring the renaming at Verona to its author is the series thesis of the sources in miniature: the cliché gives the play the names, the documents give the names to Da Porto, and the dictionaries give Da Porto's names their meanings.

## The Career of a Name: Romeo from Da Porto to the London Stage

The name's documented career runs through five forms in four languages, and the forms are the evidence. Da Porto's novella, composed in the 1520s and published at Venice after his death, presented the hero as Romeo, a young man of the Montecchi; the full style Romeo Montecchio, the given name locked to the house name, settles into place as the tradition fixes the feud's personnel, and it is under that double name that Matteo Bandello retold the tale in the second part of his Novelle, printed at Lucca in 1554. Bandello kept Da Porto's names intact, Romeo and Giulietta, Cappelletti and Montecchi, and in keeping them he certified them: the second great Italian telling treated the names as part of the story's furniture, as non-negotiable as Verona itself.

Pierre Boaistuau carried the tale into French in the Histoires tragiques of 1559, and French spelling laid its hand on the hero: Rhomeo, with an h inserted after the R in the humanist manner that liked to mark a word's classical ancestry in its letters. The inserted letter is a small lesson in how names travel. A French printer's convention, not a new idea about the character, separates Rhomeo from Romeo; the man is the same, the road he walks is the same, but the name has been re-dressed for a new readership. Boaistuau's heroine became Juliette, with the French double t, a respelling that kept the diminutive ending audible in a new phonology. Nothing in the plot changed when the h appeared or the t doubled, and that is precisely the point the collation teaches: name forms register the journey of the book, the printing houses and language borders it crossed, far more faithfully than they register any fresh thought about the lovers.

Arthur Brooke's English poem of 1562, The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet, Latinised the hero outright. Romeus is not a translation of Romeo but a re-formation of it, the Italian name pulled back toward its Latin ancestor Romaeus and set down in a shape that rhymes with the poem's classical ambitions. Brooke's heroine, by contrast, he clipped: Juliet, the Italian diminutive ending struck off, the name naturalised as an English girl's name might be. The poem's title page thus holds the two operations side by side, one name classicised and swollen, the other domesticated and trimmed. William Painter, whose prose version appeared in the second tome of The Palace of Pleasure in 1566, made the opposite pair of choices: his hero is Romeo, the Italian form kept whole, and his heroine is Julietta, the diminutive kept whole with it. Between Brooke and Painter, English readers of the 1560s could meet the lovers under four forms, Romeus and Juliet, Romeo and Julietta, and the variation was not carelessness. Each reteller dressed the names for the genre being written, verse tragedy in poulter's measure on one side, prose novella on the other.

The play, when it came, chose one form from each English predecessor: Romeo from the prose line, Juliet from the verse line. The first quarto of 1597 announced An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet on its title page, and the second quarto of 1599, The Most Excellent and lamentable Tragedie, of Romeo and Juliet, confirmed the pairing. Whether the playwright took Romeo from Painter's pages directly or reached the Italianate form by the same instinct that kept it there, the collation stands: the play's couple is a hybrid, half Brooke and half Painter, and neither of their names is in the shape Da Porto gave them both. The hero's name, fittingly for a word that began as a traveller, was naturalised at every border and arrived in London with a French h lost, a Latin ending lost and an Italian pilgrim still audible inside it to any ear that knew the word.

One further stage of the career belongs to the play itself, and it is sung rather than spoken. The lovers' first exchange at the feast is built, as the next sections show, on pilgrim matter, and the hero there introduces his suit in the vocabulary of shrines and saints. A name whose buried sense is pilgrim meets a scene that makes the bearer act the pilgrim: the career of the word and the action of the play cross at that point, and the crossing is the strongest argument that the etymology is not inert in the finished work. Before that argument can be weighed, the heroine's name needs its own descent set out, because Giulietta's journey is the reverse of Romeo's: his name gained a meaning's visibility as it travelled, while hers steadily lost the meaning it started with.

## In the Printers' Hands: The H, the Latin Ending and the Letter J

Between Da Porto and the play, the names passed through printing houses in Venice, Lucca, Paris and London, and each house left its fingerprints on the spelling, because a name in the age of hand printing was not a fixed string but a small negotiation among author, copy, compositor and house style. The negotiations are legible in the forms themselves, and they teach a lesson the romantic account of the names always misses: several of the most visible facts about the pair, the French h, the Latin ending, the clipped heroine, are facts about print culture before they are facts about anyone's intentions. Boaistuau's Rhomeo wears the inserted h of French humanist orthography, the fashion that respelled French and classical words to display their Greek and Latin ancestry, giving the town of Rheims its h and a hundred names their learned padding. The h in Rhomeo asserts that the word has a classical pedigree, Rhōmaios behind Romaeus, and the assertion happens to be true; but the letter was added by the fashion, not by research, and the same fashion padded words whose pedigrees were imaginary. A spelling, the case warns, is evidence of a printing house's habits first and of an etymology only by accident.

Brooke's Romeus shows the opposite negotiation, the pull of metre and of Latin dignity on an English poem. The Tragicall Historye is written in poulter's measure, the alternating twelve and fourteen syllable lines that swing like a see-saw and punish any word that will not fit the swing. Romeus, two syllables with a classical cut, sits in the measure as Romeo, three syllables with an Italian lilt, could not sit without elbowing the line; and the Latinised form also advertises the poem's ancestry, Romaeus Englished but still wearing Rome on its face, for a readership Brooke's preface addresses in a high moral tone. Whether metre or dignity did the deciding cannot now be separated, and the honest collation records both pressures without choosing. What can be said is that the form is over-determined, the kind of choice a working poet makes with his ear and his page at once, and that its survival was short: Painter's prose, owing nothing to the measure, kept Romeo, and the play followed the prose. Verse had Latinised the hero; prose and the stage restored the pilgrim word.

The heroine's letters tell the third negotiation, the slow separation of the letters I and J in English print. Through the period of the sources, I served English printers for both the vowel and the consonant, and the heroine's name moves through the witnesses under both letterforms, the consonantal J only gradually claiming its own sort in the type case. The variation is pure orthography, no more meaningful than the long s, and its presence in the record is a useful inoculation: a reader collating the early texts who treats every letter as significant will invent phantom variants out of typefounders' stock. The names note therefore records forms at the level of the word, not the letter, crediting Juliet and Iuliet to one entry, exactly as it credits Rhomeo's h to the French fashion rather than to any French theory of the hero. Understood so, the printers' handling becomes part of the names' meaning in a roundabout way: the forms that posterity received were filtered through the material habits of four print shops, and the pair that emerged, Romeo and Juliet, is as much a product of the type case as of the tale. The legend's flag was sewn by compositors.

## The Full Style: Romeo Montecchio and the Settling of the Pair

Da Porto did not only christen the lovers; he filed them. The hero of the novella is Romeo of the Montecchi, the heroine Giulietta of the Cappelletti, and the full styles matter because they mark the moment the given names acquired their public halves, the surnames that the feud would make lethal. The houses themselves, and their long descent from Dante's Purgatorio, belong to their own study and are touched here only at the length the pairing requires: what the novella's naming did was to bolt a pair of resonant Christian names to a pair of faction names, so that every lover thereafter would carry a private word and a public one, a pilgrim or a little Giulia in the orchard and a Montecchio or a Cappelletti in the street. The play's whole machinery of naming is present in that bolt. Before Da Porto, Mariotto Mignanelli carried a surname that signified nothing to his tale, a Sienese family label without a feud inside it. After Da Porto, the hero's second name is a death sentence waiting for a speaker, and the tragedy's naming plot, the doffing and the baptism and the name lodged in the anatomy, is the working out of a join made in the 1520s.

The settling of the pair in their full styles was gradual, and the gradualness is instructive about how traditions fix their persons. Da Porto's frame presents the tale as history, set in the days of Bartolomeo della Scala, and his lovers move through it under their house affiliations, the affiliations doing the work of identity that a chronicle requires. Bandello, expanding the tale, keeps the full apparatus and burnishes it, his rubric advertising the lovers by their story rather than their surnames yet his narrative never letting the houses go. The French and English versions inherit the styles entire, Brooke's poem ringing the changes on Cappellets and Montagues in the spellings English gave them, Painter's prose likewise. By the time the play assembles its Verona, the full styles are load-bearing: remove Montecchio from Romeo or Cappelletti from Giulietta and the plot has no engine. Yet the titles of every version, from Da Porto onward, bill the lovers by their given names alone. The tradition's shop sign never once reads Montecchio and Cappelletti. The given names are the legend's flag; the surnames are its machinery; and the split between flag and machinery, established at the founding, is why the etymology of the given names is a subject at all: they are the part of the naming that faced the public, the part that had to be chosen for sound and sense and posterity, while the surnames were chosen for war.

A last observation on the settling concerns order within the full style. Italian usage in the novella can put the affiliation before the person in narrative, the Montecchi youth, the Cappelletti girl, defining the individual by the house at first mention and only then releasing the given name. The play inherits the habit in its opening minutes, where the Chorus files the lovers under their households before either has spoken, and the Prologue's grammar subordinates both children to the two households alike in dignity. Against that filing habit, the given names' career reads as a slow emancipation: by the tomb scene the houses have spent themselves, and the story closes, as the flag section below shows, on the given names alone, in Da Porto's order. The full style was the feud's way of owning the lovers. The given names were the tale's way of keeping them.

## Giulietta: The Little Giulia and the Claim of the Diminutive

Giulietta is a diminutive, and the fact is grammatical before it is anything else. The Italian suffix -etta, attached to Giulia, produces the little Giulia, the dear Giulia, the Giulia held at the scale of affection or of youth. Giulia is the feminine of Giulio, the Italian form of the Roman family name Julius, the nomen of the gens Julia, the clan that claimed descent from Iulus, the son of Aeneas, and through Aeneas from Venus herself. The proposed deeper derivation of Iulius from the Greek ioulos, the first down of a young man's beard, would make the root of the heroine's name a word for the threshold of adulthood, for youth at its first soft sign. That proposal is ancient and attractive and unprovable, and it is filed here exactly where it belongs, among the resonances: the documented chain runs Giulietta from Giulia, Giulia from the gens Julia, and there the documents stop. A name that can be traced to the founding family of Rome's mythology is pedigree enough without borrowing a Greek beard.

The diminutive, though, is not a resonance. It is morphology, visible on the face of the word to every Italian reader Da Porto wrote for, and its claims are two. The first claim is youth: the -etta suffix marks its bearer as small, young, not yet at full stature, and Da Porto's heroine is a girl at the edge of marriageability in a tale where her youth is the engine of the catastrophe. The second claim is possession, or at least appropriation: a diminutive is the form a name takes in the mouths of those who hold the bearer dear or hold her under their authority. Parents, nurses, lovers and narrators use the little form; the world of courts and chronicles uses the full one. To name the heroine Giulietta rather than Giulia is to place her, before she speaks a line, inside the circle of the household, spoken of in the grammar of affection and control. Bandello, who enlarged so much of Da Porto's tale, left the diminutive untouched, and so did the tradition after him, in every language, until English clipped it.

Da Porto's title itself deserves a pause, because he printed the heroine first: Giulietta e Romeo, her name before his, the little Giulia leading the pilgrim. The order is a reminder that the tale began as her story as much as his, the story of a Veronese girl and the young man who came to her family's feast. The play's reversed order, Romeo and Juliet, was inherited from the English title pages of Brooke and the quartos, not chosen fresh, and the reversal quietly re-centred the tale on the hero. For the full account of the novella that made the pairing, the study of [Luigi da Porto and the birth of Giulietta](/2025/10/03/luigi-da-porto-giulietta-origin/) gives the tale's making in detail; the point here is confined to the names he fixed. He fixed them well. Giulietta carries, in four syllables, a Roman clan, a goddess behind the clan, and a suffix that calls its bearer little and young and held. No subsequent teller improved on the freight. The subsequent tellers, in fact, spent it.

### What does the diminutive ending do to a heroine in an Italian novella?

The ending shrinks the name and, with it, the heroine's public scale. A Giulia might stand in a chronicle among the women of a great house; a Giulietta belongs to the household and the bedchamber, named in the form used by family and lovers, the little form for a closely held girl.

The suffix therefore does quiet structural work in the novella: it tells the reader, before any event, that the heroine is young, closely held, and spoken for by the voices around her, so that her later defiance of those voices measures itself against the smallness her own name asserts.

Consider what the tale gains from the smallness. The tragedy of the Italian novella is not that a woman of the world is destroyed but that a girl, named as a child is named, outmanoeuvres a household, a confessor, a husband chosen for her and a city, and is beaten only by a letter's failure. The diminutive keeps her scale before the reader at every mention, so that each of her decisions registers at full size against it. When the English tradition clipped the suffix away, it did not merely shorten a word; it removed the standing reminder. Juliet, in English ears, is simply a name, pretty and foreign. Giulietta, in Italian ears, is a description renewed at every utterance. The loss is the clearest case in the whole transmission of a meaning that travelled inside a name and was confiscated at a language border, not by any teller's decision but by the ordinary customs of English naming, which had no living diminutive machinery to receive the -etta and so set it down as excess baggage.

## The Diminutive Heroine: Fiammetta, Lauretta and the Grammar of the Little Name

Da Porto's choice of a diminutive for his heroine was not an isolated fancy; it placed Giulietta in a recognised company, the company of Italian prose fiction's little-named heroines. Boccaccio had set the pattern two centuries earlier. His Fiammetta, the narrator of the Elegia di Madonna Fiammetta and a presence in the frame of the Decameron, carries a name that is a diminutive of fiamma, a little flame, and the name is a programme: the heroine is a small fire that consumes the book she tells. Among the Decameron's own storytellers sits Lauretta, a diminutive built on Laura, Petrarch's beloved, so that the frame of the great collection seats a little Laura among its narrators. The Italian novella tradition, in other words, had a standing convention of heroines named in the diminutive, names that promise youth, intimacy and a scale of feeling rather than of state. A reader of Da Porto's generation meeting a heroine called Giulietta read the suffix with the fluency of long practice. The little name belonged to the genre the way the pilgrim word belonged to the road.

The convention cuts two ways, and both edges matter for the verdict on the name. On one side, the diminutive conventionalises Giulietta: she is typed by her suffix as the novella's young beloved, and some of the poignancy a modern reader finds in the name is simply the genre speaking. On the other side, the convention is precisely what makes the name informative, since it shows Da Porto composing inside a system where names carried legible signals and authors chose among them knowingly. A teller who wanted a heroine of full stature had Giulia ready; a teller who wanted the kept girl of the household had Giulietta. The choice between a name and its diminutive is a choice between two conceptions of the same character, and Da Porto's tale, in which the heroine's whole struggle is to act at full stature from inside the household that keeps her small, reads like the biography of its heroine's suffix. She begins as the name says, the little one, summoned, instructed, disposed of; she ends having dictated letters, defied a father, outfaced a confessor and chosen her own death, and the name never grows with her. The diminutive becomes an irony the tale carries in every line that speaks of her, the measure of the distance between what the household calls her and what she does.

English, as established, struck the suffix off, and with it struck off the convention that made the suffix readable. Brooke's Juliet stepped out of the company of Fiammetta and Lauretta into an English naming world where heroines were not diminished by their names, and the play's Juliet completed the emigration. What replaced the suffix's work is worth naming exactly, since compensation is the pattern everywhere in this transmission. The play states the heroine's youth in words, in the repeated reckoning of her fourteen years; it states her kept condition in action, in the summonses and negotiations of the first act; it states her intimacy in the invented privacy of the orchard scene. Every item the suffix had carried is rebuilt by other means, at greater length and with greater force. The name lost its meaning, and the play, characteristically, rebuilt the meaning as drama. That rebuilding is the strongest evidence available for what the diminutive had meant: the playwright, receiving a name whose grammar he could not use, supplied by structure what the grammar had supplied by suffix, whether he knew the suffix's sense or merely felt the tale's need of it.

## Giulia Before Giulietta: The Clan Name and the Roman Way with Daughters

The diminutive's parent name deserves its own descent, because Giulia was not a name like others in the Italian stock: it was a Roman clan name still wearing its ancient grammar. In the classical system, a Roman woman bore the feminine form of her father's clan name as her whole public appellation. Every daughter of the gens Julia was Julia, distinguished within the family by order or by nickname, Julia the elder, Julia the younger, or by a familiar diminutive. The system meant that a Roman girl's name announced her house and nothing else, her individuality carried by the qualifiers the household added. The practice survived in the Italian Giulia of the Renaissance as a fossil of that world, a girl's name that was still, at root, a family's name, and it gives Da Porto's choice an extra layer that his first readers were placed to feel: the heroine bears, in her full form, exactly the kind of name the feud deals in, a house word. Her tragedy begins inside her own name, which files her under a clan before it permits her to be a person.

The diminutive is then legible as the household's answer to the clan system, the private form coined to pick one girl out of the Julias. Roman usage itself had worked this way, piling qualifiers and pet forms on the shared nomen until the individual emerged, and Italian -etta continues the same labour with better tools. Seen in this line, Giulietta is not merely a pretty small name; it is the point where the public system of clan naming is softened into the private system of the nursery, the exact border the heroine's life is lived on. She is a Cappelletti by the feud's filing and a Giulietta by the household's affection, and the play's Juliet will re-enact the same border in English dress, filed as a Capulet in the street and addressed as a person in the orchard. The name's deep structure, clan word plus diminutive, is the tragedy's structure in miniature, the public claim and the private self sharing one word and pulling opposite ways. Da Porto cannot be proved to have designed the miniature. The materials he chose had the design in them, as the pilgrim word had the shrine in it, and a names note's duty is to show the design in the materials without inventing the designer's memorandum.

The clan behind the name also fixes the outer limit of the documented descent, and the limit should be marked plainly. Beyond the gens Julia the trail enters the clan's own mythology, the descent from Iulus and Aeneas and Venus, which is evidence about what Romans believed, not about where the word came from. The proposed Greek root, ioulos, stands outside the gate as well, an attractive conjecture about the clan name's own childhood. A name, like a family, has a point beyond which its papers are legends, and intellectual honesty consists in admiring the legends from the gate rather than forging the papers. Giulietta's papers, from a Veronese novella back to a Roman clan, are in excellent order; that is more continuity than most famous names can show, and it is enough.

## From Giulietta to Juliet: What the Journey Took from Her Name

The stages of the heroine's name can be laid out like the strata of a road. Da Porto's Giulietta, with the Italian diminutive whole, passes to Bandello unchanged. Boaistuau's Juliette carries the diminutive across into French dress, the -ette ending being one French shares with Italian as living equipment, so that a French reader still heard the littleness. Brooke's Juliet drops the ending altogether. Painter's Julietta keeps an Italianate ending but swaps the diminutive's force for a foreign flavour: -etta in an English sentence no longer means little, it merely means Italian, a touch of local colour on a name the reader is not expected to parse. The play adopts Brooke's clipped form, and the clipping held: Juliet she is in the quartos, in the Folio, and in every edition since, while Julietta survived only in Painter's prose and in the title of Brooke's poem, where the poem itself prefers the shorter name inside its couplets. The sequence shows two different kinds of change running in opposite directions. The spelling of the names registers every border crossing with perfect fidelity. The meaning of the heroine's name registers only the crossings, and dies at the Channel.

It would be easy, and wrong, to call the clipping a blunder. English in the 1560s was naturalising Italian names by the cartload, and a final unstressed syllable that carried a grammatical sense English could not reproduce was exactly what naturalisation removed. Brooke, whose poem moralises its heroine severely, arguably gained by the removal: his Juliet stands in the preface's dock as a responsible agent, and the full-sized name suits the indictment better than the little one would. The play gained something too, though of a different kind. Juliet, unmarked for size, lets the heroine's stature be set by her speeches rather than by her name; the girl who caps the pilgrim sonnet, out-reasons the Nurse, and faces the potion alone is not diminished in advance by a suffix calling her little. Where Da Porto's name states her youth as a fact of language, the play states it as a fact of the calendar, in the counting of her fourteen years that Act 1 performs three times. The information survived the journey; only its vehicle changed, from morphology to arithmetic.

The hero's name suffered no comparable loss, because its meaning was lexical rather than grammatical, and lexical meanings can ride inside a word even when hearers no longer parse them, the way pilgrim survives inside Romeo for any reader who is told it is there. This asymmetry between the two names is one of the neatest findings the collation yields. Romeo's buried sense remained available for reactivation, and the feast scene reactivates it. Giulietta's buried sense depended on a suffix, and suffixes do not survive translation; there was nothing left in Juliet for the play to reactivate. The heroine's name arrived in London as pure sound and pure association, which is why her great speech about names, examined below, can treat the name as an arbitrary label with a plausibility the hero's name, freighted with its pilgrim, never quite permits. Her name really had become, by the accidents of transmission, the kind of empty counter her argument needs names to be. His never entirely had.

There is also the matter of what each language's form does to the pairing itself. In Italian the names balance as opposites in miniature: Romeo, a common noun turned name, masculine, occupational, pointing outward to a road; Giulietta, a clan name turned diminutive, feminine, domestic, pointing inward to a household. The tale's whole geography is in the pairing, the boy defined by journeying, the girl defined by being kept. French kept the contrast. English blurred it, setting Romeo, still faintly a word, beside Juliet, now only a name, so that English audiences meet one lover whose name can be glossed and one whose cannot. Editors' notes have been compensating for the imbalance ever since, printing the pilgrim in the hero's gloss and falling back, for the heroine, on the one association her name retained after the suffix fell: the summer month that shares her root.

## The July Question: Documented Root or Midsummer Resonance?

The association of Juliet with July is the most repeated claim in the popular literature of the names, and it is the claim that most needs the discipline this article exists to enforce. The facts first. The month July takes its name from Julius Caesar, born in the month the Roman calendar called Quintilis and renamed Iulius in his honour after his death. The heroine's name descends, through Giulia, from the same Julius, the nomen of the gens Julia. Name and month are therefore cousins, both descended from the Roman clan name, and neither is descended from the other. Juliet is not named for July, and July is not named for any Juliet. The two words meet at a common ancestor three removes back, in the Rome of the late republic, and the meeting is genuine kinship, not coincidence: it is not that the words happen to sound alike, as unrelated words can, but that a single Roman name stands behind both. Philologists have a category for this, cognate resonance, and it is stronger than a pun and weaker than a derivation. The claim, stated at its honest strength, is that the heroine's name and the summer month are branches of one root, so that an audience hearing the name in a play saturated with midsummer heat hears the season inside the name whether anyone intended the hearing or not.

The play's calendar then does its warming work. The action is fixed at high summer with unusual precision for a drama that never names a month. The Nurse's reckoning makes the heroine's birthday fall on Lammas Eve, the night before the first of August, when she will complete her fourteenth year: "On Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3). The feast, the duel in the fierce afternoon heat that Benvolio dreads at the opening of Act 3, the whole action, falls in the last days of July, within a fortnight of the birthday. Editors have long noted the concentration. Levenson's Oxford edition, in framing the play's swift calendar, treats the Lammas reckoning as the anchor of the action's season, and the season matters to the reading of the name: the heroine whose name is cognate with July lives her whole tragedy inside July, meets, marries and dies within the month her root-name governs, and reaches her birthday only as a corpse. The resonance, in other words, is not floated on a sound. It is anchored in the text's own dates, which are among the most exact in all the plays, and the anchoring is what lifts the July claim above the level of the parlour game that finds meanings in names by ear alone.

### What does Lammas Eve fix about the heroine that her name only suggests?

Lammas Eve fixes her position in the year to a single night, the last night of July, and so places the entire action inside the month that shares her name's root. The name can only suggest the season, through the kinship of Juliet and July in the Roman clan name behind both.

The calendar confirms what the name hints, and the confirmation is why the midsummer association, alone among the name's proposed meanings, can be argued from the text rather than merely from the dictionary.

And yet the verdict must still go against derivation, for two reasons that the resonance party rarely faces. The first is the chain of custody: Da Porto chose Giulietta in a tale he set, by his own frame, in the time of Bartolomeo della Scala, and nothing in the novella dates its action to July or plays on the heroine's season. The name was chosen in a text without the calendar the play later built around it, so the July fitness, however perfect, is an accident the playwright inherited a name for and then, perhaps, exploited by setting the action where he set it; the setting is his, the name is not, and the marriage of the two is the play's work, not the name's meaning. The second reason is silence. The play that puns on nearly every word it touches, that finds a jest in a name as plain as Mercutio's or a trade as plain as a nurse's, never once sounds July inside Juliet, never lets a speaker glance at the month in her presence. For the hero's name, as the next section shows, the text does the opposite, building a whole scene out of the buried sense. The contrast is the verdict in miniature. Romeo's pilgrim is in the text. Juliet's July is around the text, in its weather and its dates, real as weather is real, and no more a part of the name's derivation than the weather is part of the calendar's names. Scholarship that prints "Juliet, named for July" as a fact of etymology overstates the case; scholarship that dismisses the association as fancy understates it. The honest formula is the one the evidence keeps forcing: cognate, seasonal, unactivated, and moving precisely because the play lets it hover.

A word should also be spent on the proposed Greek root, since it belongs to the same border country. If Iulius does descend from ioulos, the down on a youth's cheek, then the heroine's name carries at its deepest stratum an image of the exact threshold she stands on, the first soft sign of adulthood on a face not yet adult. The fit is exquisite, and exquisiteness is the warning sign. The derivation of the Roman clan name is genuinely unsettled; the clan's own account traced it to Iulus the son of Aeneas, a person, not a beard, and ancient etymology and modern philology part company over the word. A claim that depends on an unsettled derivation of a name three languages upstream of the play is a resonance at two removes, and it is recorded here only to be graded: beautiful, possible, unusable as evidence. The grading instinct, applied ruthlessly to attractive claims, is what separates a names note from a names mythology.

## The Play Enacts the Pilgrim: Romeo at the Shrine

The feast scene of Act 1 is where the dictionary and the drama meet. The hero, masked and unknown, takes the heroine's hand and opens his suit in the vocabulary of pilgrimage: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5). The exchange that follows, the shared sonnet, runs on the same fuel. She answers in kind, "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much," grants that "saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch," and caps his conceit with the definition that turns his metaphor into permission: "palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5). Every term of the scene is drawn from the pilgrim's world, the shrine, the saint, the pilgrim's hand, the palmer's palm, the sin purged and taken back. The close reading of that sonnet, its structure and its consent, belongs to the scene's own studies; the concern here is only the name that stands behind the speaker. The man performing the pilgrim at the shrine is the one character in the play whose given name means pilgrim. The scene does not mention the fact. It does not need to. It enacts it.

Three grades of explanation compete for the coincidence, and they should be kept apart with the same rigour as the July claim. The weakest is accident: the pilgrim sonnet was a available devotional-erotic conceit, Petrarchan stock, and any hero might have spoken it. The middle grade is convention: Brooke's poem and the Italian tradition had already dressed the lover as a devotee at his lady's shrine, so the imagery came with the story like the feud and the friar. The strongest grade is design: the playwright, meeting a hero named pilgrim, built the first meeting out of the name's buried sense, whether he took the sense from Painter's Italianate spelling, from the living word's currency, or from the simple fact that Romeo and romero and pilgrim breathed the same air in any port where Italian was traded. The evidence cannot certify the strongest grade, because no document records the playwright glossing the name to himself. But the evidence is not neutral between the grades either. The sonnet is not inherited from Brooke, whose feast meeting is narrative summary rather than a pilgrim dialogue; the pilgrim machinery of the exchange is the play's own construction, placed at the exact threshold where the hero first speaks. A playwright constructing that scene around any other hero of romance, a Troilus or a Philaster, would have had the conceit available; around this hero, the conceit is also a gloss. The probability that the fitness went unnoticed by the most word-drunk dramatist of the age is low, and the honest verdict is that the name's meaning is activated by the scene whether or not a programme for the activation survives. Activation, not intention, is the claim the text can bear, and it is enough: an audience that knows what romeo means watches the feast scene with the name turned into action, and an audience that does not know loses nothing but a layer.

### Does any character in the play gloss the hero's name as a pilgrim?

No character glosses the name, and the play never pauses to explain what Romeo means. The pilgrim sense is carried entirely by enactment, by the feast sonnet in which the hero casts his lips as pilgrims at a shrine, and by nothing else; the meaning is performed, never annotated, in the theatre of the play.

The absence of a gloss is itself instructive: the meaning was left for hearers who brought it with them, in a city of ports and Italian merchants, rather than spelled out for those who did not, which is how buried name-meanings generally work on a stage.

The enactment also throws light backward on the name's selection by Da Porto, and a speculation may be permitted as long as it is labelled one. A teller choosing a name for a young man who will travel by night to a beloved's house, be exiled to a neighbouring city, and make a last journey to a tomb might reach, half-consciously, for the traveller's word in his language. Da Porto's hero is the most journeying of lovers: the tale is a sequence of roads, Verona to the feast, the feast to the orchard, Verona to Mantua, Mantua to the tomb. If the pilgrim sense coloured the choice, the colouring has never been documented, and the chain of custody forbids asserting it. What can be asserted is the fitness, and the fitness compounded at every retelling until, in the play, the name and the action rhyme. Readers who want the wider account of how naming works as a theme across the whole drama, the house names, the nicknames, the nameless, will find it in the study of [names and identity, and how the play's people become and unbecome themselves](/2024/04/12/names-and-identity-romeo-and-juliet/); the etymological thread followed here is one strand of that larger fabric, the strand that runs under the stage rather than across it.

## What's in a Name, Tested Against the Sources

The balcony speech is the play's own treatise on the subject, and it must be quoted as evidence here, though its close reading belongs to the scene articles that own it. The scene's dedicated study, ['What's in a Name?', the balcony's big question](/2023/08/04/whats-in-a-name-romeo-and-juliet/), reads the argument line by line; the speech is used here only for what it proves about the names themselves. Juliet, not knowing she is heard, reasons her way out of the feud's grammar: "'Tis but thy name that is my enemy; / Thou art thyself, though not a Montague" (2.2), and then the general proposition, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2). Her demand follows, "Romeo, doff thy name, / And for that name, which is no part of thee, / Take all myself" (2.2), and the hero answers by accepting a new baptism in her word: "Call me but love, and I'll be new baptised; / Henceforth I never will be Romeo" (2.2). The speech's target deserves the precision the sources make possible. The enemy she names is the surname, the house name, Montague; the given name she wields as the instrument of escape, calling him Romeo precisely to strip Romeo of Montague. Her argument, aimed at the inherited clan label, quietly assumes that the personal name is the person, the sweet-smelling rose itself. The source history shows how much machinery stands behind that assumption. The surname she wants doffed descends from Dante's Montecchi through four retellings; the given name she trusts descends from a pilgrim noun through the same four. Both are inheritances. Neither is natural. Her philosophy of names, tested against the names' actual biographies, is exactly half right, and the half it gets wrong is the half she is standing on.

This is the point at which the critics must be brought in and made to disagree. Catherine Belsey, in The Subject of Tragedy, reads the name speeches as the play's deepest exposure of how identity works. On Belsey's account, the subject is not a self that owns a name as property; the subject is produced in language, installed by the names, titles and kinship terms that a culture assigns, so that Juliet's wish to separate the man from the name asks for an essence that speech itself cannot deliver. The speech performs its own defeat: she can only unname him in words, by naming, and the rose she reaches for as proof is, as Belsey notes in effect, another word doing the same work. Against this stands the source-scholar's account, represented by Bullough's great collection and the editorial tradition that descends from it, in which the names are counters in a transmission, their interest lying in the route they travelled, Romeus to Romeo, Giulietta to Juliet, and any meaning beyond the route being the reader's embroidery. On the transmission account, asking what the names mean in the play is a category error; the play did not choose them, it received them, and received counters have no semantics worth the name. Between them stands the editorial practice of Levenson and of Brian Gibbons in his Arden edition, glossing the feast scene's pilgrims, annotating the calendar, restoring the words' period senses, a practice that assumes meanings survive inside received words and can be switched back on by a note.

The disagreement adjudicates cleanly once the two names are separated, which neither party, arguing about the names as a pair, quite does. For the surname and the house system, Belsey wins outright: Montague and Capulet in the play are nothing but social installation, labels with a body count, and the tragedy is her evidence that a subject cannot step out of the name that the city's grammar assigns. For the heroine's given name, the transmission account wins: Juliet's meanings, the diminutive and the clan root, were genuinely shed in transit, and the play activates none of them verbally; Belsey's framework, applied to Juliet, would find a constitution that the historical record shows dissolving at the Channel. For the hero's given name, the editors win against both: the pilgrim sense is neither a social installation nor a dead counter but a lexical survival, present in the word, enacted in the scene, available to any hearer who held the key, and the feast sonnet is the proof that a received name can carry a received meaning into new action. The verdict, then, is distributive. Names in this play are three different things wearing one grammatical dress: the surname is a sentence, the heroine's name is a souvenir, the hero's name is a script. Juliet's speech is right about the first, wrong about the second in an interesting way, since her own name's emptiness is an accident of translation rather than a law of language, and silently refuted by the third, since the man she addresses had already, at the feast, been his name.

### How often is the hero addressed by his full style in the play?

The full style, Romeo Montague, is assembled by the audience rather than spoken as a routine address; the play keeps the given name and the house name in separate mouths, hers and the city's. Juliet speaks Romeo as a private word; Tybalt and the Prince wield Montague as a public one.

The split delivery is the feud's grammar made audible, one person filed under two names, and it lasts until the tomb forces the two into a single story.

For readers tracing a name's meanings across a whole library of retellings, editions and translations, the working method is collation, and collation lives or dies by the notes it keeps; a personal research system such as [a structured vault for source notes](https://vaultbook.net/) is what keeps five spellings of two names, and the evidence for each, from blurring into one another. The discipline is the same whatever the shelf: date every form, name every witness, grade every claim.

## The Names as the Story's Flag: Titles, Title Pages and the Reversed Order

A story that travels for a century travels under its names, and the title pages of the journey are documents in the names' biography as surely as the dictionaries are. Da Porto's novella led with the heroine: Giulietta e Romeo, the little Giulia before the pilgrim. Bandello's telling, gathered into the Novelle under a rubric that advertises the unhappy death of two most unhappy lovers, keeps the pair in the tale's heading in her-first order in the tradition he founded. Boaistuau's French title preserves her precedence. Brooke's title page of 1562 reverses the order for English: The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet, the hero promoted to the front of the bill, and every English witness after him follows the promotion, Painter's tale heading pairing Romeo with Julietta in that order, the quartos pairing Romeo with Juliet, the Folio after them. The reversal happened once, at the Channel, at the same crossing where her suffix was lost, and the two losses belong together: the English tradition that clipped the diminutive also demoted its bearer from first place in the title. Neither change was argued for anywhere; both are the silent work of a receiving culture arranging foreign goods to its own habits, the hero first because English title pages of tragedies led with their men, the name clipped because English mouths clipped it.

Against that settled English order, the play's own last naming lands with the force of a restoration. The Prince closes the tragedy by summoning the audience to hear the tale rehearsed, and his summary names the pair in the order Da Porto had printed: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3). Juliet first, and Romeo grammatically possessed by her, her Romeo, in the play's final couplet. No argument is made for the inversion, and none is needed; the rhyme with woe asks for Romeo at the line's end, and the inversion that the rhyme requires happens to undo, in the tragedy's last breath, the demotion the English title pages had performed. The heroine who lost her suffix and her precedence in the crossing recovers the precedence at the grave, and the play's naming ends where the story's naming began, with her name leading. The detail is small and it is exactly the kind of detail a names note exists to catch, because it shows the two great facts of the transmission, inheritance and revision, operating inside a single couplet: the words are the tradition's, the order, for one line, is Verona's again.

The title pages also record the one sense in which the cliché about the names is right. Whatever the names meant, they worked, as flags work, gathering the story's identity into two words that could survive translation when nothing else could. Plots were summarised, moralised, versified and prosified out of recognition; settings shifted from Siena to Verona; friars changed their names and nurses acquired childhoods; but from 1530 onward the pair Romeo and Giulietta, in one spelling or another, is the constant by which every version is recognised as the same story. A name, the balcony speech argues, is the least part of a person. The transmission history answers that a name is the most durable part of a story, outlasting every other element the tellers revised. Both claims are true, and the tension between them is the real subject the names open up: what is negligible for a life is indispensable for a legend.

## What the Names Do Not Mean: A Cautionary Catalogue

A subject with this much charm attracts false coin, and a names note earns its keep as much by the claims it refuses as by the claims it files. The refusals fall into kinds. The first kind is the folk derivation, the meaning assigned because the sound invites it. Juliet is said, in popular retellings, to mean youthful, a gloss built by squeezing the name toward the Latin for a young person and tidying away the clan that actually stands behind it; the genuine proposal about ioulos, the down of youth, is a conjecture about a Greek ancestor of the Roman clan name, not a meaning of the Italian name, and the popular gloss survives by silently promoting the conjecture three generations. Romeo is said, in the same literature, to mean Roman, as if the hero were named for the empire; the dictionaries give the pilgrim, a word about a journey to Rome rather than about Romanness, and the difference between a citizen and a traveller is the difference between the myth and the record. Both glosses err the same way, by reaching for the meaning that flatters the story, the eternal Roman and his youthful bride, instead of the meanings the words were carrying when the tale took them up, a pilgrim on a road and a little girl of a great clan.

The second kind of false coin is the historical romance, the claim that the names are famous because they were first real. Verona's tradition of the historical lovers, the tomb, the house, the balcony assembled for visitors, treats Romeo and Giulietta as documented persons of the Scala period, and Da Porto's own frame lends the treatment colour by presenting the tale as recovered history. The documents do not follow. No record of the Scala age names the lovers; the houses are Dante's, the plot is Masuccio's, and the names are Da Porto's gift to both. The persons may condense some lost Veronese scandal, as legends do, but the names, which are the only part of the legend that can be dated with precision, date to a desk in Vicenza in the 1520s. A name with a birthday in a study is not a name from a tomb, however fine the tomb.

The third kind is the intentionalist romance, the mirror image of the second, which assigns every resonance to the playwright's design: the play, on this account, chose the pilgrim name for the shrine scene and the July name for the midsummer calendar, as a jeweller chooses stones. The chain of custody refutes the strong form of the claim at both points, since the names arrived with the story a lifetime before the playwright was born into the tradition, and Brooke's and Painter's pages had fixed the English forms before the play existed. What design the record allows is narrower and, being narrower, more impressive: a playwright who inherited both names, perceived the pilgrim inside the one because the feast scene he built required the perception, and set the other's action inside its cognate month whether by instinct or by the calendar's own logic. Scholarship that begins from the inheritance and grades the design will never need to retract; scholarship that begins from the design must keep explaining away the documents. The catalogue of refusals thus returns the argument to its foundation. Documented descent first, activation where the text proves it, resonance labelled as resonance: the rule that files the true claims is the same rule that rejects the charming ones, and a subject this charming needs the rule more than most.

## After the Play: When a Name Becomes a Common Noun

The career of the names did not end at the play; it changed direction. Until the 1590s the story of the names is a story of transmission, forms passing from book to book. After the play, the names begin passing from the book into the language, and the surest sign of the passage is grammatical: Romeo becomes a common noun. The dictionaries record the word's second life, a Romeo, meaning a lover, a passionate or romantic suitor, used with the indefinite article and the plural, of men who are not Veronese and have never heard of Mantua. A name that can take an article has completed the journey from person to type. The pilgrim noun had become a proper name in Da Porto; the proper name became a common noun again in English, and the wheel came full circle with the meaning replaced: the word that once meant a man travelling to a shrine came to mean a man travelling to a lady. Juliet made the same passage more quietly, becoming the standing name for the beloved girl of a tragedy, the answering half of the pair whenever English needs a shorthand for young love and early death, and the pair together hardened into the language's standard emblem, two names doing the work of a whole plot in any sentence that borrows them.

The afterlife confirms the transmission account in an unexpected quarter, because what the language took from the play was the pair in their English clipped and hybrid forms, Romeo and Juliet, not Romeus, not Julietta, not the Italian originals. The forms that conquered the language are the forms the London stage settled, which is a measure of where the names' power was finally minted: not in their roots, which the language discarded, but in their performance. English speakers who call a suitor a Romeo are not thinking of pilgrims, and the word works perfectly in their mouths with the pilgrim forgotten, exactly as Juliet's speech says a name should work, as a label whose history is no part of its bearer. The afterlife of the names is thus the balcony philosophy vindicated in the dictionary, at the price of everything this article has recovered. Both things are true, and a names note exists to hold them together: the meanings were real, the play enacted the deepest of them, and the language then spent the names as pure counters anyway, because counters are what a language, in the end, needs names to be.

## The Names in the Study: Editors, Dictionaries and the Glossing Tradition

The afterlife of the names has a scholarly branch, quieter than the common noun and more consequential for readers, and it runs through the notes of editions. An editor of the play inherits the pair with their buried senses in unequal repair: the hero's pilgrim recoverable by a gloss, the heroine's diminutive unrecoverable in the text's own language, the July kinship hovering over the calendar. The glossing tradition shows editors solving the inequality in characteristically different ways. Brian Gibbons, in the Arden edition, annotates the feast scene's pilgrim matter fully, giving the shrine exchange its devotional vocabulary, so that a reader meets the hero performing the pilgrim with the note's blessing at the foot of the page. Levenson's Oxford edition spreads the annotation wider, framing the play's calendar and its source inheritance in the introduction, so that the names arrive preceded by the Lammas reckoning and the Italian chain. G. Blakemore Evans, in the Cambridge edition, keeps the textual apparatus at the centre, the forms of the names in the early editions recorded among the collations, the spellings of the quartos treated as witnesses rather than ornaments. Three editorial temperaments, three different distributions of the same evidence, and a student meeting the play in any of them meets a slightly different pair of names, more or less glossed, more or less Italian, according to the edition's theory of what a reader needs.

The dictionaries, meanwhile, have kept the two histories apart with a discipline the popular literature rarely matches. The Oxford English Dictionary's entry for the hero's name files the pilgrim derivation where it belongs, at the head of the entry, and then records the word's second career as a common noun for a lover, the two senses standing in historical order, the shrine before the suitor. Name dictionaries, in their separate tradition, do the same work for the heroine: Giulia traced to the Roman clan, the diminutive explained, the July association handled with a caution that varies instructively from volume to volume, the careful ones printing the kinship as kinship and the careless ones promoting it into a derivation. The variation among reference books is itself a finding. Where the evidence is documentary, the books agree to the letter; where the evidence is resonance, they diverge in proportion to their standards, and a reader can grade a dictionary by its treatment of this single pair, as by a touchstone.

Bullough's collection stands behind the whole scholarly branch as its enabling act, because the collation on which every claim in the present article rests, the dated sequence of forms from Da Porto to the quartos, is possible in a study only because the sources were gathered, translated and printed in one place. Before the gathering, the names' history was a rumour distributed across rare books in three languages; after it, the history became a checkable sequence, and the checking changed the questions scholarship asked. The older literature asked what the names meant, and answered from ingenuity. The collated literature asks where each form stands, and answers from dated witnesses, letting meaning follow at its proper grade. The present article is written in the second manner and owes the manner to the gathering: its verdicts are distributive, its resonances labelled, its certainties confined to the custody of forms, because the evidence, once assembled, permits nothing stronger and demands nothing less. ## The Verdict the Names Carry

The evidence, held to its grades, supports a conclusion with three clauses. First, the names are inherited, not invented, and their inheritance is fully documented: a pilgrim noun and a diminished clan name, chosen by Luigi da Porto in the 1520s when he renamed Masuccio's ordinary lovers, and carried through Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke and Painter to a play that took one form from the poem and one from the prose. Second, the meanings divide by grade, and the division is the finding: Romeo's pilgrim is documented etymology defined by Dante and enacted by the play at the feast, where the hero performs his name's sense at a shrine; Giulietta's diminutive is documented morphology that the English crossing destroyed, and whose work the play rebuilt as calendar and action; Juliet's July is cognate resonance, anchored in the play's midsummer dates but never spoken, and the Greek down of youth is resonance at a further remove, to be admired and set aside. Third, the critics' quarrel over names resolves by distribution: Belsey's constituted subject governs the surnames, the transmission account governs the heroine's emptied name, and the editors' glossing practice governs the hero's, where a received meaning demonstrably survives into action. What the names carry, finally, is the whole method of the sources in two words: nothing about them is the play's invention, and the play's genius shows nowhere more clearly than in what it found to do with them, building a shrine for the pilgrim, a calendar for the little Giulia, and a last couplet that hands her back her precedence. The rose speech says a name is nothing. The names' biography answers that a name is a history, and that this pair's history, read at its proper grades, is the story of the story itself.

One consequence of the verdict deserves plain statement, because it reverses the usual direction of praise. The play is often celebrated for the freshness of its lovers, as if the pair had been minted with the verse; the names show the opposite economy at work, a dramatist spending inherited capital so skilfully that the expenditure looks like invention. Romeo and Juliet came to the stage as a pilgrim word and a little Giulia, worn by four retellings in three languages, and left it as the language's permanent emblem of love, their roots forgotten by the very audiences who made them immortal. Recovering the roots does not diminish the emblem. It restores the emblem's makers to view, Da Porto choosing, the translators respelling, the printers clipping, and the playwright, at the end of the line, finding the shrine inside the pilgrim and the summer inside the girl, and building both into a tragedy that has carried two Italian names further than any Italian book could carry them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch names note, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch names note is the table in this article that sets every historical form of the two lovers' names in sequence, from the Italian common noun romeo and Da Porto's Giulietta through the French and English respellings to the play's Romeo and Juliet, grading each claim about them as documented or resonance. The pattern it exposes is a split in the evidence: the history of the names' letters is complete and provable at every link, while the history of their meanings divides sharply, the pilgrim and the diminutive standing as documented fact and the July association standing as cognate resonance. One column settles; the other can only be graded.

### Q: Did Shakespeare coin the names of his two lovers?

No. Both names reached the playwright through the source chain, fixed in their pairing by Luigi da Porto in the 1520s and transmitted by Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke and Painter. Shakespeare's contribution was selective rather than inventive: the play's title and text combine the hero's name in the Italianate form Painter used, Romeo, with the heroine's name in the clipped form Brooke used, Juliet. The hybrid pairing is the play's own, and the feast scene's activation of the hero's pilgrim sense is the play's own, but the names themselves, their roots and their union belong to the Italian novella tradition a lifetime before the London stage.

### Q: What did the word romeo mean in Italian before it was a man's name?

It meant a pilgrim bound for Rome. The word descends from the medieval Latin Romaeus, and in the Italian of the centuries before the novella it was a term of the roads, one of the sorted titles by which pilgrimage distinguished its travellers by destination. Dante defines it with exactly that sense in the Vita Nuova, setting romei, those who go to Rome, beside palmieri, those who go overseas to the Holy Land. The meaning was neither obscure nor bookish: it was stamped on the badges pilgrims wore home and renewed on every Italian road each year, which is why the name carried its sense so plainly for the tale's first readers.

### Q: What language does the name Romeo ultimately come from?

The trail runs from Italian back through medieval Latin to Greek. The Italian romeo comes from the Latin Romaeus, a Roman or a Rome pilgrim, and Romaeus is borrowed from the Greek Rhōmaios, meaning a Roman, the name the eastern empire's subjects used for themselves. Each stage of the journey narrowed the word: a nationality in Greek, a pilgrim in Latin as the Rome journey became the word's defining act, and a lover's name in Italian after Da Porto fixed it to his hero. The Greek root is a fact of the dictionary; the narrowing is a fact of the pilgrimage centuries; only the last step, the fixing to a Veronese hero, is an act of literature.

### Q: How is a romeo different from a palmer in the pilgrim vocabulary?

The two words sort pilgrims by destination and by badge. A palmer is a pilgrim to the Holy Land, named for the palm branch brought back as the journey's proof. A romeo is a pilgrim to Rome, whose badge was the vernicle, the image of the Holy Face kept at St Peter's. Dante's taxonomy in the Vita Nuova keeps the classes distinct, adding the peregrini bound for St James in Galicia as a third. Later usage, including the play's own feast scene, blends the terms freely for poetic purposes, which is why Juliet can answer Romeo's pilgrims with a palmer's kiss; but in strict period usage the hero's name belongs to the Roman road, not the palm.

### Q: Who first gave the lovers the names Romeo and Giulietta?

Luigi da Porto, in the novella composed in the 1520s and published at Venice after his death, is the first teller on record to name the lovers Romeo and Giulietta. The earlier Sienese version by Masuccio Salernitano had called its pair Mariotto and Gianozza, ordinary names without literary ancestry, and Da Porto's rebuilding of the tale at Verona involved a deliberate renaming of both lovers, hers drawn from the Roman clan name in diminutive form, his drawn from the pilgrim vocabulary. The pairing he fixed proved indestructible: Bandello kept it, the French and English translators respelled it, and the play inherited it, so that the names are Da Porto's most durable single contribution to the story.

### Q: What were the lovers called in the earliest surviving version of the tale?

In Masuccio Salernitano's tale in Il Novellino, printed in 1476, the lovers are Mariotto and Gianozza, the heroine's name also spelled Ganozza in some witnesses. The Sienese tale already contains the secret marriage, the killing, the exile, the feigned death and the fatal tomb, but its names are ordinary local stock, chosen, apparently, for their unremarkableness, and neither carries a meaning the tale uses. The contrast with Da Porto's renaming half a century later is the cleanest evidence in the sources that the famous names were a deliberate literary act: the plot worked perfectly well under names that meant nothing, and the Verona teller chose names that meant a great deal.

### Q: Why does Da Porto's title put Giulietta before Romeo?

The order reflects the tale's centre of gravity at its founding. Da Porto's novella is framed as the story of a Veronese girl and the catastrophe that gathers around her household, and his title, Giulietta e Romeo, bills the heroine first in the manner of a tale named for its principal sufferer. The French tradition kept her precedence, and only the English crossing reversed it: Brooke's title page of 1562 leads with Romeus, Painter and the quartos follow with Romeo, and the reversed order became the pair's permanent English billing. The play's final couplet, naming "Juliet and her Romeo" over the bodies, restores the founding order for a single line at the tragedy's close.

### Q: What does the ending of the name Giulietta claim about the heroine?

The ending is the Italian diminutive suffix -etta, and it claims two things at once: youth and keeping. Morphologically it makes the heroine the little Giulia, marking her as young and not yet at full stature; socially it gives her the form of name used inside a household, by parents, nurses and lovers, rather than the full form a chronicle would use. For Da Porto's readers the suffix renewed its statement at every mention of her name, keeping her scale before them as the tale's engine. The claim was grammatical, not decorative, which is why its loss in the English clipping of the name to Juliet counts as a real loss of information, later rebuilt by the play as stated age and staged action.

### Q: From which name is Giulietta the diminutive?

Giulietta is the diminutive of Giulia, the feminine form of Giulio, the Italian descendant of the Roman family name Julius. Behind the name stands the gens Julia, the clan that traced its descent to Iulus, son of Aeneas, and through Aeneas to Venus, so that the heroine's name reaches back, by documented steps, to the founding mythology of Rome itself. A further proposal derives Julius from the Greek ioulos, the first down of a young man's beard, which would bury an image of youth at the root; that derivation is unsettled and belongs with the resonances rather than the documents. The secure chain stops at the clan, and the clan is pedigree enough.

### Q: What does Brooke call the lovers in his English poem?

Brooke's title page of 1562 names them Romeus and Juliet, and the pair of forms shows the two opposite operations English performed on the names. Romeus is the hero's Italian name Latinised, pulled back toward its ancestor Romaeus in a classicising gesture suited to a poem with classical ambitions. Juliet is the heroine's Italian name clipped, the diminutive ending struck off and the name naturalised to an English shape. One name swells toward Rome, the other shrinks toward London, and the play, choosing between the English witnesses before it, took its heroine's form from Brooke while taking its hero's from the prose tradition instead.

### Q: Why does Painter's heroine keep the name Julietta when Brooke's is Juliet?

Painter, translating prose from Boaistuau for The Palace of Pleasure, kept the Italian colouring of his material, and his heroine's name Julietta preserves the full Italianate ending as part of that colouring; his hero is likewise Romeo, unLatinised. Brooke, writing original English verse with a moralising programme, naturalised the heroine to Juliet. The difference is one of genre and purpose rather than of knowledge: the prose translator displays the foreignness his collection advertises, the verse moraliser domesticates his persons for his English readers. For the names' history the contrast is precious, because it leaves the 1560s reader two English heroines, one with her diminutive's ghost still on her name and one without.

### Q: Which forms of the names did the first printed editions of the play use?

The quartos settled the pair in the forms the language has kept ever since. The first quarto of 1597 announced An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet, and the second quarto of 1599, The Most Excellent and lamentable Tragedie, of Romeo and Juliet, confirmed Romeo and Juliet on the title page and in the text, the heroine's speech prefix shortened to Jul. in the playhouse manner. Neither early edition knows Romeus or Julietta: the hybrid selection, Painter's hero with Brooke's heroine, was made before the first printing and never revisited, and the Folio text that followed inherited the quartos' pairing without disturbance.

### Q: Is Juliet named for the month of July?

Not directly, and the honest answer separates kinship from derivation. The month takes its name from Julius Caesar, in whose honour the Roman Quintilis was renamed; the heroine's name descends from the same Julius through Giulia, the feminine of the clan name. Juliet and July are therefore cousins, two branches of one Roman root, and neither is named for the other. The association is stronger than a chance resemblance, since the kinship is real, and weaker than an etymology, since no teller named the girl for the month. The play, by fixing its action in the last days of July around a Lammas Eve birthday, warms the kinship into a resonance without ever converting it into a statement.

### Q: Why does the heroine's name survive translation while its meaning does not?

Because the meaning of Giulietta was carried by its suffix, and suffixes are the least portable part of a word. The name's sound and letters could be respelled across French and English borders, Juliette, Julietta, Juliet, but the diminutive force of -etta depended on Italian grammar, which English lacked as living equipment; in English mouths the ending registered as foreign decoration and was eventually clipped as surplus. Romeo's meaning survived better because it was lexical, a whole word's sense riding inside the word, and a determined gloss can always recover a word. The contrast is the transmission's neatest lesson: what a name means by its parts dies in translation, what it means as a whole word may live.

### Q: Where in the play does Romeo act out the pilgrim his name describes?

At the feast in Act 1, in the shared sonnet of the first meeting. The masked hero takes the heroine's hand and frames his suit entirely in pilgrimage: his unworthiest hand against her holy shrine, his lips as "two blushing pilgrims" standing ready, her answering grant that "saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch," and the exchange's seal in the palmer's kiss of palm to palm. The scene nowhere mentions his name's meaning; it simply casts the one character whose name means pilgrim in the role of pilgrim at a shrine, for fourteen lines, at the exact moment the love begins. The enactment, not any gloss, is the play's contribution to the name's history.

### Q: Which name does Juliet attack in the balcony speech, the given name or the surname?

Her argument is aimed at the surname, and its machinery depends on the distinction. The enemy she names is Montague, the house name that files the man under the feud; the given name, Romeo, she uses as the handle of the person himself, calling him by it precisely while asking him to doff the other. Her rose proposition, that a name is no part of the thing, is advanced to dissolve the clan label, not the personal one, which her own speech keeps treating as the man's true address. The irony the sources supply is that both names are inheritances of the same kind, the given name as borrowed as the surname, so her philosophy holds for the half of his style she attacks and quietly fails for the half she trusts.

### Q: Do the sources anywhere explain why Da Porto chose these two names?

No surviving document records Da Porto's reasons, and the silence is complete: the novella offers no gloss on its names, and no letter or preface of his discusses them. What scholarship has is inference from the words themselves, the pilgrim sense defined by Dante and the diminutive's grammar, and from the contrast with Masuccio's ordinary Mariotto and Gianozza, which shows the renaming to have been deliberate. Deliberate choice among meaningful words is as far as the evidence reaches; the chooser's motives, devotion, fitness for a travelling hero, the sound of the pairing, remain conjecture. The discipline of the names note is to file the inference as inference, however strong the fitness appears.

### Q: How does the name Romeo live on in English after the play?

It completes a grammatical circle, becoming a common noun again. The dictionaries record a Romeo, with article and plural, as a word for a lover or passionate suitor, applied to men with no connection to Verona; the proper name that Da Porto built from a pilgrim noun returns to common-noun duty with its meaning replaced, the journey to a shrine exchanged for the pursuit of a lady. Juliet travels with it as the standing name for the tragedy's beloved girl, and the pair together become the language's shorthand emblem for young love and early death. The afterlife vindicates the balcony speech in the dictionary's own pages: the names flourish as labels whose buried histories their users neither know nor need.

### Q: How can an essay use the names note to argue that the play inherits its lovers?

The note gives an essay a complete documentary spine: begin from the control case of Masuccio's Mariotto and Gianozza to show the plot working under meaningless names, then trace Da Porto's renaming, the pilgrim noun and the diminished clan name, through the dated respellings of Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke and Painter to the play's hybrid pair. At each stage the essay can separate what the record proves from what it merely suggests, grading the pilgrim and the diminutive as document and the July association as resonance. The conclusion the evidence supports is precise: the play's originality in the names lies not in coinage but in activation, building a feast scene that performs the hero's buried meaning and a calendar that warms the heroine's.
