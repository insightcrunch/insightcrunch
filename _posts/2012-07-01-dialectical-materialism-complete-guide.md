Dialectical materialism is the phrase under which Marxism was taught to hundreds of millions of people, printed on the first page of textbooks across a third of the world, and examined in schools where it functioned less as an argument than as a civic creed. Yet the man whose name the doctrine bears never used the phrase once. Karl Marx wrote of a dialectical method, of a materialist conception of history, of turning Hegel right side up; the compound noun that later generations treated as the title of his philosophy appears nowhere in his published work, nowhere in his manuscripts, nowhere in his letters. That single fact, small enough to fit in one sentence, reorganises everything a reader thought was settled about the subject. A doctrine with an author can be checked against the author. A doctrine assembled after the author's death, from materials he left behind and by hands other than his, has to be checked against a chain of assembly, and each link in that chain is a place where choices were made, alternatives discarded, and emphases set that no founder ever ratified.

This guide follows the chain link by link. It begins with what the doctrine claims when its defenders state it in their own strongest terms, because a system that governed the intellectual life of great states deserves to be understood before it is judged. It then separates the three different things that share the dialectical vocabulary: a general ontology, meaning a set of laws said to govern all reality from electrons to revolutions; a theory of history, which is what historical materialism actually is; and a method of presentation that Marx himself used in Capital, where categories are developed out of one another in a carefully ordered ascent. These three objects have different authors, different evidence, different vulnerabilities, and different fates. The failure to separate them is the reason almost every popular argument about dialectics talks past itself: a critic demolishes the ontology and believes Marx's economic analysis has been touched; a defender exhibits the subtlety of the method in Capital and believes the ontology has been vindicated. Neither inference holds, and the discipline of refusing both is what this guide calls the three-object rule.

The history matters as much as the logic. The raw material for the doctrine lies in Engels, above all in Anti-Duhring of 1878 and in the unfinished manuscripts later published as Dialectics of Nature, where three laws of dialectics are formulated and illustrated from physics, chemistry, biology and history. The phrase itself is generally credited to Joseph Dietzgen, a tanner and autodidact philosopher whom Marx respected, writing in the 1880s, and it was Georgi Plekhanov, the founder of Russian Marxism, who popularised it in the 1890s and gave it systematic ambitions. The codification came later still: a short text published under Stalin's name in 1938 reduced the dialectical method to four principal features, fixed the catechism in a form that could be memorised and examined, and, as scholars of the period widely note, quietly left out one of the three laws that Engels had placed at the centre. Between Plekhanov and that codification lie the philosophical battles of the Soviet 1920s, settled not by argument but by administrative decision in the early 1930s, and after the codification lies the doctrine's strangest career: its categories invoked to settle questions in genetics, linguistics and physiology, with consequences that ranged from the merely absurd to the catastrophic.

Against that career stands an equally long record of rejection from inside Marxism itself. Western Marxism, as the tradition running through Lukacs, Korsch and Gramsci came to be called, was born in an explicit refusal of the doctrine, beginning with a famous footnote in Lukacs's 1923 essay collection denying that dialectics could be extended to nature at all. Analytical Marxists later rejected dialectical reasoning as obscurantist; philosophers of science charged that the three laws are either empty truisms or false generalisations, true only because they can be stretched to fit anything. Those charges deserve their full force in this guide, and they receive it. But the record also contains serious defences that cannot be waved away: Bertell Ollman's philosophy of internal relations, Roy Bhaskar's dialectical critical realism of the early 1990s, and above all the dialectical biology practised by Richard Levins and Richard Lewontin from the mid-1980s, in which two working scientists used the categories self-consciously inside a real research programme. A guide that hid the defences would be as dishonest as one that hid the Lysenko affair.

What follows is therefore organised as an exercise in separation. Each section isolates one object, one episode or one claim, states whose it is, and asks what evidence could possibly bear on it. The reader who finishes with three distinct diagrams in mind, rather than one blurred composite labelled Marxist philosophy, will be able to read the enormous secondary literature on this subject without being conscripted by it. The reader will also be able to answer the questions that actually recur wherever the topic is examined, debated or searched for: what the doctrine is, who invented it, whether Marx held it, what its laws assert, why one law disappeared from the official version, whether anything in it survives scrutiny, and what, precisely, a person is committing to when the phrase is used with approval or contempt.

## What dialectical materialism claims when its defenders state it

The doctrine, as its classical expositors presented it, is a complete philosophy with two inseparable halves. The materialist half asserts that matter is primary: the world exists independently of any mind, consciousness is a product of highly organised matter rather than its source, and everything that occurs, from the cooling of a star to the fall of a government, occurs within one material order governed by discoverable regularities. Against idealism, which in the doctrine's vocabulary means any philosophy that makes spirit, idea or mind the foundation of reality, materialism insists that thought reflects a world it did not create. Against crude mechanism, the older materialism of the eighteenth century that treated nature as a clockwork of externally colliding particles, the doctrine adds its second half. The dialectical half asserts that this material world is not a collection of fixed things but a web of processes, that change is driven from within by contradiction rather than only imposed from without, and that development proceeds through patterns general enough to be called laws. Matter in motion, internally related, developing through its own tensions: that is the doctrine in one breath, and its expositors were proud of the synthesis, which they presented as the first worldview to be at once materialist and historical through and through.

From that core, the classical presentations unfolded in a characteristic order. First came ontology: the three laws, treated in the next sections of this guide, which described how all processes whatsoever unfold. Then came epistemology, the theory of knowledge, in which truth was understood as the reflection of objective reality in consciousness, tested and corrected in practice; the doctrine's defenders stressed that reflection was not passive copying but an active, socially organised approximation, endlessly refined. Then came logic, reconceived: formal logic, with its fixed identities and excluded middles, was assigned a legitimate but subordinate place, valid for stable everyday distinctions, while dialectical logic claimed the higher territory of movement, transition and interconnection. Finally came history, where historical materialism appeared as the extension of the same principles to society: the celebrated formulation held that dialectical materialism was the general theory and historical materialism its application to social life. That nesting of the two doctrines inside one another is exactly the point at which later confusion begins, because an application relation sounds modest but performs enormous work: it lets the authority of the general theory flow downward into the theory of history, and lets the apparent confirmations of historical analysis flow upward as evidence for the ontology.

It helps to feel the attraction the system exercised at its height, because contempt for its adherents explains nothing about its career. The doctrine promised unity. A student who mastered it possessed, in principle, a key that opened nature, history and thought with the same turns of the wrist: the same pattern of accumulating quantity breaking into new quality could be found in boiling water, in the evolution of species and in the maturation of a revolutionary crisis; the same interpenetration of opposites governed the electron and the class struggle. For movements that understood themselves as scientific, this universality was not decoration but the substance of the claim to legitimacy. Marxism presented itself as the science of society, and dialectical materialism was the certificate that the science rested on the deepest available knowledge of how reality itself behaves. Cadres educated in the system experienced it as enlightenment: a single intelligible order behind the chaos of events, a refutation in advance of religion, of bourgeois eclecticism, of the idea that history is accident. One may conclude that the certificate was forged; one cannot understand the twentieth century without grasping why so many intelligent people framed it.

The doctrine also carried a distinctive attitude towards its own past, which its expositors made explicit. Philosophy before Marx, on this account, had achieved two great partial truths that had never been united: Greek and then Hegelian dialectics, which grasped universal connection and development but housed them in a system where spirit was the protagonist, and materialism from the atomists through Feuerbach, which grasped the priority of matter but pictured it statically, as a world of things to be contemplated rather than processes to be transformed. Marxism, in the standard narrative, performed the union: it took Hegel's dialectic, inverted its direction, and grounded it in a materialism itself made dynamic. The famous image of the kernel and the shell, which Marx himself used of Hegel in the afterword to the second German edition of Capital, was extended by later expositors into a complete genealogy of the doctrine, as though the union of the two traditions had been Marx's own announced philosophical programme. Here the separation discipline must already be applied: Marx's remark concerns his method of inquiry and presentation, a matter this guide treats later in its own right. The claim that he thereby founded a general ontology of nature is a further step, taken by others, and it is the step on which everything controversial depends.

A last feature of the classical presentation deserves emphasis because it determined how the doctrine behaved when challenged. The system was offered not as one philosophy among competitors but as the philosophical expression of a science and a movement, and its texts were correspondingly written in a voice of settled authority. Where academic philosophy hedges, the catechisms declared. Where a scientist reports a result with its error bars, the textbooks reported laws. This tone was not incidental vanity; it followed from the doctrine's institutional position after codification, examined below, in which it served states as an official worldview and parties as a test of loyalty. But the tone was present in embryo from the beginning, in the confidence with which very general patterns, abstracted from selected examples, were promoted to the rank of laws of being. To state the doctrine fairly, as this section set out to do, is to state that confidence as its defenders felt it: the sense of possessing, at last, the grammar of reality itself. The sections that follow ask, piece by piece, how much of that grammar survives when its authorship, its history and its logic are examined separately.

## A term with a birthday: Dietzgen, Plekhanov and the making of a label

Names are not neutral containers. When a body of thought receives a single compact name, the name begins to do argumentative work: it suggests a single author, a single act of creation, a finished system that can be summarised on one page and defended or attacked as a unit. Dialectical materialism acquired exactly that kind of name, and the story of the naming is the quickest route into everything this guide needs to separate. Marx died in 1883. The phrase that would become the title of his supposed philosophy enters the record after his death, in the writings of a man who was not his disciple in any organisational sense and whose philosophical work Marx had read with a mixture of respect and exasperation.

Joseph Dietzgen was a master tanner from the Rhineland, self-educated, active in the socialist movement, and the author of philosophical essays that attempted to restate materialism in a form adequate to modern science. In the 1880s, in writings including his excursions of a socialist into epistemology, he used the expression dialectical materialism to name the standpoint he was developing. The attribution is the standard one in the scholarship and should be stated as such: the phrase is generally credited to Dietzgen, which is the honest form for a coinage that spread through a movement's journals rather than being trademarked in a single famous book. What matters for understanding is less the exact page than the kind of thinker doing the coining. Dietzgen was reaching for a label that would distinguish a dynamic, historically minded materialism from the static materialism associated with popularisers such as Buchner and Moleschott, whose force-and-matter picture of the world Marx and Engels had both criticised as crude. The label was, from birth, a piece of positioning inside a crowded nineteenth-century field.

### Did Marx use the term dialectical materialism?

No. The phrase is generally credited to Joseph Dietzgen in the 1880s and was popularised by Plekhanov after Marx's death. It was codified as official doctrine in a Soviet text of 1938. Marx wrote of his dialectical method, never of dialectical materialism.

The word then travelled. Georgi Plekhanov, the most philosophically ambitious of the Russian Marxists, took it up in the early 1890s, in the polemical writings against the Russian populists and the German revisionists where he was constructing an orthodox Marxism for a movement that needed doctrinal boundaries. In Plekhanov's hands the phrase acquired its mature shape: it named not just a standpoint but a system, with Engels's works treated as its founding texts and with the dialectics of nature built into its foundations. Plekhanov's authority in the international movement was immense; Lenin studied his philosophical writings closely and said so. Through Plekhanov, the term passed into the common property of the Second International's Marxist parties, and from there into the Bolshevik tradition, where it would eventually be fixed by state power. The journey is worth tracing slowly because it shows how a label can acquire an author retrospectively. By the time the phrase was ubiquitous, its users naturally assumed it must be Marx's own, since everything authoritative in the movement was referred to Marx. The assumption is false, and its falsity is not a pedant's footnote: it relocates the doctrine from the category of a thinker's work to the category of a tradition's construction, where it must be evaluated by the standards appropriate to constructions, including fidelity, selection and institutional interest.

What did Marx himself call his standpoint? The textual record gives several answers, none of them the later label. In the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy of 1859, he described the guiding thread of his studies, the materialist account of law, civil society and the state that generations would call historical materialism, though that phrase too is later coinage, popularised by Engels. In correspondence, Engels reported that they had settled on materialism as the designation for their view against idealism, a deliberately modest label. In the afterword to the second German edition of Capital in 1873, Marx described his dialectical method and contrasted it with Hegel's, insisting that with him the dialectic stands on its feet rather than its head. A method, a conception of history, a materialist standpoint: the components are all present in Marx's own vocabulary, but they are components, named separately, each attached to a specific work and problem. The synthesis into a single named philosophy of everything is the work of successors. Recognising this does not refute the synthesis; traditions are entitled to systematise their founders. It does, however, change the question a critical reader asks. Instead of asking whether Marx's philosophy is true, the reader asks two questions: whether the systematisation is faithful to the components it assembles, and whether the assembled system is true on its own merits. The three-object rule, which this guide will state formally later, is simply the habit of keeping those questions distinct.

One more step in the naming story carries a lesson about how doctrines harden. Within the Soviet tradition the phrase was eventually compressed into an acronym, diamat, taught as a subject with its own departments, textbooks and examinations, paired with its social counterpart, historical materialism, abbreviated in parallel. Acronyms are the tombstones of arguments: once a doctrine can be abbreviated, it can be administered. The compression also produced a fateful symmetry in the curriculum. Students met two parallel subjects, one about nature and thought in general, one about society, each with its laws and its examination questions, and the symmetry itself taught the nesting claim, that the second subject was the first applied to a special domain, more effectively than any argument could have done. A reader who wants to understand why the conflation of the three objects proved so durable should look less at any single text than at that curricular architecture, which reproduced the conflation in every cohort of students for two generations. The parallel in other intellectual traditions is instructive: wherever a founder's explorations become a school subject, the school subject acquires boundaries the founder never drew, and the boundaries then read back into the founder's texts as though they had been found there.

## Engels and the raw material: a polemic and a drawer of manuscripts

If the doctrine has a quarry, it is the later work of Friedrich Engels. Two texts supply nearly all of its ore. The first is Anti-Duhring, published in 1878, a polemic against the Berlin lecturer Eugen Duhring whose system had been winning converts inside German social democracy. Engels wrote it with Marx's knowledge and a contribution from Marx himself on political economy, and the movement received it as the nearest thing to an official exposition of their shared outlook. Scattered through its attack on Duhring's philosophy is the positive material from which the doctrine was later built: discussions of motion as the mode of existence of matter, of the interconnection of the sciences, of quantity passing into quality in chemistry and physics, of contradiction in mathematics and mechanics, of the negation of the negation illustrated from the life cycle of a barley seed and the history of philosophy. The second text is stranger. Between the 1870s and the 1880s Engels filled notebooks with studies toward a planned work on the dialectics of nature, following the physics, chemistry and biology of his day with formidable energy. The work was never finished, never published in his lifetime, and slept in manuscript until 1925, when it appeared in Moscow. The founding text of the dialectics of nature, in other words, reached its readers as a posthumous archive, complete with fragments, reading notes and drafts at every stage of polish, and the doctrine built upon it treated those fragments with a reverence their unfinished state could not support.

Engels's own account of what he was doing deserves careful statement, because later codifications abandoned precisely the caution he expressed. He presented the laws of dialectics as abstracted from nature and history, not imposed on them: discovered by studying how processes actually unfold, in the same spirit in which the laws of any science are distilled from instances. The dialectic, in a much quoted formulation, was the science of the general laws of motion of the external world and of human thought, and the two sets of laws were identical in substance though different in expression, since human thought itself was a natural and historical product. Two implications followed for Engels that later orthodoxy found inconvenient. First, if the laws are abstracted from the sciences, then the sciences are the tribunal: a dialectical claim that collided with established scientific results would have to yield, because the abstraction has no other source of authority. Second, the illustrations Engels chose were hostages to the science of his period. His examples from caloric theory, from the electrodynamics of the 1870s, from cell theory and Darwinian evolution, belong to a scientific landscape that has been rebuilt several times since, so that a defence of his project must constantly distinguish the pattern he claimed to see from the period evidence through which he saw it. A doctrine that canonised the illustrations along with the pattern disabled that distinction, and much of the embarrassment that later attached to the dialectics of nature flowed from exactly that canonisation.

### What did Engels claim to be doing with the laws of dialectics?

Engels claimed to abstract the laws from nature and history rather than impose them, treating the sciences as the tribunal for his generalisations. His examples came from the physics, chemistry and biology available to him, and his unfinished manuscripts reached print only decades after his death, in a fragmentary state.

The question of whether Engels spoke for Marx has generated a library of its own, and this guide must place it without pretending to settle what belongs elsewhere in the series. The biographical evidence is genuinely mixed, and honest scholars have drawn opposed conclusions from it. Engels was Marx's collaborator of forty years, his editor, his financial support and the executor of his literary remains; Marx read Anti-Duhring in manuscript and contributed to it, and there is no record of his objecting to its philosophical chapters. Against any simple identification stands the division of labour the two men themselves acknowledged: Marx's life work was the critique of political economy, and the excursions into natural philosophy were Engels's project, pursued in years when Marx's health and his work on Capital consumed him. Later critics, from Lukacs onward, argued that Engels's extension of dialectics to a nature without subjects quietly changed the character of the dialectic itself, replacing the interplay of subject and object in historical practice with a contemplative gaze at processes in which no one participates. Defenders reply that Marx's own writings on the metabolism between humanity and nature, and his enthusiasm for Darwin, show a thinker for whom nature was no mere backdrop. The dispute is real, and its detailed adjudication belongs to the guide devoted to [Engels's dialectics of nature](/2012/09/01/engels-dialectics-of-nature-guide/) later in this series. What matters here is narrower and can be stated with confidence: whatever Marx thought of Engels's project, he did not himself formulate a dialectics of nature, did not state three laws of anything, and did not build a general ontology. The raw material of the doctrine is Engelsian. The doctrine built from it is posthumous to both men.

There is a further texture to the raw material that the catechisms ironed flat. Engels was a dialectician of considerable suppleness when he was arguing rather than systematising. In his hands the categories were instruments for embarrassing opponents who treated fixed abstractions as eternal: against Duhring's final truths he mobilised the fluidity of boundaries, the relativity of distinctions like cause and effect at different levels of analysis, the way a definition adequate at one scale fails at another. Readers who come to Anti-Duhring expecting a system often find instead a polemicist's handbook, closer in spirit to a manual of intellectual self-defence than to a metaphysics. The codifiers needed a metaphysics, however, and so the handbook was mined for propositions, the propositions were numbered, and the numbered propositions were enthroned as laws. Something similar happens whenever a supple thinker is converted into a syllabus, and the pattern will recur in this guide's account of Marx's own method, which suffered an analogous flattening. Keeping the quarry distinct from the building is the first application of the separation discipline, and it costs nothing but attention.

## The first law: how quantity passes into quality

The law of the transformation of quantity into quality, and the reverse, asserts that change is not uniformly gradual. As a magnitude accumulates, a process may cross a threshold at which its character alters: not merely more of the same, but something different in kind, with new properties that the accumulated quantity did not display. Engels, following Hegel's doctrine of measure, illustrated the pattern from the chemistry of his day, from the series of hydrocarbons whose members differ by increments of composition yet display distinct properties, and from water, which remains water through a range of temperatures and then, at the boiling point, becomes steam. The social illustrations came just as readily to the tradition that inherited the law: a sum of money becomes capital only when it reaches a magnitude sufficient to command the labour of others, a point Marx himself had marked in Capital when describing how the owner of a few workers must cross a threshold before he ceases to labour alongside them and becomes a capitalist in the full sense. Of the three laws, this one has always worn the most respectable face, because threshold phenomena are real and ubiquitous in the sciences that matured after Engels wrote: phase transitions in physics, critical masses, tipping points in ecology, the sudden crystallisation of a crowd into a movement. Nobody disputes that nature and society are full of thresholds. The dispute concerns what the law adds beyond the observation.

### How does quantity pass into quality in the standard examples?

Water heated degree by degree stays liquid until a threshold, where it becomes steam; a workshop owner employing a few hands remains a worker among workers until the scale crosses a point where he becomes a capitalist. The law names the breakpoint where accumulation changes character.

The critical analysis of the law, when it arrives later in this guide, will press a distinction that can already be prepared here. A threshold in a particular process is a fact about that process, explained by its specific mechanism: the boiling of water is explained by molecular kinetics, not by a general law of quantity and quality, and no physicist consults the dialectical law before predicting the phase behaviour of a new substance. The law's defenders have two replies available, and the difference between them matters. The modest reply treats the law as a heuristic, a standing instruction to look for thresholds, nonlinearities and emergent properties rather than assuming smooth proportionality everywhere; as a heuristic it is honourable and has genuinely guided researchers, including the dialectical biologists examined below. The ambitious reply treats the law as a piece of knowledge in its own right, a truth about all processes that retains content even before any particular threshold is identified; it is this ambitious version that critics charge with emptiness, since a law compatible with every possible course of events, gradual or abrupt, anticipated at any point or none, predicts nothing in particular. Engels's own formulation, attentive readers note, often sits closer to the modest version: the examples do the work, and the law summarises them. The catechisms, needing certainties, habitually taught the ambitious version. The lesson generalises across the whole doctrine: at almost every point where the codified system is vulnerable, the vulnerability was introduced by the codification's promotion of a pattern into a law, and the original texts contain resources for a humbler and stronger position that orthodoxy could not afford to occupy, because humility is not a quality that state philosophies can teach.

## The second law: the interpenetration of opposites

The second law asserts that processes are driven by internal opposites: that within any developing whole there are opposed tendencies, aspects or forces, each defined partly through the other, whose interplay constitutes the motor of change. Engels's illustrations ranged from the poles of a magnet and the positive and negative charges of electricity to the differential and integral of the calculus and the struggle of species in Darwinian nature; in society, the tradition pointed to classes, to the forces and relations of production, to use value and exchange value coiled inside the commodity. The law also carried an epistemological rider that its defenders prized: categories that formal thought holds apart, identity and difference, cause and effect, necessity and chance, were to be understood as interpenetrating, each passing into the other at the limits of analysis, so that the intellect must learn to hold opposed determinations together rather than sorting them into sealed compartments.

Stated at that level of generality, the law divides its audiences. To readers formed by traditions that prize paradox and process, it articulates something they recognise from experience: organisations are undone by the very strengths that built them, reforms entrench what they were meant to dissolve, a medicine at one dose is a poison at another. To readers formed by analytic philosophy, the law looks like a licence for vagueness, since the notion of opposite is stretched across logical contradiction, physical polarity, biological antagonism and social conflict, four relations with almost nothing in common beyond a metaphor of tension. Much depends, as the detailed treatment of [contradiction in Marxist method](/2012/08/01/contradiction-in-marxist-method/) shows in its own right, on which of these relations is meant in a given use; Marx's own practice in Capital works with a specific and disciplined notion, the internal tension of a form that must move because its own conditions undermine it, which is far narrower than the universal polarity the second law proclaims. The codified doctrine, by contrast, needed the wide version, because only the wide version could claim jurisdiction over nature as a whole. The width is the weakness: a category that covers magnets and revolutions alike risks explaining the behaviour of neither, and the law's career in Soviet natural science, where opposites were discovered in everything from linguistics to genetics with results examined below, illustrates the risk in institutional form.

### What does the interpenetration of opposites actually assert?

It asserts that developing wholes contain opposed tendencies defined through each other, and that their interplay, rather than external impact alone, drives change. Its examples span magnetic poles, electrical charges, species in competition and classes in society, which is exactly the breadth its critics question.

There remains a defensible core that even unsympathetic readers have acknowledged. The insistence that the relevant unit of analysis is often a relation rather than a thing, and that the terms of a relation can be constituted by it, anticipated themes that later philosophy and science developed independently: structuralism's relational account of meaning, ecology's constitution of organisms by their niches, the philosophy of internal relations that Ollman would build into a systematic defence. Whether those developments owe anything to the second law historically is doubtful in most cases; they proceeded from their own problems. But the convergence shows that the intuition behind the law was not foolishness. It was an intuition about the primacy of relations, expressed by Engels in the vocabulary available to him, and then frozen by his successors into a formula whose repetition substituted for the work of showing, case by case, which relations are internal and why. The formula is what failed; the intuition, detached from the formula, survived to be rediscovered under other names, which is a pattern the reader will meet again before this guide ends.

## The third law: the negation of the negation, and its strange disappearance

The third law is the most Hegelian and the most vulnerable. It asserts that development proceeds through negation of the negation: a state of affairs generates its opposite, and that opposite is in turn negated, not by a return to the starting point but by a supersession that preserves something of both earlier stages at a higher level of organisation. Engels's barley seed supplied the homely illustration: the seed is negated by the plant that grows from it and destroys it as a seed; the plant is negated in turn by its death, which yields many seeds, so that the cycle closes at an enriched level, one seed having become a sheaf. Higher illustrations followed: in the history of philosophy, in geology, in mathematics, where a magnitude negated and the negation negated yields not the original but a power. The law was meant to capture the spiral shape that the tradition discerned in development generally, progress through loss, continuity through rupture.

### What is the negation of the negation meant to capture?

It is meant to capture development as a spiral rather than a circle or a straight line: a stage generates its opposite, the opposite is itself superseded, and the outcome preserves elements of both at a different level. The barley seed, negated by the plant and restored many times over at harvest, is the standard example.

Even sympathetic readers have always stumbled here. The barley seed illustrates a life cycle, but nothing in botany requires the vocabulary of negation to describe germination, growth and reproduction, and the multiplication of seeds is a fact about reproductive biology rather than the resolution of a contradiction. Worse, the pattern can be imposed on any sequence of three stages whatsoever, since with sufficient ingenuity a middle term can always be cast as a negation and an end term as its overcoming; a law that every narrative satisfies is a law that no narrative tests. Within the Marxist tradition itself the law embarrassed serious thinkers: it seemed to guarantee progress by logical machinery, to promise that supersession must come and must conserve, and guarantees of that kind sit badly with a politics that also insists on the openness of history and the possibility of catastrophe. Marx, characteristically, used the phrase once in a famous passage of Capital about the expropriation of the expropriators, where it names a specific historical sequence, the negation of individual property by capitalist property and of capitalist property by socialised production, argued from the tendencies of the system rather than deduced from a universal pattern. The distance between that single argued use and a law of all development is the distance between Marx's method and the ontology built in his name, compressed into one example.

Then comes the episode that no account of the doctrine may skip, because it reveals more about the doctrine's nature than any of its propositions. When the official codification appeared in 1938, in the short text on dialectical and historical materialism published as part of the Short Course history of the Soviet party, the dialectical method was presented through four principal features: the interconnection of phenomena, their incessant movement and development, the passage of quantitative into qualitative change, and the internal contradictions and struggle of opposites that drive development. Three of Engels's themes are recognisably there. The negation of the negation is not. The law that described how every stage is itself superseded, how no order is final, how the negation applies to the negators in their turn, had vanished from the official summary of the philosophy of a state whose ruling stratum had every institutional reason to dislike it. Scholars have debated the motive: the law's defenders in the Soviet philosophical establishment had been associated with defeated factions; the law smacked of the Hegelianism that the codifiers were concerned to prune; above all, a doctrine whose third law whispers that the existing order too shall be negated is an awkward creed for an order demanding permanence. Whatever the weighting of motives, the structural lesson is the one this guide draws: a philosophy whose central laws can be edited by political authority, silently and without acknowledgement, is functioning as something other than a philosophy. It is functioning as a state doctrine, and its subsequent history must be read with that function in view. The question of how the codification related to the broader construction of an official Marxism-Leninism is pursued in the series guide to [Stalin and Marxism-Leninism](/2019/03/15/stalin-and-marxism-leninism/).

## From open controversy to settled doctrine: the philosophical battles of the Soviet 1920s

The doctrine was not born settled. In the Soviet Union of the 1920s, philosophy was a genuinely contested field, and the contest is worth recovering because it shows what was lost when the contest was closed. Two broad camps disputed the foundations. The mechanists, strong among natural scientists and their philosophical allies, held that the sciences themselves, pursued materialistically, would progressively dissolve philosophical questions, and that dialectics as a separate discipline was a Hegelian survival destined to wither as physics and biology matured. Their opponents, gathered around Abram Deborin and often called the dialecticians, defended the autonomy of dialectical philosophy, its Hegelian depth and its jurisdiction over the interpretation of science. The arguments were sophisticated on both sides; journals flourished; the heritage of Plekhanov, himself a dialectician of the Hegelian school, was claimed and counterclaimed. Underneath the conceptual dispute lay an institutional one about who would govern the philosophy of a revolutionary state: the laboratories or the institutes of philosophy.

The resolution, when it came at the end of the 1920s and the beginning of the 1930s, was not a resolution by argument. The Deborin school was condemned in 1930 and 1931 by party decision as a species of Menshevising idealism, a label whose political charge did the work that refutation had not; leading mechanists had already been marginalised. In place of both camps, a new orthodoxy was installed under administrators whose qualification was reliability. The episode established the pattern for the doctrine's mature form: disputed questions of philosophy were to be settled by the authority that also settled disputed questions of everything else, and the settlement was then taught as the outcome of the doctrine's own logic. A reader evaluating the four features of the 1938 codification should hold this prehistory in mind. The codification did not merely summarise Engels; it closed a battlefield, and its silences, above all the missing law, are the marks left by the manner of the closing. The philosophy that emerged was deliberately simplified for transmission: a small set of features, a small set of laws reduced in the summary to tendencies, a vocabulary of examples drawn from a fixed stock, all of it examinable, all of it reproducible by teachers who were themselves products of the same examinations. What such a system gains in uniformity it loses in the capacity for self-correction, and the loss was compounded by the doctrine's claim to stand above the sciences as their philosophical court of appeal. Engels had made the sciences the tribunal of the laws, since the laws were abstracted from them. The administered doctrine inverted the relation: the laws, now fixed, became the tribunal of the sciences, and scientists whose results sat awkwardly with the fixed patterns learned to redescribe their work in the approved vocabulary or to suffer for the discrepancy.

### How did a philosophy become a state doctrine?

Through institutional capture in stages: party schools and institutes gave the doctrine a teaching apparatus, the philosophical controversies of the Soviet 1920s were closed by administrative condemnation rather than argument, and a short codifying text then fixed four features of the method as the examinable standard for a whole society.

The career of the finished doctrine across the socialist world followed the Soviet template with local variations. In China, the doctrine arrived through translation and was reworked by Mao, whose essay On Contradiction gave the interpenetration of opposites a distinctive emphasis, elevating contradiction to the central category and treating the other laws as subordinate or derivative; the essay remains the most widely read single text the tradition produced on the subject, and its demotion of the negation of the negation went largely unnoticed by readers who had never met the law it displaced. In the European people's democracies after 1945, diamat departments were installed in universities on the Soviet model, staffed increasingly by scholars trained in the catechism, while genuine philosophical talent in those countries often migrated into logic, history of philosophy or quiet dissent. The doctrine's global footprint was thus enormous and shallow at once: enormous, because for two generations it structured the official intellectual life of states containing a large fraction of humanity; shallow, because its transmission depended on the institutions that enforced it, and where those institutions relaxed, the doctrine's hold relaxed with them in a way that the hold of, say, Darwinism or calculus, which rest on demonstrable fruits, did not. That contrast, between doctrines that survive their patrons and doctrines that fall with them, is one of the most instructive tests the history offers, and this guide returns to it when the defences are weighed.

## A philosophy with jurisdiction: the doctrine and the sciences, including Lysenko

A general ontology that claims jurisdiction over all processes will eventually be asked to rule on particular ones, and the record of those rulings is the doctrine's heaviest liability. The most consequential case is the rise of Trofim Lysenko, the agronomist whose rejection of Mendelian genetics, dressed in the vocabulary of dialectics and of the malleability of nature under practice, won state backing and culminated in the 1948 session of the Soviet agricultural academy where genetics was effectively proscribed as a bourgeois and idealist science. Soviet genetics, which had been world class, was dismantled; its leading figure, Nikolai Vavilov, had already died in prison in 1943. The harvests promised by Lysenko's methods did not materialise, the ban on genetics outlasted Stalin and was dismantled only in the mid-1960s, and Soviet biology paid for the episode with decades. The episode must be handled factually, as the record of what happened when a state philosophy met a science it disliked: the dialectical categories did not generate Lysenko's agronomy, which had roots in practical promises, institutional ambition and the politics of the period, but they supplied the language in which genetics could be condemned as static, metaphysical and hostile to development, and the institutional machinery of the doctrine ensured that the condemnation was not one opinion among others but a verdict with administrative force. Language plus machinery: that is the precise indictment, and it is stronger for refusing the melodrama of blaming three laws for a famine.

The pattern repeated at lower intensity across the sciences. Relativity and quantum mechanics were periodically suspected of idealism, because their departures from classical intuitions about matter and causality could be made to sound like departures from materialism itself; Soviet physicists of real distinction defended their disciplines, often by demonstrating that the suspect theories could be redescribed in dialectical vocabulary, a protective colouring that wasted talent on translation exercises. Cybernetics was denounced for a time as a reactionary pseudoscience before being embraced as a tool of planning. Linguistics was abruptly reorganised by a personal intervention in 1950 that, by a curious inversion, freed it from a previously enforced orthodoxy. In each case the philosophical merits were secondary to the jurisdictional fact: a doctrine installed as the court of appeal for science will be used, by somebody, to appeal against science. Defenders of the doctrine reply, with some justice, that these were abuses of a philosophy by a police state rather than consequences of the philosophy itself, and that liberal orders have their own records of science bent by power and money. The reply deserves its weight. But it meets a counter-consideration that this guide regards as decisive in kind if not in degree: the doctrine claimed, as part of its content, that its categories described reality more adequately than the supposedly one-sided abstractions of the special sciences, and it institutionalised that claim. A philosophy that merely exists alongside science cannot be blamed for what tyrants do with it; a philosophy that asserts appellate jurisdiction, and is given it, shares responsibility for the judgments of its court.

Set against this record one genuine complication, which honest treatment requires. The same decades produced Soviet and East European scientists, mathematicians and philosophers of science who worked creatively with or around the doctrine, and in a few fields, parts of psychology and the theory of activity among them, Hegelian and dialectical motifs contributed to original research programmes whose value is recognised independently of the politics that housed them. The blanket identification of the doctrine with intellectual sterility is a Cold War inheritance that the record does not support in its simple form. What the record supports is a subtler claim: the doctrine's institutional form made its intellectual content hostage, so that even its fruitful uses occurred under a permanent risk of administrative interruption, and its practitioners knew it. Fruitfulness under such conditions testifies to the practitioners, not to the arrangements.

## The Western rejection: a footnote that founded a tradition

Western Marxism, the body of thought that runs from the Lukacs and Korsch of 1923 through the Frankfurt School to Gramsci's prison notebooks and beyond, was constituted by a refusal, and the refusal was aimed precisely at the object this guide has been isolating. In a footnote to the essay on reification in History and Class Consciousness, published in 1923, Georg Lukacs remarked that Engels's account of dialectics in nature missed the decisive point: the dialectic, on Lukacs's reading of Marx, lives in the relation of subject and object within history, in the self-knowledge of a society acting on itself, and a nature contemplated from outside, following its laws whether or not any subject knows them, offers no purchase for that relation. Engels, following Hegel's mistaken example, had extended the method beyond the domain where it has meaning. The argument is compressed to a few lines, and its consequences were vast. If dialectics belongs to history alone, then the general ontology collapses, while historical materialism and the method of Capital survive the collapse untouched; the three-object rule was being practised, avant la lettre, by the tradition that most needed it.

The footnote detonated inside the movement. Karl Korsch, whose Marxism and Philosophy appeared in the same year, mounted a parallel defence of the historical character of Marxist theory against its hardening into a worldview of eternal laws, and both books were condemned by the Communist International, whose spokesmen correctly perceived that a dialectics confined to history could not serve as the universal creed of a universal movement. Zinoviev's denunciations at the Fifth Congress of 1924 mark the moment when the two Marxisms, the administered doctrine of the East and the critical philosophy of the West, formally parted. Gramsci, writing in a fascist prison a decade later, carried the historical reading further under the name of the philosophy of praxis, treating the doctrine's truth as inseparable from its historical efficacy and quietly discarding the dialectics of nature as a theological survival. The Frankfurt thinkers, Adorno above all, radicalised the suspicion of system itself: Adorno's negative dialectics, elaborated in the 1960s, kept the name dialectics while refusing every law, every closure and every guaranteed supersession, a deliberate inversion in which dialectical thinking survives by renouncing everything the catechism meant by it. Whatever one makes of these constructions philosophically, their collective testimony is historical evidence of the first rank: the most original Marxist minds of the century, working in freedom or in prison but outside the administered system, converged on the judgement that the general ontology was the expendable part of the inheritance.

A second wave of rejection arrived from a different direction and with different weapons. The analytical Marxism that emerged in the late 1970s, associated with G. A. Cohen's defence of historical materialism and with Jon Elster's methodological polemics, subjected dialectical reasoning to the standards of analytic philosophy and found it wanting in clarity, in mechanism and in falsifiable content; Elster in particular treated dialectical formulations as a refuge from the obligation to specify how one thing produces another. The analytical critics are instructive precisely because their rejection was selective in the three-object pattern: Cohen's book is a rigorous reconstruction of historical materialism that discards the dialectics of nature without ceremony, and the broader debate is followed in the series treatment of [analytical Marxism and dialectics](/2012/10/01/analytical-marxism-and-dialectics/). Meanwhile the Althusserian school in France, influential in the 1960s and 1970s, rebuilt Marxist philosophy on structural rather than dialectical-ontological foundations, retaining a transformed notion of contradiction while breaking with the Hegelian lineage the catechism had canonised. By the final decades of the century, the map was clear. Inside living Marxist philosophy, the general ontology survived mainly where institutions protected it; everywhere else, Marxists had either quietly dropped it, transmuted it beyond recognition, or retained it only in the heuristic and methodological registers examined among the defences below. The doctrine that had claimed to be the philosophy of Marxism had become, in the West, a position a Marxist adopted only by arguing for it against other Marxists.

## The strongest objection: laws that are trivial where true and false where substantive

The philosophical objection to the doctrine deserves statement at the strength its best proponents gave it, because weaker versions are easily batted away and the strong version is the one any honest defence must survive. It runs as follows. Take any of the three laws and ask what observation would count against it. Water boils at a threshold: quantity into quality. A gas expands smoothly without any threshold the law can specify in advance: also compatible, since the law never says where thresholds lie or that every accumulation has one. A seed becomes a plant and then many seeds: negation of the negation. A species evolves without any three-stage rhythm, a star collapses without conserving anything at a higher level, a revolution produces a restoration worse than what it replaced: each can be redescribed, with patience, in the law's vocabulary, or quietly left unmentioned. A law that no possible event can embarrass is not a law of nature but a scheme of redescription, and schemes of redescription earn their keep only if the redescription illuminates; the objector's follow-up is that the illumination is borrowed, since all the explanatory work in every standard example is done by the specific science, molecular kinetics for the steam, genetics for the seed, political history for the revolution. Remove the dialectical commentary and the explanations stand complete. Keep it, and nothing is added except a vocabulary of applause for whatever occurred.

The objection sharpens against the law of opposites. Contradiction in logic is a relation between propositions; polarity in physics is a relation between charges or poles, governed by exact mathematics; antagonism between classes is a relation between groups with opposed interests, mediated by institutions, beliefs and organisation. To gather these under one law is, the objector charges, an equivocation that trades on a single word for three unlike relations, and the equivocation is not accidental but functional: it lets the doctrine transfer the certainty felt in one domain, where the relation is precise, into domains where the claim is loose. The Popperian version of the charge, developed by Karl Popper in his wartime and postwar writings on historicism and on dialectics, adds that the tolerance of contradiction corrodes the machinery of criticism itself: a method that welcomes contradictions as the engine of reality has disabled the alarm by which thought detects its own errors, since any refutation can be welcomed as the negation that the theory predicted. Defenders protest that Popper attacked a caricature, that dialectical contradiction was never the assertion of both sides of a logical contradiction in the same respect, and the protest has force against careless readings. But the burden the objection lays down survives the protest: if dialectical contradiction is not logical contradiction, the doctrine owes its critics a precise account of what it is in each domain of application, and the history of the catechisms is a history of that debt going unpaid, the single elastic word doing duty wherever precision was requested.

A third strand of the objection is predictive. No discovery in the natural sciences was ever derived from the three laws in advance of the science that established it: no element, no particle, no biological mechanism was predicted by dialectics and then found. The tradition's examples are uniformly retrospective, patterns discerned in results obtained by other means, and a method that only applauds retrospectively is, by the standards the doctrine itself proclaimed when it called itself scientific, not a science but a commentary. This guide has been charged by its own structure to state that case at full strength, and it should be recorded that the case, in this form, is very strong against the codified doctrine's ambitious self-presentation. What it does not touch is equally important, and the separation discipline now earns its keep: the objection says nothing against historical materialism, which makes risky claims about social formations that can and do fail, and nothing against Marx's procedure in Capital, which is an argument about a specific object, capitalism, whose categories can be criticised on their merits. The objection demolishes a universal ontology's claim to be a science. It leaves standing, for separate judgement, a theory of history and a method of presentation. The defences considered next are best understood as attempts, by thinkers who had absorbed some part of this objection, to rebuild on exactly that separated ground.

## The defences that deserve a hearing: internal relations, critical realism, dialectical biology

The defences that matter do not restore the catechism. Each of them abandons the claim that three laws govern all reality, and rebuilds something more modest from the materials: a method of thinking about relations, a philosophy of science hospitable to emergence and stratification, or a working style inside one science. Their common structure is itself the strongest available commentary on the objection just stated: the tradition's most serious minds, when free to argue, defended dialectics by shrinking its claims until the claims could do work.

Bertell Ollman's defence, developed from the 1970s onward, rests on the philosophy of internal relations. On this view, the core of Marx's dialectics is not a set of cosmic laws but an ontology of social reality in which relations are internal to their terms: capital and wage labour are not two things that subsequently interact but moments of a single relation, each unintelligible without the other, and the same holds, in varying degrees, for the categories of political economy generally. From that ontology Ollman derives a method: abstraction must be practised self-consciously, choosing levels of generality and boundaries of the system under study with explicit care, because a dialectical analysis lives or dies by how it carves its object. The defence is formidable within its chosen territory, the interpretation of Marx's social theory, and Ollman's readings of alienation and of Capital's procedure command respect well beyond his school. Its limits are equally clear, and Ollman is candid about them in practice if not always in slogan: internal relations is a philosophy for the social world of mutually constituting relations, and it offers no particular guidance about electrons. The dialectics of nature is quietly set aside, and with it goes the doctrine's claim to universality. What survives is a sophisticated account of Marx's method, which is to say, a defence of the third of this guide's three objects, mounted by abandoning the first.

Roy Bhaskar's intervention came from the philosophy of science. His earlier critical realism had argued that the world is stratified and differentiated, that mechanisms operate in open systems where their effects are not constant conjunctions, and that emergence is real: higher levels of organisation have powers irreducible to those of their components. In the early 1990s, in the work published as Dialectic: The Pulse of Freedom in 1993, Bhaskar turned that realism dialectical, arguing that absence and negation are real features of the world, that change is the absenting of constraints, and that a rationally reconstructed dialectics could serve as the logic of emancipation in thought and practice. The construction is elaborate, idiosyncratic and contested even among critical realists, many of whom took the realism and left the dialectic. For this guide's purposes, its significance is taxonomic. Bhaskar's dialectics is not Engels's three laws under new management; it is a new philosophy built in conversation with the tradition, and its existence shows what a living development of dialectical themes looks like when no institution stands guard over the catechism: it looks like an individual thinker's system, argued in the open, vulnerable to criticism, and free to depart from every formula the catechism held sacred. That freedom is precisely what the administered doctrine could not permit, and the contrast measures the cost of the protection the doctrine enjoyed.

The strongest test case, as the brief for this article insists and as this guide concurs, is the dialectical biology of Richard Levins and Richard Lewontin. Here were no party philosophers but two eminent working scientists, at Harvard, who from the mid-1980s, above all in The Dialectical Biologist of 1985, argued explicitly that a dialectical standpoint improved biological science: organism and environment as mutually constituting rather than externally related, wholes as constraining their parts while being built from them, heterogeneous causes operating at several levels at once, rejection of the reductionist programme that assigns every phenomenon to a single favoured level such as the gene. Their standpoint issued in practice, in Levins's modelling of complex agroecosystems and in Lewontin's critiques of genetic determinism, including his demonstrations that the standard partitioning of human genetic variation undermines the biological conception of race. Sceptics may reply, and some did, that good holism needs no dialectical baptism, that everything Levins and Lewontin actually did can be redescribed as sophisticated systems ecology and population genetics. The reply is partly just, and the dialectical biologists' own framing concedes its force in advance: they presented the categories as heuristics, as a discipline of attention that guards against characteristic reductionist errors, not as laws from which results could be deduced. That concession is the verdict this guide reaches about the whole first object: where the dialectical inheritance has demonstrably done work in a real science, it has done so as heuristic, in the humble register Engels's own formulations often occupied and the catechisms expressly disdained. Heuristics are not nothing; the history of science is partly a history of fruitful heuristics. But a heuristic that must be vindicated case by case, by results it does not guarantee, is not a law of nature, and a doctrine honest about that distinction would have had a smaller and better history.

### Can the dialectical categories do real work inside a science?

In the strongest available case, yes, as heuristics rather than laws. Levins and Lewontin used organism and environment as mutually constituting, parts and wholes in two-way determination, and multiple levels of causation as working disciplines in biology, and they presented the categories in exactly that modest register.

## The second object: historical materialism is a different and riskier theory

Everything changes when the reader turns from the general ontology to the theory of history, because historical materialism, whatever its faults, behaves like a theory: it says definite things about definite objects, and the world can answer back. Its core claims are familiar from the series guide to [historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/): that the way a society organises production, its relations of property and labour, constrains and largely explains its law, politics and dominant ideas; that history exhibits a sequence of modes of production whose internal tensions drive transitions; that class struggle is the mechanism nearest the surface of those transitions. Each claim can be, and has been, battered by counterexamples, refined by distinctions, defended by reconstructions such as Cohen's functional interpretation, and attacked by methodological individualists demanding the micro-mechanisms. That battering is the point. A theory that can lose arguments is alive in a way a scheme of universal redescription is not, and the scholarly literature on historical materialism, vast, technical and inconclusive in the ordinary way of serious literatures, has the texture of inquiry rather than catechism.

The nesting claim of the old doctrine, that historical materialism is dialectical materialism applied to society, must therefore be reversed to be understood. Historically, the theory of history came first: its elements are in Marx's work of the 1840s and in the 1859 preface, decades before the general ontology was assembled. Logically, the theory of history neither needs nor receives support from the three laws: nothing in the claim that property relations constrain legal forms follows from the boiling of water or the life cycle of barley, and nothing in the fate of the laws touches the evidence for or against the constraint claim, which lies in comparative history and social science. The nesting was a piece of doctrinal architecture, not an inference, and its function was authority transfer in both directions, as this guide noted at the outset. Dismantling it costs historical materialism nothing it ever legitimately owned. Indeed, the theory's most rigorous modern defenders have typically performed the dismantling themselves, keeping the constraint claims and the mechanisms, discarding the cosmic scaffolding, and accepting the ordinary risks of social theory. A reader who has grasped the separation can watch that operation being performed across the literature and recognise it for what it is: the tradition's serious members applying the three-object rule in practice, usually without naming it.

There is a subtler entanglement to clear away as well, concerning the word materialism itself. Historical materialism is not the philosophical thesis that only matter exists, applied to society; it is a research programme about which features of social life explain which others. A thinker could accept the programme while holding almost any metaphysics, and several have, while a metaphysical materialist could reject the programme's explanatory priorities entirely, as many materialist philosophers of a mechanistic bent did in the nineteenth century, to Engels's irritation. The old doctrine exploited the shared word to imply that the programme and the metaphysics stood or fell together. They do not, and the proof is sociological as much as logical: the postwar decades are full of historians and sociologists who worked fruitfully with Marx's historical explanations while believing nothing about laws of nature, alongside physicists who believed in matter and motion and nothing about modes of production. Keeping the two materialisms distinct is not pedantry; it is the difference between knowing what a person is claiming and being recruited by an equivocation.

## The third object: the dialectical method inside Capital

The third object is the one Marx actually left behind, and it is neither a set of laws nor a theory of history but a way of building an argument. In Capital, Marx begins with the commodity, the simplest social form of the capitalist order, and unfolds from the tensions inside that form the categories of money, capital, surplus value, wages, accumulation and crisis, in an order dictated not by chronology but by the internal dependencies of the concepts. The presentation mimics its object: because capitalist society is a system of mutually presupposing forms, the exposition must present each form in the place where the presuppositions are ready, and must let each category be criticised, in the older sense of being driven to its limits, by the movement of the exposition itself. Marx described the procedure in correspondence by distinguishing the method of inquiry, which appropriates the material in detail, from the method of presentation, which must reconstruct the life of the subject matter in thought; the afterword of 1873 added that his dialectical method was not only different from Hegel's but its direct opposite, since for Marx the ideal is the material world reflected and translated in the human mind. The youthful alienation manuscripts, the Grundrisse with its methodological introduction, and the finished volumes of Capital all belong to the long elaboration of this one object, and its study is the study of Marx's actual intellectual practice.

Two features of this method deserve emphasis because they separate it decisively from the ontology that borrowed its name. The first is its locality. The method makes no claim about nature in general, about thought in general, or about history as a whole; it is a procedure for one kind of object, a structured social totality, and its warrant is the success or failure of the specific reconstruction it produces. When the procedure is applied elsewhere, to feudal society or to a modern bureaucracy, the application is a new piece of work requiring new justification, not an instance of a universal law firing. The second feature is its exposure. Capital's argument can be wrong, in identifiable places, for identifiable reasons: its value theory can be challenged on its treatment of prices, its crisis theory on the evidence of cycles, its account of the falling rate of profit on the logic and the data. Generations of critics have done exactly that, and generations of defenders have answered in kind. This is the normal metabolism of a serious work. Nothing remotely similar ever happened to the three laws, which were never in their administered career exposed to a test they could fail, and the contrast in metabolism is the clearest practical sign that the method and the ontology are different kinds of thing. The three-object rule simply generalises the lesson into a rule of reading: before joining any argument about dialectics, establish which object is under discussion, who authored it, what would count for or against it, and refuse every inference that crosses from one object to another without a bridge built for the crossing.

### How does the method of Capital differ from the three laws?

The method is a procedure for presenting one structured object, capitalism, by unfolding its categories from the commodity onward in an order set by their internal dependencies. It claims nothing about nature as a whole, and its warrant is the success of the specific reconstruction, which critics can contest at identifiable points.

## The three things called dialectics, and the three-object rule

The separations argued through this guide can now be set out in one place. The table below, the three things called dialectics, fixes the distinctions in a form a reader can return to whenever the vocabulary starts to blur: three objects, three different provenances, three different relations to evidence, three different sets of critics.

| Object | Who formulated it | What it claims | What evidence bears on it | Which criticism applies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dialectical materialism as general ontology | Engels in raw material; Plekhanov and the codifiers as system | Three laws govern all processes in nature, society and thought | Whether the laws add anything to the specific sciences that supply their examples | Trivial where true and false where substantive; no prediction ever derived; equivocation on contradiction |
| Historical materialism as a theory of history | Marx in the 1840s and the preface of 1859, systematised by successors | Property and production relations constrain law, politics and ideas; modes of production succeed one another through their tensions | Comparative history, social structure, the mechanisms linking economy to institutions | Disputed scope, functional explanation without mechanisms, counterexamples from politics and culture |
| Marx's dialectical method in Capital | Marx himself, above all in Capital and the Grundrisse | A structured social totality is best presented by unfolding its simplest form through its internal tensions | The success of the reconstruction: value, money, capital and crisis as argued at each step | Challenges to the value theory, to the crisis account, and to the claim that presentation order tracks the object |

From the table, the rule follows directly, and it is the namable claim this guide exists to establish: dialectical materialism, historical materialism, and Marx's method are three distinct objects, so a refutation of the Soviet ontology leaves the method untouched, and a defence of the method establishes nothing about the ontology. The rule cuts symmetrically, which is what makes it a rule rather than a manoeuvre. It forbids the critic's favourite shortcut, in which the absurdities of an administered catechism are allowed to stand as a verdict on Capital. It also forbids the defender's favourite shortcut, in which the evident power of Marx's exposition of the commodity form is allowed to launder a universal ontology that Marx never stated. And it forbids the historian's laziest habit, the composite figure called Marxist philosophy, in which texts by Marx, Engels, Plekhanov, Lenin, Stalin's ghostwriters and Lukacs are quoted interchangeably as expressions of one doctrine. Under the rule, every quotation carries its author's name and date, every claim carries its object, and every criticism is addressed to the object it actually hits. Applied consistently, the rule dissolves a surprising fraction of the literature on both sides, the fraction that lives on cross-object inference. What remains is smaller and better: arguments about a set of laws that probably cannot bear the weight placed on them, a theory of history that remains a live research programme with real defeats and real successes, and a method of presentation whose power and limits can be studied in the text that embodies it.

### How should the three objects be kept separate in practice?

By attaching every claim to its object and author before arguing about it: the laws to Engels and his codifiers, the theory of history to Marx's historical work and its modern reconstructions, the method to Capital itself. No verdict earned against one object may be spent on another without a separate argument.

## Knowing the world: reflection, practice and the doctrine's theory of knowledge

The doctrine's epistemology is often skipped in summaries, yet it did much of the system's daily work, because it told adherents what kind of thing truth is and who may be trusted to have it. Its classical form is the reflection theory: consciousness reflects a material world that exists independently of it, as a mirror reflects or, in the more sophisticated versions, as a map represents, selectively and with instruments. Lenin's philosophical notebooks and his polemics against Machism gave the theory its orthodox shape, insisting against empiricist fashions that sensations are copies, photographs or symbols of things that exist outside us, and that the criterion of truth is practice: an idea is tested by being acted on, in experiment, industry and politics, and the world's resistance or compliance grades the attempt. The practice criterion was the theory's living element. It connected epistemology to the tradition's activism, echoing Marx's second thesis on Feuerbach, where the truth of thinking is declared a practical question, and it supplied generations of scientists and engineers inside the system with a pragmatist-sounding warrant for trusting results that worked.

The difficulties are instructive. A copy theory of perception sits awkwardly with everything the sciences of perception have established about the constructive character of seeing, and the doctrine's defenders spent considerable ingenuity softening copy into model, image into sign, without ever quite surrendering the reassurance the mirror metaphor provides: that the world is simply there and thought need only polish its surface. The practice criterion, meanwhile, is double-edged in a way the catechisms never faced. Practice tests engineering admirably, since bridges fall down or stand; it tests vast social doctrines very poorly, since the failure of a policy can always be attributed to conditions, enemies or impatience, and the success of a rival can always be redescribed. Worse, in the administered system the tribunal of practice acquired an institutional address: the party claimed to embody the practice of the working class and therefore to hold, in disputed cases, the criterion of truth in its keeping. An epistemology that begins with the world's independence of mind thus ended, by institutional steps each defensible in the vocabulary of the system, with an organisation authorised to overrule minds about the world. The trajectory from reflection to jurisdiction parallels the trajectory from Engels's abstractions to Lysenko's tribunal traced earlier, and the parallel is not a coincidence: in both cases a doctrine of humble content, knowledge reflects reality and is tested in practice, was converted by its administrative setting into an instrument of command. Readers who want the general lesson can state it without reference to Marxism at all. Any theory of knowledge, however fallibilist its wording, becomes dangerous when an institution is licensed as its final court, and becomes safe exactly to the extent that the courts of appeal are plural, public and empowered to embarrass the powerful. The doctrine's epistemology, read against its career, is the cleanest available case study of that conversion.

## Against the older materialism: what the adjective dialectical was meant to add

The word dialectical in the doctrine's name is a claim about a predecessor's failure, and the claim deserves reconstruction, since it explains why the founders' generation thought a new materialism was needed at all. The materialism available in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the materialism of the French philosophes, of Feuerbach and of the popular scientific lecturers, had scored real victories: it had driven spirit out of nature, grounded mind in body and taught educated Europe to explain before it worshipped. Its limits, as Marx and Engels diagnosed them, were threefold. It was mechanical, picturing nature as the sum of externally related parts and smuggling that picture into history, where it produced the view that people are products of circumstances while forgetting that circumstances are changed by people. It was contemplative, treating knowledge as the beholding of an object rather than an activity within the world beheld. And it was unhistorical in both directions, lacking any account of how nature itself develops and any account of how societies transform. Feuerbach, the immediate target of Marx's theses of 1845, stood convicted in that document of grasping the material world as an object of contemplation but not as human practice, and of retreating, when he reached history, into an anthropology of feeling that explained nothing historical at all.

The dialectical adjective promised to repair all three defects at once: process instead of mechanism, because the laws describe a world of internal relations in ceaseless transformation; practice instead of contemplation, because knowledge is an activity tested in the world it knows; development instead of fixity, because quantity into quality and the negation of the negation install history inside nature itself. A reader can now see the architecture of the promise clearly enough to judge its fulfilment piece by piece. The repair of mechanism by process thinking was genuine as an aspiration and is the element that later holisms in biology and ecology would independently pursue. The elevation of practice was Marx's own and belongs, properly, to his method and his politics rather than to any ontology. The installation of history inside nature was the speculative leap, the point where the promise outran every argument offered for it, since the mere fact that both nature and society develop does not show that they develop under common laws, any more than the fact that rivers and rumours both spread shows a common science of spreading. The doctrine's name thus encodes its tragedy in miniature: a legitimate dissatisfaction, a fertile repair, and an overclaim, fused into two words and inherited as one indivisible creed. To separate the words' work, assigning dissatisfaction, repair and overclaim to their respective owners and tests, is once more the three-object rule, now operating inside the doctrine's very title.

## Sorting the neighbours: the formula it is not, and the philosopher behind it

Two confusions cling to the doctrine's name so persistently that a complete guide must discharge them explicitly, while leaving their full treatment to the articles that own them. The first is the triad of thesis, antithesis and synthesis, endlessly attributed to Hegel and then to Marx as the supposed machinery of dialectics. Neither man's work runs on that machinery: the triad belongs to the popularisation of German philosophy, fits Fichte better than Hegel, and appears in Marx mainly when he is mocking Proudhon's imitation of it. The doctrine's actual categories, contradiction, quantity and quality, negation, are not a three-step dance applied to topics from outside, and any exposition that begins with the triad has begun with a counterfeit; the full demolition, with its history, is the business of the series guide to the [thesis antithesis synthesis myth](/2012/07/15/thesis-antithesis-synthesis-myth/). The second confusion concerns Hegel himself. Marx's debt to Hegel is real and specific, centred on the Logic's treatment of categories that develop through their own inadequacies and on the Phenomenology's vision of forms of life undermined by their internal tensions; his repudiation is equally specific, centred on Hegel's protagonist, spirit realising itself, where Marx puts material production and social relations. The celebrated inversion metaphor, turning the dialectic right side up, is Marx's own and conceals as much as it reveals, since a method is not a liquid that can be poured from an idealist into a materialist vessel unchanged. How the two dialectics actually differ, text by text, is the subject of the series comparison of [Hegel's dialectic and Marx's](/2012/08/15/hegel-dialectic-vs-marx-dialectic/), and the reader is urged to keep the questions separate: what Marx took from Hegel is a question about his method, the third object, and it neither raises nor lowers the standing of the ontology, the first object, which Hegel would have found as alien in its materialist dress as any other system that claimed his Logic as a natural science.

## After the patrons fell: the doctrine's afterlife

The events of 1989 to 1991 subjected the doctrine to the test its own epistemology prescribed: practice, at civilisational scale. When the European states that had maintained diamat as an official philosophy collapsed or transformed, the doctrine's institutional ecology vanished within a few years. Departments were renamed or dissolved, textbooks withdrawn, the examination subject abolished, and a scholarly discipline that had employed thousands evaporated with a speed that confirmed, more eloquently than any polemic, how completely its existence had depended on its patrons. In Russia, philosophy after the transition pluralised chaotically; the old professors retrained, emigrated into new fields or retired, and the three laws survived mainly as cultural memory and as material for historians. In China, by contrast, the doctrine's institutional career continued under new management: Marxist philosophy remained a required component of higher education and a living academic field, increasingly professionalised, increasingly in dialogue with analytic and continental work, and decreasingly describable as the frozen catechism of the Short Course era, though the curricular frame that houses it descends directly from the architecture described earlier in this guide. The divergent fates are themselves data. Where the doctrine was only administered, it died with the administration; where it was absorbed into a continuing state's self-understanding and staffed by a profession with genuine research incentives, it mutated, professionalised and persisted in altered form.

For the wider intellectual world, the afterlife has been quieter and more interesting than either triumph or oblivion. The phrase survives in three habitats. It survives as a historical object, studied by historians of philosophy and of the Soviet world as one of the most consequential intellectual constructions of the twentieth century, whose career illuminates how ideas behave when fused with state power. It survives as a term of art in debates among Marxists, where taking a position on dialectics remains one of the ways the tradition's tribes recognise one another, and where the defences examined in this guide, Ollman's, Bhaskar's and the biologists', continue to be argued with. And it survives as a fossil in the wider culture, in the debating habits of people who invoke dialectics to mean the consideration of opposing views, or historical inevitability, or a style of paradoxical aphorism, meanings the doctrine's own texts would not have recognised. A complete guide owes its readers a clear statement of this distribution, because the question whether the doctrine is dead has no single answer: dead as a state creed in Europe, persistent as a curriculum in China, alive as a contested inheritance in the philosophy that bears its name, and immortal as a cautionary tale about what institutions do to ideas. The cautionary tale may prove the most durable of its forms, and there are worse fates for a doctrine than to become the standing example by which students learn to ask who codified a system, whose interests the codification served, and which of its laws were quietly edited out.

## Reading the primary texts without being recruited by them

A reader who wants to go to the sources faces a peculiar library, in which the founding texts are not by the founder, the most influential text is a catechism its subject would have complicated, and the fragments of one major work were published in an order chosen by editors in another country. A sane reading order exists, and stating it is a service a guide can perform. Begin with Marx's own methodological statements, which are short and decisive: the theses on Feuerbach, the introduction of 1857 with its account of rising from the abstract to the concrete, and the afterword of 1873. These fix the third object in the reader's mind before the other two can blur it. Then read Anti-Duhring, or at least its philosophical chapters, as what it is, a brilliant polemic by a collaborator, noting where Engels generalises toward nature and marking every place where the generalisation outruns the example. Then read the codification of 1938 entire, since it is brief, and read it against Engels with a pencil, listing what was selected, what was sharpened and what, above all the third law, was dropped. The exercise takes an afternoon and teaches more about the doctrine than a semester of commentary, because the doctrine is, in large part, the set of differences between those three layers. Only after that archaeology should a reader open the critics and defenders, Lukacs's footnote and its afterlife, Popper's charges, the analytical reconstructions, Ollman, Bhaskar, Levins and Lewontin, in whatever order curiosity dictates; each of them is arguing about a specific layer, and the reader who knows the layers can hear the arguments at their proper addresses.

Keeping that archaeology straight across months of reading is a practical problem as much as an intellectual one, since the literature is enormous and its authors use identical words for the three objects without warning. Readers who organise their study notes by object rather than by author, filing every claim under ontology, theory of history or method, with its evidence standard attached, find the confusions dissolving before they form; the structured notes at [the VaultBook study page for this series](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built on exactly that filing principle, and the discipline transfers to any notebook: three folders, three questions, no cross-object inference without a bridge argued in writing. The habit sounds clerical and is in fact the whole method of this guide, made portable. Whoever keeps the folders separate can read anything in this contentious field, from a Short Course reprint to a critical realist treatise, and come away knowing precisely what was claimed, by whom, and what would settle it. Whoever merges the folders will be recruited, sooner or later, by whichever author writes with the most confidence, because in a merged folder confidence is the only remaining criterion.

A final word of method concerns the spirit in which the whole subject should be approached, and it applies to admirers and detractors equally. The doctrine this guide has dissected was, for two generations, the official philosophy of states that commanded armies, police and universities; it was also, for many of its adherents, a sincere attempt to think the unity of nature and history in a disenchanted age, and an instrument with which some of them resisted worse orthodoxies or simply organised a confusing world. Both facts are true, and a treatment that keeps only one of them in view will fail as history exactly as it fails as philosophy. The three-object rule is this guide's contribution to keeping both in view: it is a device for precision, not a verdict machine, and the verdicts it permits are the earned kind, specific to objects, authors and arguments, delivered without proselytising in either direction. The reader who closes the guide able to say which object a given claim belongs to, what its evidence would look like, and which criticism actually reaches it, possesses everything the subject has to teach a careful mind, including the lesson its own history teaches best: that the most dangerous moment for an idea is the moment it stops having to argue.

## Marx or Engels: the attribution dispute and why it cannot be waved away

Every account of the doctrine eventually faces the question it would most like to postpone: did Engels, in building toward a dialectics of nature, speak for Marx or depart from him? The question cannot be waved away by pointing to their friendship, because the doctrine's authority in the movement rested entirely on the claim of joint authorship, and the scholarship of the later twentieth century made the question precise by publishing the texts in full and reading them against each other line by line. The evidence divides into three piles, and honesty requires exhibiting all of them. In the first pile stands everything that speaks for deep agreement: the two men collaborated for four decades, read each other's manuscripts, divided intellectual labour by mutual consent and repeatedly described their outlook as shared; Marx contributed a chapter to Anti-Duhring and heard the whole in manuscript; Engels's natural studies were no secret from him. In the second pile stands the evidence of divergence in practice: Marx's own dialectical writing is invariably about social forms, his references to nature are about the labour process and the metabolism of society with its environment, and he never once, in the vast Nachlass that scholars have now mined for a century, sketches a law of nature or a threefold pattern of development in the physical world. His one extended engagement with natural science, his mathematical manuscripts and his reading in chemistry and geology, shows an inquirer following the sciences, not a legislator dictating their grammar. In the third pile stands the testimony of the tradition's own rebels: Lukacs argued in 1923 that the extension to nature was Engels's error and not Marx's method; later scholars, including some with no Lukacsian commitments, documented systematic differences in how the two men handled Hegel, abstraction and the concept of nature itself; still others defended the unity thesis as the only reading compatible with the correspondence.

The mature scholarly position, insofar as a contested field has one, distinguishes levels. At the level of broad outlook, the two men were materialists together, hostile to speculative idealism, convinced that Hegel's dialectic, inverted, was an instrument of the first rank. At the level of the specific constructions that became the doctrine, the constructions are Engels's, and Marx's assent to them is undocumented where it is not merely presumed from silence, a silence that may signify consent, indifference, deference to a partner's province, or the priorities of a dying man racing to finish his own work. The distinction matters because of the use to which presumed assent was put. A movement is entitled to develop its founders' ideas; it is not entitled to backdate the development into co-authorship and then wield the doubled authority against internal critics, which is what the administered tradition did whenever a Lukacs questioned the nature dialectic. The three-object rule handles the whole dispute with its usual economy: it assigns the ontology to Engels and his successors on the evidence, notes Marx's documented positions separately, and treats the unity thesis as a historical claim requiring historical proof rather than a premise available free of charge. Readers who adopt that discipline will find the enormous Engels controversy literature suddenly legible, since most of its combatants are found, on inspection, to be fighting over whether an unproved premise may be assumed.

## Lenin's consolidation: the notebooks, the polemic and the inheritance

Between Plekhanov and the codifiers stands Lenin, whose role in the doctrine's formation is double and often conflated. His book of 1909, Materialism and Empirio-criticism, written against Russian followers of Mach who sought a subtler, experience-based Marxism, fixed the reflection theory of knowledge as orthodoxy and established a style: philosophical deviation treated as political deviation in disguise, opponents answered by classification before argument. The book's epistemology, examined earlier in this guide, became the doctrine's official theory of knowledge, and its polemical manner became the template for settling philosophical questions in the administered period. Yet the same Lenin, in the philosophical notebooks he filled during the war years while reading Hegel's Logic in Bern, left a record of dialectical thinking far richer and stranger than the catechism that would claim his authority: aphorisms on the identity of dialectics, logic and the theory of knowledge, on the splitting of a whole and the cognition of its contradictory parts as the essence of dialectics, on the demand that categories be studied in their movement and interconnection. Those notebooks, published after his death, embarrassed the codifiers even as they canonised him, because the thinking they preserve is exploratory, Hegelian in depth, and directed at the logic of Capital rather than at a catalogue of nature's laws. Scholars of the period have traced how the notebooks were selectively used: quoted for authority, shelved for content.

The Lenin inheritance thus reproduced the founding pattern at one remove. A complex body of work, part polemic and part private exploration, was mined by successors for what could be administered, and the remainder was preserved in editions that specialists could consult and the curriculum could ignore. When the codification of 1938 arrived, it claimed descent from Marx, Engels, Plekhanov and Lenin in a single apostolic line, and the claim was the doctrine's charter: four names, one philosophy, no visible seams. This guide has been at pains to make the seams visible, not from antiquarian fussiness but because the seams are where the decisions were taken, and a reader who can see the decisions can evaluate them. The alternative to seam-showing history is the composite doctrine with a single birthday and a single author, which is precisely the fiction the naming story at this guide's opening dispelled. It is worth adding that Lenin himself, the politician, treated philosophy as a field where correctness mattered for the movement's fate, a conviction that helps explain both his seriousness as a reader of Hegel and the ferocity with which his successors policed the field in his name. Seriousness and policing are the doctrine's twin inheritances from him, and its history is unintelligible without both.

## The standard examples under pressure: chemistry, steam and seeds revisited

Textbook doctrines live in their examples, and the doctrine's canonical examples repay a slow second look, because in each case the distance between what the example shows and what the law claims is measurable. Take the chemical series that Engels loved: the hydrocarbons, where adding a unit of composition steps the substance from gas to liquid to solid across a homologous series, each step a new quality from accumulated quantity. The series is real, and it genuinely impressed a century accustomed to thinking of qualitative difference as primitive and unanalysable. But the example shows something about how properties depend on structure, which chemistry explains through bonding, symmetry and molecular weight, and the explanation, once achieved, owes nothing to a general law and was in fact achieved by methods, spectroscopy and synthesis among them, that the law neither suggested nor guided. The example is a fine illustration of emergence for a lecture; as evidence for a law of all processes it is an anecdote in search of a denominator, since the same chemistry supplies endless accumulations without qualitative leaps and endless leaps, isomerisation for one, where quantity stands still and structure alone rearranges. A law illustrated only by favourable instances, with the unfavourable filed under other laws, has the shape this guide's objection section described: compatible with everything, therefore empty as a claim, however vivid as a picture.

The seed fares no better under pressure, and it is worth pressing because the negation of the negation lived in it. Germination destroys the seed as a seed, growth produces a plant, reproduction returns seeds multiplied. For the sequence to instantiate a universal law, the middle term must negate the first and the third must negate the second while conserving it, and each verb must mean the same across botany, geology and the history of philosophy, the other theatres where the law was exhibited. In botany the verbs are metaphors laid over mechanisms, pollination, meiosis, nutrient transport, which plant science states without loss in its own vocabulary. In the history of philosophy, the law's theatre of choice, the fits are retrospective narrations: materialism negated by idealism, idealism negated by a higher materialism, a story whose terms can be permuted to yield the opposite moral by selecting other protagonists. The contrast with a genuine law's career is stark. When the conservation of energy was stated, it forbade perpetual motion machines in advance, including machines not yet imagined, and engineers who ignored it wasted fortunes on schedule. The negation of the negation has forbidden nothing, predicted nothing and wasted nothing, because nothing could count as its violation; a plant that yielded exactly one seed, or none, would be absorbed as readily as the sheaf. This is the trivial-or-false objection wearing the examples' own clothes, and the guide lets it stand as the strongest single exhibit in the case against the first object, while repeating the now familiar boundary: the exhibit condemns a law of development, not a method for analysing the commodity form, and not a theory of history that risks itself on evidence in the ordinary way.

One more example deserves rescue rather than prosecution, because it shows the tradition at its best and explains its hold on intelligent readers. Marx's threshold in the chapter on the rate and mass of surplus value, where the possessor of money becomes a capitalist only when the scale of employment crosses a line, is a quantity-quality observation with its mechanism attached: below the line, the owner must labour and can extract only limited surplus; above it, he can live from the labour of others and accumulation becomes self-expanding. The observation persuades because the mechanism is visible, the threshold is derived rather than proclaimed, and the claim is local, about a specific institution, open to historical testing. Generalised into a law of everything, the same observation evaporates; kept in its place, it is a model of what dialectical thinking in Marx's hands actually was: attention to the points where gradual change in a structured whole reorganises the whole. The catechism's deepest error can now be named precisely. It took a style of attention that Marx practised locally, with mechanisms, and promulgated it as a cosmology, without mechanisms, and the promulgation destroyed exactly the quality, answerability to the specific object, that had made the style worth admiring.

## How the doctrine was taught: inside the textbook machine

To understand the doctrine's hold, it is not enough to read its propositions; one must picture its classroom. Across the socialist world, and in party schools far beyond it, dialectical materialism was taught as the first subject, the foundation course through which every student of Marxism passed before history, economics or law. The textbook architecture was standardised to a degree unusual even for state curricula: an opening on the subject matter of philosophy and the basic question of the relation of thinking to being, then matter and its forms of existence, then the laws and categories in their fixed sequence, then consciousness, then knowledge and practice, then the hand-off to historical materialism as the second volume of the same edifice. Examinations rewarded accurate reproduction of definitions, the canonical examples in their canonical order, and the correct classification of deviations, since every error in the system had its labelled heresy, mechanical materialism on one flank, idealist dialectics on the other, eclecticism in the middle. A student who mastered the course possessed a complete map of permitted thought, with the borders drawn.

Two features of that classroom deserve analysis rather than mockery. The first is its genuine pedagogical craft. The doctrine was teachable, in the way Euclidean geometry is teachable: a closed system of definitions and theorems, each lesson presupposing the last, every question possessed of a right answer that the diligent could reach. For educational systems charged with forming millions of cadres quickly, in societies emerging from mass illiteracy, that teachability was a real administrative virtue, and the men who built the curricula were not cynics about it; they believed a scientific worldview could be mass-produced like literacy, and in the purely formal sense, it could. The second feature is the shadow of the first. A closed system examined by reproduction selects against the very capacities its subject matter celebrates: a student who applied the doctrine's own teaching about contradiction, development and the supersession of stages to the doctrine itself, in the examination hall, would fail the examination. The system's defenders were not unaware of the tension; the approved resolution described dialectics as complete in its principles and open only in its applications, a formula whose convenience is visible at a glance. Every orthodoxy in history has needed some version of that formula, and the need is diagnostic: it marks the point where a doctrine's custodians have decided, in practice, that the doctrine's survival outranks its content, since the content, taken at face value, licenses its own revision. Those decisions, repeated across decades and continents, are the deep cause beneath the episodes this guide has catalogued, the edited law, the condemned schools, the tribunal over genetics. The classroom is where the editing was normalised, one cohort at a time, until the edited version was simply what the words meant for everyone the system had taught.

## The doctrine in plain English: a translation and a warning

Strip the vocabulary, and the doctrine's component claims can each be stated in a sentence of ordinary English, an exercise its defenders sometimes resent and its critics sometimes skip, both to their loss. The first claim is that everything that exists is part of one natural order, with no supernatural annex. The second is that this order is in constant change, and that things are better understood as processes with histories than as fixed essences. The third is that change often comes from tensions inside a thing or system rather than from outside pushes alone. The fourth is that gradual accumulation sometimes tips a system into a different state. The fifth is that later states grow out of earlier ones in ways that preserve traces of what they replace. Stated so, the claims range from the foundational, the first being the working assumption of all modern science, through the wise, the second and third being counsels that most good historians and many good scientists would endorse as reminders, to the contestable, the fourth and fifth being patterns that hold sometimes, in some systems, for reasons the specific sciences supply. Nothing in the plain version is absurd, and nothing in it is a discovery procedure. The translation exercise thus performs the guide's whole argument in miniature: what is true in the doctrine is largely the inheritance of science and of thoughtful historiography, what is distinctive in it is the promotion of reminders into laws, and what was catastrophic in it belonged not to the sentences at all but to the institutions that enforced them.

The warning follows from the translation. Because the plain claims are mostly reasonable, the vocabulary survives its refutations: a speaker can invoke dialectics, be pressed, retreat to the plain claims, and emerge having defended something nobody denied, a manoeuvre familiar in the literature under many names. That rule is the antidote in conversation as in reading. Ask which object is meant. If the answer is the method of Capital, the discussion can proceed to the text. If the answer is the theory of history, it can proceed to evidence. If the answer is the ontology, ask for the mechanism, the prediction, the forbidden outcome, and watch whether the reply supplies them or changes the subject to steam and seeds, the two exhibits that have carried the doctrine's plausibility for a century and a half. None of this requires hostility to the tradition. It requires only the refusal to let a word do the work of an argument, which is a refusal Marx, who spent a lifetime forcing words to confess their content, would have recognised as his own.

## The supporting cast: the categories and what they were for

Beneath the three laws, the classical doctrine deployed a battery of paired categories, essence and appearance, content and form, cause and effect, necessity and chance, possibility and actuality, the abstract and the concrete, and these pairs did more of the system's ordinary work than the laws themselves. Each pair was a small instruction in how to think. Essence and appearance instructed the student that the surface of a phenomenon is not its explanation, that wages appear as the price of labour while concealing the relation that produces them. Cause and effect instructed that the pair interpenetrate, an effect becoming in its turn a cause in chains without absolute beginnings. Necessity and chance instructed that laws operate through accidents, that what is necessary at the level of the process is chancy at the level of the event, a reconciliation Engels prized in his reading of Darwin, where the chance variation of individuals carries the necessity of selection. Possibility and actuality instructed that the real is a subset of the possible selected by conditions, and that politics consists largely in ripening possibilities into actualities. The abstract and the concrete, inherited from Marx's methodological introduction, instructed that a concrete phenomenon is a synthesis of many determinations, reachable only by ascending from thin abstractions rather than by staring at the empirical blur.

Assessed as the laws were assessed, the categories divide into the same registers. As a checklist of distinctions a social researcher neglects at peril, they are excellent: few habits damage inquiry more than mistaking appearance for essence, isolating a cause from its chain, or treating a statistical surface as a population of identical units, and the Marxist tradition's celebrated empirical works are full of the categories operating as quiet disciplines. As ontology, they inflate: to say that every phenomenon whatsoever has an essence distinct from its appearance is to make a claim about being that no list of good examples can underwrite, and the categories' pairing has the catechism's familiar elasticity, since any distinction a researcher draws can be baptised with one of the pairs after the fact. The critics of the system, interestingly, have usually been gentler with the categories than with the laws, because the categories wear their heuristic character openly; they are tools in search of tasks, and a tool is judged by the work. It is one of the doctrine's ironies that its humblest components have aged best, precisely because they never claimed the status that ruined the grander ones. Teachers of method in several traditions still drill students in essence and appearance under other names, in the distinction between a variable's label and its mechanism, in the difference between a model and the modelled, and the drill works whether or not the student has heard of dialectics. The three-object rule suggests the correct filing: the categories belong with the method, the third object, as instruments of inquiry refined by practice, and only the codifiers' insistence on a complete ontology dragged them into the first object, where they were made to pose as furniture of the universe.

## The doctrine's most-read text: Mao on contradiction

No account of the doctrine's career is complete without the text through which more human beings encountered it than through any other: the essay On Contradiction, delivered as lectures in 1937 at the height of the war against Japan and later canonised among Mao's selected works. The essay reorganised the inherited system around a single category. Contradiction, for Mao, is universal and absolute, present in every process from its beginning to its end; each process contains a principal contradiction that determines its character, and within each contradiction a principal aspect that fixes the nature of the whole at a given stage; contradictions are both universal and particular, and the task of analysis is always to find the particularity, the specific configuration that distinguishes this process from every other. The other laws recede in the essay to the status of instances or corollaries, and the law of the unity and struggle of opposites, as Mao renamed the interpenetration, becomes the fundamental law of thought and of reality alike.

The essay's strengths are the strengths of its intended function. It is a manual for strategists: it teaches cadres analysing a province, a campaign or an alliance to identify the main contradiction, to expect it to change as conditions change, to distinguish antagonistic contradictions, which are resolved by rupture, from contradictions among the people, which are to be handled by persuasion and adjustment. Whatever its standing as metaphysics, as an instrument for training political judgement it was formidable, and it shaped the strategic culture of a revolution that succeeded. Its philosophical costs are equally visible. Elevating contradiction to the single law completes the elasticity problem this guide has tracked: a category under which every phenomenon falls, whose principal instance is whatever the analyst's politics selects, and whose taxonomy of antagonistic and non-antagonistic sorts disputes in advance into those to be settled by force and those by discussion, is a category that can license as readily as it can illuminate. The later history of the doctrine in China, including the campaigns in which the classification of a contradiction determined the fate of the classified, exhibits the licensing at scale. Scholars of Chinese philosophy have traced subtler strata in the essay, including Mao's engagement with the Soviet textbooks of the 1930s from which he partly learned the system and against which he partly reacted, insisting on particularity against their mechanical universalism. The fairest verdict is the three-object verdict in a new costume: a strategic method of real power, an ontology of the usual vulnerability, and a historical document whose influence on events exceeds that of almost any philosophical text of its century. Conflating those three judgements, in either direction, is the error the rule exists to prevent.

## A stress test: the doctrine meets Darwin

Evolution was the scientific theatre where the doctrine's claims and the sciences' autonomy collided most instructively, because both sides wanted Darwin. Engels greeted The Origin of Species as a triumph for the developmental view of nature, a blow against fixed species and static essences, and folded it into the dialectical panorama as the proof that nature has a history. The tradition that followed him claimed Darwin as a forerunner and, in the same breath, reserved the right to correct him: Darwinian selection, operating on chance variation, could be faulted for onesidedness, for neglecting the active role of organisms and the internal dynamics of development, complaints that sound, in the mouths of later dialectical biologists, like the research programme of evolutionary developmental biology stated early and in another vocabulary. Yet the same doctrinal world that celebrated Darwin licensed Lysenko's destruction of genetics, on the grounds that the gene was a static, metaphysical fiction and acquired characters must be heritable if nature was to be as malleable as the doctrine's voluntarist moods required. The whiplash is the datum: a philosophy capacious enough to claim Darwin as its own and to license the ruin of Darwin's science within two generations was not functioning as a constraint on its adherents' conclusions. It was functioning as a flag under which conclusions reached on other grounds, institutional ambition, ideological comfort, genuine insight in the biologists' case, could march.

The constructive residue, once more, is real and should be recorded without flinching. The questions the dialectical tradition kept asking biology, about levels of organisation, about the organism's activity in constructing its environment, about the poverty of explanations that move in one direction from gene to trait to fitness, were good questions, and the sciences eventually asked them in their own idiom, in niche construction theory, in developmental systems theory, in the extended synthesis debates. Intellectual historians dispute how much of that convergence owes anything to the tradition causally, as against parallel pressures inside biology itself, and the honest answer is that the causal thread is thin except where, as with Levins and Lewontin, the scientists themselves testified to it. But a tradition that kept good questions warm through decades when the dominant idiom found them embarrassing performed a service, and the service is of the heuristic kind this guide keeps identifying: the doctrine as a memory of unfashionable questions, dangerous as a court, useful as a conscience. Whether that is enough to justify its name is a judgement the reader now has the materials to make, object by object, which is all this guide has ever asked.

## The doctrine in the workers' movement: orthodoxy before the state

Between Plekhanov's systematisation and the Soviet codification lies a phase historians sometimes slight, the doctrine's life as the orthodoxy of the mass parties of the Second International, above all German social democracy. Karl Kautsky, the era's chief theoretician, absorbed the Darwinian and materialist synthesis into a Marxism of developmental confidence: history moves by laws as reliable as nature's, capitalism ripens toward its supersession, and the party's task is to organise the class that history has designated while the ripening proceeds. The philosophical temper of that orthodoxy differed from the later Soviet one in instructive ways. It was evolutionist rather than voluntarist, patient rather than insurrectionary, and its materialism leaned toward the natural-scientific, sometimes to the edge of the mechanical materialism the dialectical adjective was meant to correct; the revisionist controversy around Eduard Bernstein at the century's turn was fought partly on this ground, Bernstein proposing to discard the dialectic as a Hegelian snare while keeping an ethical socialism, the orthodox replying that the dialectic was the guarantee of the movement's scientific character. The exchange is a gift to the historian, because in it both sides say plainly what later periods would encode: that the dialectic functioned, for the movement, as the certificate of inevitability. Whoever controlled its interpretation controlled the movement's sense of where history was going and therefore of what patience or audacity the moment required.

That function explains a paradox that runs through the doctrine's whole career, its coexistence of rigidity and flexibility. As a set of propositions the doctrine was rigid: fixed laws, fixed examples, fixed heresies. As an instrument in the hands of leaderships it was remarkably flexible, since a leadership that monopolised interpretation could find, in the interpenetration of opposites, a warrant for alliance or for rupture, in the transformation of quantity into quality a warrant for patience while quantities accumulated or for the leap when the threshold was declared reached. The Russian debates of 1917, in which Lenin's reading of the conjuncture overrode the orthodox stages his own party had been taught, show the flexibility at its most world-historical: the master of the doctrine used its categories to break the schedule the doctrine was supposed to guarantee. One need not decide whether that was dialectics vindicated or dialectics exposed to see the structural point. A system of universal patterns, interpretable only by its custodians, is an instrument of authority before it is anything else, and its propositional content, however sincerely believed, is inseparable from that use. The Soviet codification did not invent this structure; it inherited it from the party-school orthodoxy of the International and armed it with a state. Readers tracing the doctrine's lineage should therefore resist the comforting story in which a subtle philosophy was betrayed by a crude regime. The instruments of betrayal, the certificate function, the custodial monopoly, the classroom catechism, were forged in the movement's own schools, in the decades of its respectability, by men who believed every word they taught.

## A fair verdict, delivered object by object

The materials are now assembled for the verdicts, and the three-object rule dictates their form: three verdicts, not one, each proportioned to its object. On the general ontology, the verdict of the evidence is severe. As a set of laws of all reality, the system fails the tests it invited: it predicted nothing, forbade nothing, survived its illustrations and was edited by its custodians when a law grew politically inconvenient. Its historical effects, when armed with jurisdiction over the sciences, include documented catastrophe. What survives of it survives demoted, as heuristic, as a memory of good questions about process, thresholds and internal relations, and a heuristic is judged kindly but it is not what the doctrine claimed to be. On the theory of history, the verdict must be genuinely open, because the theory is genuinely a theory: it has been refined, mechanised, damaged by counterexamples and defended by reconstruction for over a century, and its central intuition, that production relations constrain the rest of social life more deeply than they are constrained by it, continues to organise research well beyond the ranks of its adherents. Nothing in the ontology's fate touches that standing, in either direction. On the method of Capital, the verdict is a matter for readers of Capital: the procedure exists, its fruits are on the page, and its assessment belongs to the long argument about that book, an argument conducted with a precision and a textual discipline that the catechisms never approached. A guide that has separated the three objects can afford to be calm about all three verdicts, because none of them is doing smuggled work for the others.

What the doctrine's career teaches, beyond its propositions, is a lesson about the sociology of knowledge that any tradition can learn from, including traditions that define themselves against this one. Systems that claim completeness attract custodians; custodians require examinations; examinations select for reproduction over revision; and a doctrine whose content celebrates universal development will then be preserved, by its own machinery, in a state of arrested development, until the machinery fails and the doctrine falls with it. The counterexamples prove the rule by their structure: wherever elements of the dialectical inheritance lived on and bore fruit, in a Harvard laboratory, in a philosopher's study, in the reading of Capital itself, they lived as arguments in the open, exposed to failure, shorn of jurisdiction and certificate. The difference between the doctrine dead in its departments and the inheritance alive in its exiles is not a difference of content. The sentences are often the same sentences. It is a difference of institutional form, and it suggests the maxim with which a reader may safely leave the subject: an idea's truth is argued in texts, but its fate is decided by the arrangements under which it is taught, and dialectical materialism, the philosophy that taught a century to watch for the unity of opposites, became in the end the purest available instance of its own favourite pattern, a system negated not by its enemies but by the logic of its success.

## The tanner in the story: Dietzgen reconsidered

The man credited with the doctrine's name deserves more than his walk-on part, because his fate inside the tradition illustrates its hierarchies perfectly. Joseph Dietzgen arrived at materialism without a university, working a tannery by day and writing philosophy at night, and his essays attempted something genuinely ambitious: a monism in which thought is a function of the material world and yet the world as known is always world-for-a-knower, a knot he worried at with more honesty than system. Marx praised his work warmly in correspondence while noting its confusions; Engels treated him with respect; the movement's popular press serialised him. Then the canon closed. When the doctrine's genealogy was officially drawn, Dietzgen's name shrank to a footnote of coinage, because his actual philosophy, untidy, un-Hegelian in places, innocent of the three laws, did not match the system his phrase had been conscripted to name. Plekhanov, who did more than anyone to fix the term's meaning, corrected Dietzgen's deviations in print, and the correction established the pattern: the label would be honoured, the labeller edited.

There is a wider significance in the episode, about the kind of authorship traditions prefer. A doctrine that presents itself as the worldview of a science and a class requires founders of a certain stature, and it will manufacture their profiles accordingly, elevating collaborators into co-founders where unity is needed and demoting inconvenient originators where their texts resist the system. Dietzgen's demotion and Engels's elevation are the same operation viewed from opposite ends, and the operation was performed not by enemies of the doctrine but by its architects, for reasons internal to the architecture: the system needed a single line of descent, and reality, which had supplied a tanner, a journalist-philosopher in Manchester and a genius in the British Museum, all thinking overlapping thoughts at cross purposes, had to be tidied into one. The tidying is exactly what the archaeology recommended in this guide exists to undo, and Dietzgen is its most sympathetic casualty: a working man who taught himself philosophy, named an aspiration, and was then written out of the doctrine that bears his name for the words on its spine, because his pages would not sit still inside its chapters. Restoring him to the story is not sentimentality. It is the naming discipline applied to persons, and it reminds the reader that traditions are made by people whose actual thoughts are always more interesting than the positions the tradition assigns them.

## Contradiction and the logicians: a late, unexpected dialogue

One late chapter in the doctrine's afterlife deserves record because it confounded both sides' expectations. The analytic objection to dialectics had always rested partly on logic: a discourse that welcomes contradiction, the objection ran, has abandoned the instrument by which error is detected, since classical logic teaches that from a contradiction anything follows and a theory that implies everything explains nothing. In the later twentieth century, however, a minority tradition inside logic itself, the paraconsistent logicians, constructed formal systems in which contradictions can be contained without explosion, and a few of its leading figures, Graham Priest most prominently, argued that the Hegelian and Marxian treatments of contradiction, of motion, of totalities that include themselves, deserved reading as anticipations of genuine logical problems rather than as confusions. Priest's dialetheism, the view that some contradictions are true, was developed with full analytic rigour and defended in the profession's own journals, and his engagements with Hegel and with the paradoxes of motion gave the old texts a hearing in rooms that had dismissed them for a century.

The episode requires careful filing, because both camps rushed to spend it across the three objects. Enthusiasts announced that logic had vindicated dialectical materialism; nothing of the kind occurred, since paraconsistent logic vindicates at most the bare possibility of theorising contradiction without incoherence, and says nothing about laws of nature, barley seeds or historical stages. Sceptics dismissed the logicians as cranks; that too failed, since the systems are rigorous, the problems genuine, and the professional standing of their authors not in question. Under that rule, the episode files where it belongs: it concerns the category of contradiction, an instrument in the neighbourhood of the third object, the method, where Marx's own use of contradiction was always specific, the tension of a social form against its own conditions, rather than a licence to affirm both sides of any proposition. That a disciplined sense can be made of reasoning with contradictions, in narrow and carefully engineered settings, mildly raises the standing of that instrument. It leaves the ontology exactly where the objection left it, and it raises, if anything, the standard the ontology would have to meet: in a world where even contradiction has learned to submit to formal discipline, a doctrine that declines all discipline has one refuge fewer.

## Plekhanov the systematiser: the man who gave the doctrine its shape

Between the quarry and the catechism stands one figure whose contribution is easy to understate because he authored neither the raw material nor the final code: Georgi Plekhanov, who took Engels's scattered philosophical writings, Dietzgen's label and the movement's need for boundaries, and produced from them the first recognisably complete version of the doctrine. His circumstances shaped his construction. An exile in Geneva for most of his career, the founder of the first Russian Marxist group, he fought on two fronts: against the populists, who located Russia's future in the peasant commune and had no use for a philosophy of universal development, and against the revisionists and Machists inside Marxism, whose challenges came dressed as modern science. A system answered both fronts at once. Against the populists, the doctrine's universal laws guaranteed that Russia too would pass through the stages the laws prescribed, whatever the commune's admirers hoped. Against the philosophical innovators, a finished materialism with Engels's authority behind it marked the border between the movement's science and its temptations. Plekhanov's monist view of history, his essays on the role of the individual, his philosophical polemics of the 1890s and 1900s, all work this double border, and they work it brilliantly: he was a writer of genuine philosophical culture, at home in Spinoza, the French materialists and Hegel, and his pages have a range and wit that the later textbooks, which mined them, never reproduced.

Yet the systematiser's virtues carried the system's future defects inside them. Plekhanov's Marxism was a philosophy of stages guaranteed by laws, and the guarantee issued in a political temperament: confidence that history's direction was settled, suspicion of voluntarist shortcuts, and, in 1917, opposition to the October seizure of power on the grounds that the stage had not ripened, a judgement he defended with the doctrine's own logic while the doctrine's other heirs used its flexibility to act. The spectacle is philosophically priceless: the system's architect, faithful to its laws, condemned by events his rivals made, and the rivals, armed with his vocabulary, declaring their breach of the schedule to be the deeper dialectics. A system that can be wielded with equal sincerity for the verdict and for its reversal has disclosed something about itself, and what it has disclosed is not that its users were hypocrites. It is that universal patterns, detached from mechanisms, function in practice as a language for decisions reached elsewhere, dignifying them afterwards. Plekhanov's personal tragedy, dying in 1918 marginal to a revolution made in the name of his philosophy, is the doctrine's structural lesson wearing a human face.

His posthumous career completed the pattern. The Soviet tradition canonised him as a philosopher while burying his political conclusions; his works were published, his name attached to institutes, his heresies of 1917 handled by silence. The Western tradition largely forgot him, so that Anglophone readers often meet the doctrine as an anonymous Soviet product, unaware that its architecture was drawn by a Genevan exile arguing with populists. Restoring Plekhanov to the centre of the story is the naming discipline applied at the level of the system: the doctrine has an architect, the architect had arguments and opponents, and the system he built bears the marks of both. Once that is seen, the doctrine's later rigidity reads differently. What the codifiers froze was not a timeless wisdom but one side of a set of arguments held in the 1890s, by a man whose own side lost the argument about 1917 to men who had learned the system from him. History's ironies are sometimes too neat to credit, but the documents here are plain, and the guide lets them stand without decoration: the systematiser systematised, the system outlived his authority over it, and the laws he trusted to govern history governed everything except the history of his own doctrine.

## Bukharin's manual and the prisoner's critique: the doctrine argued from inside

One more episode belongs in the record because it shows the doctrine being criticised, from inside Marxism and before the codification closed debate, on precisely the grounds this guide has developed. In 1921 Nikolai Bukharin published his theory of historical materialism as a popular manual, a systematic sociology for the movement's schools, lucid, energetic and schematic in the manner of a man who believed the system was finished and needed only exposition. The book was a success, translated widely, and it became the nearest thing the 1920s had to an official textbook. Its temper, however, was the temper the dialectical adjective had been invented to correct: society rendered as a system in equilibrium, disturbed and restored, its laws modelled on a mechanics of forces, its individuals as products of environments in a prose from which practice and self-activity quietly leaked away. Bukharin, the party's most gifted populariser, had written the catechism before the catechism, and in its most mechanistic register.

The critique came from a cell. Antonio Gramsci, imprisoned by Mussolini from 1926, filled notebooks with a sustained engagement with Bukharin's manual, which he called the Popular Manual and treated as the representative specimen of what Marxism becomes when it hardens into a sociology of laws. His objections are surgical. A manual that begins from a finished metaphysics and descends to society has reversed the philosophy of praxis, for which theory is itself a historical act. A sociology modelled on natural science mistakes the status of its object, since social regularities are made and remade by the people they govern, who can learn the alleged laws and act on the knowledge, a reflexivity no law of nature tolerates. And a doctrine presented as the property of specialists, to be believed by the movement, betrays the movement's own epistemology, in which truth is proved in the practice of masses rather than certified in textbooks. Gramsci's alternative was never systematised, the notebooks being fragments written under censorship and illness, and later readers have quarrelled over how far his historicism can be pushed before it dissolves the theory of history into the history of ideas. But the encounter has a permanent place in this guide's argument, because it demonstrates that the separation discipline was available inside the tradition, in real time, against the very textbook through which the hardening proceeded. The codifiers did not lack warning. They had, in a prisoner's notebooks they could not read, the most penetrating internal account ever written of what a manual does to a living theory, and the doctrine's subsequent career, the examinations, the edited laws, the tribunal over genetics, reads in retrospect like the working out of everything the prisoner feared. Intellectual history rarely provides so clean a control case: the same inheritance, one branch administered into a catechism, the other thought through in a cell without hope of publication, and posterity has had no difficulty deciding which branch produced the philosophy.

## Why the word will not die: the doctrine in ordinary speech

Outside scholarship and curricula, the word dialectical enjoys a vigorous vernacular life that a complete guide should acknowledge, because many readers arrive at the doctrine through it. In ordinary usage the word has drifted into a cluster of loose senses: a process in which opposing views contend and something better emerges; a complicated situation with forces pulling in contrary directions; a style of argument that proceeds by paradox and reversal; even, in management and commentary prose, a compliment paid to any analysis that notices more than one side. None of these senses would survive contact with the texts, yet none is random either. Each is a fossil of the system's genuine content, worn smooth by handling: the contention of views preserves the ghost of contradiction, the contrary forces preserve the interpenetration of opposites, the emergence of something better preserves the supersession that the third law promised. Vernacular words often live this double life, as technical terms escaped from disciplines that no longer police them, and the escape itself testifies to a real intellectual hunger the doctrine once fed, the hunger for a vocabulary in which conflict is productive, situations develop, and the present arrangement of things is a stage rather than a fate.

The hunger deserves respect even where the vocabulary has decayed, and the guide's closing counsel for the general reader can be built from it. When the word dialectical appears in speech or print, ask the three-object question in its vernacular form: is the speaker pointing at a method, patiently unfolding a situation through its internal tensions, or at a mood, the feeling that things are complicated and history moves? The method can be engaged, tested against the case and learned from; the mood can only be shared. Much public argument trades the mood for the method's authority, and the trade is painless precisely because the doctrine spent a century teaching audiences to hear its vocabulary as the sound of depth. A reader immunised by the separations of this guide will hear the sound for what it is, will ask for the mechanism, the object and the evidence, and will discover that the request, politely made, dissolves a surprising share of the profundity on offer, leaving behind, occasionally, the genuine article: a thinker actually doing the slow work the word once named. That occasional discovery is worth the immunisation, and it is the note on which a guide to a much abused phrase may properly end its survey of the phrase's fortunes.

## For students and exam candidates

The mark-carrying move on this topic is the three-object distinction, stated early and used consistently: dialectical materialism as a general ontology, historical materialism as a theory of history, and Marx's method in Capital are separate objects with separate authors and separate evidence. Answers lose marks in predictable ways: by attributing the phrase and the three laws to Marx, who used neither; by treating the doctrine and historical materialism as synonyms; and by evaluating all three objects with one verdict, whether favourable or hostile. Secure the chronology first: Engels's raw material in 1878, Dietzgen's coinage in the 1880s, Plekhanov's popularisation in the 1890s, the codification of 1938 with its four features and its missing law. Then allocate criticism correctly: the trivial-or-false charge hits the ontology, the mechanism demand hits lazy uses of contradiction, and Lukacs's footnote hits the extension of dialectics to nature while leaving the method standing. An answer that can run that allocation cleanly, with one named defender such as Levins and Lewontin conceded at heuristic strength, demonstrates exactly the discrimination examiners reward.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Resist the temptation to teach the three laws first. A class that begins with the laws inherits the catechism's confusions and spends the term unlearning them: students memorise patterns, hunt for threes in every topic, and leave believing Marx invented a cosmology. Begin instead with the naming story and the three-object table, which together give students a map before they meet any territory. Then set the archaeology exercise this guide recommends: Engels's chapters, the codification read against them with a pencil, the omissions listed and debated. Seminars divide productively over the missing law and over Lukacs's footnote, and both disputes force the separation discipline without lecturing about it. Close with the defences, assigning the dialectical biologists against the trivial-or-false objection, so that students must decide at what strength, law or heuristic, the categories can be honestly held. Assessment should reward correct allocation of criticism to object, and penalise the composite phrase Marxist philosophy wherever it substitutes for an author and a date.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Treat the doctrine as an institutional object before treating it as a set of propositions. The interesting questions are about codification and enforcement: who fixed the four features, which controversies the settlement closed, how the curriculum reproduced the system, and where its appellate jurisdiction over the sciences produced documented damage, with the Lysenko affair as the central case and Vavilov as its human measure. Archival and textbook evidence outranks later polemic, and the primary sequence, Anti-Duhring, the posthumous Dialectics of Nature of 1925, Lenin's two very different philosophical legacies, the condemnations of 1930 and 1931, the Short Course text of 1938, supports fine-grained work on how a philosophy becomes an administration. Journalists covering the afterlife, whether the doctrine's persistence in Chinese curricula or its fossil presence in political rhetoric, should name the object precisely and resist the composite label. Researchers in the history of science will find the protective-colouring phenomenon, scientists redescribing sound work in dialectical vocabulary to survive it, an understudied mechanism with parallels in other enforced orthodoxies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is dialectical materialism?

Dialectical materialism is the official philosophy constructed within Marxism after Marx's death. It joins a materialist claim, that the world exists independently of thought and forms one natural order, to a dialectical claim, that this order develops through internal contradictions, thresholds where quantity passes into quality, and stages that supersede one another. Engels supplied the raw material, Plekhanov gave the system its name and shape, and a Soviet text of 1938 codified it. It claims jurisdiction over nature, society and thought alike, which is what distinguishes it from historical materialism, a theory of history alone, and from Marx's own method in Capital, a procedure for presenting one social system.

### Q: Did Marx ever use the term dialectical materialism?

No surviving text by Marx contains the phrase. He wrote of his dialectical method, of a materialist conception of history, and of inverting Hegel, but the compound label appears only after his death in 1883. The phrase is generally credited to Joseph Dietzgen in the 1880s and was popularised by Plekhanov in the following decade. The distinction matters because a term coined by successors signals a doctrine assembled by successors: the system must be assessed as a tradition's construction from Marx's and Engels's materials, not as a work Marx authored and could have corrected.

### Q: What are the three laws of dialectics?

Engels formulated three laws. The transformation of quantity into quality holds that accumulated gradual change can cross thresholds into change of kind, as water becomes steam. The interpenetration of opposites holds that processes contain opposed tendencies defined through each other, whose interplay drives development. The negation of the negation holds that a stage generates its opposite, which is itself superseded in a way that preserves something of both, as the seed is negated by the plant and restored multiplied at harvest. The laws were presented as abstracted from nature and history, and their standing as laws, rather than as heuristics or patterns, is the doctrine's most contested point.

### Q: Who invented dialectical materialism?

No single inventor exists, which is the honest answer the question resists. Engels supplied the materials in Anti-Duhring and his manuscripts on nature, without using the label or completing a system. Joseph Dietzgen is generally credited with the phrase in the 1880s. Plekhanov built the materials into a named system in the 1890s and carried it into the international movement. Lenin fixed its theory of knowledge and its polemical style. The codifiers of 1938 fixed its final, examinable form. Invention here means assembly over five decades, and each stage of the assembly made choices a reader is entitled to examine separately.

### Q: How does dialectical materialism differ from ordinary materialism?

Ordinary or mechanical materialism, the kind associated with the eighteenth century and with Feuerbach, holds that matter is primary but pictures it as fixed things in external relation, moved by outside forces and contemplated by a passive mind. Dialectical materialism adds three corrections: the world is process rather than a stock of things; change arises from internal contradiction rather than external impact alone; and knowledge is an activity, tested in practice, rather than a beholding. The additions repair genuine weaknesses in the older doctrine. Whether they amount to laws of all reality, or to a set of counsels about how to study processes, is the point on which the doctrine's critics and its careful defenders divide.

### Q: Why did Stalin drop the negation of the negation?

The codifying text of 1938 presented the dialectical method through four features and omitted the third law without acknowledgement or explanation. Scholars have proposed several motives: the law's association with the Hegelianising philosophers defeated in the controversies around 1930; a drive to simplify the doctrine for mass instruction; and the law's awkward implication, since a pattern in which every stage is superseded by its negation sits poorly as the creed of a settled ruling order. No documentary proof settles the weighting among these, and honest accounts present them as interpretation. The omission itself, and the silence around it, remain the clearest evidence that the text functioned as an instrument of rule as well as a summary of philosophy.

### Q: Is dialectical materialism a science or a philosophy?

Its defenders claimed both statuses at once: a philosophy that was also the most general science, the science of the laws common to nature, society and thought. Critics reply that it behaves as neither discipline permits. A science must risk predictions that could fail, and no finding was ever derived from the three laws in advance of the special sciences. A philosophy must expose its arguments to refutation, and the administered doctrine settled disputes by authority. The fairest classification is historical: it was a state philosophy that borrowed the prestige of science, whose individual claims can still be assessed philosophically, case by case, once the borrowed prestige is set aside.

### Q: Did Western Marxists reject dialectical materialism?

The founding generation did, explicitly and at the moment of the tradition's birth. Lukacs argued in a footnote of 1923 that Engels had mistakenly extended dialectics to nature, since dialectics lives in the relation of subject and object within history. Korsch mounted a parallel critique the same year, and the Communist International condemned both, marking the formal parting between the administered doctrine and Western Marxism. Gramsci recast the inheritance as a philosophy of praxis, Adorno kept the word dialectics while refusing every law, and analytical Marxists later rejected dialectical reasoning as obscure. Western Marxism is therefore best defined by this refusal: it kept Marx's history and method and discarded the universal ontology.

### Q: Does dialectical materialism apply to nature?

That is the question on which the tradition split. Engels held that it must, since thought itself is a natural product and the laws are abstracted from natural as well as historical processes. Lukacs and most Western Marxists held that it cannot, because dialectics requires the interplay of subject and object found only in social practice, while nature contemplated from outside shows processes but no self-mediation. The sciences, for their part, have never needed the laws to proceed. The strongest modern attempt to show application, the dialectical biology of Levins and Lewontin, presents the categories as heuristics guiding research rather than laws nature obeys, which concedes the form of the question while defending a modest version of the answer.

### Q: Is dialectical materialism still defended by anyone?

Yes, though almost never in its codified form. Bertell Ollman defends a dialectics of internal relations as the logic of Marx's social theory. Followers of Roy Bhaskar's dialectical critical realism argue that absence and negation are real features of a stratified world. Levins and Lewontin defended dialectical heuristics inside biology on the strength of their scientific practice. In China, Marxist philosophy remains an institutional academic field, increasingly professionalised and dialogic. What unites the living defences is what they drop: the three laws as a universal code, guaranteed in advance. Defence in the tradition survives by shrinking the claim until it can do identifiable work.

### Q: How did the term dialectical materialism pass from Plekhanov into orthodoxy?

Plekhanov's philosophical writings of the 1890s gave the term systematic content and enormous prestige inside the Russian and international movements, and Lenin's close study of them carried the label into Bolshevism. After the revolution, party schools and institutes made it a taught subject; the philosophical camps of the 1920s fought over its interpretation until party decisions around 1930 and 1931 closed the contest; and the Short Course text of 1938 fixed the four features as the standard every textbook reproduced. The passage from a thinker's coinage to an orthodoxy thus ran through institutions: journals, schools, condemnations and a catechism, in that order.

### Q: What should a student separate in Engels's three laws before using them?

Three separations protect a student from the standard errors. Separate the pattern from the mechanism: a threshold or a polarity may be real in a case while the mechanism that explains it belongs entirely to the specific science. Separate the law's two registers: as a heuristic instructing the researcher to look for thresholds, internal tensions and superseded stages, the laws are defensible; as guaranteed features of all reality, they are contested and probably untenable. Separate the authors: the laws are Engels's formulations, elaborated by successors, not propositions from Capital, and an essay that attributes them to Marx has failed its first test of accuracy.

### Q: Why do Western Marxists reject the label dialectical materialism?

The label stands, for that tradition, for everything it defines itself against: a universal ontology administered as orthodoxy, in which dialectics was detached from historical practice and imposed on nature. Lukacs's founding objection was theoretical, that a dialectics without a subject is a contradiction in terms, but the rejection hardened as the doctrine's institutional career unfolded, since the label came to signify philosophy by decree. Western Marxists therefore preferred other banners, the philosophy of praxis, critical theory, negative dialectics, or plain historical materialism. Rejecting the label did not mean rejecting dialectical thinking; it meant refusing to let the thinking be identified with the catechism that had monopolised its name.

### Q: How does dialectical materialism differ from historical materialism in scope?

Historical materialism is a theory of one domain, society in its history: it claims that production and property relations constrain law, politics and ideas, and that modes of production change through their internal tensions. Dialectical materialism claims every domain: nature, society and thought under common laws. The old doctrine nested the first inside the second, as an application, but the chronology runs the other way, since Marx's theory of history predates the assembled ontology by decades, and the logic runs the other way too, since the evidence for the theory of history is historical, and stands or falls without any support from boiling water or barley seeds.

### Q: What makes a law of dialectics different from a finding of natural science?

A finding of natural science is local, quantitative where possible, and exposed: it states the conditions under which it holds, the instruments that test it, and the observations that would overturn it. A law of dialectics, as codified, is universal, qualitative, and protected: it states a pattern said to hold everywhere, illustrates it by selected examples, and supplies no observation that would count as its failure, since any process can be redescribed in its vocabulary. That asymmetry, not hostility to Marx, is the core of the scientific objection. Defenders who present the laws as heuristics rather than laws are, in effect, accepting the distinction and relocating their claims to ground where the asymmetry does not bite.
