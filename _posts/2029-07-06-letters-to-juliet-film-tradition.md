## Letters to Juliet: The Verdict on the Film and Its Tradition

Letters to Juliet, Gary Winick's romantic drama of 2010, is a modest picture built on an immodest premise: that a single unanswered letter, written to a fictional girl in 1957 and left in a Verona wall, could wait half a century for its reply and then repair the life that wrote it. The verdict of this article is plain. As drama, the film is slight, predictable and content to be both. As an act of cultural transmission, it is the most consequential screen treatment the play's afterlife produced in its decade, because it did something no straight adaptation attempts. It took the one piece of machinery the tragedy runs on, the letter that fails, and rebuilt an entire romance out of that machinery succeeding. The play ends because a message miscarries. The film begins because a message, against every rule of the postal system and of probability, does not.

![A courtyard wall in Verona covered with letters addressed to Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That inversion is the film's real subject, and it is why the picture belongs in a series on the play rather than in a footnote to the romantic comedy shelf. Verona receives thousands of letters a year addressed to a heroine who never lived, and a small corps of volunteers answers them in her name, by hand. Lise Friedman and Ceil Friedman chronicled that practice in their 2006 non-fiction book, also called Letters to Juliet, and the film draws on their account. What Winick and his screenwriters, José Rivera and Tim Sullivan, added was a plot: a young American fact checker, a buried letter, a widowed Englishwoman, a skeptical grandson, and a search across Tuscany for one man among seventy-four namesakes. Around that invented spine, the picture wraps a documented institution, a documented city and a documented literary descent, for the letter that arrives too late is not Hollywood's invention. It is Shakespeare's engine, inherited from his sources, and the film is best understood as the engine running in reverse.

The argument proceeds in order. First the plot, compressed to the machinery that matters. Then the chain from the Friedmans' book to the screenplay, and what the screenplay invented. Then the inversion itself, set against the play's text, where Friar Laurence's charge miscarries in Act 5 and the catastrophe follows in a matter of stage minutes. Then the casting fact that no reviewer could ignore: the reunited lovers are played by Vanessa Redgrave and Franco Nero, whose own separation and reunion across four decades runs beneath the fiction like a second film. Then the critical quarrel, between Roger Ebert's openly personal embrace and the contempt of reviewers who found the picture corn from title to credits, adjudicated against the box office and audience record. Then the fiction measured against the documented practice, element by element, in the InsightCrunch Letters note. Last, the effect: what the film did to the mail, to the courtyard and to the city's visitor economy, and why the series thesis holds. In the standard account, the tourist cliche floats free of the play, a balcony and a statue with a brand attached. This film closes the loop. The play feeds the ritual, the ritual feeds the film, and the film feeds the ritual again, at greater volume.

Start with the title, which is the thesis in four words. The film is not called Sophie and Charlie, or Claire and Lorenzo, or any of the names a romance uses to announce its couple. It is called after the correspondence: Letters to Juliet, letters in the plural, addressed to the heroine in her capacity as a recipient of mail. No adaptation of the play takes its title from a piece of the plot's plumbing. This one takes its title from nothing else, and the poster sold the arrangement honestly, the young star in the foreground and the wall of letters behind her. A viewer who takes the title at its word has already understood the film better than most of its reviewers did. The couples are the occasions. The post is the subject. The rest of this article is an attempt to take the title at its word, and to grade the film by the standard the title sets: not whether its romances convince, though that question gets its hearing, but whether its account of the letter, as an object, an institution and an inheritance from the play, is the fullest the screen has offered. By that standard the picture is not a footnote to the play's screen history. It is a chapter of it, and the chapter was overdue.

A note on evidence before the argument begins. The institution at the centre of the story, the Juliet Club and its secretaries, has its own article in this series, and the house whose courtyard opens the film has another. Those records are linked at the points where they bear on the argument and are not re-tried here. This article owns the film: its making, its meaning, its reception and its consequences. Where the record is thin, the article says so and grades the claim. Where the film and the documented tradition part company, the parting is named. The romance is allowed its warmth. It is not allowed its facts for free.

## What Happens in Letters to Juliet? A Plot Built on One Unanswered Letter

Sophie Hall, played by Amanda Seyfried, is a fact checker at The New Yorker who wants to write and is not yet trusted to do so. She travels to Verona on a pre-honeymoon with her fiancé Victor, a chef played by Gael García Bernal, who is opening a restaurant in Manhattan and treats the Italian trip as a sourcing expedition. Victor races from vineyard to auction to truffle supplier. Sophie, left to the city, drifts to the courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta, where women from every country leave letters for Juliet in the wall, and where, each evening, a group of volunteers collects them. The collectors are the secretaries of Juliet, four women in the film's telling, played by Luisa Ranieri, Marina Massironi, Milena Vukotic and Lidia Biondi, who carry the day's harvest to an office and answer it. Sophie asks to help. Helping, she finds what the institution's routine should have made impossible: a letter that was never collected at all, sealed behind a loose stone, dated 1957.

The letter's writer is Claire Smith, a young Englishwoman who had studied in Italy, fallen in love with a farm worker named Lorenzo Bartolini, and promised to run away with him. Her courage failed at the appointed hour. She went home, and the letter, asking Juliet whether she had done wrong and what she ought to do, waited in the wall for fifty-three years. Sophie reads it and does the thing the play's messengers never manage. She answers. Her reply, written in Juliet's name and in the secretaries' spirit, tells Claire that love deferred is not love extinguished, and that the question of 1957 still deserves its answer. Within days Claire arrives in Verona, played by Vanessa Redgrave, widowed, composed and entirely unwilling to treat the reply as a courtesy. She intends to find Lorenzo. Her grandson Charlie, a barrister played by Christopher Egan, accompanies her in a state of sustained disapproval, convinced that the errand is a fantasy that will end in a stranger's doorstep or a graveyard.

The middle of the film is the search, and the search is engineered as a study in the play's own arithmetic of names. There are seventy-four Lorenzo Bartolinis in the region the telephone records cover, and the three travellers work through them: farmers, mechanics, grandfathers, men too young, men too old, men who light up at Claire's description and men who plainly do not. One Lorenzo Bartolini is dead, and the film pauses at his grave while Charlie turns on Sophie and accuses her of spending other people's grief on a magazine story. The quarrel produces the film's one piece of back-story with weight: Sophie's mother left her as a small girl, and the searcher for lost people is herself one of the lost. Claire, who has overheard, orders the apology. The thaw between Sophie and Charlie follows, then the kiss, then the retreat from the kiss, for Sophie is engaged and Victor's telephone calls, all appetite and logistics, keep arriving like weather from another climate.

### Is the letter Sophie finds a real letter from the Club's archive?

No documented letter stands behind the device. The Friedmans' book prints scores of genuine letters, chosen from the thousands the secretaries handle, but no fifty-year-old survivor from 1957 appears in its pages or in the Club's own accounts, and the film's production record claims none.

The buried letter is the screenplay's compression of a system into an object: one envelope made to carry the weight of an institution. Its status is examined again in the comparison of film and fact below.

On the final day of searching, resigned to failure, the party stops at a vineyard for a farewell drink, and Claire sees a young man on a horse whose face is the face in her memory. The young man is Lorenzo Bartolini the third, played by Stefano Guerrini, and the estate belongs to his grandfather. The reunion of Claire and Lorenzo, played by Franco Nero, is staged without irony and at the pace of a meal: a long table, an extended family, two widowed people in their seventies discovering that the interruption of 1957 was an interruption and not an ending. Sophie returns to New York with the story. Her editor, played by Oliver Platt, accepts it for The New Yorker, the professional door she had pushed at in vain from the fact-checking desk. The engagement to Victor dissolves off the same recognition that powered the search: a relationship conducted entirely by telephone, between a woman asking to be attended to and a man attending to everything else, cannot be repaired by a wedding. Sophie flies back to Verona for Claire and Lorenzo's wedding, where Claire reads Sophie's letter aloud to the guests, the film's buried text brought into the open at last. There follows the closing feint. Charlie appears with a Patricia, Sophie believes the Patricia is the former girlfriend he had mentioned, and she retreats to a balcony to make her confession of love and her farewell in one speech. The Patricia is a cousin. Charlie climbs the vine toward the balcony, in the film's deliberate restaging of the play's most famous approach, and falls. The kiss the film ends on is exchanged on the ground, among the wedding guests, with the climber flat on his back and unhurt. The tragedy inverted in a single gag: in Verona, in this telling, the young man who climbs to the balcony does not die of the height. He is caught by the comedy of the piece, which is to say by its conviction that descent, not ascent, is where happiness is found.

Two features of the plot's carpentry deserve more than summary. The first is the number. Seventy-four Lorenzo Bartolinis is a joke, but it is also a census, and the search that works through it is the film's disguised portrait of the tradition itself. The secretaries' real labour, in the Friedmans' account, is exactly this: the patient processing of a multitude, name by name, letter by letter, with no guarantee that any single file conceals a destiny. The film converts that clerical patience into a road movie, and the conversion preserves the ethic while changing the stakes. Most of the seventy-four are not candidates in any serious sense; they are the multitude through which the one must be found, and the film is honest enough to let several of them be kind, several be bewildered, and one be dead. The dead Lorenzo is the second feature. Every retelling of this story is required to produce its tomb, and the romance, having renounced the vault, produces a graveyard in the middle of the search: a grave visited, a widow's hope briefly buried, a grandson's cruelty spoken aloud among the stones. The scene is the film's concession to the tragedy's gravity, the moment the comedy pays its toll. It matters that the toll is demanded and then refunded, for the search resumes and the true Lorenzo is found alive at a vineyard, at a table, in the sun. The play's structure places the tomb at the end and fills it. The film places its tomb at the midpoint and empties it. Between those two placements lies the whole distance the picture travels from its source, measured in a single prop.

The feasts tell the same story in the opposite direction. The play's one great gathering is Capulet's ball, where the lovers meet under surveillance, in a room owned by the feud, and where Tybalt's recognition of Romeo's voice turns hospitality into a death sentence postponed. The film's great gathering is the Bartolini table at the vineyard, outdoors, multi-generational, owned by nobody's enmity, where the lovers' reunion is witnessed by grandchildren and ratified by food. Where the ball compresses the feud's whole future into one evening, the table dissolves a fifty-year grievance in one afternoon. The film is sometimes charged with having no antagonist. The charge is accurate and beside the point. Its antagonist is the arrangement the ball represents: the world organized so that love must be conducted in code, by letter, through intermediaries, in stolen minutes. Against that world the film sets the table, where everything is said aloud and nobody is poisoned by the wine.

The plot is, as its detractors said, a machine whose outcomes are visible from the first reel. What the detractors undervalued is the machine's design. Every element is the play's element with its polarity reversed. A letter delayed becomes a letter delivered. A messenger who fails becomes a stranger who persists. A young woman paralyzed by the fear of her family's judgment in 1957 becomes an old woman whom no judgment can reach. A balcony that framed a promise becomes a balcony that frames a pratfall. Even the soundtrack carries the argument, for among its songs sits Taylor Swift's "Love Story", the chart hit that had already rewritten the play as a proposal accepted and a father persuaded, discussed in its own place in this series. The film is not an adaptation of Romeo and Juliet. It is a systematic antithesis of it, and an antithesis requires its thesis at every point. The play is present in nearly every scene, not as quotation, of which the film uses almost none, but as the negative space the romance is cut from.

## From the Friedmans' Book to the Screen: How a Non-Fiction Chronicle Became a Romance

The film's source is not the play. It is a work of reportage. Letters to Juliet: Celebrating Shakespeare's Greatest Heroine, the Magical City of Verona, and the Power of Love, by Lise Friedman and Ceil Friedman, was published by Stewart, Tabori and Chang in New York in 2006, a compact illustrated volume of 176 pages that Publisher's Weekly, announcing the film, accurately described as the production's basis. Lise Friedman came to the subject as an editor and former dancer; Ceil Friedman as an art historian and translator resident in Italy. Between them they produced neither a novel nor a guidebook but a chronicle of a practice: the arrival, across more than seven decades, of letters addressed to a heroine who has no address, and the volunteers who answer them. The book prints more than seventy-five of the letters, in the writers' own words, interleaved with the history of the tale, the city and the monuments. A paperback edition followed in May 2010, timed to the film, which is itself a datum: the adaptation economy ran in both directions, the book supplying the film with its world and the film returning the favour in sales.

The record the Friedmans assembled is the bedrock under everything the film builds. Letters to Juliet began arriving long before any club existed to receive them. Visitors left notes at the site identified as Juliet's tomb in the years after the First World War, and in the 1930s the tomb's custodian, Ettore Solimani, began gathering the messages and answering them himself, the first secretary of Juliet, acting privately and at his own expense of time. The Friedmans' phrase for the mature practice sets the standard the film would have to meet: the secretaries, they write, "read, often translate, and answer each one, personally and by hand." Each one. The ethic of the institution is total response. No letter with a return address goes unanswered, however broken its question, however distant its writer. The volunteers who first acted privately were in time sanctioned by the city of Verona, and the work was coordinated, from the mid-1980s, by the Juliet Club founded under Giulio Tamassia, with a registration system built to guarantee that every letter is logged, answered and preserved. The institution itself, its founding generation and its working disciplines, is the subject of [the Juliet Club and the letters it answers](/2029/06/29/juliet-club-letters-to-juliet/), where the Club's record is set out in full. For the film's purposes what matters is narrower: by 2006 a documented, orderly, city-sanctioned practice existed, with a literature of its own, waiting for a dramatist to notice that it was, structurally, a story machine.

### Did a real Sophie ever answer a fifty-year-old letter in Verona?

No such case appears in the Friedmans' chronicle, in the Club's published accounts or in the reporting around the film. The secretaries answer the mail of the season in which it arrives, and the archive preserves letters as documents rather than as pending obligations.

A reply delayed by half a century would contradict the institution's founding discipline of total, prompt response. Sophie is an invention, and the buried letter she answers is the mechanism by which the invention enters the real office without breaking its rules: the one message the system never saw is the one message a stranger may answer on its behalf.

What Rivera and Sullivan took from the book was the world entire: the courtyard, the wall, the daily collection, the office, the ethic of the handwritten reply, the writers in their thousands asking a dead girl for counsel. What they invented was everything that makes a film: a protagonist with a stalled career and a failing engagement, a single letter with a date and a name, a quest with a countable field of seventy-four candidates, a wedding to end on. The screenplay's boldest invention is also its most faithful gesture. The book's letters are, nearly all of them, requests for rescue addressed across an unbridgeable distance, to a recipient who cannot answer because she does not exist. The film grants one such request its answer, and then asks the question the book cannot ask: what would the answer cost, and whom would it move? José Rivera brought unusual credentials to that act of ventriloquism. His screenplay for The Motorcycle Diaries, the film that made a star of the young Gael García Bernal, had made him the first Puerto Rican screenwriter nominated for an Academy Award, and his stage work had long traded in the traffic between the living and the absent. There is a neatness the production cannot have missed: the writer who had scripted Bernal's breakthrough scripted him again as the fiancé too absorbed to notice his own displacement, the dead weight the heroine's arc exists to shed.

The letters the Friedmans print deserve a paragraph of their own, because the film's plausibility, such as it is, rests entirely on their strangeness being real. The writers in the book address Juliet as confidante, judge and last resort. Schoolgirls ask whether a boy's silence is a verdict. Wives describe marriages conducted across continents and ask whether endurance is a virtue or a sentence. The bereaved write to the one girl in literature who is certified to understand a love ended by death, and the divorced write to ask whether the fault was theirs. A share of the mail is not unhappy at all: thanks for a love that held, reports filed to the patron of the fortunate. The Friedmans print the letters with their spelling, their crossings of language, their addresses at the head, and the effect is documentary in the strict sense, a cross-section of the world's romantic correspondence filed under a fictional name. Two features of the archive matter for the film. The first is that the writers know Juliet is fictional and write anyway, which makes the practice a literary act before it is a therapeutic one: the letter to Juliet is a genre, with conventions, and its writers are performing in it knowingly. The second is that the secretaries answer in kind, by hand and in character, sustaining the fiction from the other side. The whole institution is a collaboration in make-believe between strangers who need it to be half real. A film that turned the practice into a romance was not decorating a quaint custom. It was extending the collaboration by other means, which is why the Club's custodians, whose business is the custody of a useful fiction, recognized the film as kin.

The book's own standing in the literature of the afterlife should be recorded before the chain is followed further, since the film tends to swallow its source in the public memory. Within six years of publication the Friedmans' chronicle had become the standard citation for the practice: the Utah Shakespeare Festival's study materials quote its pages on the secretaries' discipline, on the tomb's early pilgrims and on the letters' volume as the authority of record, and Eleonora Oggiano's scholarly survey of the Verona brand, discussed below, builds its account of the first secretary on the Friedmans' chapter "The Letters Begin." A 176-page illustrated volume from a New York art-book house thus became the archive through which scholars, journalists and screenwriters alike approach a Veronese institution. That is the precondition of the film. Without the book there is no documented world for the screenplay to enter, only a picturesque rumour of women answering letters, unverifiable and unusable. The Friedmans did the verification. The film inherited it, the way an heir inherits a house someone else built, and the inheritance is acknowledged in the picture's own credits and in every account of its origins.

The adaptation chain matters to the verdict because it explains the film's odd double nature, half documentary and half confection. The confection is the plot. The documentary is the premise, and the premise keeps breaking through the plot's surface: the collection at dusk, the letters read aloud in the office and sorted by language, the handwriting. Film students assembling the adaptation question for a seminar, laying the book's pages beside the screenplay's inventions in a deck built with [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), can watch the seam run through scene after scene: everything institutional is inherited, everything biographical is invented. That seam is the film's honesty and its limitation at once. It never pretends the Club is a fantasy. It reserves its fantasy for the people, and people are where a romance is permitted to lie.

## The Letter on Screen Before Winick: The Tradition the Film Inherited

Winick's picture did not invent the screen's interest in the play's correspondence. It inherited a problem every film of the tragedy has to solve, and solved it by abdication in the opposite direction: where the adaptations treat the letter as an embarrassment to be cleared, the romance treats the letter as the whole estate. The problem is real. Act 5, Scene 2, in which Friar John explains a failed errand to Friar Laurence in a bare room, is the least cinematic scene in the play and the most load-bearing. Remove it, and the ending loses its logic; the audience must simply accept that Romeo never learned the truth. Keep it, and the film halts for an exposition duet between two friars at the exact moment the tragedy needs speed. The history of the play on screen is, in part, a history of directors paying or refusing that bill.

The prestige versions pay it. The reverent adaptations in the line of George Cukor's 1936 production and Franco Zeffirelli's of 1968 keep the scene's substance, because their contract with the audience is fidelity: the explanation is spoken, the plague is named, and the catastrophe proceeds on documented rails. Franco Castellani's 1954 film, the most literary of the early versions, likewise preserves the causal chain, trusting the audience to follow a message's failure as attentively as a duel. The modernizing versions refuse the bill and pay a different one. Baz Luhrmann's William Shakespeare's Romeo + Juliet of 1996, whose handling of the text is dissected elsewhere in this series, abolishes the plague house and substitutes the machinery of its own world: the Friar's letter travels by express courier and fails against the logistics of Verona Beach, a missed delivery in a city of couriers, the twentieth century's equivalent of a sealed door. The substitution is witty and fatal in the right way, but it is a beat, seconds long. The letter in Luhrmann remains what it is in every adaptation: a device, cleared off the desk so that the poetry can proceed.

Two further features of the tradition deserve record. The first is the fate of Romeo's own letter, the one he writes to his father and entrusts to Balthasar before the vault, which the Prince reads aloud in the play's closing movement as the documentary proof that reconciles the houses: the letter as evidence, the written word auditing the spoken. Screen versions habitually thin or drop the Prince's reading, since a film that has shown the audience everything has no need of a document to certify it, and so the play's one successful letter is routinely cut by the very medium that could most easily show it arriving. The second is the treatment of the play's other great act of correspondence, the one conducted in person. Films linger, rightly, on the orchard scene, on Juliet's soliloquy overheard, because it photographs beautifully. None of them notes what the scene is in the story's economy: a message composed for an absent recipient and delivered by accident, the template for every letter later left in a Verona wall. The tradition on screen, surveyed whole, is thus a tradition of avoidance. Directors film the lovers, the feud, the ball, the tomb. They film around the letters, cutting the failed one to a beat and the successful one to nothing, and they leave the most consequential object in the story, a folded sheet of paper, without a single close-up in ninety years of trying.

A taxonomic point follows, and it disciplines the verdict. The play's screen tradition contains adaptations, which retell the story; appropriations, which raid the story for parts; and a third, rarer kind, works that take neither the plot nor the characters but the story's surrounding practice as their subject. Letters to Juliet belongs to the third kind, and it may be the kind's most successful commercial instance in any Shakespeare afterlife. It contains no Romeo, no Juliet, no feud, no duel and no death worth the name, yet it is saturated with the play to a degree few faithful adaptations achieve, because the practice it films is the play's own behaviour continued by other means: strangers performing belief in the lovers, in writing, at the lovers' address, under the lovers' names. The secretaries are the play's longest-running production, nightly, unreviewed, with a cast of volunteers and an audience of the lovelorn, and the film is best filed as that production's screen record with a romance built into it for carriage. Taxonomy explains the critical confusion the picture met. Judged as an adaptation it is an absence, a film with the play left out. Judged as an appropriation it is a theft of prestige for a confection. Only under the third heading does its design become visible entire, and under that heading the design is exact: take the one element of the afterlife that is still a living practice, film the practice faithfully, and invent only the people the practice deserves.

That avoidance is the vacancy Letters to Juliet occupies, and occupying it required the film to leave the play's plot entirely. So long as a film tells the lovers' story, the letter is condemned to be machinery, because the story's interest lives elsewhere. Only a film willing to abandon the lovers as protagonists, to hand the story to the correspondents instead, could give the letter its close-up. Winick's romance is that film. Its heroine is not a lover but a secretary; its climax is not a death but a delivery; its Verona is not the city of the feud but the city of the answer. Whatever its faults as drama, and they are catalogued below, the picture saw the empty chair in the play's screen tradition and sat in it. The letter had been the tragedy's engine for four centuries and its adaptations' afterthought for one. The romance of 2010 is the first screen work to notice that the engine and the afterthought were the same object, and to build accordingly.

## The Letter That Arrives Fifty Years Late: How the Film Reverses the Play

To see what the film reverses, the mechanism it reverses must be stated from the text. The catastrophe of Romeo and Juliet is administrative. Juliet's feigned death is a message in itself, a false signal sent to her family; the true signal, the explanation, travels separately, in a letter Friar Laurence entrusts to Friar John for Romeo in Mantua. Friar John never leaves Verona. Seeking a companion for the road, he enters a house suspected of plague, and the searchers of the town seal both friars inside it. His report in Act 5, Scene 2 is the plainest prose of failure in the canon: he could not send the letter, nor find any messenger to carry it, "so fearful were they of infection." Laurence's answer names the stakes exactly. "The letter was not nice but full of charge," he cries, "of dear import; and the neglecting it may do much danger." Meanwhile a faster, falser message has already travelled. Balthasar, who saw Juliet laid in the Capel vault, rides to Mantua with the news of her death, and Romeo, asking after letters from the Friar and hearing of none, buys poison on the strength of the report that arrived. The play's Verona runs on correspondence and dies of it: the true letter slow and sealed, the false report swift and fatal, and Romeo's own final letter, to his father, the one message in the tragedy that reaches its reader precisely as intended, confirming the Friar's account over the bodies.

Nor is the fatal letter an isolated device. The play runs a whole correspondence economy, and every branch of it underperforms. Juliet's first answer to Romeo travels by the Nurse in Act 2, and the errand that should take minutes takes three hours of stage complaint, the old woman returning footsore and teasing, doling out the wedding hour line by line while Juliet begs for speed: the comic version of the same delay that will kill in earnest three acts later. Tybalt's challenge to Romeo travels as a letter to old Montague's house, announced by Mercutio in Act 2, Scene 4, a formal written summons that its addressee never opens on stage, the feud conducting its deadliest business by post. Romeo, banished, maintains his own intelligence service, paying Balthasar to bring him news of Juliet and, in the event, of nothing else: "If I may trust the flattering truth of sleep," he says as the man rides in, his dreams having promised him good tidings (5.1). The information network of Verona is complete, staffed and busy. It simply never delivers the one message that matters, while delivering, with terrible efficiency, every message that harms. Read against that economy, the film's Verona is a reformed state. Its correspondence system is the Club: registered, translated, answered, archived, an institution built across seven decades with the single purpose of ensuring that no message addressed in love goes unanswered. Where Shakespeare's city has a postal service of cousins, servants and friars, each failing privately, the film's city has professionalized the answer. The comedy of the romance is the sound of the machine, at last, working.

The machinery is older than the play. In Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet of 1562, the long poem from which Shakespeare drew the story, the explanatory letter is likewise placed in a friar's hands and likewise never arrives, hindered on the road while the false news travels free; behind Brooke stands the Italian novella line descending from Luigi da Porto, where the tale already turns on messages entrusted, delayed and outrun. Across four retellings and a century, the story keeps one invariant: the lovers do not die of the feud, or of poison, or of the dagger. They die of a delivery failure. The feud loads the weapon; the post fires it.

Letters to Juliet is the first major screen treatment to build its entire structure on reversing that invariant, and the reversal is systematic rather than decorative. In the play, a letter written in urgency arrives nowhere; in the film, a letter written in doubt arrives fifty-three years late and arrives completely, read closely, answered at length, its writer located, its plea granted. In the play, the messenger is stopped by a sealed house and a frightened town; in the film, the messenger is stopped by nothing at all, since Sophie appoints herself to the office and pursues the delivery across two countries and seventy-four doorsteps. In the play, the letter's failure kills the young; in the film, the letter's success resurrects the old, returning to Claire and Lorenzo the half-century the first failure of nerve had confiscated. Even the film's geography obeys the inversion. Shakespeare's fatal journey runs from Verona to Mantua, outward, toward exile. The film's redemptive journey runs from Verona outward into the Tuscan hills and back, a loop rather than an exile, ending where it began, at a wedding inside the walls Romeo insisted define the world: "There is no world without Verona walls" (3.3).

One parallel fixes the whole design, and it is worth isolating. The play ends with a public reading of a letter. In the vault, over the bodies, the Prince receives Romeo's letter to his father from Balthasar and reads its contents into the record: the letter "doth make good the Friar's words," confirming the purchase of poison, the marriage, the intent to die beside Juliet, and on the strength of that document the two fathers are reconciled and the golden statues are promised. A letter, accurately delivered at last, performs the tragedy's only act of repair, and it performs it too late to repair anything but the feud. The film ends with the same ceremony, rebuilt. At the wedding reception, Claire stands and reads Sophie's letter aloud to the assembled guests: the reply to the 1957 appeal, the document that set the plot in motion, entered into the public record of the community it created. The parallels are exact and the inversions are exact. In the play, a son's letter is read over his corpse to explain a death. In the film, a stranger's letter is read over a wedding breakfast to explain a life. In the play, the reading reconciles two houses that have lost their children. In the film, the reading unites two families that have gained their parents back. Screenwriters are sometimes accused of stealing structures unconsciously. This one has the fingerprints of intention all over it: Rivera and Sullivan took the tragedy's closing documentary ritual, the reading that audits everything, and staged it at the opposite pole of the human calendar. The letter in Verona always ends up read aloud before the community. The only variable the tradition has ever changed is whether the community is standing at a tomb or at a table.

### Why does Sophie's reply succeed where the Friar's letter failed?

Because the film removes every condition that makes the play's delivery fail. The Friar writes to a man in exile, by a single carrier, on a deadline set by a potion's forty-two hours, through a town sealing its doors against plague.

Sophie writes to a woman at a settled address, with no deadline at all, in a world of telephones, motor cars and return tickets, and the half-century that would be failure in the play's terms is, in the film's, merely the measure of how long the answer took to ripen. The tragedy runs on hours. The romance runs on decades. Each is the other's clock reversed.

There is one more correspondence in the play that the film quietly professionalizes, and it unlocks the secretaries' meaning. Juliet's original go-between is the Nurse, the tragedy's beloved, unreliable postal service in person: entrusted with the lovers' messages in Act 2, dilatory with them, and finally, in Act 3, Scene 5, withdrawn from the service altogether when her counsel turns pragmatic and Juliet dismisses her with the coldest line in the play's domestic register, "Ancient damnation!" The lovers' correspondence fails in the end partly because it always ran through amateurs, a nurse, a friar, a servant, each with a private agenda and a mortal body that plague or panic could seal indoors. The Club is the Nurse, reformed and incorporated: the same office, the carrying of love's messages by an older woman who has seen the world's arrangements, but regularized, multiplied, given a desk and a duty and a rule that the message is never dropped. The film's four secretaries, middle-aged and older women all, are the Nurse's descendants with the damnation removed. What Verona's tragedy lacked was not love or courage. It was a reliable intermediary profession. The film's Verona has one, and the difference in outcomes is the difference between the two works entire.

The balcony completes the pattern. The play's orchard scene is itself a correspondence conducted in person: Juliet, alone, speaks a soliloquy addressed to an absent man, "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2), and the man, hidden below, receives the message she never sent. Every letter in the courtyard wall repeats that structure, an address to someone who cannot hear it, left in the hope that the structure itself will do the work of delivery. The film's closing balcony reverses the orchard as it reverses the letter. Sophie, above, confesses a love she believes is already lost; Charlie, below, corrects the error of fact on which the despair rests; and the climb that in the play's world leads, by way of the ladder and the chamber, to the vault, leads in the film to a fall of a few feet into grass and laughter. Nobody in this Verona dies of a misunderstanding, because the film's whole discipline is the correction of misunderstandings by post, by telephone, by turning up in person. It is a world in which the mail works. Shakespeare's Verona is the world in which it does not, and the distance between those two worlds is the exact distance the film asks its audience to travel.

## Vanessa Redgrave and Franco Nero: The Reunion Inside the Film

Every account of the film, friendly or hostile, converges on the same observation: its centre of gravity is not the young couple it advertises but the old couple it discovers. Amanda Seyfried carries the point of view, and carries it capably, fresh from the success of Dear John and working in the open, level register the part requires. Christopher Egan, as Charlie, is set the thankless task of thawing on schedule, from prig to partner, across a dozen vineyards. Gael García Bernal plays Victor as a man in love with the contents of markets, a performance of appetite so complete that the character's neglect of Sophie reads as appetite's side effect rather than cruelty, which is precisely what the film needs: an engagement that ends not in villainy but in the recognition of mismatched devotions. These are competent arrangements. The event of the film is elsewhere.

Vanessa Redgrave was seventy-three when the picture appeared, and the role of Claire asks her to play the film's entire thesis in a single face: a woman who treated the great question of her life as settled for fifty years and discovers, from a stranger's handwriting, that it was only postponed. She plays the discovery without a trace of the whimsy the script keeps offering her. The search scenes give her a repeating structure, hope raised at a doorstep and hope withdrawn, and she varies the withdrawal each time, so that the audience watches something like evidence accumulating against the film's own promise. Franco Nero, as the Lorenzo finally found, has perhaps twenty minutes of screen time and the obligation to justify a fifty-year-old obsession in one afternoon. He does it with stillness. The reunion at the vineyard table works because neither performer reaches for the audience's tears; the tears arrive on schedule anyway, which the hostile reviews noticed and resented, and which the friendly ones noticed and forgave.

Beneath the fiction runs a documented second story, and the film knows it. Redgrave and Nero met in 1966 on the set of Camelot, where she was Guenevere and he was Lancelot. They fell in love; their son Carlo was born in 1969; the relationship ended, and each married other people, and in 2006, forty years after the meeting, they married each other. The casting of Nero as the Lorenzo of Claire's youth is therefore not a reunion staged for the camera. It is a reunion the camera was permitted to attend. The raw material of that resonance is unusually well documented. Camelot, Joshua Logan's film of the Lerner and Loewe musical, was shot in 1966 and released in 1967, with Richard Harris as Arthur, Redgrave as Guenevere and Nero, an Italian actor in his middle twenties fresh from the European westerns that had made his name, as Lancelot. The love the production required its two younger leads to simulate became, on the documented record, the thing itself. Their son Carlo Gabriel Nero was born in 1969. The couple's paths then ran apart for decades, through other marriages and other households, until the marriage of 2006 closed the interval at forty years. Nero's screen persona brings its own contribution to the reunion scenes: the stillness that serves Lorenzo so well is the quality the Italian western had built on, the gunfighter's economy of gesture transferred to a vintner who has waited half a century and sees no reason to hurry the last ten minutes. Redgrave's contribution is the reverse, a face that has spent a career, from her Academy Award for Julia onward, specializing in intelligence under emotional pressure. Between the western's patience and the tragedian's precision, the reunion at the vineyard table acquires a gravity neither the script nor the younger plot can supply, and the audience's knowledge of the off-screen record turns gravity into something close to documentary. The film did not have to argue that a fifty-year separation can end in an afternoon. It cast the proof.

Roger Ebert, reviewing the film, disclosed the ground of his response with a candour reviewers usually reserve for memoirs: the first film set he ever visited, before he was a critic at all, was Camelot in 1966, where he interviewed the not yet thirty Redgrave and the twenty-six-year-old Nero. Watching them play lovers separated for half a century and restored to each other stirred, he wrote, memories of his own life, and he declined to pretend otherwise. The autobiographical charge runs both ways. For the performers, the film re-enacts the shape of their own history, separation and late reunion, inside a fiction that softens it. For the audience that knows the history, every scene of Claire's search is doubled: the woman looking for Lorenzo is also Vanessa Redgrave, and the man at the end of the search is the man she actually found. Casting directors are rarely able to buy that kind of resonance. Here it arrived as a fact of the record.

### What song carries the film's argument about the play's ending?

The soundtrack's one indispensable cue is Taylor Swift's "Love Story", the hit single that had taken the play's names and rewritten its ending in advance: in the song's arc, the father's refusal softens, the suitor returns with a ring, and the tomb is traded for a proposal. Winick's film makes the same trade at feature length.

Placed over a story in which a lost love is found alive and a wedding replaces a funeral, the song functions as the picture's thesis in miniature, the play with its polarity reversed, sung over the credits of the prose version.

The film was also its director's last. Gary Winick, born in 1961, had begun as a pioneer of cheap digital features, a co-founder of the InDigEnt collective that set out to prove a film could be made for the price of a car; his Tadpole had carried that proof to festivals in 2002, and the studios had then absorbed him into the mainstream, where he directed 13 Going on 30 and Charlotte's Web with a craftsman's ease. Letters to Juliet, released in May 2010, was the final film of that career. Winick died on 27 February 2011, of a brain tumour, aged forty-nine, less than a year after the picture opened. The fact lends the film a retrospective gravity its makers cannot have intended: a story about a message answered after fifty years, directed by a man with months to live, whose own subject, across his independent and studio work alike, had been people running out of time in which to say the necessary thing. Winick's formation shows in the picture's method, and the method partly explains the divide in its reception. A director trained in the InDigEnt discipline, where a feature was an exercise in doing without, shoots a story rather than a budget: real streets rather than sets, an office that looks worked in, a courtyard used at its own scale. Set inside a studio production with stars and a Tuscan unit, that formation produced a film whose surfaces are plainer than its genre's gloss, even under Pontecorvo's golden light. The plainness reads, to a hostile eye, as television. To a sympathetic one it reads as trust in the material, a refusal to varnish a story whose premise, an answered letter, is already the most extravagant claim in the picture. Either way, the signature is legible, and it connects the farewell film to the proof-of-concept feature that began the career: both are arguments that the machinery of cinema matters less than the errand it runs.

The film does not know it is a farewell. The record knows, and the record forms part of the afterlife.

## Two Correspondents: Sophie, Claire and the Film's System of Messengers

The construction underneath the sentiment repays attention, because the film is built as a system of correspondents, and every character is defined by a relation to the mail. Claire is the correspondent manquée, the writer whose single letter defines her life by its failure to be answered; her arc is the receipt, at last, of the reply. Sophie is the correspondent by vocation, a professional verifier of other people's facts who wants to originate a text of her own; her arc is the writing of two documents, the reply to Claire and the article for her editor, and it is not accidental that her professional emancipation and Claire's romantic one are accomplished by the same sheet of paper. The reply is the making of both women. One of them receives it. The other signs it, in a borrowed name, and discovers that the signature she wants is her own.

Charlie and Victor complete the system as its two failures, symmetrically arranged. Victor is the correspondent of appetite: he communicates constantly, by telephone, in a stream of logistics, prices, producers and timings, and never once transmits the single message his fiancée is waiting for, which is that she has been noticed. His calls arrive throughout the Italian journey like dispatches from a war of groceries, vivid, urgent and addressed to no one. Charlie is the correspondent of refusal: a barrister, a professional arranger of other people's documents, who begins the film determined that the letter should never have been answered and that its errand is an imposition on the dead. His thaw is measured in postal terms. He moves from suppressing the correspondence to driving it, from mocking the search to conducting it, and his climactic act is the play's own gesture of delivery, the climb to the balcony, undertaken to carry a correction of fact to a woman standing above him with a false report in her head. He falls, as established, because the film protects its messengers. But the point of the climb is the delivery: Charlie reaches the balcony not to serenade but to amend the record, to tell Sophie that the Patricia of her despair is a cousin, which is to say that the message she acted on was incomplete. In a film about mail, even the love scene is an erratum.

The doubling of the heroines is the design's centre. Claire in 1957 is Sophie in the film's present: a young woman at the edge of a consequential choice, paralyzed between the life arranged for her and the life she wants, addressing her dilemma to Juliet because Juliet is the patron of exactly that paralysis. Sophie answering the letter is therefore answering, across fifty years, a version of herself, and the advice she writes, that it is never too late to follow the heart's instruction, is advice her own plot immediately requires her to take. The film's much-mocked earnestness is the sound of that circuit closing. Whether the circuit is a profound device or a greeting card's is the critical quarrel in miniature, and the quarrel is adjudicated in the reception section below. What cannot be disputed is the engineering: the film's two romances are one romance written twice, once in the past tense and once in the present, and the letter is the wire between them. Shakespeare's play has the same doubling, inverted: Juliet's feigned death is a message sent to the future that its intended reader, Romeo, never receives, while the explanation travels behind it and arrives at a vault. The play wires its two plots, the comedy of the feast and the tragedy of the tomb, through a correspondence that fails. The film wires its two plots through a correspondence that, at ruinous delay and against its institution's every rule, succeeds. The symmetry is the argument, and it is load-bearing: remove the letter, and neither work has a plot at all.

The system has a chorus, and the chorus is the office. The four secretaries appear in a handful of scenes, yet their function in the construction is out of proportion to their footage, for they are the film's licensed interpreters, the body authorized to say what the letters mean. It is the secretaries who receive Sophie, the secretaries who explain the rules of the house, the secretaries whose reading of the mail teaches the audience how to read Claire's letter when it surfaces: not as a curiosity but as a claim. In the play, that interpretive office belongs to Friar Laurence, the one man who knows the whole story and whose long final narration audits it for the Prince. The film distributes the Friar's office among four women in an office, none of whom knows any whole story, each of whom knows the rules of the correspondence entire. The change is the film's quiet thesis about its own tradition. The tragedy's knowledge is concentrated, priestly and fatally slow. The romance's knowledge is distributed, lay and quick, a network rather than a confessor. When the buried letter emerges, no single keeper of secrets must decide its fate in a cell at dawn. A committee of secretaries has already normalized the extraordinary, by answering the ordinary ten thousand times, and the extraordinary letter is processed by the same hands, at the same desks, under the same rule that nothing addressed to Juliet goes unanswered. The film's deepest difference from its source is not optimism. It is administration.

## Making Letters to Juliet: Production, Place and Release

The production was assembled with a discipline its script is often accused of lacking. Summit Entertainment, then riding the extraordinary commercial weather of its vampire franchise and building a slate around it, backed the picture through Applehead Pictures and Mark Canton's production company, with Canton, Caroline Kaplan and Ellen Barkin producing, at a budget of 30 million dollars, a modest figure for a two-country location shoot with an international cast. The modesty was strategic. A romance of this kind is not expected to open like a spectacle; it is expected to hold, weekend over weekend, on word of mouth among the audience it was built for, and a 30 million floor makes that arithmetic survivable. The arithmetic, as the reception record below shows, is exactly how the film behaved.

Casting did the rest of the positioning. Amanda Seyfried arrived carrying two audiences at once: the young readership of the Nicholas Sparks adaptation Dear John, released the same spring, and the older audience that had watched her hold scenes against Meryl Streep in Mamma Mia! two years earlier. Vanessa Redgrave supplied the prestige, and with Franco Nero the living history. Gael García Bernal supplied the international markets and the irony, since his presence let the production advertise a Latin American star in the role of the man the heroine must learn to leave. The Italian company around them was cast from the domestic profession rather than from the expatriate pool: Luisa Ranieri, Marina Massironi, Milena Vukotic and Lidia Biondi as the four secretaries give the office scenes the texture of a working Italian institution rather than a tour guide's performance of one, and the long tail of Lorenzos is played by Italian actors of the regions the search crosses. Marco Pontecorvo, the cinematographer, shot the picture for the golden register the hostile notices complained of, Tuscany and the Veneto rendered at permanent late afternoon; Andrea Guerra composed the score. The register is a decision, not an accident, and it divides the film's viewers as cleanly as its script does. To the prosecution it is falsification, a country that does not exist. To the defence it is the visual form of the film's premise, a world photographed as the lovelorn believe it to be when they write their letters.

Context sharpens the achievement the box office represents. The spring of 2010 was crowded with literary romance aimed at the same audience: Dear John, adapted from Nicholas Sparks, had opened in February on its way to some 80 million dollars in North America alone, and The Last Song, also from Sparks, arrived at the end of March. The market's lesson, as the trade press drew it, was that the audience for screen romance was young, female and loyal to a brand of author. Letters to Juliet entered that market with no bestselling novelist behind it, a source book of non-fiction that had sold respectably rather than enormously, and a premise anchored in a municipal institution in a foreign city. Its performance, third place on opening and a long hold thereafter, is the more creditable for the company it kept: the film demonstrated that the drawing power in the formula was not the novelist's brand but the ritual's, the one piece of intellectual property in the contest that no studio owned and no season could exhaust. Summit, which distributed all three pictures, was in the unusual position of selling the same audience its romance in competing wrappers and watching the wrapper made of a real tradition hold its own against the wrappers made of a famous name.

The geography of the shoot deserves its own sentence in the record, because the film's map is one of its arguments. The Verona material is Verona: the courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta, the streets and piazze of the old city, shot on location rather than rebuilt. The search then leaves the city and unspools across the Tuscan hills, through Siena and the vineyard country, so that the film's structure becomes a tour of the two Italies the tradition owns, the urban shrine and the rural idyll, joined by a small car and a list of seventy-four names. The picture premiered as a gala at the Tribeca Film Festival on 25 April 2010 and opened across North America on 14 May, in some 2,900 cinemas, counter-programmed against the second weekend of Iron Man 2 and the opening of Robin Hood: three films dividing the audience by appetite, spectacle, legend and romance. It finished third and outlasted the legend. Running time is 105 minutes; the classification PG, a certificate that tells its own story about the film's design, since the play it reverses cannot be staged for children without apology. The romance was engineered, at every level from budget to certificate, to be the version of the story a grandmother and a granddaughter could attend together, and the ending of its plot, two weddings in prospect and nobody dead, is only the most visible piece of that engineering.

The soundtrack performs the same engineering in miniature, and its track listing is a document of the production's double address. For the American audience the album leads with Colbie Caillat, whose "You Got Me" opens the record and whose "What If" closes the film's account of itself, and it carries the Taylor Swift cue that ties the picture to the play's pop afterlife. For the Italian audience it is stocked with the domestic catalogue: 883's "Un giorno così", Zero Assoluto's "Per dimenticare", Malika Ayane's "Sospesa", Caterina Caselli's "Sono bugiarda" and Fred Buscaglione's "Guarda che luna", standards and modern hits interleaved so that the film sounds, in its home market, like a native production rather than a visiting one. Andrea Guerra's score threads the two repertoires together. The design is the film's method applied to music: an American frame, an Italian interior, and a single Shakespearean cue doing the work of a title. A production that had treated Italy as backdrop would have scored Italy as backdrop. This one scored Italy as a co-author, which is one more reason the city and the Club received the picture as something other than an intrusion. The romance was exported worldwide, but it was manufactured, deliberately, as a joint production of the two cultures the letter tradition joins: the country that wrote the play's most famous address, and the country that writes back.

## What the Critics Said About Letters to Juliet

The critical reception divides with unusual neatness into two questions argued as if they were one. The first question is whether the film is well made. On that question the consensus was severe and consistent. Rotten Tomatoes recorded approval from 42 per cent of 161 reviews, with a consensus summary that granted the picture a "refreshingly earnest romantic charm" and charged it with "limp dialogue and an utter lack of surprises." Metacritic averaged the notices at 50 in 100. Nick Schager, in Slant Magazine, prosecuted the case in its purest form: the film, he wrote, props up "cornball fairy-tale romanticism" with its Shakespeare reference, its story is of "dime store novel-quality," its heroine prototypically bland and its hero laughably phony. Peter Bradshaw in The Guardian called the picture "cheerfully ridiculous," taxed Seyfried and Egan with their wandering accents, and exempted only Redgrave, whose performance he found "likably, if not quite intentionally mad." Bill Gibron, at PopMatters, argued that the film contains two stories of which only one is interesting, and that even the scenery looks filtered past plausibility, no nation being that golden.

The trade and festival notices filed the same charges in cooler language. Variety, reviewing the picture at its Tribeca premiere, called it a syrupy Italy-set romantic comedy and concluded that its two leading women, the indomitable femmes of the notice's phrase, carry the day and the film between them: the machinery faulted, the carriers exempted. Screen Daily judged it a romantic fantasy aimed principally at teenage girls, with a secondary claim on adult women possessed of a high tolerance for cliché, a sentence that manages to be a market forecast disguised as a dismissal, and an accurate one. Between the daily reviewers' scorn and the trades' shrug, the shape of the consensus is consistent: a film whose premise everyone admired, whose execution the profession respected only in its two senior performers, and whose intended audience the profession described with a condescension the audience itself declined to share.

The second question is whether the film works, which is a different inquiry, and on that question the defence is narrower, warmer and better credentialed than the prosecution expected. Ebert gave the picture three stars of four and surrendered in print: "I know the ending is preordained from the setup. I know the characters are broad and comforting stereotypes. In this case, I simply don't care. Sometimes we have personal reasons for responding to a film." He praised countryside photography he called unimaginably beautiful, and he closed his account by invoking Pauline Kael's discipline, that a critic goes to a movie, and the movie happens, and the review records what changed within the critic. Amy Biancolli, in the San Francisco Chronicle, built the craft case for the defence on Redgrave alone: an "elegant, clear-eyed and nurturing" performance that "commands the corniest dialogue to stand up and sound like poetry." Elizabeth Weitzman, in the New York Daily News, made the same exemption do double duty, crediting Redgrave with "a lovely gravity to the lightweight proceedings" and Seyfried with an unusually levelheaded presence.

Adjudication requires separating the questions the reviewers merged. On dialogue, construction and surprise, Schager and Bradshaw are right, and Ebert concedes their entire case in his first three sentences. The screenplay's conversations do stop for slogans; the search's outcome is never in doubt; the Patricia misunderstanding at the wedding is a contrivance of the kind the picture elsewhere avoids. Where the prosecution overreaches is in treating those findings as a verdict on the film's worth, because the film's genre contract never promised surprise. It promised delivery: of a letter, of a lover, of a wedding, of the audience's prepared emotion at the prepared moment. Measured against that contract, the evidence is the audience record. CinemaScore's polled audiences graded the film A minus. The picture opened third in North America, behind Iron Man 2 and Robin Hood, with 13.5 million dollars, and then held: a fall of only a third in its second weekend, on its way to 53 million dollars at home and just over 79 million worldwide, against a production budget of 30 million, by Box Office Mojo's accounting. Romance audiences were not deceived about what the film was. They were satisfied with what it was, in numbers that turned a modest production into a clear commercial success.

One charge in the prosecution's case deserves a fuller hearing before judgment, because it identifies the film's real structural gamble. Gibron's complaint, that the picture contains two stories of which only one is interesting, is the most intelligent version of the negative case. The young plot, Sophie and Charlie sparring across Tuscany while an engagement decays by telephone, is assembled from the period's standard parts, and no performance in it surprises. The old plot, Claire's search, carries the film's entire claim on the memory. The defence usually offered, that the young plot is the price of the old, concedes too much. The construction is better understood as a relay, and relays are judged by the handover. Sophie's story exists to deliver the audience to Claire's, as the reply exists to deliver Claire to Lorenzo; the young woman is the letter's carrier, and carriers in this tradition are not required to be the most interesting person in the story, only the most faithful. Friar John is not interesting either. What matters is whether the message arrives, and Sophie's plot, for all its standard parts, is engineered so that every one of its beats, the stalled career, the failed engagement, the mother's desertion revealed at the graveside, loads the carrier with a reason to persist that the audience can feel. The film's gamble is that persistence can be dramatized as attractively as passion. The box office says the gamble succeeded with its audience. The reviews say it failed with the profession. Both measurements are accurate, and the disagreement between them is the precise shape of the film's reputation: a picture the trade found ordinary doing a thing the trade had not thought to value, carrying a message the long way round and arriving, like its own heroine, improbably intact.

The scholarly frame settles the quarrel from a third direction. Eleonora Oggiano, writing in Multicultural Shakespeare in 2021 on "The Shakespeare Brand in Contemporary 'Fair Verona'", treats the film neither as drama nor as fraud but as infrastructure: one of the formats in which Verona manufactures and maintains what she calls its R&J-influenced spaces, the civic, cultural and narrative territories generated by the myth and elaborated for the tourism market. On Oggiano's account, the play in Verona behaves as a brand behaves, and the film is a brand extension that enlarged the brand's territory. The frame is unsentimental to the point of chill, and it explains the evidence better than either camp of reviewers. It explains why the picture's aesthetic ceiling did not limit its effect, why the mail rose after a film the critics had dismissed, and why the city that hosts the ritual treats the romance as an asset rather than an embarrassment. The verdict of this article follows the evidence in that order: as cinema, a minor and frequently clumsy work; as an event in the play's afterlife, a major one; and the second judgment is the one this series exists to make.

## Film Against Fact: The Movie's Secretaries and the Documented Practice

The complication the brief for this article insists on, and the point on which the film is most often misdescribed in both directions, is the line between the movie's Verona and the documented one. Admirers speak as though the picture were a documentary with dialogue. Detractors speak as though the secretaries themselves were a screenwriter's fancy. The record supports neither. The institution is real, older than the film's makers, and independently documented by the Friedmans six years before the screenplay was shot; the plot is invented entire. The honest method is to take the film element by element and grade each against the record, which is the work of the InsightCrunch Letters note set out below.

### Does the film show the real courtyard where visitors leave their letters?

Yes. The production shot in Verona, and the courtyard sequences use the Casa di Giulietta itself, the courtyard beneath the balcony in Via Cappello, with its statue and its walls carrying the visitors' messages. The statue and the balcony in the frame are the courtyard's own.

What the film inherits with the location is a constructed shrine: the house's own biography is a twentieth-century act of civic imagination, as [Casa di Giulietta and the balcony that was added to it](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/) demonstrates in full. The film's courtyard is therefore doubly framed, a real place playing a fictional girl's home, photographed by a fiction about the real letters the place attracts.

Begin with what the film keeps. The secretaries exist, and the film's portrait of them is faithful in structure if compressed in scale: a corps of women volunteers, multilingual, who collect the letters left at the house and left in the city's dedicated post boxes, who read them, translate where necessary and answer each one that carries an address, personally and by hand, in Juliet's name. The Friedmans documented the practice in detail, down to the sorting of the mail and the tone of the replies, counsel rather than therapy, signed by a girl who never lived on behalf of women who answer in shifts. The film shows the collection at dusk, the office, the reading aloud, the handwriting. It shows four secretaries where the real corps is larger and turns over across the week. It shows the writers leaving letters in the courtyard wall, which matches the practice the city has spent years trying to civilize, since messages fixed to the masonry with adhesive and chewing gum damaged the fabric and had to be channelled onto removable panels. On the institution's skeleton, the film is a reliable witness.

The flesh is invented. No secretary answers a letter fifty years old, because the system's whole discipline is that letters do not wait; the buried envelope behind the loose stone is a gothic device, the lost letter of melodrama, imported into an institution whose pride is that nothing is lost. No visiting fact checker joins the corps for an afternoon and is handed the archive's deepest secret. No reply from the office has ever, in the documented record, summoned its writer across an ocean to resume a love affair in person; the replies are endings, not beginnings, a stranger's kindness posted back into a stranger's life. And the film's Claire, a writer of one letter in 1957, stands in for a population the book portrays more truthfully: thousands of writers a year, most of them young, most of them writing about loves that are present and failing rather than absent and lost. The film needs the exceptional letter. The tradition is made of unexceptional ones, and its distinction lies precisely there, in the refusal to rank the questions.

A further distinction runs through the film's treatment of the writers themselves. On screen, the letter writers are encountered in one place, the courtyard, in one posture, weeping at a wall, and in one demographic, young women in the first crisis of love. The camera needs them visible and simultaneous, a chorus at the shrine. The Friedmans' writers are elsewhere: at kitchen tables and office desks on every continent, of every age from schoolchild to great-grandparent, writing in dozens of languages, and reaching Verona by post rather than by pilgrimage, most of them never seeing the city at all. The book's geography of the tradition is planetary and domestic; the film's is local and photogenic. The difference is not a falsification so much as a change of instrument, the book being able to range where a camera must stand. But it has one consequence worth naming. The film teaches its audience to look for the tradition at the wall, where the writers are at their most theatrical and least typical, and to overlook the postbag, where the tradition actually lives. Visitors who arrive in Verona asking to see the letters being written are looking for the film's scene. The office, where the letters are answered, is the book's scene, and it is the one that matters. Grading the film against the record means keeping those two scenes distinct, and crediting each work with the one it owns.

The InsightCrunch Letters note grades the exchange element by element. Its purpose is not to convict the film of lying, which no romance can be convicted of, but to fix the exchange rate between the two currencies, so that a reader who meets the tradition through the film knows what the film paid for its plot and what it received for free.

## The Feedback Loop: What the Film Did to Verona's Letters and Verona's Visitors

The series thesis for this cluster of articles holds that the modern cliche separates the play from the tourism, treating the balcony, the statue and the letter wall as a commercial crust over a literary monument. The film is the strongest counter-evidence the record offers, because in its case the loop can be traced in both directions with dates attached. The play generated the ritual: a tomb visited, a custodian answering, a club chartered, a literature of counsel accumulating across seven decades. The ritual generated the film: the Friedmans' chronicle of 2006 carried the practice to a New York publisher's list, and from the publisher's list to a producer, a screenplay and a location shoot. The film then generated more ritual. That third leg is the one that requires care, because its evidence is real but uneven, and the grading matters more than the claim.

What is documented firmly is the film's reach. It earned just over 79 million dollars worldwide on a 30 million budget, held its audience across its run, and carried the courtyard, the wall and the secretaries into cinemas in every market Summit Entertainment served, with Vanessa Redgrave's face as the tradition's poster. The Friedmans' book returned to print as a tie-in in the film's release month. Verona's own chroniclers of the brand, Oggiano among them, list the film among the formats through which the city elaborates the myth for the tourism market, and the Club's officers, in the interviews that followed the release, described a steep rise in the volume of mail and in visitors asking for the secretaries by name. That rise is reported rather than audited: the Club does not publish verified annual counts, and the figures that circulate in travel coverage, which run from thousands to tens of thousands of letters a year, descend from the Club's own estimates at different dates. The honest statement is the narrower one. Before the film, the practice was a traveller's discovery, famous among the readers of one admired book. After the film, it was a destination with a story attached, and the story had a plot a visitor could step into: leave a letter, and be answered; or, in the film's stronger magic, be answered fifty years late and be healed. Travel writing in the film's wake repeats the sequence so uniformly that the film, rather than the book or the Club, became the tradition's standard introduction for the English-speaking visitor.

The tradition the film inherited had already migrated once, and the migration explains the film's choice of shrine. The letters began at the tomb. By the early nineteenth century, on the Friedmans' account, the site identified as Juliet's grave outside the walls drew pilgrims in increasing numbers, visitors whose devotion ran to carrying away fragments of the crumbling sarcophagus as keepsakes or jewellery. The correspondence gathered where the body was supposed to lie: the dead girl received her mail at her grave, and the custodian who began answering it in the 1930s, Ettore Solimani, was the tomb's guardian, not the house's. Across the twentieth century the cult's centre of gravity shifted across the city, from the tomb in the former Franciscan monastery to the courtyard in Via Cappello, as the house acquired its balcony, its statue and its museum and became the shrine the world's visitors could find. The mail moved with the cult. The film, faithful to the tradition's mature geography, stages its letters at the house and never visits the tomb at all, a choice so complete that most of its audience never learns the letters had another home. The migration matters to the verdict because it shows the tradition behaving exactly as the film does: following the audience, consolidating at the site of greatest emotional convenience, and letting the older shrine keep the scholars. The film did not vulgarize a pure practice. It arrived late in a practice that had been adapting itself to its public for two centuries, and it adapted the practice once more, into the medium that public had moved to.

The mechanism of the loop is worth stating precisely, because it is the film's most durable effect and the least visible. The address is the mechanism. The Friedmans' publisher described the mail as arriving "frequently addressed simply, 'Juliet, Verona'", and the phrase records a functioning wonder of the postal age: a letter with no surname, no street and no code, directed to a fictional person in a real city, reaches a real desk and is answered. The Italian postal service, the city's custodians and the Club between them operate a standing agreement with the world's imagination, that this one impossible address is valid. The film grasped the address's power instinctively. Its buried letter is addressed the same way, to Juliet at the house, and its plot is the address working at maximum range, across fifty-three years. After the film, the address itself became famous, and fame, for an address, is traffic. Each new writer who learned from the film that "Juliet, Verona" is deliverable became a user of the system the film depicts, which is the strict definition of a feedback loop: the representation recruits for the reality, and the reality, answering by hand, confirms the representation's central claim, that the letters are answered. On the evidence available, graded as reported rather than audited, the film is the largest single recruiting instrument the tradition has ever had, larger than the book that fathered it and larger than the shrine that houses it.

### How much mail did the tradition answer each year before the film?

The Friedmans, writing in 2006 from inside the Club's office, describe a flow of thousands of letters a year, arriving from every continent, frequently addressed with nothing more than "Juliet, Verona," and every one of them answered by hand where an address was given.

No audited series of annual totals exists in the public record, and larger round numbers quoted in later travel coverage should be read as the Club's estimates rather than as counts. What the pre-film record establishes is scale enough: a volunteer office handling the love letters of the world as routine correspondence.

The loop's final turn is the one the film could not have designed. A visitor who comes to Verona because of the film leaves a letter because of the ritual, and the letter is answered by a secretary because of an ethic established in the 1930s, by hand, in the name of the girl in the play. The film's invented letter, the impossible one from 1957, thereby produced real letters by the thousand: fiction performing the office of the fact. The traffic sustains more than the Club. It feeds the hotels, the guides, the courtyard economy reckoned in [Verona's economy built on two fictional lovers](/2029/07/27/verona-economy-fictional-lovers/), and it redraws the visitor's map of the city around sites the film taught the world to read, the geography surveyed in [Romeo's house and Verona's tourist geography](/2029/07/20/romeos-house-verona-tourism/). Romeo's claim that there is no world without Verona walls has become, by this route, a statement of economic fact for the city that quotes it.

There is a last turn of the screw, and it is the film's neatest piece of self-knowledge. Sophie's deliverable, in the plot, is not only the reply. It is the article she writes about the search, the text her editor accepts, which launches the career the fact-checking desk had denied her. The letter therefore generates a second text about itself, and the second text is what the world reads. The film the audience watches is the third text in the chain, the article's expansion into pictures; the Friedmans' book is the zeroth, the chronicle that made the chain possible; and the chain's first link is a play in which a letter fails and is still being read aloud, nightly, four centuries on, before audiences who write letters of their own on the way home. Every text in the sequence is a reply to the one before it. The play is answered by the ritual, the ritual by the book, the book by the film, and the film, in the years after its release, by the sacks of new mail addressed in fresh hands to a girl who never lived, each one asking, in its own words, the question Claire asked in 1957. The tradition's real medium was never paper. It is the reply, and the reply is still being written.

The verdict, then, in full. Letters to Juliet is a flawed romance and a formidable adaptation, provided adaptation is understood as this series understands it: not the copying of a plot but the transmission of a work's energies into a new form. The energy it transmits is the play's most neglected one. Critics of the tragedy speak of its haste, its fevers, its poetry. The film speaks for its plumbing, the messages and messengers on which everything silently depends, and it makes the case, across two hours of Tuscan light, that the difference between tragedy and comedy in Verona was never the lovers. It was the postal service. A letter failed, and two children died in a vault. A letter succeeded, fifty years late, and two old people married in a vineyard. Between those two deliveries lies the whole afterlife of the play, and this film, for all its corn, is the afterlife's clearest single document. That is the standing assigned to it here: ahead of finer films that noticed less, behind none that noticed more. And the letters, by every account the Club has given of its postbag, kept arriving.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the film Letters to Juliet about?

The film tells of a young American fact checker, Sophie Hall, who finds a fifty-year-old unanswered letter in the wall of Juliet's courtyard in Verona, answers it, and joins the letter's elderly writer, Claire, and her grandson Charlie in a search across Tuscany for Claire's lost Lorenzo. Gary Winick directed the picture, released in 2010, with Amanda Seyfried, Vanessa Redgrave, Christopher Egan, Gael García Bernal and Franco Nero. Beneath the romance plot runs the film's true subject: the real Verona practice of answering letters addressed to Shakespeare's heroine, and the reversal by which a late letter saves the people the play's failed letter doomed.

### Q: Who directed Letters to Juliet, and when was it released?

Gary Winick directed the film, which premiered as a gala presentation at the Tribeca Film Festival on 25 April 2010 and opened across North America on 14 May 2010. Winick, born in 1961, had begun as a pioneer of low-budget digital features and a co-founder of the InDigEnt collective before moving into studio pictures such as 13 Going on 30 and Charlotte's Web. Letters to Juliet was the final film of his career. He died on 27 February 2011, aged forty-nine, less than a year after the picture reached cinemas, a fact that has coloured the film's afterlife ever since.

### Q: Who wrote the screenplay for Letters to Juliet?

José Rivera and Tim Sullivan wrote the screenplay, adapting the world documented in the Friedmans' non-fiction book into an invented plot. Rivera was the senior literary figure of the production: his screenplay for The Motorcycle Diaries had made him the first Puerto Rican screenwriter nominated for an Academy Award, and that film had starred Gael García Bernal, who appears in Letters to Juliet as the heroine's neglectful fiancé Victor. The screenplay's inventions include Sophie, Claire, Charlie and the buried letter of 1957. Its inheritance from the book includes the courtyard, the wall, the secretaries and the discipline of the handwritten reply.

### Q: Is Letters to Juliet based on a book?

Yes, on a work of non-fiction rather than a novel. Letters to Juliet: Celebrating Shakespeare's Greatest Heroine, the Magical City of Verona, and the Power of Love, by Lise Friedman and Ceil Friedman, appeared from Stewart, Tabori and Chang in New York in 2006. The 176-page illustrated volume prints more than seventy-five genuine letters from the Verona mail and chronicles the volunteers who answer them, a practice the book traces across more than seven decades. A paperback edition followed in May 2010 as a film tie-in, an instance of the adaptation economy running in both directions between the two works of the same title.

### Q: Who plays Sophie, Charlie, Victor, Claire and Lorenzo in Letters to Juliet?

Amanda Seyfried plays Sophie Hall, the fact checker who answers the buried letter. Christopher Egan plays Charlie Wyman, Claire's barrister grandson, who begins as the search's chief skeptic. Gael García Bernal plays Victor, Sophie's fiancé, a chef whose devotion to his coming restaurant empties their engagement of attention. Vanessa Redgrave plays Claire Smith, the letter's writer, and Franco Nero plays Lorenzo Bartolini, the lost love found at the end of the search. The four secretaries of Juliet are played by Italian performers: Luisa Ranieri, Marina Massironi, Milena Vukotic and Lidia Biondi, with Oliver Platt appearing as Sophie's editor in New York.

### Q: What is the 1957 letter at the centre of Letters to Juliet?

The letter is the screenplay's central invention: a young Englishwoman, Claire Smith, who had fallen in love with an Italian farm worker named Lorenzo Bartolini, wrote to Juliet in 1957 after her courage failed at the hour appointed for their elopement, asking whether she had done wrong and what she ought to do. The letter was left in the courtyard wall, sealed behind a loose stone, and never collected, so that it waited unanswered for fifty-three years until Sophie found it. No such letter is documented in the Juliet Club's archive or in the Friedmans' book. The device compresses an institution's whole ethic into a single object.

### Q: How many men named Lorenzo Bartolini must the searchers visit in the film?

Seventy-four. The film states that the telephone records for the region hold seventy-four men of the name, and the search that Claire, Charlie and Sophie conduct across Tuscany works through the list: farmers, grandfathers, men too young and men too old, and one Lorenzo Bartolini who has died, whose grave supplies the romance with its single scene of mourning. The true Lorenzo is found last, through his grandson, at the family's vineyard. The number is the film's disguised portrait of the secretaries' real labour, the patient processing of a multitude in which any one file may conceal a destiny.

### Q: Is the story told in Letters to Juliet a true story?

The setting is true and the story is invented. The secretaries of Juliet exist, the letters arrive in their thousands, and every letter carrying an address is answered by hand, all as Lise Friedman and Ceil Friedman documented in their 2006 chronicle. Sophie, Claire, Charlie, Victor and the buried letter of 1957 are the screenplay's creations, and no documented reply from the Club's office has ever reunited two lovers in person. One element of the fiction rests on a documented fact stranger than the plot: Vanessa Redgrave and Franco Nero, who play the reunited lovers, met on Camelot in 1966, had a son, parted, and married each other in 2006.

### Q: How does the ending of Letters to Juliet rewrite the balcony scene?

The film ends at Claire and Lorenzo's wedding, where Charlie climbs a vine toward the balcony from which Sophie has just confessed her love and delivered her farewell, and falls a short distance into the grass, unhurt, to be kissed on the ground among the guests. The staging quotes the play's orchard scene and reverses its trajectory: in the tragedy, the climb to the balcony begins the sequence that ends in the vault, while in the film the fall from the balcony is the happy ending itself. A misunderstanding over a woman named Patricia, a cousin mistaken for a former girlfriend, is corrected in person and in time, the film's whole postal ethic enacted in a single gag.

### Q: How much money did Letters to Juliet make at the box office?

The film earned just over 79 million dollars worldwide against a production budget of 30 million, by Box Office Mojo's accounting, with 53 million of the total from North America. It opened in third place, behind Iron Man 2 and Robin Hood, with 13.5 million dollars, then held unusually well, falling by only a third in its second weekend, the pattern of a romance sustained by word of mouth. Audiences polled by CinemaScore graded the picture A minus. The performance is the commercial half of the evidence in the critics' quarrel: a film the profession largely dismissed returned nearly three times its production cost and satisfied the audience it was built for.

### Q: What did Roger Ebert say about Letters to Juliet?

Ebert gave the film three stars of four and defended it on openly personal grounds. He praised countryside photography he called unimaginably beautiful, and then surrendered the professional case in advance: "I know the ending is preordained from the setup. I know the characters are broad and comforting stereotypes. In this case, I simply don't care. Sometimes we have personal reasons for responding to a film." His review, titled "Reading the memories between the lines," disclosed the reason. The first film set he had ever visited was Camelot in 1966, where he interviewed the young Vanessa Redgrave and Franco Nero, whose reunion on screen stirred his own romantic memories.

### Q: Why did Roger Ebert take Letters to Juliet personally?

Because the film's fiction ran parallel to a history he had witnessed at its start. As a young journalist in 1966, before his career as a critic, Ebert visited the set of Camelot and interviewed Vanessa Redgrave, not yet thirty, and Franco Nero, twenty-six, as they fell in love playing Guenevere and Lancelot. The couple's son was born in 1969; the relationship ended; and in 2006 Redgrave and Nero married each other, forty years after the meeting. Watching the two of them play lovers separated for half a century and restored, Ebert wrote, stirred memories of romantic episodes in his own life, and he invoked Pauline Kael's principle that a review records what changed within the critic.

### Q: Are Vanessa Redgrave and Franco Nero a couple in real life?

Yes, and their history is the film's underground river. Redgrave and Nero met in 1966 during the filming of Camelot, in which she played Guenevere and he played Lancelot, and fell in love. Their son, Carlo Gabriel Nero, was born in 1969. The relationship ended, each married other people, and in 2006, forty years after the first meeting, Redgrave and Nero married each other. Letters to Juliet cast him as the Lorenzo of her character's youth, the man lost in 1957 and found in old age, so that the reunion the plot imagines is one the performers had already lived in a longer and less tidy form.

### Q: Was Letters to Juliet the last film Gary Winick directed?

Yes. Letters to Juliet, released in May 2010, was the final film of Gary Winick's career. Winick died on 27 February 2011 of a brain tumour, aged forty-nine, less than a year after the premiere. His career had run from the digital independent sector, where he co-founded the InDigEnt collective and made Tadpole in 2002, to the Hollywood mainstream of 13 Going on 30 and Charlotte's Web. The obituaries remembered him as a pioneer of inexpensive digital production as much as a director of comedies. The farewell lends the film a retrospective weight: a story about a message answered just in time, made by a director whose own time was nearly gone.

### Q: Where in Italy was Letters to Juliet filmed?

The production shot on location in the two Italies the tradition owns. The Verona material uses the city itself, including the courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta in Via Cappello, with its statue and its letter-covered walls, and the streets and piazze of the old town. The search then moves south into Tuscany, through the country around Siena and the vineyard landscapes where the seventy-four Lorenzos are visited and the right one is found. Marco Pontecorvo photographed the picture in the golden register the hostile reviews complained of and the friendly ones praised: Verona and Tuscany at permanent late afternoon, the Italy of the lovelorn imagination rendered as documentary fact.

### Q: What did the critics' consensus find weak in Letters to Juliet?

The consensus charged the film with limp dialogue and a total absence of surprises. Rotten Tomatoes recorded approval from 42 per cent of 161 reviews, while granting the picture a refreshingly earnest romantic charm; Metacritic averaged the notices at 50 in 100. Nick Schager, in Slant Magazine, called the story dime-store in quality and its romanticism cornball. Peter Bradshaw, in The Guardian, called the film cheerfully ridiculous and taxed its young leads with their accents, while exempting Vanessa Redgrave. Bill Gibron, at PopMatters, argued that the picture contains two stories of which only one is interesting. The defence rested on Redgrave, on the scenery and on the audience record rather than on the script.

### Q: How does Letters to Juliet reverse the failed letter of the play?

Point for point. In the play, Friar Laurence's letter explaining Juliet's feigned death is sealed inside a plague house with Friar John and never reaches Mantua, while Balthasar's false report of her death travels at speed, and Romeo dies on the strength of the wrong message. In the film, a letter asking Juliet's counsel waits fifty-three years and is then answered completely: its writer is found, her question is honoured, and the answer reunites her with the man she lost. Shakespeare's Verona is a world in which the true message fails and the false one flies. Winick's Verona is the same city with its postal polarity reversed, and the difference converts the tragedy into a wedding.

### Q: Which parts of the film's picture of Juliet's secretaries match the documented practice?

The structure matches; the scale is compressed and the plot is invented. The Friedmans document a volunteer corps that collects the letters left for Juliet, reads them, translates where necessary and answers each one carrying an address, personally and by hand, in Juliet's name. The film shows exactly that skeleton: the collection at dusk, the office, the reading aloud, the handwritten replies, performed by four secretaries where the real corps is larger. The film departs from the record in its events. No secretary answers a letter fifty years old, no visitor joins the corps for an afternoon, and no documented reply has ever brought a writer back to Verona to resume a love affair in person.
