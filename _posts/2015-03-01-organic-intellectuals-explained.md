Few terms in the vocabulary of Marxist theory have travelled as far from their original meaning as organic intellectuals. In education faculties, in cultural studies, and in a great deal of activist writing, the phrase now serves as a compliment: it praises the scholar who came from a poor background, the academic who marches with movements, the writer whose sympathies lie with the marginalised. Each of those usages answers a question about a person, where they started in life or where their loyalties sit, and each therefore misses the thing the concept was built to name. In the prison notebooks of Antonio Gramsci, the category answers a question about a position inside a structure: what organising work is being performed, and for which class is it performed? The distance between the two usages is total. On the functional definition, a corporate lawyer and an industrial engineer are organic intellectuals of capital in exactly the sense the term specifies, needing no apology and no inverted commas. A professor born into a mining family may be nothing of the kind, whatever the compliment intends.

That is the correction this article exists to make, and it is worth making slowly, because the misreading is not a harmless looseness. A concept that once analysed how classes organise themselves, how they acquire coherence and an awareness of their own function, has been converted into a badge of personal identity, and a badge cannot do analytical work. Once the term means a clever person of humble origin, it can no longer tell you that every class, including the dominant one, is elaborated by its own intellectual stratum, or that the stratum a class inherits from an older order behaves differently from the stratum it creates. The pages that follow restore the functional criterion piece by piece: the full definition, a test that can be applied to any occupation, a grid that classifies eight real positions, the traditional intellectuals whose claim to independence the notebooks treated as both illusion and social fact, the party understood as a collective intellectual, the breadth objection stated at full strength, and the concept's later career in education theory, where the biographical reading took hold most firmly.

## Organic intellectuals and the misreading to correct

The popular usage deserves a fair description before it is corrected, because it is held by serious people and grew from real features of Gramsci's text. He wrote as a revolutionary about the intellectuals a rising class would need, and he wrote warmly of the need for intellectuals who were not a caste apart from the class whose life they organised. Read quickly, and read in translation through several layers of secondary literature, the notebooks can seem to celebrate a particular kind of person: the thinking worker, the organiser with dirt under the fingernails, the intellectual who has not been alienated from the shop floor by a university. Add the genuine pathos of Gramsci's own biography, a Sardinian of modest means writing in a fascist prison, and the biographical reading assembles itself almost without effort. The term then migrates into usage as an honorific, a way of saying that somebody's thought is rooted, authentic, grown from the soil of a community rather than imposed on it from above.

Three distinct forms of the error recur, and separating them helps the correction land. The first form treats organic as a compliment, a word for intellectual work the speaker approves of, so that allies receive the label and opponents are denied it. The second treats organic as a certificate of origin, so that the label follows the biography: working class parentage confers it, a privileged upbringing withholds it, and the person's actual work drops out of the classification entirely. The third treats the term as a synonym for activist, so that commitment and presence at demonstrations settle the question that function was meant to settle. The three forms often arrive together, folded into a single warm usage, and they share a common root. All of them classify persons by their attributes. The notebooks classify positions by their work.

The word organic itself misleads here, because in ordinary speech it suggests the natural, the unforced, the grown rather than the made. Gramsci's use belongs to a different register. A stratum is organic to a class the way an organ belongs to an organism: it is elaborated from within the class's own development, it performs functions the class cannot do without, and it has no existence independent of the whole that produced it. The metaphor is structural, not pastoral. Nothing in it praises or blames. An organ can serve a body whose purposes the anatomist detests, and the classification proceeds in exactly the same way. Gramsci was explicit that the bourgeoisie, long before any working class organised itself, elaborated its own organic intellectuals on a grand scale, and the analysis of how it did so occupies some of the most concrete pages of the notebooks. A reader who reserves the term for the left has therefore not simplified the concept but amputated it, and the amputation is the first thing the function test repairs.

### Is an organic intellectual just a working-class academic?

No. The category is functional, not biographical. An organic intellectual performs the organising and technical work a class requires as it develops, and that work includes the engineers, corporate lawyers and managers who act for capital. Where a person comes from settles nothing; the organising function is what defines the position.

Consider what the biographical reading cannot see. It cannot see the management consultant, whose entire occupation consists of reorganising other people's labour for capital, as an intellectual at all, because the consultant writes no books and holds no chair. It cannot see the trade union official who left school at fifteen as performing intellectual work of a high organising order, because the official lacks credentials. It cannot distinguish between two professors in the same department, one of whom spends a career elaborating the worldview of a class in formation while the other administers an inherited discipline in undisturbed routine, because their biographies and titles are identical. In each case the biographical reading asks the wrong question and gets a confident, useless answer. The functional reading asks what the position does, whom the doing serves, and whether the served class created the position or inherited it, and on those questions the consultant, the official, and the two professors sort themselves immediately.

There is a forum question that surfaces whenever this concept is taught, usually phrased as a request for personal classification: am I an organic intellectual, and is my professor a traditional one? The honest answer the notebooks support is that the question is malformed until it is translated. Translated, it asks what organising, connecting, and elaborating work your activity performs, for which class that work is organic, and whether your position was created by that class's development or inherited from an earlier formation. A person can perform the function for a class they were not born into, and a person can fail to perform it for the class they were. The category is indifferent to the answers that flatter or embarrass, which is precisely what makes it worth keeping in its original sharpness.

## The definition: function before biography

The notebooks approach the category through a preliminary distinction that most popular accounts quote and then ignore. Gramsci observes that intellectual activity is universal: every human being thinks, connects, elaborates a conception of the world, and in that thin sense every person is an intellectual. The sentence is famous, usually quoted as 'all men are intellectuals', and it is almost always quoted alone, which inverts its purpose. In the argument of the twelfth notebook it is a ground clearing move, not a finding. Its continuation supplies the actual distinction, between having intellectual activity and exercising the social function of an intellectual: 'but not all men have in society the function of intellectuals'. Everything in the category flows from the second clause. Intellectuals, as a stratum, are the people whose position in the division of labour consists of organising, administering, elaborating, and connecting, and the question the theory asks about them is never whether they are clever but what their organising connects, and on whose behalf.

### What separates intellectual activity from the intellectual function?

Activity belongs to everyone, since every person thinks, generalises, and holds some conception of the world, however unexamined. The function belongs only to those whose social position consists of organising, elaborating, and connecting the life of a group: the stratum a class creates or inherits to give itself coherence, direction, and an awareness of its own role.

From that starting point the definition assembles itself in three parts. First, an organic intellectual is a person who performs the organising, technical, and directive functions that a class requires in the course of its development: the work of giving that class homogeneity, internal discipline, a programme, a memory, a legal and technical apparatus, and an awareness of its function across the economic, social, and political fields. Second, the stratum is not recruited from outside and then attached to the class; it is elaborated by the class itself as the class emerges on the terrain of production, so that the relation between class and intellectuals is internal, a moment of the class's own formation rather than a service it purchases. Third, the category is defined by contrast with intellectuals of the traditional kind, who were elaborated by earlier formations, survived the disappearance of the class that produced them, and consequently experience themselves as a self sufficient caste standing above the struggle of classes. The three parts belong together. Remove the second and the concept collapses into a theory of hired expertise. Remove the third and it loses its account of why the old universities, churches, and professions behave as they do.

The contrast carries an implication that readers resist, because it reverses a natural assumption. In this scheme it is the organic intellectual who is the specialist in a class's own project, and the traditional intellectual who is the generalist drifting between projects. The corporate counsel who spends a career perfecting the legal instruments of a merger regime is bound by daily practice to a determinate class function, knowing more about its actual working than any commentator. The professor of letters whose discipline predates the existing order by centuries can pass a whole career without once being required to say whom the work serves, and the freedom from that requirement is exactly what the notebooks mean by the traditional intellectual's autonomy. Origin, schooling, and radicalism of opinion are all absent from the definition, and their absence is deliberate. A definition of a structural position that could be satisfied by a curriculum vitae would be a definition of nothing.

One further element of the definition is easy to pass over because it hides in a subordinate clause of the notebooks: the function is graded, and it is exercised collectively as much as individually. The greatest creators of a science or a philosophy sit at one end of the stratum; at the other end sit the humblest administrators and popularisers of an intellectual heritage they did not create; between them stand the teachers, technicians, officials, and organisers who do most of the connective labour of a social order. The concept therefore names a workforce, not a pantheon. When later sections of this article arrange the stratum in grades and then consider a party performing the entire function as a body, both moves are developments of this original element rather than additions to it.

For the reader who wants the definition in a single portable sentence, it is this. An organic intellectual is a person performing the organising and elaborating functions that a particular class generates out of its own development, as distinct from a traditional intellectual, who performs intellectual functions inherited from a superseded order and experiences the performance as the life of an autonomous caste. The source of the category is the twelfth of the prison notebooks, the notes on the formation of intellectuals written in the early nineteen thirties, and its fuller biographical setting is traced in the [antonio gramsci intellectual biography](/2015/01/01/antonio-gramsci-intellectual-biography/) elsewhere in this series.

## The function test

The definition earns its keep only if it can be applied, and the application is simple enough to state as a discipline, which this article calls the function test. Confronted with any person, occupation, or institution, the test asks three questions in a fixed order. What organising, elaborating, or connective work does the position actually perform, as opposed to what its holder says it performs? For which class is that work organic, meaning which class's development elaborated the position and which class's coherence the work sustains? And is the position one its class created, or one it inherited from an earlier formation and merely staffed? The first question excludes self description. The second excludes biography. The third produces the organic and traditional distinction. A classification reached any other way, by accent, by parentage, by library, by voting record, is a classification of something else.

### How does the function test decide a hard case?

Take the position, not the person, and describe the work before naming the allegiance. Where a post exists because a class's development required it, and its holder organises that class's activity or elaborates its worldview, the post is organic to that class. Where the post predates the class, surviving from an older order, it is traditional.

The order of the questions matters, because the test fails when it is run backwards. Begin with allegiance, with the class a person sympathises with or was born into, and every hard case dissolves into propaganda: the sympathetic lawyer becomes organic to the workers, the unsympathetic union official becomes a traitor to his own category, and the scheme reproduces the compliment usage under a technical disguise. Begin instead with the work, and awkward results follow honestly. A barrister who defends strikers in court performs, in that courtroom, a legal function within a machinery of labour law that industrial capital and the state elaborated between them, and the test says so, while also registering the secondment of that skill, in the evenings, to a union's education programme, where a genuinely different function is being exercised. Positions are classified; afternoons and evenings can be classified separately; persons, as totalities, are not classified at all. This is not pedantry. It is the feature that makes the concept usable by historians and sociologists who have no interest in awarding or withholding praise.

Applied at scale, the test rearranges the map of a whole society. Professions that common sense treats as neutral technique, accountancy, logistics, human resources, public relations, reveal themselves as named positions inside the organising apparatus of determinate classes, each with its professional associations, its training pipelines, its journals, and its codes, which is to say each with the full equipment of an intellectual stratum. Activities that common sense treats as the essence of intellectual life, the writing of poetry or the solving of pure mathematics, sort unpredictably: a mathematics chair sustained by an ancient university may be among the most traditional positions in the society, while a statistician inside a trade union research department is an organic intellectual of the labour movement by the strictest application of the criterion. Nothing about the dignity of the work correlates with the classification, and the test's indifference to dignity is the surest sign that it is a sociological instrument rather than a vocabulary of praise.

The test also explains a feature of intellectual life that purely cultural accounts leave mysterious: the ferocity with which strata defend their corporate interests. If intellectuals were merely people with ideas, their collective behaviour, the closed shops of the professions, the defence of credential monopolies, the priesthood's centuries of resistance to disestablishment, would be a series of accidents. On the functional account the behaviour is structural. A stratum whose position was elaborated by a formation defends the conditions of that position with the same instincts as any other corporate body, and traditional intellectuals defend theirs with the additional confidence of men who believe their position depends on no one. The notebooks' treatment of the clergy turns on exactly this: the category that experienced itself as most independent was, in its corporate conduct, among the most tenacious defenders of a material arrangement.

## How a class produces its own intellectuals

The notebooks insist on a genetic claim: every fundamental social group, arising on the terrain of an essential function in production, creates together with itself, organically, one or more strata of intellectuals which give it homogeneity and an awareness of its own function. The claim is historical before it is definitional. It says that the elaboration of an intellectual stratum is not an ornament a mature class adds later, the way a rich family endows a library, but a constitutive part of the class's emergence. A class that cannot produce the people who organise its enterprises, draft its laws, train its administrators, and articulate its conception of the world remains a crowd of producers, formidable in numbers and helpless in direction. The rising bourgeoisie of Europe is Gramsci's standing example: the capitalist entrepreneur, in the very act of organising production, creates alongside himself the industrial technician, the specialist in political economy, the organiser of a new culture and a new legal order, and the class becomes a class partly in and through that creation.

### Why does a rising class elaborate its own organisers?

Because direction cannot be borrowed for long. A rising class needs its enterprises organised, its interests translated into law, its members trained and persuaded, and its world explained to itself. Hired specialists serve a master whose purposes they may not share; elaborated intellectuals carry the class's project as their own, which makes them organs rather than rented instruments.

Notice the direction of the argument. The stratum is not produced by the class's wealth but by its activity, and its growth tracks the class's real development rather than its self image. Where an emerging class finds an existing stratum ready made, it uses it, staffs it, and gradually remakes it in its own image, which is one reason the organic and traditional distinction classifies positions historically rather than eternally. The school systems of the nineteenth century show the process at industrial scale: a bourgeois order taking possession of universities founded for the clergy, bending their curricula toward law, engineering, and administration, and slowly converting cathedrals of traditional learning into the training apparatus of a new class's organisers, while continuing to borrow prestige from the older forms it was hollowing out. The same process, run in the opposite historical direction, is what the notes on the Italian Risorgimento describe as a failure: a national movement whose intellectuals remained attached to cosmopolitan and literary traditions, never becoming the organic elaboration of a national popular class, and whose political project consequently stalled for want of organisers rooted in the country's actual life.

Subaltern classes face the production problem in its hardest form, and the notebooks do not pretend otherwise. A class whose members sell their labour have no surplus with which to endow universities, no leisure in which to train a directing stratum, and no inherited apparatus awaiting capture. Its organic intellectuals must be produced inside organisations built from subscriptions and sacrifice: the union official educated in the movement's own schools, the party journalist formed by the party press, the cooperative administrator trained by administering. Whether such production can match, in quantity and in polish, the elaboration that propertied classes fund from their revenues is a genuine historical question, and Gramsci's writings on party organisation are in large part an attempt to answer it practically. The asymmetry also explains the strategic weight the notebooks place on winning over sections of the traditional intellectuals, whose training a rising class cannot quickly reproduce: a stratum's inherited skills can be detached, at least in part, from the formation that produced them, and reattached to a new historical project.

One limit case in the notebooks sharpens the genetic claim by violating it. The peasantry, Gramsci argued, does not elaborate its own organic intellectuals, and does not assimilate a traditional stratum of its own either, although other social groups recruit many of their intellectuals from peasant ranks. The village priest, the rural lawyer, the notary, and the elementary teacher who govern the peasant's contact with the wider world arrive from outside his class, staffed by strata whose formation belongs to landowners, church, and state, and their mediation, examined in his unfinished essay on the southern question, is one mechanism by which a class that produces no organisers of its own remains organised by others. The example matters because it proves the genetic claim is a claim, not a tautology: production of a stratum is something classes can succeed or fail at, and failure has identifiable political consequences, visible in the peasantry's chronic dependence on intellectuals it did not create and cannot direct.

## The intellectual function grid

Definitions classify in the abstract; a grid forces the classification onto named cases and shows its working. The table below applies the function test to eight occupations, asking of each the three questions in their fixed order: what organising work the position performs, which class if any the position is organic to, whether it is a traditional intellectual position inherited from an earlier formation, and how the position could shift its classification over time. The intellectual function grid is the article's central instrument, and the worked discussion that follows it argues with its own rows, because several of them classify differently depending on the historical formation in which the occupation is found, which is a feature of the scheme rather than a defect in the table.

### Why does the grid count the corporate lawyer as an intellectual?

Because the work is intellectual in the only sense the scheme recognises: it elaborates and maintains the juridical forms through which a class's activity proceeds, drafting its instruments, anticipating its conflicts, and training its members in a common legal sense. That the work is done in a boardroom rather than a study changes its address, not its category.

Three rows deserve emphasis because they carry the argument. The management consultant is the grid's purest modern specimen: an occupation with no traditional past at all, created entirely by the corporation's need to reorganise labour, selling nothing but the organising function itself, and almost never described as an intellectual by the compliment usage, which looks for manuscripts and finds only memoranda. The school teacher is the grid's most contested row, and the contest is instructive: teaching was for centuries a clerical function, the state school arrived as a project of the rising bourgeois order, and the teacher in the classroom transmits a common sense whose content no ministry fully controls, so the position sits at the crossing of three formations and its classification follows the actual work and allegiance in each national case. The university philosopher inverts the popular picture most completely. The position that common sense takes as the intellectual's very type is, in the scheme, usually the most traditional: a chair elaborated by a vanished order, sustained by an institution whose prestige rests on continuity, and occupied in most periods by men whose work elaborates no living class's project at all. None of this diminishes the philosophy. It merely refuses to let prestige do the classifying.

## Traditional intellectuals and the illusion of autonomy

Every class that rises inherits an intellectual landscape it did not make. The clergy, the jurists, the professors, the men of letters were already there, elaborated by formations that have since dissolved, and the notebooks call this inherited stratum traditional. The word does not mean conservative. It means continuous: these are categories which, in Gramsci's analysis, represent an historical continuity uninterrupted even by the most radical transformations of political and social form, because each successive order found them in place and made use of them. The ecclesiastics are his type case. For a long epoch they monopolised the superstructural services of their world, religious ideology and with it philosophy and science, the school, morality, justice, charity, and they held that monopoly as the intellectual stratum of the landed aristocracy, sharing its privileges and bound to its land. When the aristocracy's world dissolved, the stratum did not dissolve with it. It survived into a social order founded by a different class, carrying its training, its corporate organisation, and its self understanding across the rupture.

### Why do traditional intellectuals experience themselves as independent?

Because their stratum's continuity predates every living class, their skills seem to answer to a discipline, not to a patron. A priesthood or a professoriate that served one order and outlasted it concludes that it serves truth itself. The notebooks treat the self image as an illusion with effects: a stratum believed autonomous is trusted differently from one known to be attached.

The notebooks are precise about the status of that illusion, and precision here is the whole point. The autonomy is not a fraud perpetrated by cynical men; it is a social effect of genuine continuity, and it works. Because the stratum really has served successive orders, because its techniques really are transferable, its members experience their activity as governed by the internal standards of a craft: canon law, philology, the disputation, the precedent. That experience generates the utopia, as Gramsci calls it, in which intellectuals conceive themselves as autonomous and independent of the dominant social group, and the utopia has consequences in every direction. It gives the stratum bargaining power, since a class that needs its services must bid for them rather than command them. It gives the stratum prestige with classes far below the dominant one, since the peasant who distrusts the landlord may still trust the priest, whose authority does not present itself as the landlord's. And it gives every rising class a standing temptation: to mistake the traditional intellectuals' self description for a fact of nature, and so to neglect the slow work of elaborating organisers of its own in the belief that neutral expertise will administer any order impartially.

Gramsci's treatment of Croce, threaded through the notebooks, is the case study of the stratum's political weight at its highest level. Croce, the philosopher as a kind of lay pope of Italian liberal culture, elaborated no rising class's programme and held himself above parties, and precisely that elevation allowed his work to function as what Gramsci calls a kind of lay religion for the educated strata, holding a whole historical bloc's common sense in solution. The analysis never reduces Croce to a servant of interests; it does something more useful, showing how a traditional intellectual of the first rank, by elaborating the problems of his epoch in the idiom of an inherited discipline, can supply a social order with its most durable connective tissue without ever signing its manifesto. The lesson generalises. The traditional stratum's detachment is the medium of its influence: because its members are not a class's organisers, their elaborations arrive in the world wearing the uniform of disinterest, and disinterest persuades where a programme would argue.

The historical behaviour of the stratum follows from its position. Traditional intellectuals defend their corporate conditions, their monopolies of certification, their control of entry, their exemptions, with a tenacity equal to any guild, and they experience this defence as the defence of standards rather than of interests, which makes them formidable negotiators. Successive orders have handled them in three recurring ways: by capture, pensioning and ennobling the stratum until its continuity is rented; by circumvention, building parallel institutions, academies, technical schools, party presses, that route around the inherited monopoly; and by conversion, winning a section of the stratum to a new project, which transfers its skills and its prestige together. The Risorgimento again supplies Gramsci's melancholy example of the third way failing: the Italian traditional intellectuals, formed in a cosmopolitan literary culture, remained detached from the national popular life of the peninsula, and the new state's organisers had to be improvised by a moderate party from the ranks of landowners and lawyers, with consequences the notebooks trace through the whole subsequent history of Italy.

A qualification protects the analysis from its own caricature. Traditional is a classification of a stratum's historical position, not a judgement on its members' politics, and the stratum contains every politics: reactionary priests and priests who blessed barricades, professors who served ministries and professors who were shot by them. What the category predicts is not opinion but a structural relation: the position predates the order it operates in, the position's skills are portable between orders, and the position's self understanding will insist on that portability as independence. A traditional intellectual may attach himself, body and work, to a rising class, and such attachments have changed history; the attachment is then an event to be explained, a conversion of the stratum's members or sections, not a refutation of the category that made the attachment visible as an event in the first place.

## Worked classifications: the grid applied in prose

The grid states results; this section earns them, walking through the occupations in the order of their difficulty and pausing on the cases where the function test overturns the classification that biography or prestige would give. The method throughout is the one announced at the outset: describe the work first, ask whose development elaborated the position second, and let the labels fall where the answers put them.

Begin with the industrial engineer, the case the compliment usage finds most indigestible. Engineering involves mathematics, design, and the direction of complex processes; it also involves, as its daily substance, the organisation of other people's labour in space and time: the layout of the plant, the sequence of operations, the speeds at which lines run and bodies move. That second content is not an accidental accretion on a pure technique. It is the function's core, and it is why the notebooks place the technician among the intellectuals the entrepreneur creates. The engineer elaborates, in technical form, a determinate organisation of production, and tests it against resistance from materials and from workers with the detachment of a professional standard, which is exactly the combination of elaboration and direction the category names. A society may call the result applied science; the function test calls the position an intellectual one, organic to the class whose production it organises, and notes that the engineer's own self description, as a solver of neutral problems, reproduces in miniature the autonomy effect that the traditional strata exhibit at scale.

The trade union officer tests the scheme from the other side, since the compliment usage would grant this figure the label on biographical grounds and the function test grants it on stricter ones. The officer's work is the administration of collective power: negotiating the price and conditions of labour, disciplining a membership through disputes, husbanding strike funds, drafting agreements that become the private law of an industry, and preserving the movement's memory across defeats. Every element is organising and elaborating work, and the position was created by the class's own organisations out of the class's own resources, which makes it the textbook case of the organic category, reached without any detour through the officer's schooling or birth. The case also shows the category's dynamic edge, for the notebooks' wider theory predicts the officer's permanent temptation: a stratum of organisers develops corporate interests of its own, in the apparatus's survival and the officials' tenure, and the history of labour movements is in part the history of that tension between the function and the stratum performing it.

### Can one occupation appear in both columns of the grid?

Yes, and the scheme expects it. Occupations are positions in history, and a post inside an inherited institution can be performed for a rising class, while a post a class created can be carried on after its organising energy has passed into administration. The columns classify a relation between position and class in a period, not a job title's eternal nature.

Journalism splits cleanly along this seam, which is why the grid refused it a single answer. The reporter on a commercial newspaper performs connective and elaborating work inside an apparatus owned by capital, selecting and framing the day's world for readers whose continued attention is the product sold to advertisers; the function is organic to the proprietors' order whatever the reporter's private opinions, since opinion does not alter the apparatus's discipline. The journalist on a party or movement paper performs the same craft operations inside an apparatus elaborated by a rising class from its own funds, and Gramsci, who edited newspapers as the central act of his political life, treated that difference of apparatus as the whole difference. The craft is identical; the classification is opposite; and any account of the press that classifies by the craft alone, whether celebrating its neutrality or lamenting its bias, misses the structural variable the notebooks isolate.

The priest, finally, demonstrates how much history the two columns compress. In the medieval formation the clergy were not traditional at all: they were the organic intellectuals of a feudal order, elaborated by it, organising its morality, its law, its record keeping, and its legitimation from within. They became traditional by surviving, persisting as a formed stratum into orders that had not created them and that alternately captured, circumvented, and converted them. The example proves that the distinction is a relation between a stratum and a formation in time, and it supplies the scheme's answer to a lazy objection: if one period's organic intellectuals are the next period's traditional ones, the objector says, the distinction is merely chronological. The reply is that chronological is not merely. A stratum's having been created by the order it serves, or merely inherited by it, changes how the stratum behaves, how it can be bargained with, how it understands itself, and how a class that wants direction must proceed, which is everything the theory was built to explain.

## Grades of intellectual function, and the school that produces them

Inside the stratum, the notebooks arrange the function by levels, and the arrangement dissolves a leftover temptation: to picture intellectuals as a thin layer of great minds. Gramsci describes two broad levels of intellectual activity superimposed within any civilisation. At the highest level stand the creators of the various sciences, of philosophy, of art, the figures whose names survive. At the lowest stand the administrators and divulgators of an existing, accumulated intellectual wealth: the clerks of culture, one might call them, who carry elaborations they did not make into the daily life of institutions. Between and beneath the creators, the mass of the stratum does the connective work of a social order, and it is a mass: the more extensive a civilisation's intellectual apparatus, the more numerous its lower grades, until the humblest rung, the elementary teacher transmitting literacy to a village, vastly outnumbers the summit. A theory of intellectuals that begins with the famous has the pyramid upside down, and the notebooks invert it deliberately, since the organising of a class is accomplished overwhelmingly at the pyramid's broad base.

### Why does the scheme arrange intellectuals in grades?

Because the function divides labour rather than naming a single gift. Some positions create a class's conception, others administer and spread what has been created, and the levels obey different laws of recruitment, training, and prestige. Grading explains why a class needs schools and apparatuses in the plural, and why its direction fails when any level is missing.

The instrument that manufactures the grades is the school, and the notebooks' remarks on education belong to the theory of intellectuals rather than to pedagogy in the narrow sense. The state, Gramsci argues, elaborates intellectuals of various levels through the scholastic system, and the complexity of the intellectual function in a given society can be measured by the quantity of its specialised schools and their hierarchisation: the more extensive the scholastic area and the more numerous the vertical grades of schooling, the more elaborate the cultural machinery the order maintains. This yields the analysis its characteristic reversal. What presents itself as the neutral provision of learning is, structurally, the production line of a society's organisers, stratified in advance: a classical secondary school that forms the traditional directing strata in disinterested culture, a technical and vocational track that forms the organisers of production, and an elementary floor that equips the rest with the literacy the order requires and no more. Gramsci's educational writings, with their defence of a common formative school against premature tracking, are the normative obverse of this analysis, arguing that the subaltern classes are cheated precisely at the point where the system assigns their children to the grade of intellectual function it intends them to occupy.

Recruitment into the grades follows the division the whole article has been tracing. The higher grades recruit disproportionately through the traditional institutions, whose continuity monopolises the credentials of creation, while the lower administrative grades are recruited more promiscuously and were historically the channel through which subaltern families entered the stratum at its base, as elementary teachers, minor officials, and non commissioned organisers of every apparatus. That channel matters politically. A rising class finds among the lower grades its readiest converts and its most instructive defectors, men and women whose daily work organises the existing order at the level where its promises and its deliveries diverge, and the notebooks' interest in the rural elementary teacher, mediating between the peasant world and the state that schooled her, is an interest in exactly this hinge position. The grade structure therefore is not taxonomy for its own sake. It maps the points at which a class's intellectual apparatus is strong, where it is staffed by inheritance rather than by creation, and where pressure or conversion is most likely to move it.

One more consequence of grading deserves extraction, because it corrects a habit of polemic. Arguments about intellectuals habitually proceed by synecdoche, taking the summit for the stratum: a debate about professors' opinions is offered as a verdict on the intellectual function of an age. On the graded account, the summit is the smallest and least representative part of the workforce under discussion, and its opinions are the least consequential part of its work. The consequential work is done by the grades that organise: the syllabus committees and textbook writers, the examiners and inspectors, the technical instructors and the administrators of archives, whose combined output determines what a society's common sense contains far more thoroughly than any celebrated intervention. The function test, applied by grades, redirects attention from what famous intellectuals say to what the apparatus does, and in that redirection the whole scheme's polemical point is concentrated.

## The party as collective intellectual

At the summit of the argument stands a claim that popular accounts quote as a metaphor and the notebooks advance as a literal thesis: the political party, for the classes it organises, performs the same function in the political field that organic intellectuals perform individually elsewhere, and does so as a collective intellectual. Gramsci's figure for the party in its classical form is the Modern Prince, the organism in which a collective will, recognised and partially asserted in action, begins to gather and prepare itself. The point of the figure is the transfer of the function. What a class's scattered organisers do fragmentarily, drafting programmes, editing newspapers, educating cadres, coordinating strategy, the party does as a single body with a memory, a discipline, and a division of labour, and the product of that collective labour is something no individual intellectual can manufacture: a class that knows itself as an historical actor and possesses the apparatus to act on the knowledge.

### What can a collective intellectual do that individuals cannot?

It can hold a class's experience in a memory that outlives every member, elaborate a strategy across defeats and decades, not one career, educate its own successors while acting, and discipline scattered organisers into a single centre of direction. An individual intellectual retires, errs, or dies as a person; the collective organ persists as a function.

The claim is usually misread in the direction of flattery, as though calling a party an intellectual ennobled it, and the misreading inverts the argument as thoroughly as the biographical error inverted the category. In the notebooks the collective intellectual is a functional description with functional consequences, several of them uncomfortable for parties. If the party is where a class's organising intelligence resides, then the party's internal life, its education of members, its press, its handling of its own errors, is not housekeeping but the very activity by which the class thinks, and a party that suppresses internal elaboration is damaging the organ of its class's awareness in the name of defending it. The analysis also specifies what kind of intellectuals a party must produce: not a caste of professional theorists suspended above the membership, but organisers formed inside the class's activity, in what Gramsci describes as the fusion of feeling and understanding, the point at which the intellectual element no longer stands outside the practical life it elaborates. The famous insistence that the popular element feels while the intellectual element knows, and that leadership consists in joining the two, belongs to this argument and not to a cult of spontaneity.

Situating the collective intellectual within the wider theory prevents two opposite exaggerations. Against the liberal reading, in which a party is a machine for winning elections that happens to employ some thinkers, the notebooks insist that the organising of a class's self awareness is constitutive, not decorative: the party does for the political field what the whole intellectual stratum does for the social field, and its relation to the class mirrors the stratum's relation generally. The full account of the end that organising serves belongs to the series' study of [gramsci hegemony complete guide](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/), and the terrain on which the function operates is mapped in [gramsci on civil society](/2015/03/15/gramsci-on-civil-society/); the present article's narrower point is only that the function exists, is collective in its highest form, and is graded and staffed like every other part of the apparatus. Against the vanguardist reading, in which the collective intellectual licenses a directing caste to substitute itself for the class, the notebooks' emphasis falls on elaboration from within: the party's intellectuals are organic precisely insofar as they are produced by the class's own development, and the substitution of an apparatus for that development is the pathology the theory elsewhere anatomises under the name of bureaucratisation. The same category that dignifies the party's work thus also supplies the criterion by which a party's degeneration can be described without metaphor.

Historical parties differ in how completely they concentrate the function, and the difference is a research instrument. A party of notables, assembled for elections from local dignitaries, performs little collective elaboration and leaves its class's awareness to newspapers, churches, and universities it does not control; a mass party with its own press, schools, and cultural organisations performs most of the function itself and can sustain a class's direction through illegality and defeat. The notebooks generalise from the second type because Gramsci's own formation occurred inside it, and the reader should register the generalisation as a claim about a spectrum rather than a definition of party as such. Even so qualified, the claim does the work the brief for this article requires: it identifies where, in a rising class's economy of organisation, the intellectual function reaches its most concentrated and self conscious form, and it explains why contests over a party's cultural apparatus, its schools and its journals and its criteria for promoting organisers, are contests over the class's mind and not administrative quarrels.

## Symmetry: the category cuts in every direction

A discipline this article has observed throughout deserves one explicit statement, because the term's popular career depends on violating it. The category applies symmetrically. It classifies the intellectuals of the dominant class with the same criteria, in the same vocabulary, and with the same absence of moral loading that it applies to the intellectuals of subordinate classes, and it classifies the intellectuals of the left, where a left formation has elaborated them, as organic to that formation's project rather than as exempt from classification by virtue of their sympathies. Gramsci's own practice observes the symmetry rigorously. His most developed examples of organic intellectual production are bourgeois: the entrepreneur's technicians and jurists, the organisers of a new legal order, the whole apparatus by which a rising class of proprietors made itself homogeneous and aware. The working class enters the analysis later and more problematically, as a class whose elaboration of its own stratum is obstructed by its poverty and must be achieved against the grain of its conditions. A reading that finds in the notebooks a celebration of one side's intellectuals has mistaken the direction of the author's admiration: what he admired, and studied, was the dominant class's organisational achievement, precisely in order to learn how it was done.

### Does the category silently favour one side of politics?

No, and the appearance that it does is an artifact of its reception. The scheme was built by analysing the bourgeoisie's own organisers first and most concretely, and it registers a labour movement's officials, editors, and theorists under exactly the same functional description, neither ennobling the second group nor sparing the first from classification as a class's apparatus.

Symmetry has teeth in both directions, and both sets of teeth bite common usages. Against the rightward evasion, in which expertise is neutral and only the other side has ideologues, the scheme replies that technique is a function and functions have addresses: the restructuring consultant and the central bank's communications staff are inside the analysis, not above it, and their professional self image as problem solvers states a fact about their stratum's ideology rather than about their work's destination. Against the leftward compliment, in which the label certifies the authentic voice of the oppressed, the scheme replies that origin certifies nothing and that a movement's own officials are a stratum with corporate interests like any other, subject to the same risks of detachment and self preservation. The category is thus mildly offensive to every camp's preferred self description, which the notebooks would have counted as evidence that it is working. An instrument that always confirms its user's side is a mirror, and Gramsci was building a lens.

There is a subtler asymmetry that the scheme does register, and registering it keeps the symmetry honest. The classes do not face the production problem symmetrically, even though the category describes them in a single vocabulary. A propertied class elaborates its intellectuals out of its ordinary operations, since organising production is its daily business and the organisers fall out of that business as a byproduct; a subaltern class must elaborate its intellectuals against the current, funding them from wages and defending them against absorption by the dominant order's apparatus, which recruits talent upward with salaries and prestige. The traditional strata likewise press asymmetrically: their inherited prestige weighs most heavily on classes whose members were schooled to revere it. The analysis therefore predicts that the intellectual field will be tilted, in every period, toward the classes that production itself endows with organisers, and that subaltern elaboration will appear, by comparison, improvised and fragile. That prediction is a finding about the world, not a bias in the concept, and it is the reason the concept's author treated the building of a collective intellectual as a strategic task rather than an eventual gift of history.

Applied to the scheme's own author, the symmetry yields the standard biographical irony in a precise form. Gramsci was, by the test, an organic intellectual of the class whose party he helped to found: formed inside its press and its schools, performing its elaborating and organising work at the highest grade, and separated from the traditional strata of his own formation, the Sardinian and university worlds that first educated him. The category describes its inventor without remainder and without privilege, and a theory of intellectuals that can classify its own author is less likely to be a device for exempting him.

## The strongest objection: a category that includes almost everyone

The objection must be given its full force, because it identifies the genuine cost of the scheme and because the notebooks concede half of it in advance. It runs as follows. If every organising, elaborating, and connective role counts as an intellectual function, then in a developed economy the category sweeps in the manager and the mandarin, the advertising planner and the systems analyst, the examiner and the logistics coordinator, until virtually everyone outside manual execution qualifies, and a category that includes nearly everyone explains nearly nothing. Worse, the theory seems to concede the objection at its root: if all people are intellectuals, as the famous sentence grants, then the stratum distinction built on top of that grant is a distinction without a population, a matter of labelling the same humanity twice. On this reading the concept survives in popular use precisely because it is empty, a dignifying label available for any occupation its user wishes to honour, and its scholarly use fares no better, since a classification that cannot exclude cannot inform.

### If the category is so broad, what does it still exclude?

It excludes the classification of persons by origin, talent, or opinion, and it excludes from the intellectual function all work that neither organises a group's activity nor elaborates its conception of the world. Pure execution under direction, however skilled, falls outside the function, as does private brilliance with no organising connection to any class's life.

The answer begins by accepting the breadth and denying that breadth is the same as vacuity. A category can be wide and still classify, provided the discriminating question is asked after the category is applied rather than before. In this scheme the label intellectual does almost no work by itself; the work is done by the second and third questions of the function test, organic to which class, and created by that class or inherited by it. Two occupations that both fall inside the wide category, the consultant and the union research officer, fall on opposite sides of every distinction the theory actually uses: different classes elaborated them, different apparatuses train them, different common senses issue from their desks. Breadth at the level of the genus is the price of precision at the level of the species, and the price was paid knowingly: Gramsci had watched narrower definitions, identifying intellectuals with writers and scholars, blind their users to the organising strata of the classes those users studied, and he chose the wider net in order to catch the technician and the manager whom the narrower one let swim away misclassified as mere employees.

The concession the notebooks make should also be stated exactly, since the answer's honesty depends on it. The famous sentence does concede that intellectual activity is universal, and the scheme built on it therefore cannot use the word intellectual to mark off a spiritually distinct kind of human being, which is what most philosophies of the intellectual caste had wanted the word to do. That loss is the point. What replaces the caste is a function located in a structure, varying in grade and allegiance, and the replacement strips intellectuals of a metaphysical alibi at the same time as it grants their work analytical dignity. Where the objection does land, and lands permanently, is on the term's popular career: the breadth that the theory disciplines with its second and third questions is, in undisciplined usage, an open invitation to treat the label alone as the finding, and the resulting dilution, the honorific sprayed over any favoured speaker, is a real degeneration that the theory's own architecture made possible. The concept's defenders should concede this and answer it with use rather than with policing: every time the full test is run and a hard case is classified against the classifier's sympathies, the category demonstrates the content the objection denies it.

## The concept in education theory

No field adopted the category more eagerly than education, and none illustrates its fortunes better, because schooling is where the functional and biographical readings collide in print. The attraction is obvious. Teachers occupy the hinge the notebooks describe, transmitting an order's common sense at its broadest grade while being recruited, in large part, from the classes that order subordinates, and a theory that takes the elementary teacher as seriously as the philosopher speaks directly to a profession accustomed to being treated as the mere delivery mechanism of other people's knowledge. Through the pedagogy of Paulo Freire, whose work on literacy and conscientisation drew on a similar insistence that the oppressed must elaborate their own understanding rather than receive it, the Gramscian vocabulary entered staffrooms and faculties of education worldwide, and through later proposals, most explicitly the programme of the transformative intellectual advanced in the late nineteen eighties, it was reworked into an ideal of the teacher as a political moral agent. The applied field is treated at length in the series' companion study of [marxism and education theory](/2021/05/01/marxism-and-education-theory/); what matters here is the concept's fate in transit.

### How did education writers alter the concept they borrowed?

Gradually, and in one direction: from function toward commitment. The borrowed version keeps the teacher's importance but relocates the criterion from the position's organising work to the teacher's intentions, so that the organic intellectual becomes the one whose sympathies are correct. The notebooks classify sympathetic and unsympathetic teachers inside the same apparatus first, and ask their questions afterwards.

The alteration was not carelessness, and describing its mechanism is more useful than deploring it. Education is a normative discipline: it studies an institution in order to improve it, and a category imported into a normative discipline is pressed toward prescription as water finds a slope. A functional classification that sorts a staffroom into an apparatus's grades offers a faculty of education little to exhort; a category that names the teacher it wishes to produce offers a syllabus, a mission statement, and a warrant for the faculty's own importance, three things no profession refuses for long. So the function test's second question, organic to which class, was quietly replaced by a question about the teacher's politics, and the third question, created or inherited, vanished altogether, since on the normative reading the traditional intellectual survives only as the villain of the piece, the hidebound colleague resistant to change. What remains after both replacements is the compliment usage in academic dress, and the proof that the replacement has occurred is simple: in the borrowed literature it is nearly impossible to find a teacher, consultant, or curriculum theorist classified as an organic intellectual of capital, although the original scheme makes that classification unavoidable for whole floors of the educational apparatus, from the examination industries to the consultancy firms that reorganise school systems for ministries.

Freire's own use stands apart from the dilution and repays separation from it, since his dialogical pedagogy preserves the structural insight that the borrowed vocabulary later lost. The banking model of education he attacked, in which the teacher deposits knowledge into passive students, is a description of a function: the reproduction of an order's common sense through a one way transmission. His alternative, problem posing education conducted as a dialogue in which teacher and student elaborate a reading of the world together, is correspondingly a proposal about function, an attempt to relocate the elaborating work inside the subaltern group rather than to certify the virtue of any individual educator. Intellectuals and the class struggle are not Freire's explicit subject, and his categories are his own, but the family resemblance is exact at the decisive point: liberation, in his account, cannot be delivered by a stratum acting on behalf of the oppressed from outside their own elaboration, which is the notebooks' doctrine of organic production translated into a classroom. Where later borrowers took the vocabulary and dropped the structure, Freire took the structure and largely renamed it, and the comparison suggests a general law of the concept's travels: it survives where its users keep asking what work is done and for whom, and it dissolves where they ask instead who deserves the title.

Schools also supply the cleanest demonstration of the category's grading argument, and education writers who keep the function test have used it to unsettling effect. A national system can be read as a diagram of the intellectual functions its order requires: the selective school forming a directing stratum in the idiom of inherited culture, the technical institute forming production's organisers under a rhetoric of pure skill, the comprehensive floor forming administrators and the literate rank and file, each tier credentialed so that the grade of intellectual function a child is prepared for appears as a fact about the child's ability. Reading the diagram aloud, in staffrooms, has been the concept's most durable educational achievement, outlasting the honorific usage that first carried the term into the field. It is an achievement the notebooks would have recognised, since their author treated the organisation of schooling as a question about the distribution of the intellectual function itself, and therefore as a question about which classes would possess organisers, and of what grade, a generation later.

## The workshop where the concept was made

Categories of this reach are rarely invented at a desk, and this one was assembled in a newsroom and a movement before it was refined in a cell. Gramsci's formation ran through the institutions his theory describes: the socialist press in Turin, the weekly he helped to found at the end of the Great War, the factory council movement in which skilled workers built organs of direction inside the plants of the city's metalworking industry, and the founding of a communist party whose statutes and schools attempted, in miniature, the production of a directing stratum from a class that had none to inherit. The Ordine Nuovo group, the new order journalists, matters to the concept's biography because its members lived the problem the notebooks later formalised. They were intellectuals elaborated inside a class's own institutions, paid from movement funds, educated by the movement's necessities, and acutely aware of the gap between their function and the self sufficient world of the university across the river, whose professors they had lately been and refused again to become. When the prison notes define the organic intellectual against the traditional one, the definition is a generalisation of that Turinese experience, tested retrospectively against the whole of European history.

The factory councils supply the experience's sharpest episode. In the council movement, the most advanced workers of the Fiat plants and their rivals were building, inside production itself, a stratum of organisers whose authority came from the work process and whose education came from administering it, and Gramsci's journalism championed the councils precisely as the embryo of a new order's intellectual and directive apparatus, elaborated by the producers rather than appointed over them. The movement's defeat, and the party's subsequent history, taught the complementary lesson that fills the notebooks' pages on traditional strata: that a class's improvised organisers, however brilliant, face an opposing apparatus centuries deep in training and prestige, and that the contest between a created stratum and an inherited one is the ordinary form of the struggle for direction in a society. The analysis of hegemony, of which this category is a component, grew from that asymmetry, and the reader who wants the full doctrine should follow the series' guide to it, linked earlier in this article; the glossary's narrower claim is only that the definition of an intellectual was forged where its author had watched both kinds at work.

Prison changed the concept's register without changing its content. Cut off from the movement whose organisers he had been, Gramsci turned the functional question onto history at large: onto the church, whose clergy supplied the type of the traditional stratum; onto the Italian intellectuals of the Risorgimento, whose failure to become organic to a national popular class explained, for him, the deformations of the state they helped to found; onto Croce, the supreme traditional intellectual of the age, whose elevation above parties the notes dissect as a political fact of the first magnitude. The breadth the objection complains of was thus acquired in the cell, as a local Turinese distinction was tested against a millennium of European material and survived the testing at the price of generality. Understanding that trajectory also explains the definition's double aspect, at once a piece of class analysis and a contribution to the sociology of knowledge: it asks of any body of thought the functional questions, who elaborated it, inside which apparatus, in the service of which class's coherence, and it asks them with the same neutrality toward a catechism and toward a treatise on thermodynamics.

## Boundary cases: institutions the grid was not drawn for

A classification proves itself on the cases its inventor did not contemplate, and four modern institutions test the function test in instructive ways. Each was chosen because the compliment usage and the biographical reading classify it wrongly or not at all, and because running the three questions in order produces a verdict that is at first surprising and then difficult to unsee.

The policy institute, or think tank, is the easiest case and therefore the best calibration. Its staff produce reports, draft model legislation, brief legislators, and supply journalists with quotable analysis; the work is elaboration and connection in their purest marketed forms, performed by people whose product is a class's or a bloc's programme rendered in the idiom of research. The function test classifies by asking who elaborated the institution and whose coherence its output sustains, and on those questions institutes sort themselves without remainder: the institute founded and funded by industrial associations to develop a deregulatory programme is a collective organ of that programme's class, whatever its scholars' individual views; the institute maintained by a labour federation to cost its policies performs the identical function for the opposing bloc. What the case calibrates is the irrelevance of self description at institutional scale, since the think tank's entire public presentation depends on the traditional stratum's claim, independence from interest, while its organisational form, a purpose built elaborating apparatus, is the organic category rendered in brick and endowment. The gap between the presentation and the form is not hypocrisy but structure: in a society where disinterest persuades, a class's organisers acquire more influence by wearing the inherited stratum's uniform, and the notebooks' account of autonomy as a social effect predicts the costume in advance.

The senior civil service tests the opposite boundary, the case where classification genuinely hesitates. The permanent official elaborates and administers the state's continuity across governments, and the state, in the theory this category serves, is not a neutral arbiter standing outside the classes but the form in which a dominant bloc's direction is organised and its concessions administered. On a strict run of the test, the higher officialdom appears as the organic intellectual stratum of the bloc in power considered as a whole: created by the modern state's development, trained in its schools and academies, performing the connective work between ministries, regions, and interests that no elected politician has the tenure to perform. Yet the hesitation is real, because the same officialdom displays the traditional stratum's defining marks: corporate continuity across regimes, recruitment by examinations in inherited disciplines, and a self understanding as servants of an abstraction, the public, the crown, the republic, that no living class admits to owning. The resolution the scheme offers is that the position is dual and the duality is historical: a state apparatus built by a rising class as its own organ ages, with the class's hegemony, into an inherited machinery whose stratum experiences itself as traditional while performing organic work. Classification here is a dating exercise, and the argument between the two labels is an argument about where in that ageing a given service stands.

Advertising and commercial persuasion form the third case, the one whose classification the scheme delivers without hesitation and common sense resists most. The copywriter, the brand strategist, and the audience researcher elaborate the desires and self images through which a class's goods find their buyers and its way of life finds its admirers; in the notebooks' vocabulary this is elaborating work upon common sense itself, conducted at industrial scale and with a sophistication the older strata reserved for theology. That the workers concerned rarely describe themselves as intellectuals, and are rarely so described, follows from the scheme's central teaching: the function is invisible to its performers when the ideology of the stratum presents the work as technique, in this instance as creativity in the service of sales. The function test is unmoved by the presentation. It asks what the work organises, and the organisation of a consuming public's imagination, conducted for the enterprises that employ the organisers, answers itself.

The celebrity intellectual, the media philosopher or pundit whose authority derives from visibility, poses the fourth case and the one the extra questions in seminars always raise. Visibility is neither a function nor an apparatus, and the scheme therefore refuses the classification that fame requests: a famous person, asked, performs elaboration only insofar as the performance is connected, institutionally, to the coherence of some class's project, and the connection, not the audience, decides. In practice the connection usually exists through the media apparatus that platforms the figure, and the classification then attaches to the position within that apparatus, the column, the chair on the panel, the institute fellowship, rather than to the personality the audience mistakes for the position. Where no such connection exists, where a figure's pronouncements organise nothing and elaborate no class's self understanding but only a personal brand, the scheme returns its most deflationary verdict: not an intellectual in the functional sense at all, whatever the gifts on display, since a function that connects to nothing is a performance and not a position. The verdict will strike some readers as inverted, demoting the celebrated and promoting the anonymous, and the inversion is the category working as intended.

## Becoming one: choice, conversion, and the stratum in motion

If the category classifies positions, what becomes of the individual's path into them? The seminar question arrives in a personal form: can a person become an organic intellectual deliberately, by decision and effort, the way one chooses a profession or a party? The scheme's answer distinguishes the two things the question conflates. A person can absolutely choose to perform organising and elaborating work, can seek the positions where such work is done, and can attach that work to a class's project with full deliberation; intellectuals do this in every generation, and movements depend on it. What a person cannot do by decision is make the work organic, since organicity names the relation between the function and the class's own development, a relation that exists only when the class's institutions elaborate, sustain, and direct the position. The would be organic intellectual who finds no class apparatus to be organic to is in the position of a would be organ without a body: the intention is real, the function is unperformed, and the gap between intention and function is precisely the space in which the collective intellectual, the organised body that gathers scattered vocations into a stratum, does its work. Deliberateness operates on the supply of candidates. It cannot manufacture the demand side, the class in formation whose coherence the candidates would organise.

### What follows when a traditional intellectual changes allegiance?

The work changes its classification before the biography does. A scholar formed in an inherited institution who places its skills at the service of a rising class's organisations begins performing an organic function in a traditional post. The notebooks treat conversions as strategic events: a rising class acquires through them training it could not produce unaided, and prestige it could not buy.

History supplies the type cases, and they cluster where a formation is dissolving. The philosophes who attached the inherited republic of letters to a rising bourgeois order, the jurists who carried Roman law's techniques into the service of new property regimes, the defecting clergy of a dozen reformations: in each instance a section of a traditional stratum crossed to a class in formation, and the crossing is legible in the record as a transfer of competence, whole libraries of technique and legitimation changing their function while their practitioners changed only their patrons and their audiences. Gramsci's notebooks return repeatedly to the Italian failure to accomplish this transfer: the peninsula's traditional intellectuals, heirs of a cosmopolitan and curial culture, held themselves aloof from the national popular classes whose organisers they might have become, preferring a Europe of the mind to the provinces at their feet, and the national movement consequently lacked the elaborating stratum that France's revolution, staffed by converted lawyers and lettered bourgeois, possessed in depth. The political moral is unsentimental. A rising class that cannot convert sections of the inherited stratum must either produce substitutes at speed, which is the party school and the movement press, or proceed with its intellectual function unperformed, which is a disability no enthusiasm at the base repairs.

The movement of strata, not only of individuals, is the deepest level of the analysis, and it is where the scheme shows its character as historical sociology rather than taxonomy. A stratum's function can change underneath its personnel, as happened to the clergy across the long dissolution of the feudal order: the same institutional body, recruiting in the same villages, celebrating the same rites, passed from being the organic elaborator of a living formation to being the traditional survivor of a dead one, because the class whose coherence it organised had ceased to organise the society around it. The test for such a passage is never the stratum's own report, since strata experience continuity precisely when their function has inverted; the test is whether a living class's development still elaborates the position, or whether the position persists by endowment, by law, and by the deference of classes that did not create it. Detecting that passage in a living society is the category's most demanding exercise, and the one its author practised on his own country's professoriate, asking of each celebrated figure not what he thought but what his thinking held together, and for whom the holding was done.

The southern question gives the motion a geography. In the agrarian south of Italy, Gramsci described a social formation in which the great landowners and the peasant masses faced each other across a broad stratum of rural intellectuals: the priest, the lawyer, the teacher, the notary, the doctor, the minor official, recruited largely from the rural petty bourgeoisie and performing, between the two fundamental classes, the whole work of mediation. Every peasant movement, every tax, every conscription, every migration passed through this stratum's hands, and its members' function was to organise the peasantry's subordination in forms the peasantry could inhabit, translating the landowners' and the state's requirements into the idioms of religion, law, and deference. The political conclusion Gramsci drew was a conclusion about intellectuals before it was about land: no transformation of the south was possible that did not split this mediating stratum, detaching its lower ranks, the village teachers and minor professionals whose own position was squeezed between the blocs, and attaching them to an alliance of workers and peasants. The general lesson survives its setting. Between any dominant class and the classes it dominates there stands a mediating intellectual workforce, and the politics of a period can be read, more reliably than in its programmes, from the question of which way that workforce is facing and whether it is beginning to elaborate the world from below rather than administer it from above.

Two implications for the reader's own classification exercises close the section. First, the stratum in motion warns against synchronising the grid too confidently: every row of the table in this article is a snapshot of a relation that history is revising, and the honest use of the scheme dates its classifications, stating the period and formation for which the function test was run. Second, the motion explains the emotional register of the literature the scheme generated. Intellectuals who change allegiance are experienced by their former stratum as traitors and by their new class as suspect gifts, and the sociology of that double suspicion, the convert's zeal and the hosts' reserve, is inscribed in the category itself, which predicts that a converted section will be valued for its skills and watched for its reflexes in exactly the proportions the historical record shows.

## Consciousness from outside, and what this category does to that doctrine

Every reader arriving from the earlier clusters will carry a neighbouring formula that this category quietly rearranges: the doctrine, canonical in the Marxism of the Second International and taken up in Lenin's most famous pamphlet, that socialist consciousness is brought to the working class from outside its own spontaneous development, by intellectuals formed in the science of society. Kautsky's version of the formula was sociologically blunt. The vehicle of science is not the proletariat but the bourgeois intelligentsia, in whose ranks modern socialism originated, and from whose minds it was communicated to the workers best placed to receive it. Set beside the category of this article, the formula's peculiar shape becomes visible: it describes the arrival of a class's self awareness from a stratum the class did not produce, and it therefore poses, in the sharpest form, the question of how such imports can cease to be foreign. The full debate over that formula, and over the consciousness it promises, belongs to the series' account of [class consciousness explained](/2011/09/15/class-consciousness-explained/); the present section asks only what the organic category contributes to it.

The contribution is a relocation. On Gramsci's scheme, the problem with consciousness from outside is not that it travels, since all elaboration travels, but that an elaborating stratum which remains external to the class it instructs reproduces the structure of the traditional intellectual inside the revolutionary camp: a caste that knows, instructing a class that feels, with the caste's corporate interests quietly governing the curriculum. The remedy the notebooks elaborate is organisational rather than rhetorical. The class must produce its own intellectuals, and the imported elements must be absorbed into that production, fused with the class's practical life until the distinction between the teachers and the taught describes a division of labour within one formation rather than a relation between two. This is why the party as collective intellectual matters so much to the scheme's architecture: it is the apparatus in which intellectuals of bourgeois formation and organisers of working class formation are jointly reworked into a stratum organic to a single project, and its educational life, its schools and press and internal argument, is the mechanism of the fusion. Where that mechanism works, consciousness ceases to arrive from outside because the outside has been institutionally abolished. Where it fails, the formula's original problem returns, whatever the regime of the day calls itself.

A historical irony completes the rearrangement. Kautsky's blunt version conceded to the bourgeois intelligentsia a permanent role as science's vehicle, and the concession was quoted against its author for decades. On the functional account the concession was half right in a way its critics rarely granted: the intelligentsia of the socialist movement's founding generation really was, in the scheme's terms, a section of the traditional and bourgeois strata in conversion, carrying an inherited formation's highest skills into a rising class's service, and the movement's dependence on converts was a fact about the subaltern class's obstructed production of its own organisers, exactly as the genetic claim predicts. The doctrine of consciousness from outside is therefore best read as the symptom of a real structural condition, stated by men who lacked the category that would have told them what condition they were describing. Gramsci, who possessed the category, could state the condition and its remedy together: the converts are welcome and necessary, the class's own elaboration is the goal, and the apparatus that fuses the two is the collective intellectual in the act of existing.

## The function the stratum serves

Why does a class need organisers at all, beyond the administration of its enterprises? The notebooks' answer reaches past administration to the constitution of the class as a historical subject. A class in the merely economic sense is a statistical reality: thousands or millions of people occupying the same position in production, sharing conditions without necessarily sharing a conception of them. What converts that aggregate into an actor, capable of discipline, alliance, sacrifice, and a programme pursued across generations, is elaboration: the work by which shared conditions are rendered into a shared understanding, grievances into demands, demands into a doctrine of society, and a doctrine into institutions that outlast their founders. That work is the intellectual function in its full extent, and the organic intellectual is the worker who performs it from inside the class's own development. The definition's apparently dry vocabulary, homogeneity, awareness of function, coherence, is a compressed description of this conversion, and the conversion is the reason the category sits at the centre of the wider theory rather than at its margin.

Seen from this angle, the stratum's product is consent as much as command. A class that rules only through coercion rules expensively and briefly; a class whose elaboration has saturated the institutions of everyday life, its schools and courts and newspapers and pulpits and professional bodies, finds its conception of the world reproduced as the common sense of the society, defended by strata who experience the defence as their professional duty rather than as obedience. The organisers of that saturation are the subject of this article, and their distribution explains a fact that purely electoral or military accounts of power leave opaque: why orders survive the visible collapse of their political leadership, persisting in the syllabus, the precedent, the editorial line, and the examination rubric long after the party of government has fallen. The stratum is the order's memory and its immune system, which is why contests over appointments to chairs, benches, boards, and editorial desks carry a charge out of all proportion to the salaries involved.

The same angle explains the category's insistence on grading, encountered earlier as an internal division of the stratum. Elaboration reaches the mass of a class, and of a society, only through the lower grades: the teacher forming literacy and its attendant worldview, the local journalist framing the week's events, the minor official whose counter is the state's face, the union convenor whose meeting is the class's parliament in miniature. A class whose higher elaboration is brilliant and whose lower grades are staffed by another class's organisers inhabits a borrowed common sense with a radical superstructure, a configuration the notebooks diagnose in more than one national history. The strategic consequence follows at once: the production of organic intellectuals is not a cultural garnish on a politics conducted elsewhere but the substance of the long contest for direction, and the class that neglects the lower grades has conceded the terrain on which the contest is decided, whatever its performance in the spectacular grades where manifestos are written.

## Common sense, good sense, and the raw material of elaboration

The notebooks give the elaborating work a specified raw material, and naming it completes the definition's circuit. Every class lives inside a common sense: the sedimented, contradictory folklore of a society, absorbed uncritically from sermons, proverbs, newspapers, schoolrooms, and the inherited wisdom of the workplace, in which fragments of every past order's philosophy lie jumbled together. Common sense is not stupidity; it is thought without inventory, containing both the accommodations a class has made to its subordination and, embedded within it, what Gramsci calls good sense, the practical core of accurate perception that daily experience forces on people who must navigate the world as it is. The organic intellectual's elaborating work, at every grade, consists in taking this material as it stands, criticising its incoherences, systematising its good sense, and returning it to the class as a coherent conception in which the class can recognise its own experience raised to consistency. The famous contrast between feeling and knowing belongs here: the popular element feels its world passionately but does not always know it, the intellectual element knows but does not always feel, and the organic relation is the one in which the two defects correct each other inside a single formation rather than confronting each other across a lectern.

This account disciplines two romanticisms at once. Against the romanticism of the spontaneous class, it denies that a coherent worldview precipitates automatically from shared conditions: the sediment must be worked, and the working is a function requiring strata, institutions, and time, which is why classes without organisers remain, in the notebooks' unsparing phrase, at the level of a corporate and particular consciousness even when their numbers are enormous. Against the romanticism of the importing vanguard, it denies that coherence can be delivered finished from outside, since elaboration that does not begin from the class's actual common sense, its language and its proverbs and its wounds, produces a doctrine the class recites rather than a conception it inhabits, and recitation collapses at the first pressure that genuine inhabitation would have withstood. The organic intellectual is defined, in the end, by standing at the point where these two failures are avoided: inside enough to begin from the material, equipped enough to raise it into coherence, and accountable, through the class's own institutions, for the distance between the two.

Philosophy, in this architecture, is not a separate kingdom above the process but its highest grade carried on by other means. The notebooks treat the history of philosophy as the history of the elaborations by which strata have systematised the common sense of their epochs, and they measure a philosophy's historical weight by its success in becoming, through the grades of the stratum, the operative worldview of people who never read it. A conception of the world that remains inside its books has performed no organising function, however refined; a crude doctrine carried by teachers, journalists, officials, and organisers into the texture of daily judgement has performed the function completely. The judgement will offend every instinct of the seminar room, and it is meant to: it is the function test applied to philosophy itself, classifying doctrines as the scheme classifies persons, by the work they do in the world rather than by the admiration they command in the study.

## One institution read closely: the university

Abstract claims about strata become testable when a single institution is read through the scheme from end to end, and the university is the most instructive specimen because it contains every element of the analysis in one set of buildings. It is an inherited institution, older than any living class, staffed by a stratum whose corporate organisation, degrees, tenure, self governing rituals, predates the industrial order by centuries: by the third question of the function test, a traditional apparatus of the first water. It is simultaneously the training ground on which the dominant classes elaborate their higher organisers, the lawyers and engineers and administrators whose organic function the grid has already classified: by the second question, an organ of the present order's production of its own stratum. And it is, at its lower grades and in its extension arms, adult education, technical instruction, the training of teachers, a machine for recruiting subaltern talent upward into functions the order requires. A classification that demanded a single label for the whole would fail the specimen; the scheme instead labels the positions within it, by function and by formation, and the resulting map is recognisably the institution everyone knows, drawn with its tensions in the right places.

Read the map by positions. The ancient chairs in the humanities, endowed when the clergy's world was intact, persist as traditional posts par excellence, and their occupants' elaborations, however brilliant, answer institutionally to the discipline's internal standards, which is the autonomy effect in its most refined habitat. The professional faculties, law, medicine, engineering, business, are the organic apparatus in session: elaborated by the order's development, funded by its fees and its states, examining to its specifications, and producing, year upon year, the credentialed organisers of its enterprises, with a directness the medieval university never attempted for any class. The newer interdisciplinary institutes and policy schools sit at the map's edge as purpose built organs of particular blocs, closer to the think tank than to the faculty. The teaching of teachers occupies the hinge identified earlier, forming the formers of the broadest grade, so that control of an education faculty's syllabus is, on this analysis, control of a multiplier: every graduate carries the formation into a thousand classrooms. Even the institution's conflicts sort themselves by the map. Disputes over appointments in the professional faculties are contests over an organic apparatus's direction; disputes in the ancient chairs are contests over a traditional stratum's succession; disputes over access and fees are contests over the recruitment channel between classes, and confusing the three, as public argument habitually does, guarantees that every party talks past the others with perfect sincerity.

The exercise generalises, and its general form is worth stating as a method. Take any institution whose work is connective, a hospital system, a broadcasting organisation, a regiment, a publishing house. List its positions. For each, ask what it organises, which class's development elaborated it, and whether it was created by the present order or inherited by it. Grade the positions by their distance from creation at one end and routine administration at the other. The result is a functional anatomy of the institution that its own organisational chart, arranged by salary and seniority, systematically conceals, and the anatomy predicts the institution's behaviour under pressure better than the chart does: which parts will defend the order as their own formation, which will rent themselves to any successor with their continuity intact, and which hinge positions, the trainers of trainers, the examiners of examiners, are worth a decade of anyone's strategic patience.

## Evidence: how the classification is done in practice

A category is only as good as the evidence that can settle its application, and the function test's questions are answerable, in historical practice, from identifiable records. The working historian of a stratum asks where its members were trained, and the curricula and examination syllabuses of a period answer with a precision that memoirs rarely match: a syllabus is a class's instruction to its future organisers about what of the world is to count as knowledge. The historian asks who paid, and endowments, subscription lists, state budgets, and party accounts answer, since elaboration is expensive and its funding lines trace the relation between a stratum and the class whose surplus sustains it. The historian asks where the stratum published and assembled, and the journals, professional associations, congresses, and alumni networks answer, because a stratum's connective tissue is archived in its own proceedings. And the historian asks what happened to the stratum at the formation's crisis, whether it held, defected, fragmented, or was purged, since behaviour under rupture reveals which class's organ a body of men had actually been, beneath every declaration made in quieter times.

Each of the scheme's central judgements in this article can be rederived from such evidence, and the exercise is worth performing mentally on the hardest of them, the autonomy of the traditional strata. The evidence for the autonomy effect is not the stratum's self praise, which any guild produces, but the transfer record: the demonstrated capacity of clergy, jurists, and professors to pass between regimes while carrying their corporate organisation through the passage intact, being courted rather than commanded by each successor, and extracting, as the price of transfer, the preservation of their examinations, their exemptions, and their jurisdiction over entry. Where the record shows a stratum dissolved and reconstituted by a new order on new terms, as happened to more than one learned estate in the revolutionary upheavals of Europe, the classification follows the record and calls the reconstituted body something else. The category thus escapes the charge of unfalsifiability that its breadth invites: it makes claims about training, funding, association, and transfer that archives can confirm or destroy, and its author's own notebooks proceed by exactly such evidence, moving from the composition of a parliament to the staffing of a ministry to the provenance of a review's contributors with the air of a man auditing the intellectual apparatus of his country line by line.

The audit metaphor is worth keeping, because it states the category's temperament. To classify by function is to audit a society's connective workforce: to ask of every position that organises, elaborates, or connects, on whose establishment it stands, by what pipeline it is recruited, and toward whose coherence its output flows. Audits are unglamorous, occasionally unwelcome, and indispensable to anyone who wants to know how an enterprise actually runs, as opposed to how its charter says it runs. The notebooks conduct that audit for the Italy of their author's lifetime and sketch it for Europe across a millennium, and the glossary article's grid is simply the audit form, reduced to the eight occupations a reader is most likely to meet misclassified.

## Two further classifications

The architect and planner repay a visit because their work organises space itself, the most durable medium in which a class's order is written. The master planner of a capital, the designer of a factory town, the housing architect deciding the dimensions in which a class will sleep and queue and assemble: each elaborates, in concrete and regulation, a conception of how the society's members are to live together, and each position was elaborated by the development of the classes that commission the building. Haussmann's Paris, whatever its aesthetics, was an organising act performed by a stratum of engineer administrators for an order that required boulevards its cavalry could sweep and its commerce could traverse, and the planners of the garden cities and the company towns performed the same function with kindlier intentions and identical structural significance. The scheme classifies the position, not the conscience: the planner who designs for a municipality against the developers is exercising the craft inside a contested apparatus, and the contest is over whose organ the planning office will be, a question settled by appointments, budgets, and law rather than by the draughtsman's sympathies.

The research scientist in the industrial or state laboratory tests the scheme's nerve in the opposite direction, since science is the modern claimant to a knowledge that serves no one in particular. The notebooks, written before the laboratory became an industry in its own right, supply the criterion rather than the case: ask where the position was elaborated, and the twentieth century's answer is that the research organisation was created, funded, and directed by the enterprises and states whose development required its output, staffed through a training pipeline those same powers built inside the universities, and set its problems, in overwhelming proportion, by the requirements of production and war. The scientist's experience of the work is of problems that pose themselves, and the experience is genuine, since a well designed apparatus does not need to dictate conclusions in order to select questions. The classification therefore attaches, as the scheme always attaches, to the position and its elaboration, and it explains without cynicism how men of complete intellectual integrity can staff an apparatus whose agenda they did not choose: integrity governs the answers a scientist gives, while the function is located in the questions the position exists to answer. The traditional residue inside the case is equally visible, for the republic of science carries the inherited stratum's marks, its own jurisdiction over entry, its international continuity across regimes, its prestige as a disinterested order, and the coexistence of the two classifications within one laboratory is the scheme's normal condition rather than an embarrassment to it.

## Neighbouring doctrines, and where this concept refuses to go

The sharpest contemporary rival to the functional account came from within sociology itself, in Karl Mannheim's description of the intelligentsia as a socially unattached stratum, recruited from all classes and unified by education into a body capable, uniquely, of seeing society whole precisely because it belonged to no part of it. The rivalry is instructive because the two accounts observe the same phenomenon and explain it in opposite directions. Where Mannheim sees education dissolving class attachment into a free floating vantage, the notebooks see the traditional stratum's defining illusion operating at its highest level of refinement: a body of men whose positions were elaborated by vanished formations, whose skills are portable between orders, and whose consequent experience of standing nowhere in particular is generalised into a theory that standing nowhere is the precondition of knowledge. The notebooks do not deny the phenomenon; the portability is real, and the breadth of recruitment is real. They deny the inference from portability to independence, pointing to the strata's corporate conduct, their defence of jurisdiction and credential, and their consistent gravitation, in crises, toward the orders that sustain their institutions. Between the two accounts the reader may judge by the evidence section's standard: follow the training, the funding, and the transfer record, and the floating stratum resolves, stratum by stratum, into positions with addresses.

A second neighbour is the moralised usage descending from Julien Benda's accusation that the intellectuals of his age had betrayed their vocation by enlisting in political passions. The accusation presupposes what the functional account denies exists: a disinterested clerisy whose proper business is eternal values, such that attachment to a class's project constitutes a fall. On the present scheme there is no unattached position from which to fall, there are only positions whose attachment is avowed and positions whose attachment is concealed by inheritance, and Benda's traitor and Gramsci's organic intellectual can be the same man described in two vocabularies, one moral and one structural. The structural vocabulary has the advantage of explaining the case Benda's cannot: the intellectual who refuses all enlistment and guards his purity inside an endowed institution is not thereby outside the analysis, since the endowment is itself a relation to the classes whose surplus built it, and its guardian performs, in his refusal, the traditional stratum's characteristic function of keeping an older order's worldview in circulation.

Two files in this series hold debates that readers habitually confuse with the present one, and the boundaries are worth policing in both directions. The argument over the professional and managerial strata, whether they constitute a class of their own with interests irreducible to the classes they serve, is a debate about class position, and it is conducted in [petty bourgeoisie and middle class](/2011/12/01/petty-bourgeoisie-and-middle-class/); the functional account of this article is compatible with several outcomes of that debate, since it classifies by work performed rather than by ownership, and a stratum can perform organic functions for a class while occupying a vexed position of its own. What the function test does settle is the prior question the class debate often skips: whatever these strata are, they are intellectuals, organisers and elaborators of determinate classes' worlds, and no account of them that treats their work as neutral technique has yet begun to describe it.

## The economics of elaboration

Elaboration has a budget, and the budget explains more of the category's historical distribution than any history of ideas. A stratum must be fed while it thinks: schools require endowments or taxes, journals require subscribers or patrons, organisers require salaries or the sacrifice of men and women maintained by their organisations, and the class that elaborates intellectuals pays for them, in currency or in kind, out of the surplus its position commands. The bourgeoisie paid out of profit, through foundations, fees, publishing markets, and the purchase of cultural prestige, and it paid so routinely that the payment became invisible, disguised as the natural support of civilisation. The church paid out of tithes and land. The state pays out of taxation, which is why the state's schools and academies express, in their staffing and syllabuses, the direction of the bloc that controls the state, and why a change in that control registers, within a generation, in the examination papers. Everywhere the funding lines run, the function follows, and the auditor of a society's intellectual life begins, unfailingly, with the accounts.

Subaltern elaboration is the same process conducted against scarcity, and its characteristic institutions are adaptations to poverty. The workers' education association funded by pennies, the movement newspaper sold at a loss and carried by the levy, the party school maintained in exile or illegality, the professional organiser whose wage is set at a skilled worker's rate as a matter of principle: these are not picturesque hardships but engineering solutions to the problem of producing a directing stratum without a surplus, and each solution leaves its mark on the stratum produced. Movement intellectuals formed in such institutions are formed collectively, inside the apparatus they will serve, with a practical curriculum set by the movement's defeats; the type differs, in texture and in instinct, from the scholar formed by endowment, and the notebooks' preference for organisers educated within the class's life is a preference with a balance sheet behind it. Scarcity also imposes the asymmetry the symmetry section registered: the dominant order can outbid any movement for talent, hiring its ablest organisers into consultancies, administrations, and media at salaries no levy can match, so that a rising class's stratum leaks upward continuously and must be replenished by formation rather than by retention. The history of labour movements written from this angle is a history of schools and salaries as much as of programmes, and it explains a recurrent pattern the purely ideological histories miss: movements lose their intellectuals not only to repression and disillusion but to recruitment, absorbed one by one into the apparatus they were formed to oppose, carrying their skills across the line their biographies were supposed to fix.

Colonial formations display the economics in their extremity, and the category's application there produced some of its most consequential afterlives. A colonising power elaborates, inside the colonised society, a stratum of interpreters, clerks, teachers, and catechists whose function is to mediate the administration to the administered: intellectuals organic to the imperial order, recruited from the colonised themselves, and trained in the metropole's languages and examinations the better to perform the mediation. Alongside them, the same schools unavoidably produce a second figure, the colonised intellectual who turns the acquired formation against the order that supplied it, and Frantz Fanon's anatomy of that figure's phases, assimilation of the coloniser's culture, a troubled rediscovery of the people's past, and a final enlistment of the pen in the people's struggle, is written in a vocabulary of function and allegiance that the present category illuminates at every step. Fanon's native intellectual passes, in the scheme's terms, from performing a traditional and mediating function inside an apparatus he did not create, to performing an organic function for a class and a nation in formation, and the drama Fanon describes is the subjective cost of the stratum transfer that the notebooks analyse in the colder language of strata and formations. The case generalises to every situation in which a people's organisers must be formed inside the enemy's schools, which is to say most situations in which subaltern classes have ever found themselves, and it is the strongest practical argument for the category's portability across the settings its author never saw.

## Failure modes of the function test itself

A glossary article that teaches a test should also teach the test's characteristic abuses, since the scheme's prestige has made its vocabulary available to lazy uses that reproduce, inside the theory's own language, the errors the theory exists to correct. Three failure modes recur, and each has a safeguard stated in the notebooks' practice.

The first is classification by outcome: the habit of inferring a position's function from the side that won, so that every intellectual of a victorious order is retrospectively organic to it and every intellectual of a defeated movement is demoted to a romantic survival. The safeguard is the genetic question, asked with dates. Organic status is a claim about elaboration during a class's development, and it is tested against the record of who built the position and when, not against the scoreboard of subsequent events; a stratum can be perfectly organic to a class that loses, as the organisers of more than one defeated revolution attest, and the category that cannot classify a loser's apparatus has confused sociology with celebration.

The second failure mode is functionalist over reading: the discovery of organising purpose in every cultural product, until a weather almanac and a love lyric are equally conscripted as a class's elaboration and the test degenerates into the all inclusive habit the breadth objection warned against. The safeguard is the standard of connection. The scheme claims functions for positions and apparatuses, not meanings for artifacts, and it requires an institutional path, training, funding, circulation, employment, between a work and the class whose coherence the work is said to sustain. Where no path can be shown, the honest classification is silence, and the notebooks model that silence in their treatment of works whose connections they could not establish, passing them by where a lesser functionalism would have annexed them.

The third failure mode is the mirror of the compliment error, classification as excommunication: the use of the traditional label to dismiss an argument without hearing it, as though locating a speaker's stratum refuted the speaker's claims. The safeguard is the distinction between explaining a position and evaluating a proposition. The function test settles where an intellectual stands in a structure; it does not settle whether anything the intellectual says is true, and the notebooks honour the distinction in their treatment of Croce, whose stratum they locate with unmatched precision while engaging his arguments, year after year, as arguments that had to be answered and not merely filed. A reader who finishes this article using organic as a compliment or traditional as an insult has not failed to learn the vocabulary. They have learned it backwards, and the grid, the grades, and the audit of evidence exist to turn it the right way round.

## The concept's afterlife among the sociologists

After its author's death the category entered the general sociology of intellectuals, where it has lived a double life as a tool and as a provocation. As a tool, it reorganised research: historians of labour movements learned to count the movement's own schools, presses, and officials as an intellectual stratum and to study its formation with the seriousness previously reserved for universities; historians of the professions learned to ask which class's development a profession's rise had served; and the study of education, as the earlier section described, acquired in the category a way of treating the school system as an intellectual apparatus in its own right. The tool's mark is a characteristic question appearing in literatures that never cite the notebooks: who trained these people, who paid for the training, and whose world does their expertise hold together. Wherever that question is asked in that form, the functional account is at work, acknowledged or not.

As a provocation, the category drew answers that define the modern debate by their opposition to it. Alvin Gouldner's portrait of intellectuals and the technical intelligentsia as a new class in embryo, a flawed universal class whose culture of critical discourse equips it to rule in its own name, accepts the functional premise and reverses the allegiance: on his telling, the stratum's organising work is ultimately performed for the stratum itself, and every class that elaborates intellectuals is gestating its successor. The notebooks anticipate the challenge in their analysis of strata's corporate interests and of bureaucratisation, and the confrontation between the two accounts, intellectuals as a class's organs or as a class in formation, remains the live form of the question in the sociology of knowledge. Edward Said's counter ideal, the intellectual as amateur and outsider whose vocation is to speak truth to power from a principled exile, poses the opposite provocation: a normative figure defined by non attachment, advanced in full awareness of the institutional world the functional account describes. The two provocations cancel instructively. Gouldner says the stratum serves itself; Said says it should serve no one; the notebooks say it serves, in fact, the class whose development elaborated its positions, and that the first step toward judging any intellectual is finding that address. Readers of this series will meet the class position side of the argument again in the neighbouring debate already linked; the glossary's duty is discharged by noting that every serious successor account has had to take its bearings from the functional definition, if only to refuse it.

## Why the word organic survived its mistranslations

The term reached English readers late and in fragments, and its biography in translation explains part of its subsequent career. The prison writings circulated first in the postwar Italian edition arranged by themes, then in selections; the 1971 English volume of selections from the notebooks, edited and translated by Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell Smith, carried the notes on intellectuals to the anglophone world in the form in which they are still most often read, with the Italian intellettuali organici rendered, unavoidably, as organic intellectuals. The adjective was a trap in waiting. In Italian, as in Gramsci's usage, organico carries the structural sense of belonging to an organism and its functions; in the English of the nineteen seventies, organic was acquiring its pastoral and commercial aura of the natural, the unprocessed, the authentic, and the translated term slid toward the compliment usage down a slope its new language had already greased. The recovery operation this article performs is therefore partly a work of translation in reverse: restoring to an English word the Latin architecture it carried in Italian, in which an organ is defined by the body it serves and the work it does for that body's life, and in which calling a stratum organic says nothing whatsoever about how natural, rooted, or virtuous its members are.

The word also survived because the phenomenon it names kept recruiting new specimens. Each generation's economy elaborates fresh organising strata and promptly misdescribes them as technique: the systems analysts of the postwar corporation, the logistics coordinators of the container age, the data specialists of the platform firms, the compliance officers of the financialised economy, none of them met in the pages of books of quotations, all of them performing, for the classes whose development created their positions, the elaborating and organising work the category specifies. A definition is vindicated less by its defenders than by the world's continued supply of its instances, and on that standard the functional definition of the intellectual, set down in a prison cell by a man forbidden to organise anything at all, has been vindicated continuously from the day it was written to the present state of the occupational census.

## The ecclesiastical case in full

The clergy earn their recurring place in this article because they are the category's limiting case: the stratum for which the traditional classification was invented, and the one whose career demonstrates every element of the analysis in sequence. Begin with their origin, which was not traditional at all. The church of the feudal centuries was the intellectual apparatus of a determinate social order, and its personnel were elaborated by that order's development with an exactness no later class has matched: a hierarchy of grades from the village priest to the theologians of the universities, a training pipeline in the cathedral schools and the seminaries, a jurisdiction over the written record, over education, over marriage and legitimacy and inheritance, over the calendar and the conscience, that made the clergy the connective tissue of an entire civilisation. They were, in the strictest sense the scheme allows, the organic intellectuals of the feudal world, and their elaboration of that world's conception, in theology, in canon law, in the philosophy of the schools, set the standard against which the organising work of every later stratum can be measured.

The transformation the notebooks isolate is what happened when the world that elaborated them died. The stratum did not die with it. Possessed of its lands, its exemptions, its corporate discipline, and its monopoly of the techniques of literacy, the clergy passed into the bourgeois order as a formed body with a history longer than the order that now employed it, and every feature of its subsequent behaviour follows from the passage. It bargained, through concordats and settlements, for the preservation of its jurisdiction and its schools. It supplied the new order with services the order could not yet perform for itself, registering births, schooling children, solemnising the marriages of a population whose habits it had formed. It experienced and proclaimed itself a power independent of the temporal order altogether, answerable to a jurisdiction above history, and that self understanding, the autonomy effect in its most majestic form, was itself a negotiating asset of the first rank, since a state that must treat with a stratum believing itself eternal treats gently. And it split, in every generation, between the defence of its corporate inheritance and the impulses of its lower grades, the parish clergy recruited from the peasantry and the artisanate, whose daily function pressed them toward the worlds of their parishioners and produced, periodically, the heresies, the reformations, and the worker priests in which the stratum's internal tensions became visible as history.

No other case shows so clearly why the notebooks refuse to equate traditional with reactionary or with unimportant. The clergy of the modern centuries organised more of the population's inner life than any party or ministry, staffed the schools in which the dominant class's own children were formed, and preserved, across the revolutions that abolished its legal existence more than once, an institutional continuity that every theorist of intellectuals since has had to explain. The explanation the notebooks give is the one this article has generalised: a stratum survives its formation when its skills are indispensable to every successor and its corporate organisation survives the transfer intact, and it survives as traditional, carrying an older order's worldview inside a newer order's institutions, where it operates as a permanent reserve of alternative direction. Every class that has risen since has found such a reserve awaiting it, in cassocks or in gowns, and has had to decide, as the Italian moderates decided and the French revolutionaries decided differently, whether to rent the reserve, to break it, or to build a counter apparatus of its own. The decision, Gramsci's Italian writings insist, is among the most consequential a rising class ever takes, and it cannot be taken intelligently by men who believe the reserve is merely a survival, a decoration, or a fraud.

## The managerial stratum: capital's characteristic organisers

If the clergy are the past's limiting case, the professional manager is the present scheme's most instructive modern one, because management is the point at which a class's intellectual function became a salaried occupation with a name of its own. The separation of ownership from control in the large corporation is usually told as a story about shareholders; told through this article's category, it is the story of the bourgeoisie elaborating a stratum to perform, as a full time function, the organising of production that the individual entrepreneur had once performed between his other businesses. The manager plans, coordinates, disciplines, forecasts, and accounts: elaborating work, conducted in the medium of other people's labour. Around the function there grew, within two generations, the complete equipment of a stratum in the notebooks' sense: its own schools, in the business faculties founded and endowed for the purpose; its own literature and professional associations; its own examinations and credentials; and its own ideology of autonomy, the doctrine that management is a neutral technique answerable to efficiency alone, which reproduces in corporate dress the traditional stratum's self image and performs the same service, presenting a class's organising apparatus as a law of nature.

The deepest stratum of the story lies one level down, in the fate of the knowledge the managers inherited. Harry Braverman's account of scientific management described the process in terms this category renders exact: the craft worker's knowledge of the labour process, accumulated in the trades over centuries, was systematically gathered up, codified, and transferred into the planning departments, so that conception was separated from execution and the intellectual function of production was concentrated in a stratum standing over the producers. Read functionally, Taylorism was an operation upon the intellectual map of the factory: an expropriation of elaborating capacity from one class and its reconstitution as the organic apparatus of another, complete with time study men, methods engineers, and rate setters performing, at the level of the single workshop, the same separation between those who conceive and those who execute that the notebooks describe at the level of society. The resistance the system met, and the entire subsequent literature of human relations and organisational culture that sought to soften its administration, are chapters in the history of a stratum negotiating its authority, and they belong to the sociology of intellectuals as properly as any dispute in a faculty of theology.

The managerial case completes the article's symmetry by supplying its most contemporary demonstration that the scheme is not a weapon aimed at one side. Nothing in the functional account permits the manager to be classified as a mere employee while the union official is classified as an intellectual, or the consultant to be treated as a technician while the party educator is treated as an ideologue; the test runs identically through both doors of the building. It also displays the grades with unusual clarity, since the corporation differentiates its organising stratum from the chief executive who elaborates strategy to the supervisor who administers its smallest routines, and staffs the grades through a scholastic hierarchy of its own, from the elite business school to the in house certificate, that mirrors, in miniature and in private hands, the state school system through which older orders manufactured their organisers.

## The democratic philosopher

One figure in the notebooks stands at the category's normative horizon, and a glossary of the concept should not omit him: the democratic philosopher, Gramsci's name for the intellectual of the highest grade reconceived as a participant in the class's own elaboration rather than a legislator above it. The traditional philosopher, in the notebooks' portrait, elaborates his system in the conviction that thought corrects the world from outside, and regards the distance between his study and the common sense of the multitude as the measure of his seriousness. The democratic philosopher inverts the relation. He understands his own thought as an element within a historical process of mass elaboration, holds that a conception of the world proves itself by becoming the operative awareness of the people whose experience it systematises, and accepts the discipline this imposes: constant contact with the simple, as the notebooks phrase the constituency, not as an audience to be instructed but as the source from which the problems, and the tests of their solutions, continually arrive.

The figure matters to the definition because it resolves the category's residual tension at the summit. Organic intellectuals at the lower grades are anchored by their work; the technician and the organiser cannot drift far from the class whose activity they organise daily. The creator at the summit works at a distance enforced by the labour itself, in archives and studies, and the history of philosophy is largely the history of that distance widening into the traditional stratum's autonomy, as each generation's elaborators are recruited into the inherited institutions and absorbed by their horizons. The democratic philosopher is the notebooks' proposed counterweight: not a job description but a standard by which the summit's relation to its class can be judged, asking whether its creations circulate downward into the grades and the common sense they organise, or whether they circulate only among the summit's peers in the republic of letters. By that standard most celebrated philosophy, including much that celebrates the people, fails of organicity at the final step, while some unfashionable work, the textbook that actually forms a generation's teachers, the manual that actually instructs a movement's organisers, performs the function in full. The standard is severe because it is the function test stated at the level where reputations are made, and its severity is the article's last demonstration that the test classifies work and never worships it.

## Strata in crisis: what war and revolution reveal

Crises audit a society's intellectual map faster than any scholarship, because they force every stratum to declare, in conduct, the allegiance its peacetime self description could leave vague. The opening of the Great War supplied the classic demonstration, as the learned strata of the belligerent nations enlisted their authority in their states' causes with a unanimity that scandalised the international republic of letters: the German professoriate's manifesto of the ninety three, in which the empire's most decorated scholars certified its army's conduct to the civilised world, remains the type specimen of a traditional stratum discovering, under pressure, that its continuity had a nationality and its independence a limit. The notebooks' framework neither celebrates nor deplores the episode; it files it as evidence of the transfer record described earlier, proof that the autonomy of inherited strata is a fair weather property, and that in the storm the strata align with the orders that sustain their institutions unless a rival apparatus has spent the peacetime years converting them. The lesson was not lost on the generation that built party schools and movement presses between the wars: conversion is peacetime work, and the side that has neglected it discovers the neglect in the first week of the emergency.

Revolution performs the audit in the opposite direction, dissolving the old order's apparatus and exposing whether the rising class has elaborated the organisers to replace it. The recurrent drama of successful insurrections, the new power obliged to reemploy the old regime's officials, engineers, and jurists because its own stratum is too thin to staff a state, is the genetic claim returning as a bill: a class can seize the summit on the strength of its organisers and still find its direction administered, at every counter and in every ministry, by intellectuals elaborated under the defeated order, whose habits and horizons bend the new policies day by day toward the old common sense. Gramsci's reflections on the Italian moderates' management of unification, absorbing the personnel and the programme of their defeated rivals until the revolution was transformed into its opposite, generalise the drama into a law of intellectual politics: the transfer of power is instantaneous only at the top, and below the top it proceeds at the speed at which a class can produce, convert, or displace the strata that actually operate a society. The category of the organic intellectual is, in the end, the instrument for measuring that speed.

## The mechanisms of production

Genesis described in the abstract, a class elaborating its stratum, resolves in practice into a handful of mechanisms, and naming them completes the answer to how such production is possible at all. The first mechanism is apprenticeship inside the class's own activity: the shop steward educated by disputes, the cooperative clerk educated by the ledger, the strike committee member educated by the strike, each acquiring the organiser's competence from the work itself and transmitting it laterally through the movement's memory. Apprenticeship is the cheapest mechanism and the most faithful, since the stratum it forms is formed in the material it will organise, but it is also the slowest and the most limited in range, producing organisers whose theory extends exactly as far as their experience and no farther. Every movement that has relied on apprenticeship alone has rediscovered, at its first encounter with law, finance, or the state, the ceiling of what experience unaided can elaborate.

The second mechanism is the press, which in the notebooks' world means the whole apparatus of movement publishing: the newspaper, the review, the pamphlet series, the publishing house. A serious journal performs three productive functions at once. It trains its own contributors, converting activists into writers and writers into theorists by the discipline of the deadline and the argument. It trains its readers, delivering the class day by day a common account of events in a common vocabulary, which is elaboration delivered at the grade of the breakfast table. And it trains its organisers in the management of an enterprise, since a paper must be financed, printed, distributed, and defended, and the men who can do those things for a paper can do them for a class. Gramsci's own decade in Turin journalism, and the care the notebooks lavish on the difference between the daily paper, the weekly review, and the theoretical journal as instruments suited to different grades of a stratum, reflect a lifetime's conviction that the press is not the movement's megaphone but its university, the place where its intellectuals are most efficiently made.

The third mechanism is the school maintained by the movement itself: the party school, the union college, the correspondence course, the summer camp of lectures. Where apprenticeship forms the organiser and the press forms the writer, the school forms the cadre systematically, selecting the ablest products of the first two mechanisms and equipping them with the inherited disciplines, law, economics, history, languages, that apprenticeship cannot supply. The school's curriculum is therefore a strategic document of the first importance, stating in its syllabus the movement's judgement about which parts of the traditional stratum's formation its own organisers require, and its chronic dilemma is equally structural: teach too little of the inheritance and the cadre is outargued by every ministry official it meets; teach it undigested and the school becomes a conduit through which the old stratum's worldview colonises the new one, producing graduates whose function quietly migrates from the movement to the academy that certified them. The fourth mechanism, association, underlies the other three: the dense fabric of clubs, circles, mutual societies, and cultural organisations in which a class practises self government in miniature, electing its secretaries, keeping its minutes, auditing its funds, and learning, in the handling of small affairs, the personnel from whom the handlers of great ones are drawn. A class rich in associations is a class running a permanent, distributed school for organisers, and the poverty of a subaltern class in exactly this fabric, its members' evenings consumed by exhaustion and its meeting rooms rented by the hour, is the material fact beneath the genetic claim's asymmetry, more decisive in the long run than any disparity of doctrine.

## The organisers of culture

The notebooks' literary journalism, often read as a diversion from the prisoner's political thought, is in fact the functional analysis conducted on the terrain of novels and plays, and it supplies the category's application to the culture industries. Gramsci's persistent question about Italian literature, why the peninsula produced no national popular novel of the kind France possessed, is a question about intellectuals: the Italian writers, he argued, were detached from the people nation, formed in a cosmopolitan and rhetorical culture, regarding the popular reader as a market to be condescended to rather than a world to be elaborated from within, and consequently the function of elaborating the nation's common sense in narrative fell to translated serials and to the church's devotional press. The argument's structure is the function test applied to fiction. A literature is classified not by its quality, on which Gramsci's judgements were sharp and independent, but by the relation between its producers and the class whose experience the literature organises, and the commercial serial novel, despised by the critics, emerges from the analysis as a more organic cultural product, in the strict sense, than the masterpiece written for the republic of letters.

The theatre criticism extends the same audit to the stage, reading Pirandello's revolutions in dramatic form with an attention the political writings explain: a playwright who broke the inherited conventions of the Italian stage was, for the young critic, performing an elaborating act upon the nation's self understanding, whatever his politics, while the subsidised theatre of official culture performed the traditional function of keeping an older sensibility in ceremonial circulation. The general principle extracted from these early writings governs the category's use across the culture industries ever since. Editors, producers, critics, and programmers are intellectuals by function, organising the imagination of publics as surely as officials organise their compliance, and the classification of a cultural apparatus follows the now familiar questions: who elaborated this institution, whose coherence does its output sustain, and does it form its audience's experience into a conception of the world or merely rent that audience an evening's distraction from it. The distinction between the two outputs is not between high and low culture, a scale the notebooks treat with indifference, but between elaboration and mere circulation, and it is available, by the audit's method, to any reader willing to follow a cultural institution's training, funding, and transfer record rather than its publicity.

## The channel and the scholarship boy

Between a class and its intellectuals there runs a channel, the route by which the class's sons and daughters reach the stratum's positions, and the channel's design decides much of what the category predicts about any given society. Where the channel runs through the class's own institutions, the movement school, the union scholarship, the party's ladder of offices, the arriving intellectual is formed inside the function and arrives with the class's experience as a native equipment. Where the channel runs through the dominant order's institutions, the state scholarship, the seminary, the competitive examination, the arriving intellectual is formed by the apparatus he will ostensibly oppose, and his trajectory becomes one of the standard dramas of intellectual history: the provincial boy from a subaltern home, lifted by a bursary into the metropolitan stratum, and suspended thereafter between the world that formed his childhood and the world that formed his mind. Richard Hoggart's portrait of the scholarship boy, uprooted and anxious at the friction point of two cultures, is the classic description of the channel's human cost, and the functional account supplies the structure beneath the portrait: the scholarship is a recruitment device by which the established order draws the subaltern classes' ablest children into its own stratum, at the price of detaching them from the class whose organic intellectuals they might have become.

The channel's traffic is not one way, and its counter currents decide political fortunes. The seminarist who returns to his village as its priest performs the traditional function with a subaltern biography, a combination the grid's logic prices exactly: the formation governs the function, the biography supplies the intimacy that makes the function effective. The scholarship boy who refuses the detachment and carries his metropolitan formation back into his class's organisations performs the conversion the notebooks prized, and the friction Hoggart anatomised becomes, in him, a resource, since he alone can translate in both directions between the class's common sense and the summit's elaborations. Whole movements have been built by such translators, and whole movements have been lost for want of them, when the channel carried the talent away and nothing carried it back. A society's educational settlement, its decisions about who is selected, at what age, by which examinations, and into which institutions, is therefore legible through the category as a settlement about the future ownership of the intellectual function: every scholarship scheme is an argument, conducted in the passive voice, about which class the next generation's organisers will be organic to.

The biography of the category's author runs through every channel this section has described, which is why the notebooks handle the type with such unillusioned sympathy. The Sardinian scholarship student who arrived in Turin with a bursary, the invalid son of a ruined petty official family, was himself the channel's product, formed by the university he would not graduate from and by the movement he joined instead, and the decision that made him an organic intellectual rather than a promising traditional one was taken, visibly and at cost, in the choice between the academy and the newspaper office. The definition he later wrote in prison classifies that choice without sentimentality and without self exemption: positions, not persons, and the work, not the wound. It is the fitting severity with which to end the exposition. What follows applies the whole apparatus, first to the three readerships this series serves, and then to the questions by which the concept is most often looked up.

## Three national sketches of a class producing its intellectuals

The mechanisms named in the abstract, apprenticeship, press, school, association, acquire their persuasive force from cases, and three national histories show them operating at a scale no single movement could improvise.

The German case is the monumental one, because the Social Democratic Party of the empire built, inside a hostile state, the most complete counter apparatus the subaltern classes have ever possessed. Its press ran from metropolitan dailies to hundreds of local papers; its associations covered its members from the cradle to the grave, choirs, gymnastic clubs, cycling clubs, lending libraries, each with its elected officers and its audited funds, a distributed school of administration in permanent session; and at the summit it maintained in Berlin, from the middle of the decade before the Great War, a central party school where selected functionaries studied economics, history, and law for terms at a time, taught by instructors who included the movement's most formidable theorist of the mass strike. The product was a stratum of professional organisers numbering in the thousands, recruited overwhelmingly from the skilled trades, formed entirely within the class's own institutions, and capable of administering a parallel society under conditions of semi legality. Whatever judgement history passes on the politics that stratum served, its existence is the standing proof of the genetic claim under modern conditions: a propertyless class, taxing itself in pennies, elaborated the organisers of a state within the state because it had decided, organisationally, that it could not proceed without them. Gramsci studied the German apparatus as the century's benchmark, and his Italian writings are a sustained argument that his own movement would have to match its productive feat without its resources.

The British case shows the same production conducted against the grain of a liberal state that co opted where the empire repressed, and its instructive episode is a quarrel over a curriculum. Ruskin College at Oxford had been founded to educate working men, with support that tied its teaching to the university's horizons; when its students struck in the years before the Great War in protest at an education they judged designed to domesticate them, and founded in the aftermath the Plebs League and the Central Labour College on the doctrine of independent working class education, the quarrel was, in this article's vocabulary, about the ownership of the elaborating function. The students' slogan might have been drafted from the notebooks: education is not a neutral good to be gratefully received but an apparatus that forms a stratum, and the only question is whose stratum it forms. The labour colleges that spread through the coalfields and the railway towns in the decades after, financed by union levies and staffed by tutors the movement trained itself, produced the organisers of British trade unionism's most confident generations, and their decline, as state provision absorbed the function on the dominant order's terms, tracks the absorption mechanism that the economics of elaboration predicts.

The Italian case is the negative plate from which the notebooks were printed, and its details have recurred through this article because the category was built to explain them: a working class in the northern factories producing, in the council movement, organisers of genuine brilliance; a peasantry in the south mediated by a hostile or indifferent rural stratum; a national culture whose traditional intellectuals remained, in their majority, estranged from the popular classes whose world they might have elaborated; and a party founded to remedy the deficiency, suppressed before its schools and presses could mature. Italy is the case in which every variable of the scheme can be seen failing or succeeding at once, which is why a glossary article can be built, as this one has been, almost entirely from its evidence while stating a concept of general application.

## Credentialism: the stratum's wall

Every stratum the article has examined defends the conditions of its reproduction, and the modern form of that defence is the credential: the licensed monopoly over entry to a function, policed by examinations the stratum sets, in institutions the stratum staffs, justified by standards the stratum defines. The functional account reads credentialism without indignation and without innocence. A class's organisers must be formed and certified somehow, and examinations can be genuine instruments of competence; they are simultaneously the wall by which an established stratum converts its position into a hereditary advantage, metering the channel through which subaltern talent must pass and charging the passage, in the coin of years and conformity, at rates the stratum itself collects. The notebooks' contempt for the merely rhetorical formation of the Italian classical school, its production of a caste fluent in dead languages and unemployable in the modern world, is the audit of a credential system whose function had decayed into pure reproduction of the stratum itself, detached from any living class's requirement. Against it Gramsci sets the demand that education be judged by the functions it actually equips its graduates to perform, a demand that retains its edge against every system in which the certificate has become the product and the function an afterthought.

Ivan Illich's attack on the schooling system, a generation later, pressed a neighbouring charge with a pamphleteer's force: that the school's deepest product is the lesson of its own necessity, a population taught to confuse learning with instruction and to deliver its children to the certified for every significant formation. Read through the present category, Illich's polemic is a description of the traditional stratum's perfected form, a worldwide apparatus whose claim to a monopoly of legitimate learning reproduces, in the idiom of development, the clergy's ancient claim to a monopoly of legitimate salvation, down to the detail that its certificates are required for employments whose tasks they do not measure. The functional account parts from Illich at the remedy, since abolishing the wall in imagination does not abolish the functions the wall surrounds, and a class that smashed the credential system without elaborating organisers of its own would find the functions performed, within a decade, by a new stratum recruited in the old ways and less accountable than the one displaced. The notebooks' question survives both men's polemics, and it is the question this article has asked of every institution it has visited: the wall will be staffed by someone's organisers, formed in someone's schools, in someone's interest, and politics begins where a society stops pretending otherwise.

## The technocracy temptation: when the organisers claim the throne

One rival reading of the managerial stratum has shadowed the category since the middle of the last century, and confronting it isolates what the functional account distinctively claims. James Burnham's thesis of the managerial revolution held that the organisers were ceasing to be any class's servants and were becoming, through their control of production's direction, the ruling class of the emerging order in their own right; the stratum's function, on this reading, is not performed for a class but accumulates into one. The notebooks' scheme and Burnham's begin from the same observation, the visible ascent of the salaried organiser, and diverge on the question the function test exists to answer: does the stratum's work remain organic to a class outside itself, or has the organ become the body? The functional answer proceeds by the audit. Follow the training and the property: the managers are formed in schools the owners endow, promoted by boards the owners elect, enriched in instruments whose value is the owners' residual claim, and dismissed, in the crises that test all such questions, at the owners' pleasure and in the owners' interest. A stratum that can be purged by the class it supposedly displaced has announced its own classification. Burnham's managers held the levers; the audit asks whose hands are on the levers' owners, and the answer returned, in the century since the thesis was framed, is the answer the notebooks give in advance: control of operations is a function, and functions are performed for the classes whose development sustains the performers.

The confrontation leaves a residue the honest exposition should keep. There are formations in which the organising stratum's corporate power grows so great, its monopoly of the society's connective work so complete, that the distinction between serving a class and succeeding it becomes a question about timescales rather than about kinds; the bureaucracies of the planned economies posed the question in that form, and the series treats their analysis in the cluster on the Soviet debates. The functional scheme does not flinch from the residue. It predicts that a stratum detached from the discipline of the class that elaborated it, neither replenished from below nor answerable to an apparatus of that class's own, will congeal into a traditional stratum within a single lifetime, experiencing its privileges as the order of nature and defending them with the tenacity the clergy taught the world to expect. Gouldner's new class and Burnham's managers are, in that prediction's light, the same temptation stated at different altitudes: the organ's perennial dream of being the body, which the category was written to describe and which its author, watching the apparatus of his own movement from a prison cell, had more occasion to ponder than most theorists of his century.

## What the category asks of its reader

A concept that classifies by function ends by classifying its reader's world, and the exposition can close by making the demand explicit. The reader of these pages consumes, every day of an ordinary life, the output of the strata this article has audited: the news selected and framed by connective workers inside media apparatuses, the expertise certified by inherited institutions, the guidance of professionals whose credentials a stratum issued, the entertainment elaborated by cultural industries, the instruction of children delivered through a graded scholastic machine. None of that output is discredited by having an address; the functional account is not an invitation to the lazy suspicion that everything is propaganda, since it applies its questions to the reader's own side with the same indifference it applies to every other. It is an invitation to literacy of a specific kind: to ask of any voice that presumes to organise one's understanding the three questions in their order, what work is this doing, whose coherence does the work sustain, and was the position that speaks created by the class it serves or inherited from an order before it. A reader who acquires the habit will find the public world rearranging itself, not into villains and heroes, but into apparatuses and strata, formations and conversions, channels and walls, and will have acquired, in the strictest sense the notebooks recognise, an intellectual instrument: the capacity to elaborate one's own experience into coherence rather than renting the elaboration, ready made and unexamined, from the strata whose business is supplying it.

## The staff officer: a stratum in uniform

One further portrait belongs in the gallery because it tests the scheme against the institution readers least associate with intellectual life: the professional officer corps, and above all its general staff. The modern staff was an invention of the same decades that produced the research university, and it answered the same problem, the organisation of a complexity no gifted amateur could hold. Its members were formed in dedicated academies whose curricula joined engineering, history, languages, and the mathematics of logistics; they elaborated, in war games, mobilisation timetables, and campaign studies, the strategic conception of the state that employed them; and they reproduced themselves through a corporate discipline, a literature of professional journals, and a jurisdiction over entry as jealous as any faculty's. By every clause of the function test this is an intellectual stratum: elaborated by the development of the national state and its ruling bloc, performing the organising and connective work of that bloc's coercive arm, and graded from the cartographers and railway schedulers at its base to the theorists of war at its summit, one of whom produced a treatise on war that stands among the nineteenth century's works of philosophy. The uniform conceals the classification from common sense, which reserves the word intellectual for men whose weapons are pens, but the scheme is indifferent to the instrument: what is organised, and for whom, decides.

The portrait earns its place by the behaviour it predicts. A staff corps displays, in concentrated form, the two strata's characteristic pathologies. As an organic apparatus it elaborates its bloc's conception so thoroughly that the conception hardens into a plan, and the plan into a fate its makers serve after its premises have died, as the mobilisation timetables of the Great War served theirs. As a corporate body it defends its jurisdiction, its academies, its promotion ladders, and its honour with the traditional stratum's tenacity, experiencing every civilian audit as an assault on competence itself. And in the crises that audit all strata, the corps' transfer record writes the bloc's history in advance: the officer corps that carries its organisation intact into a successor regime announces that regime's real continuities more reliably than any constitution, while the corps that fragments, partisans of the old order against converts to the new, marks the exact line along which the historical bloc is breaking. Armies are usually counted in divisions when societies are weighed. The functional account suggests counting their staffs as well, and asking of the men who plan a state's wars the same three questions this article has asked of its priests and its professors, since the answers locate a part of the state's mind that the order of battle leaves out.

The staff portrait also closes a circle in the category's own history, because the war that made the modern staff ubiquitous also made its analyst. The generation of organisers from which Gramsci came was formed in the trenches and the factories of a total war that had conscripted every stratum this article has surveyed: professors certifying causes, engineers organising production, clergy blessing regiments, journalists manufacturing the day's world, and officers elaborating the plans that consumed them all. Watching the strata perform their functions under that pressure, and watching the workers' movement improvise counter organisers in the councils of the war's last years, the young editor learned the lesson the notebooks later generalised: that a society's coherence in crisis is the work of its intellectual strata, and that a class which has elaborated none of its own enters the crisis directed by the strata of others. The theory of the organic intellectual is that lesson written out as a definition, and the staff officer, planning in his academy the movements of millions, is its most unlikely and most confirming instance.

## For students and exam candidates

Examiners reward the functional definition and punish the compliment, so the revision task is to make the function test reflexive. Learn the definition in its three parts, be able to run the grid on an occupation the paper invents for you, and prepare the two distinctions markers probe hardest: activity versus function, and organic versus traditional as relations to a formation rather than as praise and blame. The commonest losing answer celebrates a thinker's humble origins or denounces a profession's privilege; the winning answer asks what work the position performs and whose development elaborated it, then argues the hard case, the teacher or the priest, through both columns before concluding. Structured notes serve this topic unusually well, since the material is a scheme rather than a narrative: keep the function test, the grid, and the grades in a single study card you can redraw from memory, using the notes at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) to hold the scheme beside the quotations and cases your syllabus requires. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

This concept teaches best against its own misreading, and the seminar room supplies the misreading free of charge in the first ten minutes. Open by collecting the group's spontaneous classifications of a dozen occupations, then run the function test in public and let the grid overturn the room's certainties in its own order: the consultant before the professor, the official before the poet. The exercise's pedagogical payload is the discovery that classification is a discipline rather than a taste. Follow with the institution the students inhabit: a school or university read through the scheme, its positions graded and its channels mapped, converts an abstract doctrine into an audit the class performs on its own formation, and the discussion of who in the room is being trained for which grade of intellectual function will sustain a term's honest argument. Set the strongest objection as the essay title, since the breadth challenge is the one students must answer rather than recite past. ## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For research purposes the category is best treated as an audit protocol with an archive attached. Its open problems are empirical: the lower grades of historical strata remain drastically understudied beside their summits, the transfer records of professions across regime changes await systematic comparison, and the economics of elaboration, who paid for which stratum's formation and at what price in independence, is a research programme rather than a settled literature. Journalists will find the function test a serviceable discipline for the daily problem of expertise: before quoting an authority, establish the position's elaboration, the institute's funding, the profession's jurisdiction, and report the coordinates with the quotation, so that the reader receives the elaboration and its address together. Historians of the present will note that the scheme's most demanding exercise, detecting a stratum's passage from organic to traditional as the formation that created it ages, is performable on living institutions with contemporary records, provided the classifications are dated and the evidence standards of the audit are kept. The adjacent question of the strata's own class position, whether the organisers constitute a class in themselves, is pursued in [the neighbouring inquiry into the petty bourgeoisie and middle class](/2011/12/01/petty-bourgeoisie-and-middle-class/).

## The white collar grades

Between the manager and the manual worker, the twentieth century interposed a vast new population whose classification the scheme settles with ease and whose existence it explains better than any rival: the clerks, the sales staff, the bookkeepers, the stenographers and their successors at the keyboard, the whole white collar world of the office. C. Wright Mills, in his portrait of that world at mid century, described its occupants as a new stratum of employees who neither own nor direct, who sell a trained capacity to handle symbols, people, and paper, and who experience their work as the performance of other men's purposes in an idiom of personal advancement. Translated into the present vocabulary, Mills had located the lower grades of capital's intellectual apparatus at the moment of their mass production. The office worker performs connective and administering work, routing the orders, the accounts, the correspondence, and the records through which an enterprise coheres, at a grade far below the elaboration of strategy and only one remove above the routine the scheme assigns to the stratum's base. The position was elaborated entirely by the development of the large corporation and the modern state bureau, it is recruited through a scholastic channel built for the purpose, the commercial school and the business course, and its self understanding, the clerk's pride in a clean collar and a salary's respectability, reproduces the autonomy effect at the level of the wage: a sense of standing apart from the manual classes that the work's actual function, administering a class's enterprise from inside its lowest intellectual grade, does nothing to justify.

The white collar case matters to the exposition because it demonstrates the scheme's temperature: nothing in the classification is an insult, and nothing is a compliment, and the same test that promotes the consultant past the poet demotes no one, since demotion is a concept belonging to the vocabulary of prestige the scheme has discarded. The clerk is an intellectual of the lowest grade of a determinate apparatus, exactly as the elementary teacher is an intellectual of the lowest grade of another, and the symmetry between the two figures, so unlike in their uniforms and their unions, is the kind of perception the category exists to produce. It also supplies the missing term in the article's account of scale. When the objection complains that the category includes almost everyone, the honest reply includes a census: in the developed economies the lower grades of the intellectual function, the administrators and connectors and record keepers, do indeed employ a substantial fraction of the whole workforce, and a theory of modern society that cannot see that fraction as an intellectual stratum, organised, graded, credentialed, and attached, has declined to describe the society's actual machinery of coherence.

## The highest grade and its economy

At the pyramid's summit the functions of creation are governed by an economy of their own, and the audit's questions illuminate it as remorselessly as they illuminate the base. Creation requires leisure purchased from somewhere: the patron's court, the church's benefice, the university's chair, the foundation's grant, the publisher's advance, and each source of purchase is a relation between the creator and the classes whose surplus sustains the creating. The systems differ in the directness of the leash. The court poet's dependence was personal and legible; the modern grant economy's dependence is institutional and statistical, exercised through the composition of panels, the fashion of programmes, and the career structure that rewards the fundable question, so that the summit's celebrated freedom is real at the level of conclusions and managed at the level of agendas, exactly as the industrial laboratory's was found to be. The academies and prize systems complete the apparatus as the stratum's self government: bodies through which the summit certifies its own succession, defines the standards by which creation is recognised, and defends the jurisdiction against the uninvited, performing for the republic of letters the same corporate functions the guild performed for the crafts.

None of this is an accusation, and the scheme's discipline forbids the tone of one. Every civilisation purchases its creation somehow, and the alternatives to patronage and endowment are not purity but other purchasers. The functional point is narrower and harder: the summit's output bears the marks of its economy in its agenda, its genres, and its silences, and a history of thought that reads only the arguments, without auditing the fellowships and the chairs and the lists of patrons, is studying the stratum's self portrait in place of its anatomy. Gramsci's notebooks conduct the audit on the Italian summit of his day with a clinician's calm, tracing how the peninsula's commanding intellects were formed, pensioned, and deployed, and the exercise remains the model for any reader who wants to know why an age's highest thought took the shape it did. The creators, on this showing, are the stratum's most expensive product and its least autonomous, which is the final inversion the function test performs upon the prestige table it was built to replace.

## From the corporate to the national: the stratum as agent of a wider passage

The deepest service the stratum performs for its class is one the grid cannot display in a row, because it concerns the class's relation to all the others. A class begins, in the notebooks' developmental sketch, at the economic corporate level: conscious of its immediate interests, organised to defend them, and indifferent or hostile to every wider claim. It becomes capable of direction, of leading a society rather than merely bargaining within it, only when its programme is elaborated beyond its own boundaries, when its intellectuals translate its interests into a conception under which allied classes and strata can recognise their own futures, and its particular cause can present itself, credibly, as the general cause. Gramsci names the passage catharsis, the movement from the purely economic to the ethico political, and the agents of the passage are precisely the figures this article has classified. Only an elaborating stratum can perform the translation, since it requires the class's experience to be raised into a universal idiom, its demands restated as principles of order, its historians and jurists and poets set to work upon the national material. The Risorgimento's failure is once more the negative proof: a movement whose intellectuals never became organic to a national popular class never performed the translation, and its programme remained the property of a section, administered after victory by the moderate personnel of the old order it had failed to displace.

The passage explains a subtlety in the category that flat readings miss: the organic intellectual's function is not exhausted by serving the class's existing consciousness, since the function includes remaking that consciousness at a higher level of generality, often against the class's own corporate instincts. The union official who argues his members out of a sectional advantage for the sake of an alliance, the party theorist who disciplines a movement's spontaneity into a national strategy, the jurist who drafts a class's claims as universal rights: each performs the elaborating work at the point where it costs the class something immediate, and each is recognisable, in the record, by the friction the work generates with the class's own base. A compliment based category would deny the title to such figures at exactly the moments they exercise it most consequentially. The functional category awards it there first, which is as good a summary as this article can offer of why the distinction between the two usages is worth the twenty pages it has taken to draw.

## The notebooks themselves

A final exhibit requires no research, since the reader has been holding it throughout. The prison notebooks are the work of an organic intellectual performing his function under the condition of its total obstruction: cut off from the class whose organiser he had been, forbidden its press, its schools, and its assemblies, the author converted the cell into the one apparatus left to him and elaborated, for a movement he could not address, the instruments by which that movement's future strata might understand their own production. The prosecution's demand at his trial, as the record is usually summarised, was that this brain be prevented from functioning for twenty years; the sentence's failure is the text the series exists to expound. The category this article has defined classifies its own author's last work without strain: elaboration addressed to a class in formation, conducted at the highest grade, in the teeth of the apparatus of the order that had elaborated its own intellectuals for centuries and understood exactly what it was imprisoning. A definition that can contain its author's case at the extremity of his circumstances has earned the generality it claims, and the reader who now turns to the questions below holds the instrument in the form in which it was left: unfinished, unsystematic, and sharp.

## The historical bloc: how the two strata combine

In any actual society the two kinds of intellectual do not confront each other as pure types across a frontier; they are combined, under the direction of a fundamental class, into the formation Gramsci calls a historical bloc, the concrete unity in which a social order's material structure and its elaborating superstructures hold each other in place. The bloc is the unit the whole analysis finally serves. A dominant class rules its bloc not by staffing every position with its own organic creations, which no class has ever had the numbers to do, but by setting the terms on which the inherited strata work: endowing their institutions, honouring their hierarchies, absorbing their ablest members into its direction, and leaving the remainder to administer a common sense whose centre of gravity its own organisers have fixed. The traditional intellectual inside a functioning bloc is thus neither an enemy nor a servant in any simple sense; he is a component, performing inherited functions inside a direction he did not choose, and the stability of the whole arrangement is measurable by the ease with which he experiences that performance as independence. Crises of the bloc announce themselves, on this analysis, as crises of its intellectual combination before they appear as crises of its economy or its state: the inherited strata begin to defect or to elaborate alternatives, the dominant class's own organisers lose their connective grip, and the common sense the bloc reproduced starts to come apart at the level of the school, the pulpit, and the editorial page. The reader who has followed the argument this far possesses the diagnostic instrument entire: to locate a society's bloc, find its strata, run the audit, and watch which of them are ceasing to believe the bloc's account of their independence.

## Congresses, conferences, and the calendar of a stratum

Movements manufacture intellectuals in gatherings as well as in schools, and the congress deserves its place among the mechanisms for the peculiar work it performs. A congress assembles a class's organisers in one hall, obliges them to elaborate a common line out of local experiences that arrive mutually unintelligible, and returns them to their districts as carriers of a coherence none of them possessed on arrival; it is the collective intellectual convening itself, an episode in which the stratum becomes conscious of its own composition. The preparation of a congress, the drafting of theses, the circulation of discussion bulletins, the education of delegates in the questions to be decided, is elaborating labour of the highest practical grade, and the quality of a movement's congresses is a fair proxy for the quality of the stratum its mechanisms are producing. The dominant orders hold their equivalents in the idioms of their own strata, the professional conference, the synod, the judicial colloquium, the Davos of every guild, and the audit reads them identically: as the periodic self assembly of an organising workforce, renewing its common sense, promoting its grades, and disciplining its deviants in the name of its standards. To ask of any such gathering the three questions, what does it organise, whose coherence does it renew, and who elaborated the body that convenes it, is to be given, in miniature and on a date, the whole anatomy this article has been constructing at the scale of centuries.

## The stratum in the census

The scheme finally predicts a silhouette that statistics can check. If the intellectual function is a real division of labour, then the occupational record of a developing society should show the function's grades growing in their characteristic order: the administering and connective grades swelling fastest as production and administration complexify, the creative summit growing slowly, the inherited strata persisting across the whole transformation with their relative weight declining and their corporate forms intact. The census tables of the industrial centuries show exactly that silhouette, in every country that kept them: the clerks and teachers and officials multiplying out of all proportion to the population, the professions closing their ranks behind examinations as they grow, the clergy holding their absolute numbers while the society doubles around them. A theory built in a prison cell, from the history of a single peninsula's failure, should not be able to predict the shape of occupational statistics on other continents; that it does so, in outline, is the kind of confirmation the category's author, who distrusted all confirmations that arrived as compliments, would have accepted from an auditor and from no one else.

## Two portraits from the record

The Prussian historians of the nineteenth century offer the record's cleanest portrait of a stratum performing an organic function at the summit grade, because their work's destination was proclaimed by its authors as a duty. The scholars who elaborated the national narrative of German unification, in the seminar and the archive, were not decorating a process occurring elsewhere; they were supplying the rising national class with its memory, its legitimacy, and its map of friends and enemies, and they were formed for the purpose inside universities the state had rebuilt as instruments of national formation. Their successors inherited the chairs, the methods, and the prestige after the class project that had animated them was complete, and the portrait's second panel, the same professoriate certifying a later empire's war in the manifesto already described, is the first panel aged into tradition: positions created for one formation's elaboration, occupied two generations on by a stratum whose skills remained and whose world had been replaced. Between the panels lies the whole biography of a stratum, from organ to inheritance, compressed into a single institution's archive.

The southern Italian lawyer supplies the portrait at the opposite grade and in the opposite key. In the agrarian south the young man of a peasant or artisan family who acquired a law degree entered the stratum that mediated the entire society: pleading the village's causes in the provincial capital, drafting its contracts, conducting its feuds into the courts, and connecting its life, thread by thread, to the landowners, the prefecture, and the state. Gramsci's southern writings treat this figure, in his thousands, as the decisive intellectual of the agrarian bloc, the human hinge on which the domination of the southern countryside turned, since the landowner who never met his peasants ruled them through the lawyer who met them daily. The portrait's lesson is about scale and recruitment. The bloc's power did not rest on the celebrated intellects of the capital but on an army of minor elaborators recruited from the dominated population itself, whose formation in the state's law schools converted the south's ablest sons into the south's administrators, and whose defection, had it ever been organised, would have dismantled the bloc more thoroughly than any rising in the fields. When the notebooks insist that the southern question is a question of intellectuals, the lawyer's portrait is what the insistence means.

## A note on sources and translations

The definition's home is the twelfth of the prison notebooks, the sustained set of notes on the formation of the intellectuals composed in the early nineteen thirties, supplemented by the notes on Machiavelli and the modern state for the party as collective intellectual, by the unfinished essay on the southern question for the rural mediating strata, and by scattered notes on education, journalism, and literature throughout the notebooks for the grades, the schools, and the cultural apparatus. The textual condition of the material matters to its readers. Gramsci wrote under censorship, in notebooks that were preserved by others, and the postwar editions arranged the notes by theme, so that the argument of the twelfth notebook reaches most readers as an essay rather than as the sequence of drafts it is; the critical edition prepared by Valentino Gerratana in the nineteen seventies restored the notebooks' order and wording, and the fuller English translations since have progressively returned the argument to its workshop. Quotations in this article have been kept to the single short fragment whose wording is stable across the standard translations, and everything else has been paraphrased, since the definition's force lies in its distinctions and not in any sentence's music. Readers pursuing the sources should begin with the twelfth notebook entire, resist the anthology habit of extracting the famous sentence about all men being intellectuals from the argument that disciplines it, and treat the sentence, as this article has, as the ground clearing for a definition rather than as the definition itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is an organic intellectual?

An organic intellectual is someone whose social position consists of organising and elaborating work performed for a particular class: giving that class coherence, a programme, a legal and technical apparatus, and an awareness of its own role. The classification depends entirely on the function exercised and on the class whose development created the position, never on the person's birthplace, schooling, or opinions. The stratum arises from within the class as it emerges, rather than being hired from outside, and it includes far more than writers and scholars: the industrial technician, the company lawyer, and the association official all qualify when their work organises the class that produced their posts. Its defining contrast is with the traditional intellectual, whose position was inherited from an older formation.

### Q: What is a traditional intellectual?

A traditional intellectual occupies a position elaborated by an earlier social formation and carried across into the present order: the clergy are the standing example, with academics and men of letters close behind. The position's skills are portable between regimes, its corporate organisation survives the fall of the class that originally produced it, and its occupants consequently experience themselves as an autonomous caste answerable only to their discipline. Gramsci treats that experienced independence as an illusion, but an operative one, since it wins the stratum bargaining power and public trust. The category describes a historical relation rather than a temperament, so a traditional intellectual may hold any politics at all; what defines the position is inheritance, continuity, and the self image of standing above the contending classes while performing functions every one of them needs.

### Q: Did Gramsci say everyone is an intellectual?

He wrote that all men are intellectuals in the sense that everyone exercises intellectual activity, thinking, generalising, and holding some conception of the world, and the sentence is often quoted as though it dissolved the category. In the twelfth notebook it does the opposite work. It clears the ground for the operative distinction, since the same passage adds that not all men have in society the function of intellectuals. Intellectuals as a stratum are those whose position is the function itself: organising, elaborating, administering, connecting. The popular quotation stops one clause too early, converting a sociological classification into a democratic compliment. Restoring the second clause restores the theory, because everything in the scheme, the grades, the organic and traditional division, the party as collective intellectual, hangs on the difference between thinking, which nobody can be prevented from doing, and occupying the social post whose work is thought's organisation.

### Q: Is an organic intellectual just a working class academic?

No, although the confusion is understandable, because the two classifications cross in real lives. A working class academic may perform an organic function, if the scholarship is elaborated inside a movement's institutions and organises that movement's world; equally, the same person may occupy one of the most traditional posts in the society, a chair inherited from a vanished order and governed by a discipline's internal standards. Origin explains how someone arrived at a post. It cannot say what the post does, which class elaborated it, or whose coherence its output sustains, and those are the questions the category asks. Conversely, the category's most unambiguous specimens include men and women of privileged birth whose organising work for capital never troubles their self image as neutral professionals. The phrase working class academic describes a biography. Organic intellectual describes a function, and the whole point of keeping the second phrase is to stop the first from doing its work.

### Q: What is the party as collective intellectual?

The phrase names the claim that a political party, for the class it organises, performs collectively the same work that organic intellectuals perform individually: elaborating a programme, educating a membership, preserving a memory across defeats, and coordinating direction over time. Gramsci's figure for the party in this role is the Modern Prince, an organism rather than a person, in which a collective will begins to take shape. The description is functional, not honorary. It implies that a party's internal life, its press, its schools, its arguments with itself, is the very activity by which its class thinks, so that suppressing that life damages the organ of the class's awareness. It also implies the standard by which parties fail: when the apparatus substitutes itself for the class whose organ it was built to be, the collective intellectual has congealed into a bureaucracy performing the function for itself.

### Q: How does a class produce its own intellectuals?

Production begins with emergence: a class arising inside production creates, together with itself, the organisers its development requires, because its enterprises need direction, its interests need legal form, and its members need training and a shared account of their situation. The mechanisms are apprenticeship within the class's own activity, a press that forms writers and readers together, schools maintained by the movement for its cadres, and the dense associational life in which thousands learn to keep minutes, funds, and discipline. Propertyless classes run the same mechanisms against scarcity, funding them from wages and sacrifice, which is why their strata form slowly and leak talent upward to better paid apparatuses. The peasantry marks the limit case in the notebooks: a class that elaborates no stratum of its own remains mediated by intellectuals other classes produced, and its movements arrive in history already translated by foreign hands.

### Q: Are engineers and managers intellectuals in this sense?

Yes, and they are among the scheme's clearest specimens, which is the result readers resist hardest. The engineer organises the labour process itself, deciding the layout, sequence, and pace through which other people's work proceeds, and the position was created outright by industrial production's development. The manager performs the directing and coordinating work of the enterprise as a salaried function, trained in schools the order built for that purpose and armed with an ideology of neutral technique. Neither writes for the reviews, and neither needs to: the category classifies the organising and elaborating work a position does for the class that elaborated it, and by that measure the technician and the administrator outrank, in functional clarity, the poet whose chair descends from a monastery. What excludes a person is not overalls or a salary but the absence of organising connection, work that neither directs a group's activity nor elaborates its view of the world.

### Q: Why does the autonomy of traditional intellectuals matter?

Because the belief in autonomy is itself a political force, whatever its truth. A stratum that experiences itself as independent, and is widely believed to be independent, is courted rather than commanded by every class that needs its services, extracts guarantees for its institutions as the price of cooperation, and lends any cause it blesses the prestige of disinterest. Its members can therefore mediate between classes with an authority no party official commands, as the priest does in the village and the professor does in the republic of letters. The belief also shapes strategy for everyone else. A rising class that mistakes the self image for a fact neglects to elaborate organisers of its own, expecting neutral expertise to administer its order, and discovers in government that the expertise arrived with a world attached. Gramsci's formula holds both sides together: the autonomy is an illusion as sociology and a reality as power, and analysis must keep the two registers separate to explain either.

### Q: How is the concept used in education theory?

Education adopted the term more widely than any other field, and altered it in transit. In its strong uses, the category directs attention to the school as an apparatus that produces intellectuals in grades, and to the teacher as the broadest grade of the organising stratum, which recovers for a routinely patronised profession its actual structural weight. In its weaker and commoner uses, the function test is replaced by a test of commitment: the organic intellectual of the classroom becomes the teacher whose sympathies are engaged and correct, and the concept is spent as a compliment staffrooms award themselves. The applied literature descending from Freire preserves more of the original structure than the compliment does, since its question remains what the school's work does and for whom. The discipline's examinations and syllabuses, meanwhile, go on illustrating the scheme's grading argument whether the term is used or not, assigning children to the level of intellectual function an order intends them to occupy.

### Q: Can you become an organic intellectual deliberately?

Partly, and the distinction inside the answer is the concept working as intended. A person can deliberately choose organising and elaborating work, seek formation, and attach that work to a class's project; nothing in the scheme makes the stratum a fate, and movements are built from such decisions. What no decision can supply is the relation that makes the work organic, since that relation exists only where a class's own institutions elaborate, sustain, and direct the position. The solitary volunteer with a correct intention and no apparatus behind the intention is a candidate for a function, not yet its performer, which is why the theory treats the building of collective bodies, parties, presses, schools, as the real answer to the individual's question. Becoming is therefore best understood as a two sided process: persons offer themselves, and classes, through their institutions, decide by elaborating posts whether the offer becomes a stratum or remains a biography.

### Q: How does the function test classify a celebrity intellectual?

By connection, not by fame. The test asks what organising work the figure's position performs and for which class or apparatus that work is organic, and celebrity as such supplies no answer, since visibility is an audience rather than a function. In practice the classification usually attaches to the platform rather than the personality: the column in a newspaper owned within one bloc, the fellowship at an institute founded to elaborate a programme, the chair on a broadcaster's panel, each a position inside an apparatus whose coherence the performance sustains. Where a celebrated figure's pronouncements organise nothing, belong to no apparatus, and elaborate no class's understanding beyond the maintenance of a personal brand, the test returns its most deflationary verdict: gifts without an intellectual function, a performance in search of a position. The severity is deliberate, and symmetrical, demoting the famous whose work connects to nothing while crediting the anonymous official whose work connects a class to itself.

### Q: What should a student separate between an organic and a traditional intellectual?

Separate the criterion from the biography, and the classification of a post from the judgement of a person. The criterion is function: an organic intellectual is defined by organising and elaborating work for the class whose development created the post, while a traditional intellectual is defined by occupying a post an earlier formation created and bequeathing to the present its skills, its corporate organisation, and its self image of independence. Biography, parentage, schooling, accent, and private opinion belong to the person's history and settle nothing in the classification, which is why the scheme can place two occupants of identical chairs in different categories across different periods. Judgement is a further separation: to locate a thinker in a stratum is to explain the position of the thought, not to grade its truth, and the strongest work in the tradition, the notebooks on Croce above all, locates with one hand while arguing, seriously and at length, with the other.

### Q: Why is the party called a collective intellectual in this scheme?

Because the work it names is the same work, done by a body instead of a person. An individual organic intellectual elaborates a class's experience into coherence within one lifetime and one skull, and the product dies with its maker unless institutions preserve it. The party, in the scheme, is the institution built to preserve and continue exactly that labour: holding memory across defeats, educating successors as a byproduct of action, correcting errors through organised argument, and holding a strategy steady across the decades a class's emergence requires. Calling it an intellectual is therefore a precise claim about function rather than a compliment about cleverness, and it carries the unflattering corollaries precision brings: a party that stops elaborating, that forbids the argument by which it thinks, has not become more disciplined but less intelligent, in the only sense of intelligence the theory recognises, the organised capacity of a class to understand its situation and act on the understanding.

### Q: How do schools produce intellectuals of different grades in the argument?

Through the structure of the system itself, in the argument, rather than through any conspiracy of teachers. A society maintains schools in kinds and levels, and the levels correspond to grades of intellectual function: a broad elementary floor that equips everyone with the literacy an order requires, technical and vocational tracks that form the organisers of production and administration, and a selective humanistic summit that forms the directing strata in the idiom of inherited culture. The state, as the system's maintainer, is therefore in the business of manufacturing intellectuals in differentiated quantities, and the complexity of a civilisation's intellectual apparatus can be read off the number and height of its scholastic grades. Assignment between the tracks presents itself as a measurement of children's ability, and functions, in the analysis, as the mechanism deciding which class's children are prepared for which grade of organising work, a generation before the posts themselves are filled.

### Q: What would show an intellectual stratum changing its function?

The signs are institutional before they are rhetorical. A stratum is changing its function when its training pipeline is captured or rebuilt by a different class's apparatus, when its funding lines move from one bloc's endowments to another's, when its associations begin to elaborate a programme at odds with the order that inherited it, or when, at a crisis, its members transfer their services wholesale to a successor they once served only on terms. The clergy across the dissolution of the feudal world exhibit the full sequence in reverse, passing from a living class's organ to a survival negotiating its rents. Rhetoric is the least reliable sign, since strata experience and proclaim continuity most loudly at the moment their function inverts; the audit therefore follows syllabuses, budgets, appointments, and conduct under rupture, and dates every classification it makes, treating the labels as properties of a relation in a period rather than as the eternal character of a profession.
