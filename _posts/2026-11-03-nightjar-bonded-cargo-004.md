## Counting Bridges

Pell Anders drove with both hands high on the wheel and the cab heater ticking against his knees, and still the cold came in. It came through the door seal on the left side where the rubber had perished the winter before, and it came up through the floor around the pedals, and it settled in the seams of his coat while the road climbed. Outside the glass the night was thin and hard at altitude. The headlamps threw a short white road ahead of him and took the rest in pieces, stone wall, pine trunk, a marker post with its paint gone, then darkness again above a drop he could not see but could hear, because the river ran far below the road and its sound rose whenever the wind dropped. He kept his speed low. The sealed box behind the cab rode quiet. He had not loaded it, he had not watched it loaded, and he did not know what it held beyond the fact that it was sealed and that men who sealed things expected the seal to arrive unbroken.

The first bridge came where the road pinched between two shoulders of rock and the river forced itself through a narrow cut below. He felt it before he saw the parapet, a change in the sound under the tyres, the hollow ring that stone made when there was air and water beneath it instead of solid ground. He counted aloud, because counting aloud was how he had been taught to fix a thing in his mind when he was tired. One, he said, and the lamps found the low stone sides and the wet sheen on the deck, and the river flashed white once through a gap in the parapet and was gone. The cab shook lightly across the expansion gap at the far end. He let out a breath he had not meant to hold. Behind the bridge the road rose again into pine and cold, and the river sound sank away behind rock.

He had taken the instruction in a yard office with a stove that smoked and a clerk who did not look up from his ledger for longer than it took to say it. Count the bridges, the clerk had said. Count them and say the count at the pass, and say nothing else because there is nothing else for you to say. Pell had waited for the rest of it, for a name to ask for or a paper to hand over, and the clerk had turned a page as if the talk were finished. Pell had asked whether there was a manifest for him to carry. The clerk had said no. He had asked whether he should check the seal numbers against anything at the depot. The clerk had said the depot would check what the depot checked. He had asked what he should do if the road was closed above the pass, and the clerk had at last raised his eyes and said that if it were closed Pell was to turn back and count the bridges on the way down as well, and say that count too. That was the whole of it.

The second bridge was a newer span, iron railed, with a level deck that sang under the tyres in a higher note than the stone bridge had made. Two, he said. A gust took the cab sideways by an inch and he corrected without thinking. The third bridge came close behind it where the road crossed a side gully full of meltwater that ran fast and brown even at night, showing in the lamps as a hurried glitter between rocks. Three, he said. He was a careful driver and he knew his carefulness was one of the reasons he was used on this route. He did not drink on a run. He kept his cab clean and his log plain and he had never once asked a loader what a crate weighed or where a consignment was bound after the depot. For a long time he had told himself that this discretion was a professional virtue and that it was respected. On this road, with the instruction still sitting in his ears in the clerk's flat voice, he understood the shape of it differently.

Four came at the wide bend where the valley opened for a moment and he could see the lights of a hamlet far below, three windows and a yard lamp, small and steady in the dark. The bridge there crossed the main river again where it ran broad and slow over shingle, and the water made a wide pale sound that filled the cab for as long as the crossing lasted. He counted and drove on and the lights fell behind a shoulder of hill. He thought about the men who loaded his cab at the lower yard. They were polite to him. They offered tea when the stove was lit. They never used a surname for the man who sent the loads, and when Pell had once asked after a foreman who had stopped appearing at the yard, the talk around the stove had gone quiet in a way that taught him more than an answer would have done. He had not asked such a question again. He knew the road in all its weathers. What he did not know could have filled the sealed box behind him many times over, and he had begun to suspect that the not knowing was not an accident of his low place but the design of the whole arrangement, and that a man who knew nothing could lose nothing under questioning except a number.

The fifth bridge had a broken parapet on the downhill side where a rockfall had taken a length of stone away and a timber rail had been bolted in its place. He slowed for it out of habit, though the deck was sound. Five, he said, and heard how small his voice sounded in the cab, and disliked hearing it. The sixth was barely a bridge at all, a short stone arch over a culvert where a spring crossed under the road, and a driver who was tired or talking might have missed counting it. Pell did not miss it. Six, he said, with a brief and stubborn satisfaction. That was the value of him, he thought. Not strength, for there were stronger men at the yard. Not knowledge of papers, for he had never been shown the papers. It was this, the counting done right by a man too careful to miss a culvert arch in the dark, and the number carried in his head over a pass and spoken in the right place to the right lamp. A number could be carried by any honest man. It did not need a trusted man. The thought rode with him up the next long grade where the engine laboured and the lamps showed only wet rock.

Seven crossed the river at its narrowest, where the water went fast between black walls and threw spray up through the joints of the deck so that the tyres hissed on wet stone. Seven, he said, and wiped condensation from the inside of the glass with the back of his glove. Eight was the high bridge, the one every driver on the route spoke of with respect, a long span across a gorge where the river was a thread of sound far below and the wind never stopped. He drove it with his eyes on the far lamp, and the cab swayed once in a cross gust, and settled. Eight, he said on the far side, and only then let his shoulders drop. His tea flask was empty. His shoulders ached from holding the wheel against the wind. He had a sister in the depot town who kept his room when he was on the road and who believed he carried ordinary bonded goods for ordinary merchants, and he had never corrected her, because the only truth he could have offered in its place was that he did not know, and that sounded worse than the lie he had not quite told.

The ninth bridge lay below the pass, where the road began its long descent and the air grew fractionally warmer and carried a smell of woodsmoke from somewhere down the valley. It was an old stone bridge, wide enough for two carts in the days before cabs, with a shrine niche in the parapet that held nothing but a smooth stone and a twist of faded cloth. He crossed it slowly. Nine, he said. Nine bridges, and the count complete, and for a moment the simple completeness of it steadied him. He had done the one thing asked. The number sat whole in his mind. Whatever the number meant to the men who would hear it, and he understood that it must mean something, that no man pays for a count of bridges for the love of arithmetic, it was not his to carry further than the checkpoint. He would speak it and be emptied of it, and that emptying was both his safety and his shame, because a man emptied of his one fact has nothing left to sell and nothing left to protect himself with either.

The pass checkpoint was a hut of stone and timber set into the hillside where the road levelled before the descent, with a lamp burning in a glazed box above the door and a brazier outside that showed red between its bars. He brought the cab to a stop where he always stopped, with the front wheel at the painted stone, and he cut the engine and sat a moment in the sudden quiet while the metal ticked and cooled around him. The keeper came out with his collar up and a book under his arm that he did not open. Pell wound down the window and the cold entered whole, smelling of smoke and iron and the river far below. The keeper looked at the cab, at the sealed box, at Pell's face, and waited. Pell said the word for a completed run, the plain word the chain used, and then he said the number. Nine. The keeper nodded once and made no mark in the book, and wrote nothing anywhere that Pell could see. He raised a hand and stepped back toward the brazier. It was finished. It had taken four seconds, and it was the whole purpose of the night's driving, and no record of it existed anywhere except in two men's memories.

Pell wound up the window and started the engine and let the cab roll forward past the hut. In his mirror the checkpoint lamp hung steady and then began to shrink as the road bent, a small fixed point of light in the black hillside, and then the bend took it and it was behind him altogether. Ahead the road went down in long turns toward the depot town, and the river sound rose to meet him again from the dark below, bridge by bridge already counted and already behind him, and he drove down toward the lights with empty hands on the wheel and the number gone out of him.

## Four Officers Free

The briefing room in Carrowmere held the cold the way stone holds it after rain. The windows had been shut since morning and the air smelled of damp wool, lamp oil and paper that had been handled too often. Outside the glass the yard was dark and wet, and the gutters made a steady thin sound against the wall. Mira Kovac stood with her back to the stove because the stove gave almost no heat and the place nearest it was still the least cold place in the room. Her coat was wet at the shoulders. She had come up from the lower offices with ink on her fingers and a tiredness behind her eyes that sleep had not quite cleared, though her body felt rested and her hands felt steady. She was full and alert in the way she needed to be before work was given.

Arvo Leyden came in with a folder held flat against his chest so the rain on the landing would not touch it. He shut the door with his elbow and set the folder on the table and did not sit. On the table he laid out three bonded copies tied with different coloured cord, a depot plan folded to a single sheet, and a small card with four lines written in his own hand. He looked at Mira once, in the assessing way he used when he was deciding how much truth a job could carry, and then he began without any greeting beyond her name.

He said the chain was ready to be taken. For months the Office had watched restricted practice materials move through three Accord members on bonded paper that never quite agreed with the loads that arrived. Binding cords and seal blanks and carrier anchor pairs and practice grade alloys had passed under consignments that looked lawful at a distance and thinned when anyone stood close. The route gathered itself at the depot in Teshkar, high on the mountain road where the bonded stores served the pass. Leyden had set a single night for the taking. Not a search and not a warning visit. A rollup. Four positions struck at the same hour in three members, with arrests made together so no part of the chain could close its doors ahead of another part.

Then he named the cost plainly, before he named any detail of the plan, and Mira marked that he did so without softening it. The Office had four officers free and no more. Four officers for four positions, with no reserve and no second pair of hands to send when a position thinned. The fourth position would go to Reni Vosk, twenty one years old, a junior officer assigned to Mira for this work, on her first field position. If the night moved, or if the timing changed, or if support had to be pulled from one place to cover another, the thinnest position would be hers. Leyden said it flatly and let it stand on the table between them beside the cords and the plan. He did not add that Reni was keen, or that she was ready, or that keenness could stand in for experience. He left the fact as a fact.

Petra Dunmore had supplied the depot plan and Leyden unfolded it with care where the paper had worn at the crease. The depot stood with its warehouse to the south, its office corridor along the eastern side, its bonded stores behind locked grilles, and its yards falling away in two levels toward the road. Dunmore had marked the service entrance with a small cross in blue ink. It was a back door on the north side of the depot, narrow and set low in the wall where carts had scraped the stone for years. Above it there was a yard lamp that burned through the night shift, and the door itself had a latch that had to be lifted, not pushed, or it would catch and sing against the frame. Leyden put his finger on the cross and said Dunmore had checked the door against two consignment plans and found it in both. If Mira had to enter without using the main yard, that door was the way, and the latch wanted lifting in silence.

Reni would not be at that door. Reni would not be inside at all. Her position was the lower yard gate approach at the Teshkar depot, the sloping track where vehicles turned in from the mountain road and waited to be waved through to the lower yard. She was to hold the approach from the shadow of the gate post, with a radio at her ear and a clear view of the track and the gate itself. Watch and count and report. That was the whole of it. She was to note every vehicle that came and went, the hour by the depot clock, the direction, and whether the gate was opened or held shut. She was not to engage, not to step into the track to stop a car, or challenge a driver, or leave the approach to follow anyone on foot. Leyden repeated the limit twice, once for the plan and once for Mira, so there could be no generous reading of it later.

The rule that held the whole night together was simultaneity. No position moved early. No arrest was made ahead of the hour, however clean the chance looked, because one arrest warned the rest. A clerk taken in Carrowmere in the afternoon would empty a warehouse in Teshkar before dark. A driver held on the mountain road would send a count or a silence down the line that every other hand in the chain would understand. The four positions had to fall together or the work would scatter. It asked officers to watch wrongdoing pass in front of them and wait for a signal while the chance to act sat close enough to touch.

She asked the questions the job required and Leyden answered in his rationed way. Whether all four positions could be staffed on the night depended on two member services releasing local officers for cordon work without claiming a position for themselves. Whether Reni was ready to hold a position alone he turned back to Mira, saying readiness would be partly made by the instructions she was given and the clarity of their limits. Whether the chain ran on the night the Office expected, or on another night folded inside the expected one, depended on counts and chits Dunmore was still reconciling. Whether four arrests could be made simultaneous across three members, with three clocks and three kinds of local pride in the way, depended on radio discipline and on nobody improving the plan at the last hour. And who outside the Office knew the night was a question Leyden did not answer at all, except to close the folder a fraction tighter and say the list of those informed was short and would stay short.

Dunmore had sent a note with the plan, in her neat dissatisfied hand, saying that the bonded copies disagreed in small ways that might be copying and might be design. Mira read the note and thought of another set of papers in Melora, in [a hearing room where the weight in the air had stopped at a line on the floor](/2026/11/01/nightjar-edge-of-nothing-002/), and of the price that had been written against auditor Inez Calloway when Mira had signed where she was told to sign. The thought came and went without weight of its own. Older still, under it, was the memory of Edran Voss in Carrowmere, a dead wharf supervisor whose last ten minutes she still carried in cold moments when a room was too quiet. She did not dwell on either. She set the note down square with the plan and asked Leyden when she would see Reni.

He said she would see her at once, because he wanted the instructions to come from Mira and from no one else. Hearing a limit from the officer she was assigned to, who would be on the same radio net on the night, made it personal and harder to bend. Mira went out into the corridor where the lamps burned low and the boards complained underfoot, and found Reni waiting by the tall window with her coat buttoned to the throat and her hands clasped in front of her to keep them still.

Reni Vosk was slight and upright, with the careful neatness of someone who had dressed for inspection and then tried to look as if she had not. She had the Accord charter by heart in the way some officers had drill or prayer, taking its sentences at their word and expecting the world to answer them in kind. Mira liked her at once and felt the liking as an added responsibility rather than a comfort. She did not give her a speech. She took her to the side table where a copy of the depot plan lay weighted by a cup, and walked her through the ground in plain order.

She showed her the lower yard and the gate and the approach track curving in from the road. She placed her fingertip where Reni would stand, in the shadow clear of the lamp spill, where the gate post would break her outline and the track lay open ahead. She told her how to keep the radio warm against her body so its workings did not stiffen with cold, and how to speak in short reports and then be silent. Count the vehicles. Give the hour. Say whether the gate opened. If a car came through her position and kept going, she was to report it and hold. Not follow it. Not step out. Not try to be more than the eyes the plan needed at that gate. Mira said the last part twice, in the same tone, and watched Reni hear it.

Reni asked whether she should challenge a vehicle that tried the gate without a bonded chit. Mira said no. She was to report it and let the inner positions decide. Reni asked whether she should move if she heard trouble from the warehouse. Mira said no, unless the order came by radio in the agreed words, because a gate left empty was a road left open. When Mira asked her to say the position back, Reni did so without error, and ended with the limit in her own phrasing, that she was there to watch and report and not to engage. Her voice shook only on the final word, and she steadied it by pressing her clasped hands tighter.

Mira kept her encouragement short. She told her that holding a position was work and that doing only what the position asked was not doing less. Reni nodded, and some of the tightness left her shoulders. Behind the window the rain had eased and the yard lamps made pale rings on wet stone.

Back in the briefing room Leyden was waiting with the folder closed. He asked whether the instructions had been given and Mira said they had, in person, with the limits stated plainly. He asked whether Reni had understood her position and Mira said she had repeated it back without error. He gave her the bonded copies to study, the depot plan to keep, and the hour by which Dunmore would send the reconciled chits. Mira took the papers under her arm and went out to begin the ground work that would decide whether the plan on the table could survive the place it described.

## Three Sets of Paper

The records room at the Teshkar depot sat at the cold end of the building, where the stone had never learned to hold the heat of the stoves. Light came through two shuttered windows in ruled bands and fell across a table long enough for six clerks, though only one clerk worked there in the mornings. Outside the glass, the yard dropped away toward the lower gate, and beyond the gate the road began its long descent, bend below bend, until it vanished into a gorge where the river made a sound like paper being torn slowly, all day, without pause. Inside, the air smelled of lamp oil, damp wool, iron keys and the particular dust that lives in bound paper and rises whenever a heavy book is opened. Mira stood for a moment with her coat still on and let the room settle around her senses before she touched anything. A depot announces itself honestly in its records room. The manifests were already laid out for her in three piles, tied with three colours of cord, and a pot of tea was going cold beside them.

She had come up the mountain the evening before with Rask and a sealed case of copies, and had slept with the case under the bed, out of six years of bonded habit at the Valtessa quays rather than any fear for its safety. She still carried other things close as well. Edran Voss and his last ten minutes rode with her into cold rooms in particular, a weight picked up in Carrowmere and not yet set down, and the depot stone was cold enough that morning to remind her of him before she had taken her gloves off. She let the reminder pass, thought once of Inez Calloway, whose career she had signed away in Melora with a clean conscience and an unclean feeling ever since, and put both of them to one side of her mind, because the table in front of her held work that deserved the whole of her attention.

The method came first. Mira had learned as a customs inspector that deduction done before procedure is only opinion wearing a uniform, and she had no intention of forming an opinion about the chain until the paper had been made to stand up in rows. So she began with the first named method of the ground work, which was manifest comparison across the three members' bonded copies. Each member of the Accord keeps bonded paper in its own fashion, and the three piles on the table were a small exhibition of the differences. The Valtessan set was formal to the point of ceremony, every consignment entered under a running number with the seal of the dispatching house impressed beside the entry and a witness line ruled under each page whether a witness had signed it or not. The Dravin set was terse almost to rudeness, numbered forms with the consignment described in the fewest words the tariff allowed, the paper thin and grey and stamped rather than sealed. The Teshkar set was local work, handwritten throughout, the depot's own books and the chits pinned to them, the writing of clerks who knew the road personally. Three states, three customs services, three habits of record. Mira untied the cords one pile at a time and laid the three versions of the same consignment side by side wherever the running numbers allowed her to match them.

A bonded manifest proves less than laymen think and more than smugglers hope. It proves that a consignment of a stated description, in stated quantities, under stated seals, was presented at a stated place and accepted into bond by an officer who signed for the accepting. It does not prove what was inside the sealed boxes, or who owned them. What it proves is passage and custody, the chain of hands through which a thing has travelled, and for Mira's purposes passage and custody were the whole geography of the case. If a consignment could be followed through three sets of paper without a gap, the route existed on paper as the Office believed it existed on the road. If a gap appeared, the gap itself would be a fact, and often a better one than the entries around it.

She worked through the first consignment of binding cords, sealed bales, Valtessa dispatch to Dravin transit to Teshkar receipt, and found the custody unbroken and the quantities in agreement to the bale. The second consignment, seal blanks in pressed cases, agreed as well, though the Dravin form described the cases by count of dozens and the Teshkar book by count of cases, and she spent a patient ten minutes converting one habit into the other before the numbers shook hands. 
It was in the fourth consignment, practice grade alloy bars under seal, that she found the small oddity, and she very nearly did not find it at all. The Teshkar entries named the depot's district in the heading of each page, as local books do, the Selvara district written in the clerk's round hand at the top of every leaf. The Valtessan copies carried the district name as well, translated into their own clerical spelling by the copying clerk at the port, and it was there, in one copy among the set, that the name sat wrong on the page. The vowel in the middle of it was not the vowel the depot register used, and a letter that stood single in the register stood doubled in the copy. Mira looked at it for a while with her pencil still. Copyists make slips the way drivers make dust, constantly and without meaning. A clerk at a port desk, working through a bundle of foreign papers at the end of a long watch, writes a mountain district the way it sounds in his own ear and moves on to the next line. She entered the odd rendering in her working book with the consignment number beside it, marked it as a copyist's slip to be queried when the port bundle was next checked, and turned the page. One wrong vowel in one copy was not a proportion that deserved her morning. In the records room, with three piles still to work, she did what procedure asked of her and filed the small thing as a small thing.

By the middle of the morning she had matched eleven consignments across the three sets and found the custody of ten of them complete. The eleventh left a small discrepancy of quantity that she set aside in a fourth pile, neither accepted nor rejected, the waiting pile that every honest comparison grows. Rask, working through the staffing rota at the other end of the table, looked up and asked whether the paper was behaving. It agrees where agreement costs nothing, she told him, and goes quiet where a signature would cost somebody something. He grunted and returned to the rota, and she untied the cord on the toll bundles, because the second method was waiting.

The second named method was toll chit reconciliation against the bridge count reports, and it was the method Mira trusted least at the start and most by the end of it, because toll paper is kept by people who have no interest in the cargo at all. A toll keeper sells passage. He does not ask what is in the box. His chits record that a vehicle of a stated weight class passed his point on a stated day in a stated direction, and his record is boring in the way that only honest records are boring. She laid the toll bundles along the table in route order and began to walk each consignment down the road on paper, chit by chit, checking the passage of the vehicles against the custody the manifests claimed. Where a manifest said a consignment had moved, a toll chit ought to say that something heavy enough to be its vehicle had passed. Where the chits showed a passage the manifests did not claim, the case would have a question to answer, and Mira liked questions that could be asked of paper before they had to be asked of people.

It was the depot's bonded clerk who brought her the last of the bundles, and she took the opportunity the way procedure teaches, which is to say she interviewed him there and then, at the table, with the tea refreshed and no formality about the occasion. His name was Sef Ardal, and he had kept the depot books for nineteen years, a broad, slow handed man whose handwriting she had already been reading all morning. She did not ask him about smuggling. She asked him about his books, and the order in which paper arrived at the depot, and what he did when a bundle came in with an entry he could not square, and he answered the way craftsmen answer when a visitor has shown the work the respect of doing some of it first. Bundles came down from the pass with the drivers or up from the valley with the mail cart. He entered consignments on the day the vehicle reached the yard, never the day the paper said it should have reached the yard, a distinction he made with some pride. Originals stayed in Teshkar. Copies went down to the valley office for the members' returns. When he could not square an entry he wrote his difficulty in the margin and sent the difficulty down the mountain with the copy, and if an answer came back he pasted the answer slip into the book opposite the entry, and if none came he let the margin stand as his witness. Mira found the pasted slips and the standing margins in roughly the proportions he had described, which raised him in her estimation by the only measure she trusted, the measure of a man whose account of his own work matches the work.

Who keeps the depot paperwork was a question the case needed answered plainly, and by the end of the hour she could answer it. Sef Ardal kept the daily books. The depot office keeper kept the sealed files and the keys to the records room. The valley office kept the copies and lost, by his account, a fair number of the questions he sent them. She asked her last question casually, as a question about staffing rather than about any man, which was whether the depot's principal, the merchant whose consignments filled so much of the book, ever came to the depot in person. Ardal considered the ceiling. In the years of the book, he said, the gentleman had come perhaps four times, always announced, always with his own clerk, and had stood in the office doorway rather than at the tables, as if the paper were a country he preferred to rule from the border. Perhaps four times in nineteen years was a kind of answer, and also not one, and Mira wrote it in the book as both.

The bridge count came into the work through the toll chits, because the chits from the pass checkpoint carried a second entry besides the vehicle class and the toll paid, and the second entry was a number, written small in the corner of the chit in the checkpoint keeper's hand. She had taken the numbers for a tally of axles until Ardal, refilling her cup, saw her frowning at a corner figure and told her what it was with the mild surprise of a man explaining rain to a visitor from the coast. The drivers counted bridges. That was the arrangement on the mountain route, older than Ardal's book, made by the carriers themselves and respected by every service on the road. A driver given a sealed box for the cab of his vehicle was given nothing else: no manifest for the box, no description of its contents, no destination beyond the depot gate. He was told to count the bridges on the route and nothing else, and at the pass checkpoint he reported his count in the chain's plain procedure, a word and a number, spoken to the keeper, who wrote the number in the corner of the toll chit and sent word of it down to the depot with the chit bundle. The number stood for the sealed box in the cab. The depot clerk receiving the bundle matched the reported count against the chit marks for the boxes held ready in the bonded store, and the box whose mark agreed with the count was the box that went down the mountain in that cab, or was taken from that cab, according to the direction of the run. What the box held, the number did not say. Who had sent it, the number did not say. The number said which box, and only which box, and the whole of a driver's knowledge of the cargo he carried could be expressed in a single figure spoken through a checkpoint window in the cold.

Mira sat with that for a while, because a procedure so plain deserves to have its plainness examined carefully. Nine bridges stood on the mountain route between the crossing and the depot town, and the expected count was therefore nine. She counted them herself off the toll map pinned above the records room stove, following the road with one finger. First came the gorge bridge at the crossing itself, where the river cut the border and a vehicle entered Teshkar with its front wheels before its rear wheels had left the far side. Then the two river spans in the lower valley, the mill race bridge, the high bridge below the pass checkpoint, the covered bridge above the toll house, the two stone spans in the gorge road, and last the bridge at the depot town itself, within sight of the lower yard gate. Nine. The counts in the chit corners agreed with the map in bundle after bundle, nine written small and plain, run after run. She reconciled the toll chits against the bridge count reports for the better part of two hours, consignment by consignment, and the reconciliation did what good reconciliation does. It closed doors. The vehicles the manifests claimed had moved had passed the toll points in the order the manifests claimed, at intervals that matched the road, in weight classes that matched the cargoes. The route crossed into Teshkar at the gorge bridge, under the pass checkpoint's eye, and there was no second crossing the paper knew of that heavy vehicles could use. Whatever else the chain was, it travelled on one road, over nine bridges, through one checkpoint.

In the afternoon she went up the road to the pass checkpoint herself, because a keeper interviewed in her own doorway gives a different quality of evidence from a keeper summoned to a depot table, and because she wanted to see the high bridge with her own eyes before she trusted a map about it. The checkpoint stood where the road narrowed between a rock face and a fall of broken ground, a toll house of stone with a roof weighted against the wind, a barrier pole painted in bands faded to the colour of old bone, and a lamp on a bracket that the keeper lit, she would learn, before she lit her own fire. The keeper was Marta Ilsen, sixty years on the mountain by her own account and forty of them in the toll house. She made Mira tea strong enough to stand a spoon in and answered questions in the order they were asked, neither helping nor hindering, which Mira recognised as the professional courtesy of one record keeper to another. The count was spoken, never written by the driver. A word and a number at the window, the word being the driver's own name for the run, a thing the carriers arranged among themselves, and the number being the bridges. She wrote the number on the chit because the depot required it and because she had always written it. She did not know what the number chose or signalled, and when Mira asked whether a count had ever come in that surprised her, Ilsen gave the question the exact weight it deserved. Drivers miscount in fog, she said. Drivers miscount when a span is down for repair and the marshals wave them over a temporary crossing, because some count the temporary boards and some count only stone. The road was the road, and nine was its number when it was whole, and her chits said nine so often that she wrote the figure, she admitted, sometimes before the driver had finished speaking, and had to scratch it out perhaps twice in a year and write the true figure over the scratch. Mira noted the scratching out with interest and without triumph. A record in which the expected answer is sometimes written early is a record that must be read with its keeper's habits in mind. Patience does the work in such interviews. Nobody had been accused of anything, and the case was richer by exactly the amount of that honesty.

The third named method waited for her back at the depot, and it was the least dramatic of the three and the one on which the night itself would eventually stand or fall. Depot plan and staffing study. Dunmore had sent the plan up from Carrowmere in the sealed case, and Mira spread it on the records room table under the lamp and walked the building on paper before she walked it in fact. The warehouse took the yard's whole eastern side, a long shed of stone and timber with doors wide enough for two vehicles abreast. The office corridor ran behind the warehouse wall, with the principal's office at its far end, the sealed files room opposite, and between them, marked on the plan as a plain rectangle with a single door and no window, a sealed store room whose inventory line in the inspection annex stood blank in a way she noticed and set aside. At the yard's lower end, the lower gate opened on the approach road from the town bridge, a stretch of perhaps eighty paces of open ground between the last bend and the gate itself, lit by one lamp at the gate post and dark at the bend, and she marked that stretch with her pencil and wrote beside it the words position four, because a place where a watcher must stand alone deserves to be named on the plan before it is named in orders. On the building's north side, a service door gave from the yard into the corridor behind the warehouse, the depot's back door, narrow, iron bound, lit by a yard lamp on a bracket above it, and fitted, the inspection annex noted, with a latch that must be lifted rather than pushed, the kind of detail that is nothing in a report and everything at two in the morning with cold hands. She copied the annex note into her working book word for word, because plans are promises made by buildings and the small print of a promise is where the promise lives.

Staffing is the second half of any plan study, because a building is only ever what the people in it are doing. The rota gave the depot a night staff of four in ordinary weeks, two in the warehouse, one at the gate, one sleeping in the office corridor room against emergencies, and a day staff three times that number. She went through the service lists with Ardal and then again with Rask, name by name, until she could have drawn the depot's human geography as confidently as its walls. Who held keys, and to what. And one answer interested her in a way she filed carefully, because the depot staff served the cargo that arrived and the cargo did not arrive sorted by allegiance. A warehouse hand who had loaded Valtessan bales on a Monday loaded Dravin crates on a Wednesday without changing his gloves. The depot was a place where the three streams of the case ran together into one yard and one set of hands, and any theory of the chain would have to survive that fact.

On her way along the office corridor she laid her palm, lightly and from long habit, against the door frame of the files room, and the frame gave her what depot wood gives a reader: routine laid down in thin layers, the small anxieties of clerks, the irritation of men waiting for signatures, a sediment of ordinary working feeling no thicker than dust on a shelf. It cost her a coolness in the fingertips that faded before she reached the corridor's end. Paper and patience would do the rest of the ground work. Her strength was a store she was keeping, deliberately and with both hands, for the night at the end of it.

By the second day the evidence had arranged itself into shapes, and Mira refused to choose among them while choosing was still cheap. Three possibilities were alive, and she kept all three alive on purpose, the way a good inspector keeps all the doors of a warehouse in view until the moment a door actually opens.

The first was the simplest and therefore, in her experience, the most suspect. The chain might be a single organisation, one hand on the whole route, moving restricted practice materials through three members under one discipline. The evidence for it had real weight. The consignments kept appointments with each other across borders in a way that loose arrangements rarely manage. The seal blanks, the cords and the anchor pairs travelled in a rhythm that suggested one calendar kept somewhere by one clerk of calendars. No consignment had ever undercut another, and no driver had ever been found carrying for two masters on the same road in the same week. Against it stood the paper itself, three sets of it, keeping their differences with a consistency that went deeper than habit, and a single organisation would have had to be disciplined enough to keep three paper styles separate for years, which was possible, and was exactly the kind of possible that kept her from closing the door.

The chain might be three organisations sharing a route, three separate houses that had discovered the same road, the same depot, the same tolerant checkpoint, and had settled into the wary cooperation of rivals who need the same bridge kept open. Every visible thing supported it. Three manifest hands, none of them imitating another. Three consignment styles, down to the way the Valtessan set witnessed its pages and the Dravin set refused to waste ink on witnesses. Three payment routes, carefully distinct: Valtessan bills drawn on a trading house at the port, Dravin settlements made through a licensed intermediary in the transit town, Teshkar balances carried in the depot's own book and settled at the valley fair. Ardal's margins even recorded quarrels, plausible, petty and entirely human, between the houses over warehouse space and the order of loading in wet weather. If three organisations shared the route, then a rollup would have to take all three in one night or merely teach the survivors caution, and the Office's planning had been built, honourably and on the evidence, around exactly that requirement. Rask favoured the second possibility with the whole of his sceptical heart. Three crooked houses sharing a toll road is the world as I know it, he told her, over the duty roster. One crooked house pretending to be three is a theatre piece, and smugglers are not theatrical, they are tired. Mira wrote his objection in the book anyway, because a partner's disbelief is evidence too.

The third possibility was the one she could not put down, and she was honest with herself about the reason, which was that it explained the evidence's good behaviour. The chain might be one organisation pretending to be three. A single house wearing three sets of paper the way a driver wears three coats against one winter. She considered what such a builder would have to understand, and the consideration sobered her, because the builder would have to understand the Office. The Office hunts single organisations. That is its training, its forms, its instincts, the shape of every warrant it writes. A chain that presents as one organisation receives the Office's full and practised attention, one investigation, one night, one taking. A chain that presents as three receives something else: three files, three assessments, three cautious approaches, and the constant professional fear that moving against one house will scatter the other two. The triple shape wastes the Office's nights exactly as a decoy wastes a marksman's cartridges, by giving him respectable targets that are not the target. Whoever built the appearance, if anyone had built it, had built it out of an accurate reading of Office habit, and his reading of Office habit was correct. She could prove that from her own table. The Office had spent, she calculated from the file dates, the better part of a Session assessing houses that might not exist.

Dunmore's dispatch from Carrowmere, which arrived on the second evening in a sealed packet, carried the same recognition in the analyst's dry hand. Dunmore had found nothing in the copies to break the three houses apart and nothing to join them, and her covering note read that the paper was either three honest sets of dishonest books or one dishonest set of books three times as honest as it needed to be, and that she declined, on the evidence available in Carrowmere, to guess which. Three possibilities. All alive. The night would have to be built to work against any of them, which is to say against the route itself, the depot, the checkpoint and the gate, and let the organisation, one or three or pretending, fall where the geography caught it.

It was during the last reconciliation of the evening, when she was checking the most recent chit bundle against the planned night, that the small wrong figure caught her eye. The bundle held the chits for the last completed run before the planned night, the freshest paper on the table, and in the corner of the checkpoint chit, in Ilsen's hand, the count stood written as eight. She turned back through the bundle slowly. Every other chit in it agreed with its manifest. The vehicle class was right. The toll was right. The direction was right. Only the corner figure stood at eight where the road, whole and unrepaired so far as any record in the bundle knew, should have given nine. She held the chit up to the lamp and looked for a scratching out, the keeper's known habit, and found none; the eight was written clean, in one stroke of the pen, by a woman who wrote what she was told. A miscount in fog was possible, Ilsen had said so herself. Mira wrote the discrepancy into her working book with the bundle number beside it and a line under it, neither alarm nor dismissal, and she put the chit back in its bundle and the bundle back in its order. It was one figure in one corner, and it would keep.

The rollup board was drawn that night in the depot office, on the back of a valley survey sheet, with the lamps lit. The board settled who would stand at each of the four positions when the night came, at what moment each position would move, and what each position was there to catch, and it settled nothing else, which Mira said aloud before she ruled the first line.

| Position | Held by | Timing | What it was to catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| The depot warehouse in Teshkar | Mira Kovac | The shared signal hour, not a breath before it | The stored consignment, the warehouse staff and the guards on duty |
| The pass checkpoint on the mountain route | Tomas Rask | The shared signal hour, held against road time | The incoming cab, its driver and the sealed box in the cab |
| The consignment desk at Carrowmere | The fourth officer released to the rollup | The shared signal hour, matched to depot time | The broker lodging the bonded copies and the payment instructions with them |

Four positions, and four officers to hold them, and no fifth officer anywhere in the Accord's gift to stand in reserve, which was the arithmetic Leyden had named at the tasking and which the board merely wrote down in a fairer hand. Simultaneous meant simultaneous. No position moves early, because one arrest warns the rest, and a chain warned is a chain dissolved into the mountains and the port towns for another Session. No position waits for news of another, because waiting is only warning by a slower road. Each officer holds the position as drawn, and the member services make the arrests under Office direction at each position. Whether the chain would run on the night the Office expected was a question the toll pattern and the loading rota could answer between them, and they had answered it; the consignments were building toward the expected night in the usual rhythm. Who outside the Office knew the night was a question nobody at the table could answer, and Mira wrote it at the head of her working book as the one item the ground work had opened and could not close, to be carried into the night itself and watched there. Everything else the tasking had asked, the ground work had settled or honestly framed. She rolled the sheet and tied it with the fourth colour of cord she had brought for exactly that purpose.

The last piece of ground work was Reni. Mira took the radio set down to the lower yard gate in the last of the light, with Reni Vosk beside her carrying the spare battery in both arms, and they walked the approach together from the bend to the gate post while Mira named the ground the way she would want it named to her. Here the lamp reached. Here it stopped reaching. Here the bank gave a watcher a place to stand where a vehicle must slow. Reni listened with the whole of her attention, which was her way in all things, and repeated the instructions back in the words of the charter rather than the words Mira had used, which was also her way. Watch, count and report. Do not engage. A position held by reporting is held in full, and a watcher who leaves her ground to chase a vehicle has not been brave, she has been absent, and absence at a gate is a hole the whole night can pour through. Reni said she understood, and then, because she was twenty one and believed the Accord charter the way other people believe in weather, she asked whether the instruction not to engage held even if a vehicle ran the gate toward the warehouse, and Mira said it held most of all then, and that her report would reach faster than her legs and do more good when it arrived.

Then they rehearsed the radio check from the positions themselves, Reni at the gate approach and Mira back at the gate post with the second set, the dusk coming down the gorge. Reni's voice came through small and clear and exact, giving her position, her count of the carts that had passed during the rehearsal, and the state of the lamp, in the order the drill prescribed, without ornament and without hurry. Mira answered in drill order and felt something in her chest settle that had been unsettled since the tasking. The thinnest position on the board would be held by the person at the lower gate, and the person at the lower gate intended to hold it by the book.

She walked Reni back up to the depot through the first cold of the evening, past the north side service door with its lamp lit above the iron bound frame, and she stopped there without planning to and showed Reni the latch, lifted and not pushed, because ground an officer might need in the dark should never be ground she has only seen on a plan. In her working book that night she closed the ground work with a single line, in the plain hand she kept for facts she did not intend to argue with later. The route was one road. The paper was three sets. The count was nine, except once, on paper, where it was eight. The rest was the night's business, and the night was not hers to rehearse any further.

## One Hand, Three Names

The depot records room held the cold longer than any other room in the building. Stone walls took the mountain night and gave it back slowly through the morning, and the air smelled of lamp oil, paper dust and damp wool. Mira stood at the long table with her sleeves pushed to the elbow and her fingers grey with file dust, listening to the yard through the shuttered window: cart iron on stone, a horse shifting in harness, the clerks in the outer office calling weights and seal numbers in the flat voices of men who had said the same words for years. Light came in thin and white between the shutters and laid bars across the three sets of paper she had spent the better part of two days learning to tell apart.

They were not, on their face, alike. That had been the point of them, and for two days she had honoured the difference because the difference was the evidence everyone else had read first. The Dravin set came on thick cream stock, its consignment marks set high, each entry closed with a flourish. The Valtessa set was leaner, blue ruled, the marks low and cramped, the payment route written out in full at the foot of every sheet. The Melora set used a cheaper paper that drank the ink, and its entries were numbered twice, once in the margin and once in the body of the text. Three hands, she had thought, or at the least three offices with three habits, three tempers, three ways of fearing an audit.

Petra Dunmore's packet had arrived from Carrowmere an hour before first light. Dunmore had written no letter. She had sent copies, forty one sheets of them, each marked in her small exact pencil with the member, the set and the page, and on the top sheet she had written four words and underlined the last of them twice. Check the district renderings. Mira had laughed, once, under her breath, because it was such a Dunmore instruction: no greeting, no reasoning, only the place to put a finger. Then she had gone back to the shelves for the originals.

The district was Selvara. It was not a large place, but conveyances were rendered to it, tolls were charged by it, and its name appeared on every manifest that passed through the depot in the space reserved for the place of taking. On the Dravin set, in the high flourish hand, the word stood in the third entry on the second sheet. Salvarra. Her first thought was the thought she had trained herself out of years before: a slip, a copyist in a hurry. She had thought the same over the bonded copies in the depot records room and filed it away as nothing, and the memory of that filing sat uncomfortably with her while she turned the Valtessa sheet to the light.

Salvarra. Blue ruled, cramped low hand, the payment route written out in full beneath it. The wrong vowel, a for e, sat in the first syllable exactly where it had sat in the cream stock set, and the doubled consonant, the second r, followed it like a companion who had been invited. She laid the two sheets side by side. The Melora set lay in its folder at her left hand. She did not open it at once. She stood with her palms flat on the table, on either side of the two errors, and made herself name what she was hoping for and what she was fearing, because the most dangerous moment in paperwork is the moment before the third sheet, when a person can still choose what the third sheet is allowed to mean.

She opened the folder. The cheap paper drank the ink and feathered every letter. The district rendering stood in the second entry, in the twice numbered hand, in a line she had read the day before and passed over because the numbering had drawn her eye. Salvarra.

For a while she said nothing, and the clerks went on calling weights in the outer office.

Rask came in with two cups of the depot tea, which was mostly hot water that had been introduced to a leaf at some earlier stage of its life, and set one down clear of the paper. He looked at the three sheets laid in a row. He looked at her face.

"Tell me it is a coincidence of spelling," he said.

"It is a district name," Mira said. "Districts are spelled by rote. A clerk learns it the way he learns his own name, by writing it until the hand knows it before the eye does. Three services, three training houses, three sets of rote. One of them could err. This is not an awkward name. And it is not one error."

She turned the sheets over to the toll reconciliation pages that travelled with each set. The bridge tolls were charged by weight class and by axle, and the totals had been carried through all three sets because all three caravans, if caravans was even the word any longer, had used the same bridges in the same season. The true total for the heaviest class stood in the depot's own toll book, which she had checked the previous day and checked again before she allowed herself to use it. Three hundred and eighty four. In the Dravin set the carried figure read three hundred and forty eight. In the Valtessa set it read three hundred and forty eight. In the Melora set, feathered and twice numbered, it read three hundred and forty eight. The eight and the four had changed places, modestly, plausibly, the kind of transposition that survives a checking eye because the digits themselves are all present and accounted for, and the same transposition had survived in three services that were supposed to check one another.

"A hand learns its errors the way it learns its letters," she said. "It is not the perfections that give a writer away. Any careful person can copy a style for a page. What they cannot do, not across forty sheets and three tempers of paper, is keep their own habits out of the work. The wrong vowel is a habit. The doubled letter is a habit. The transposed figures are a habit of the eye, taking the number in the wrong order and writing it down in good faith. One clerk wrote all three sets. One hand. The three names are costumes."

Rask set his cup down. "Then G one is closed," he said, and there was no pleasure in it, because closing a question in a rollup this close to its night was another way of saying that the ground had moved. "One organisation."

"One organisation pretending to be three," Mira said. "And we should be precise about the rest of it, because the pretense was not vanity. Look at what it bought."

She walked him through it the way she would have to walk Leyden through it, and as she spoke she felt the false lead die the particular death of a thing that had been built well. It did not collapse in disgrace. It completed itself, and stood revealed as workmanship. Three organisations sharing a route had explained everything the Office had found for months: the different consignment styles arriving at the same depots, the payment routes that never touched, the cargoes that never shared a cart, the drivers who knew a number and nothing else. It explained why every arrest the Office had sketched had been an arrest of a third of a thing. Every piece of evidence supported the threefold shape because the threefold shape had been manufactured out of evidence, sheet by sheet, by a man who had studied the hunters as carefully as the hunters had studied him.

"He knows us," she said. "That is the part Dunmore will not like, and Leyden will like even less. He built three because we hunt one. A single organisation is the shape the Office is made to find: one head, one ledger, one night on which the whole body can be taken at once. So he gave us a shape we would refuse. Three rivals sharing a road, three services to watch, three cases to argue about in Carrowmere while the season went past. Every night we spent deciding whether the Dravin traffic or the Melora traffic was the true spine of it, he spent moving cargo. His reading of our habit is correct. He counted on the Office doing exactly what the Office did, and the Office did it."

"Who holds the pen?" Rask asked. "That is the live one. If one clerk wrote all three sets, the clerk is a door. Behind the door is either the principal himself, keeping his own paper because he trusts nobody, or a servant who sees the whole shape of the thing every time he sits down to write it."

"The depot keeps the paperwork," Mira said. "That much the ground work settled. The sets were copied outward from here, not gathered here. The originals live in this room, the copies travel. So the hand is a depot hand, or a hand that sits at this table often enough to use its ink and its toll book." She looked at the shelves, at the rows of bound seasons, at the second desk by the stove where the senior manifest clerk worked. "Whether the hand belongs to the man we want is a question the paper will not answer. Paper tells you a hand. It does not tell you whether the hand takes orders or gives them. We take the depot on the night and we take the desk with it, and we ask the question with the man in the room."

She sent for the toll keeper's log before she sent anything to Carrowmere, because the second matter had been waiting in her notes for a day and a half, and the death of the false lead had changed what it was allowed to mean.

The bridge count was procedure, and procedure was sacred on the mountain route precisely because it was so bare. A driver counted bridges and nothing else. He did not know his cargo, his consigner or his destination beyond the next checkpoint. At the pass he spoke a word and a number to the toll keeper, and the number told the depot which sealed box rode in the cab, because each count belonged to one route shape and one route shape only. Nine bridges meant the high road complete: the river span, the two gorge crossings, the toll bridge at the district boundary, the five lesser spans over the side valleys. Nine was the expected count, and nine had come in on every run of the season the Office had managed to observe.

The last run had reported eight.

She had noted the discrepancy in the chit bundle during the ground work and let it sit, because a single wrong count has a dozen innocent parents. A driver can miscount in the dark. A bridge could close for repair and be skipped by a ford. Among three organisations a changed count might signify nothing beyond one caravan's trouble on one night. Among one organisation it signified differently. She spread the toll keeper's log beside the chits and went through the season entry by entry, with Rask reading the bridge book aloud. No bridge on the high road had closed. No bridge had been under repair. The new span below the gorge stood unfinished, its deck unlaid, and no cart had crossed it. The road had offered nine bridges on the night of the last run. The driver had crossed eight because he had been sent across eight. The route itself had been reordered, and the count had announced it to anyone who knew how to listen. Eight was not an error. Eight was a message in the chain's own bare language, and the message said that the run order had changed.

"Folded into it," Rask said, when she had laid it out. "You think the principal moves with this one."

"I think a man changes his run order for two reasons," Mira said. "Because the road compels him, and the road did not. Or because the shape of the night compels him: because someone has to be inside the movement rather than waiting at the end of it. His own movement, Tomas. He has put himself into the order of the run the way he put himself into the shape of the paperwork, quietly and in the middle of it, where a watcher counting organisations would never think to look. Eight bridges is the sound of him travelling."

That left the oldest thread in the case, and she took it up before midday because it was the one thread that could not be hurried. Three weeks earlier, by the border post's own register, a man answering in the loosest terms to the principal's measure had crossed into Teshkar at the high post where the road narrowed between two stone pillars. The guard had recorded the passage in the ordinary way: a traveller, a pack horse, a bonded parcel declared and sealed and not opened, a name given and not verified. If the traveller had been the principal, then the iron bound gate bar at that post, and the pillar stone where travellers waited while their parcels were weighed, were the last anchors that held any trace of his passage in a place she could still reach.

She rode up with Rask in the early afternoon. The crossing post stood as it always stood: a hut, a barrier, a weighing beam, two pillars of unworked stone with the iron gate bar resting in its slots between them. The wind worried at the post's flag and at the loose corner of the tariff board, and the guard, an old soldier, brought out the register with the air of a man producing evidence that the world was exactly as dull as he had reported it to be.

Mira put her hands on the gate bar first, because metal holds best, and the bar had been handled by every traveller of three weeks of mountain weather, which was the heart of the matter and she knew it before she began. Weight fades. It thins, the way ice thins, from the edges and from the surface, and what three weeks of wind and handling and ordinary passage had left in the iron was a smear of everything and a portrait of nothing: a low residue of impatience, of cold hands and held breath and the small daily fear of men carrying parcels they had not packed across a line they did not draw. It cost her what it always cost her, a portion taken on as her own: a restlessness in the shoulders, a thin, sour impatience that was not hers, sitting behind her teeth like the taste of the depot tea. She kept her hands in place past the point where courtesy to herself would have lifted them, because she owed the attempt its full length. There was no decision in the iron, no sustained intent standing out from the routine the way a voice stands out in a quiet room. Three weeks of travellers had crossed over whatever one man had left, and the weather had done the rest, and the sum of it was almost nothing, and almost nothing was a finding and not a failure.

The pillar stone gave her less: a faintness that might once have held a shape, tiredness perhaps, or the alertness of a man listening to a guard's questions with his answers ready, and it dissolved under her attention the way breath dissolves off glass. She could not have sworn it belonged to the traveller in the register, and swearing falsely on a read was the one professional sin she had never allowed herself, not at fifteen when the reads came on her like weather and not since.

"It is gone," she said to Rask, at last, and took her hands off the stone. The impatience she had taken on went with her down the first stretch of the road, riding in her shoulders, and she let it ride, because carrying it was the receipt for the attempt. "Not taken. Not cleaned. Gone the ordinary way. That distinction matters, so I will say it once, plainly. In Carrowmere I once stood in [a transit room that was worked in every day of its life, and it held nothing](/2026/11/01/nightjar-clean-room-001/). Not a thinning. Not a smear. Nothing, in a place that should have been heavy. That was an absence with edges. This is a trail with its edges worn off, which is what three weeks does to anything a man leaves in iron and stone on a mountain. Decay is a door closing slowly. The other thing was a room that had never had a door. I will not call them by the same name."

Rask, who was a Stiller and therefore a man professionally acquainted with what a place did not say, nodded once and asked whether the border route was closed to them. She said that it was: the one road that might have told her where the principal had gone had been erased by nothing more sinister than time and traffic, and they would take the depot without it.

She did the arithmetic of the night at the records room table, with the lamps lit and the yard going quiet outside and the three dishonest sets of paper pushed into a single folder. If the run order had changed, then the run would come in the new order's shape, and that shape, read against the toll keeper's season of entries and the depot's receiving habits, brought the principal's movement and the cargo with it six hours ahead of the hour the Office had built its night around. A sting built for the old hour would arrive at a depot already emptied, its warehouse cleared in the dark, its desk cold. She had Rask check the toll intervals against the pass times while she checked them, and the columns agreed with each other both times.

There was a price, and she named it to herself before she named it to Leyden, because a price unnamed has a way of being paid by somebody else in the dark. Moving the sting forward by six hours moved every position with it, and the Office had no depth anywhere in the plan: four officers, four positions, no reserve. The support officer who was to have covered the lower yard, walking the stretch between Reni Vosk's gate approach and the yard wall, would have to be pulled to the upper yard to make the moved warehouse timing hold. By her reckoning, there was a stretch in the moved night when Reni would hold the lower gate approach alone: nineteen minutes, by the honest count, from the support officer's leaving to the relief's arrival at the wall. Nineteen minutes in which a chain car could come down the lower road and find a girl of twenty one with a radio and orders to watch, count and report, and in which everything the position asked of her was that she do exactly that and nothing braver.

Mira sat with that figure for a while. She thought about the girl's hands on the radio during the rehearsal checks, steady and a fraction too careful, the care of a person who believes that the charter means what it says. She thought about Edran Voss, whose last ten minutes she still carried in cold rooms, and about a signature of hers on a page in Melora that had ended an auditor's career with Inez Calloway's name spelled correctly in full, which had felt at the time like mercy and had not stopped feeling like a debt. The arithmetic did not change for the thinking. The run would come in six hours early whether the Office met it or not. The choice was not between Reni supported and Reni alone. It was between Reni alone for nineteen minutes inside a sting that had a chance of taking the network whole, and Reni supported through a long, safe, useless night at a depot that had already emptied itself around her. She accepted the nineteen minutes, and she wrote the figure in her notebook before she reached for the signal forms, so that it would exist in her own hand before it existed in the orders, and nobody, afterwards, would be able to say the gap had been an oversight.

Leyden's voice came up the wire from Carrowmere thin and flattened, and she gave him the whole of it in order: one hand, three names, the district rendered wrong in the same way in every set, the figures transposed in every set, the count changed from nine to eight with no bridge closed and no new span open, the run order changed and the principal travelling inside his own change. She gave him the border post and its three week silence, ordinary decay, nothing yielded, that road closed. She gave him the arithmetic. Then she gave him the nineteen minutes, without decoration, and there was a pause on the wire long enough for the yard watch to cross below the window and back.

"You are certain of the hand," Leyden said, in the tone he used when he was already agreeing and wanted the certainty on record.

"I have the originals under my hand and Dunmore has the copies," Mira said. "The error is the same in all three because the writer is the same in all three. Move the night. Six hours. I will put Reni's gap in the orders myself, in figures, so that every position knows it exists and the relief rides for it."

The agreement came back in his own rationed way, three words and the countersign, and she sat for a moment with her hand flat on the folder of false paper, feeling the ordinary cold of the room and the impatience still lodged in her shoulders from the gate bar, before she turned to the signal desk and began to give the orders. Rask took the warehouse timing to the upper yard party in person, because some messages should arrive with a face attached. The depot party received its new hour under seal. The pass checkpoint received its instruction to report and not to stop, to count and to let pass. The relief rider for the lower yard was dispatched to the town muster with the nineteen minutes written on his order as a debt to be closed at speed. And last, on the channel reserved for the positions, Mira sent the new hour to the lower gate approach herself, and heard Reni Vosk read it back in a voice that did not change at all, steady and a fraction too careful, position confirmed, watch and count and report, and the orders went out into the mountain dark in every direction at once, and the night, six hours early, began.

## The Warehouse Take

The back door on the north side of the depot warehouse opened onto cold that had been shut in all day, concrete cold and dust cold and the thin iron smell of shelving that never warmed through a Teshkar winter of use. Mira lifted the latch the way the plan had taught her, up and only then inward, and the door gave without a scrape and let the yard lamp fall in a narrow slab across stacked crates and a floor marked with chalk lines and drag scars. Beyond the crates the warehouse ran long and dim under a roof ticking in the mountain wind, and deeper in the building a stove pipe knocked once and settled. She shut the door with the heel of her hand and stood with her back to it, listening to the place arrange itself around the disturbance she had made. Her hearing came back slower than it should have done. Two days of reading and procedure had thinned something in her that rest had not been given time to mend, and the edges of the room reached her a fraction late, sound first and shape after it.

The first crates on her left were bonded stock, seal cords looped and tagged, the tags swinging in the air she had let in. She did not touch them. Touching was for later, for the accounting, and her errand in the first minutes was the warehouse itself, the long central aisle and the office corridor door at the far end and the men the night count said would be inside. The depot plan Dunmore had spread for her in Carrowmere lived in her head as distances, thirty paces to the cross aisle, twelve more to the stove and the clerks table, nine from the table to the corridor door, and she counted her way by those distances because counting kept her attention narrow and narrow was what she had. Her attention had been narrowing since the early afternoon, since the last of the originals at the depot office and the long toll chit work before that, and she recognised the shape of it and did not fight it. At the cross aisle she put one hand flat on a crate end, and the cold wood cleared her for three breaths, and the warehouse gave her its ordinary sounds in their right places, dripping meltwater at the far wall, roofing iron lifting and dropping, the stove drawing. Nothing else. She went on.

The radio spoke before she reached the clerks table, and it was Rask with the yard report in the flat phrasing they had rehearsed. Lower yard held. Clerks room held. Two men at the table with their hands shown and their names given. That was the second position answering inside the minute the plan allowed, and her own position made the third. The fourth was the road gate in Carrowmere, where the bonded lorries were to be stopped on the same signal, too far away for its report to reach her set directly. She pressed her transmit key twice, the agreed acknowledgement that asked for nothing and confirmed everything, and Rask sent back a single click and was gone. Simultaneity had been the fragile part of the design from the first hour Leyden laid it out, four arrests in three members with no position moving early because one arrest warned the rest, and moving the sting forward by six hours had tested it harder than any of them wanted. She reached the clerks table and found it empty, its ledger shut, its lamp still lit, a cup gone cold beside an ink stand. The corridor door beyond it stood open a hand width. From the yard side, faint through walls, she heard the depot bell begin and stop short, cut off by whoever held the bell rope for the Office side.

Reni came on the radio then, and Mira stopped walking to hear her properly. Position three, the junior officer said, her words arriving with the clean spacing of someone reading from a card she had written for herself. Lower gate approach quiet. Yard wall in sight. No movement on the track. Mira answered with the short form they had agreed, heard and understood, position held, report changes only. Reni repeated it back word for word, the discipline exact, and the channel closed. Nineteen minutes was the gap the reassignment had cut into the night, nineteen minutes in which the officer who should have stood within call of the lower gate was instead walking the upper yard with Rask second team, because the moved timing had needed him there and there had been nobody else to send. Mira had done that arithmetic herself at the table where the orders went out, and she had accepted it herself. Reni knew her instructions. Watch, count, report, do not engage. She set it where she could see it and kept walking.

The guard came from the gap between the alloy crates and the stove, a man who had heard a door and decided against the alarm, with a pry bar carried low in one hand and his other hand open and reaching. Mira saw the reaching hand first and the bar a fraction after it, and that fraction was the depletion tax she had been paying all evening, small and honest and dangerous only if she pretended it was not there. She stepped toward the stove instead of away from him, because the plan of the room in her head said the ground there was clear and the table behind her was not, and his reaching hand closed on air where her shoulder had been. He was a big man in depot clothes, and he swung the bar the way a man swings a tool he knows, without flourish and without waste. She got her forearm up in time for the first swing to spend itself against the shelving upright beside her head, and the iron rang and the shelves shook and seal blanks in their trays rattled like teeth. The second swing she took on a crate end she pushed between them, and the bar bit wood and stuck for a heartbeat. In that heartbeat she closed the distance, put her shoulder into his chest, and drove him backward into the stove guard rail. The rail held him and hurt him and he dropped the bar to get both hands free. His hands found her coat. Her hands found his wrist and the rail. The room narrowed to the rail and the wrist and the cold floor under her boots, and she worked inside that narrow place by patience and by placement rather than by strength she did not have to spare.

He tried to turn her toward the shelves, and the turn cost her more than it should have done, a grinding price paid in the shoulder and in the breath she could not quite finish. For three counts she simply held on and let his effort spend itself against her weight set low. When his feet shifted she moved with the shift, put her boot behind his heel and her shoulder under his arm, and took him down across the clerks table edge. The table went over with them and the ledger and the lamp and the cold cup scattered across the concrete in a clatter that sounded enormous in the shut building. The lamp went out. Stove light through its grate door made the only light left on that side of the warehouse, a low red striping across the floor, and in it she saw the second man for the first time. He was younger and already running, a driver by his coat and his soft cap, and he ran not for the doors but for the cross aisle and the bonded stacks, where a man who knew the building might lose himself among the crates or find the side hatch the plan had marked as sealed. The guard under her hands heaved once and tried to rise. She put her knee and her whole tired weight across his back and told him, in the plain depot voice she used for men on floors, that rising would go badly and staying would not. He stayed. She got the restraint cord onto his wrists with fingers that obeyed her a beat slower than she asked them to.

The driver had stopped running. She heard it in the building before she saw it, the scuff and skid of soft boots on concrete and then nothing, a held quiet among the crates where a running man had decided hiding served him better. Mira rose, and rising cost her a long moment with one hand on the overturned table, the room tipping a degree and righting itself. She stood in the stove light and spoke toward the stacks in the same plain voice. She told him the yard was held and the doors were held and the bell was held, and that the Office took names and not injuries when it was offered names. She told him the crates around him were bonded stock under seal, and climbing them would break seals and make his night considerably worse in law. Silence answered her, and dripping meltwater, and the roofing iron lifting and dropping. She walked toward the cross aisle without haste, because haste was a currency she was short of and because a hidden man listening to unhurried steps did his own arithmetic about doors. Between the alloy crates and the tall stack of carrier housings she found his cap on the floor, and she stopped beside it and waited.

From among the crates ahead of her, close enough that she heard his breathing change, the driver said a word that was not quite surrender and not quite a question. She answered it with his choices spoken slowly. Come out with hands shown and be counted with the drivers. Stay among the seals and be fetched. He came out sideways, the way a man leaves a narrow place he does not trust, with his hands up at shoulder height and his eyes on her hands rather than her face. She had him sit against the crate stack with his hands on his knees where she could see them, and she stood over him long enough for his breathing to slow. Then the radio at her shoulder spoke again and split her attention cleanly in two. It was Reni, and Reni voice had changed, not higher and not faster, but stripped down to the reporting form, as if she had taken hold of the card in front of her and was holding it with both hands. Position three. A car on the track. One car, lights hooded, coming down from the upper road at walking pace. Passing the wall marker. She added nothing to it and asked nothing of it. Mira stood in the stove lit warehouse with a taken guard breathing hard on the floor behind her and a driver watching her face for what the report meant, and she could not go to the lower gate and she could not send anyone to the lower gate, because the upper yard team was committed and her own hands were the warehouse. Mira keyed the set and kept her voice at the level she had used all night. Heard. Hold position. Report its passing. Do not engage. Reni repeated it back, word for word, and the channel closed on the small click of her key.

The guard on the floor chose that moment to test the cord, and the driver against the crates chose it to look toward the cross aisle as if measuring it. Mira felt her attention pull at both men and at the silent radio and at the corridor door beyond the table, four spans at once from a mind that had been telling her since afternoon it could hold one span well and a second span briefly. She chose the order and imposed it. Guard first, because he was closest to the loose pry bar, and she kicked the bar away under the shelving with a boot that cost her balance for a step. Driver second, because he was watching her choose, and she told him looking at the aisle was free and entering it would be counted as running, and his eyes came back to his knees. Corridor door last, and it stayed where it was, open its hand width, with the dark of the office corridor beyond it. The fight had spent the last of the reserve she had been husbanding since the toll chits, and what remained was a thinner, plainer capacity, enough for procedure and enough for walking and enough for speech. She could grip, at a price. She could turn, at a price. She could hold a door or a man or a position, at a price she could pay a few more times before the night presented a bill she could not meet.

Rask voice came through with the relay at last, broken by the building and by distance into something she had to assemble as it arrived. Upper yard held. Gate team on the road reporting by relay, lorries stopped, drivers counted. That was the fourth position, late by the relay and complete, and it closed the shape of the rollup around the depot like a hand closing. Four positions, four holdings, on a timing none of them had chosen and all of them had met. She keyed her acknowledgement and gave the warehouse report in the fewest words the form allowed. Warehouse held. Two men held inside. Proceeding to offices. Then the radio spoke again from the lower gate, and Reni voice came back at its ordinary level, as if the stripped down reporting form had been a coat she had put on for the cars passage and had since taken off. Position three. The car has passed through and gone down the lower road. No stop. No one got out. I stayed at the wall. She reported her own obedience as a fact of the position, neither proud nor apologetic, and Mira closed her eyes for the length of one breath in the stove lit aisle. Heard, she said. Well held. Report anything else that moves. Reni repeated the instruction back, word for word, and the channel went quiet around the plain fact of a junior officer who had held.

What remained in the warehouse was procedure, and she did it at the pace her body set rather than the pace her impatience wanted. She walked the guard to the clerks table, righted it with his unwilling help, and sat him against its leg with the cord checked and his hands where the yard team would see them. She walked the driver to the same table and sat him on the other side of it, far enough from the guard that neither could reach the other, close enough that one pair of eyes could hold them both. Her eyes were the pair until Rask people came in through the lower yard door with lanterns and the formal words. She gave them the room in a short report, names as given, the bar under the shelving, the broken seals none, the stacks unclimbed, and watched the room pass out of her sole keeping into the wider keeping of the Office. The yard alarm, which had rung in fragments through walls all through the taking, gave one last short sound as the team reached its rope and housing and set it under Office control. She collected the ledger from the floor where the table had spilled it, because paperwork was evidence and evidence was the spine of the whole night, and she carried it in her left hand with her right hand free.

The corridor door was as she had left it, open its hand width, and beyond it the office corridor ran dark and close with the smell of stove ash and shut rooms. She stood in the doorway with the ledger against her side and listened, giving each sound its turn, and her hearing came quicker this time because there was less to hear. Nothing running, nothing being carried, no haste left anywhere in the place, and that absence of haste told its own story about the offices and how long ago any emptying of them had finished. She had reached the door in time to take the building. Whether she had reached it in time for anything else was a question the corridor was keeping. She put her hand on the office door frame to steady herself for the last steps, and the wood there was warm from the stove pipe that ran behind it, and she stood with her palm flat on that warmth and her orders complete behind her and the office door in front of her, and she went forward to it through the dark at the pace she had left.

## The Warm Chair

The office held the cold that the corridor had carried in with her. Stone underfoot gave it back through her boots, while the desk lamp kept a small circle of heat over paper, ink and dust. Shutters stood closed against the yard. Beyond them the warehouse sounds had thinned to boots, doors and low voices. Mira stood inside the door and let the room settle, the desk ahead, the shelves to one side, the chair behind the desk turned a little away from it, as if its occupant had risen without setting it straight.

She had the papers with her, three sets meant to belong to three separate concerns. The manifests lay in order on the desk edge, where the lamp touched their corners. In each set the depot district name carried the same fault, the wrong vowel and the doubled consonant in the same place, neat and repeatable. The toll figures carried it further, the same digits transposed in the same way. One hand had made all three sets. One hand had kept the appearance tidy enough for the Office to spend its nights dividing what had never been divided.

Under the manifests lay the chit bundle with the bridge count. Nine bridges made the expected run, the count drivers were told to give and nothing else, the number that stood at the pass checkpoint for the sealed box in the cab. The last count had come in as eight. Eight had changed the order she believed the chain was keeping. Eight had brought the sting forward by six hours, placing its strain on four positions held by four officers, with no others to spare. She held the figure beside the papers, neither larger nor smaller than the evidence that had carried it.

Off the corridor lay the sealed store room she had found after the warehouse was held. She had put her attention to it in the plain way the work required, and it had read as nothing at all. No routine pressed there. No fear thinned there. No long use left its ordinary mark. In a depot where the office carried intent and the warehouse carried the night just passed, that room carried no reading she could take hold of. It was a different kind of room, in another member state, under another roof, and she held the fact separate. She did not give it a reason.

The chair drew her back. She crossed the small floor and stopped beside the desk without touching the papers again. The seat was warm. Not with lamp heat, which fell short of it, and not with the cold that lay everywhere else in the stone room. Warmth remained where a man had sat during the working of the night. Warmth, and emptiness. The desk held his order. The chair held his absence. Between them stood everything the take had won and the one figure it had been arranged to catch.

Mira stood there and let the pieces stay in their places. Three sets of paper, and one hand. Nine bridges expected, and eight reported. A store room that read as nothing, and a chair that was still warm. She said nothing. The yard went on beyond the shutters, orderly and held, and the office kept its quiet while she looked at the empty chair and understood the shape of the night in parts, not whole.

## Eleven Taken

The depot office held its cold in the stone. Heat from the warehouse lamps did not reach this far along the corridor, and the air smelled of lamp oil, damp paper, and the iron tang of the stove that had burned low hours before. A chair stood behind the broad desk, turned a little from its place, its leather seat still warm through Mira Kovac's palm when she set her hand to it without thinking. Outside the one high window the yard lamps made pale squares on wet ground, and beyond them the mountain dark pressed close, with river sound carried faint on the wind that found the loose frame and set it tapping. Desk, ledger chest, coat hook with no coat, a cup rinsed and set mouth down on a folded cloth. Nothing overturned. Nothing hurried into ruin. The man who used this room had left it in order.

She read the office from the desk first, because metal and wood that had held a body through long work would hold the shape of that work longest. She set both hands flat to the desk edge and let the weight come, not as a picture and not as a voice, but as pressure settling behind her own ribs. Sustained intent came first, hours of it, staying at one pitch while papers were moved and remade. Impatience lived under it, checked each time it rose, a decision approached and set aside and approached again. The chair back gave her the standing moment. He had risen to decide, on his feet, with the desk between him and the door, and the intent in the wood sharpened there into something clean and final. Relief was not in it. Anger was not in it. It was the cold satisfaction of a man who had moved a piece before anyone else saw the board shift.

The last four hours lay in that order across the room. Early patience at the desk. A middle stretch of waiting that cost him more than he allowed his hands to show. Then the standing decision, and after it a brief, tightened attention toward the corridor, as if he had listened once with his whole body. The weight thinned at the threshold. She followed it to the door with her fingers on the frame, where iron and stone should have held him best, and there it ended. Not faded over distance in the manner of an old trail. Ended, because weight gave the shape of presence inside its anchor and never a direction a body took after leaving it. She could say he had been here, how long, in what temper, and that he had decided something standing. She could not say whether he had turned toward the yard or the back stairs or the north service door with its latch that had to be lifted. The door kept the fact of his passing and refused the rest. The cold of his impatience stayed in her hands after she let go, a borrowed restlessness in her own chest. She made herself stand still until the feeling named itself as his and not hers. It would keep her from sleep when sleep finally came. That was the price of the read, paid in a portion of the affect taken on and carried as if it were her own.

Dunmore's copies had said one hand wrote three sets of paper. The originals on this desk said the same with their own error repeated plainly, the depot district name rendered with the wrong vowel and the doubled consonant, toll figures copied with digits transposed in the same places. One clerk serving one organisation that had dressed itself as three. He had built the triple shape because the Office hunted single organisations and spent its nights trying to prove a centre existed. A shape with three apparent centres wasted those nights in argument. His reading of Office habit was correct, and the paper on his own desk proved he had counted on it. She closed the ledger she had opened and set it square. The chain was one organisation. It was broken in the yard and on the road and at the other positions, in reports still coming in by radio in short, careful sentences. The man at the top of it was not among the taken, because the man whose chair this was had already gone.

The accounting began in the warehouse under the yard lamps, with the depot doors held open by Office hands and the Accord seal ready on its cord beside the manifest chest. Eleven taken across the four positions, counted twice by two separate voices and written once. At the warehouse, the guard and the driver who had run were held for their own service, hands shown empty at the door, names read aloud, and led one at a time to the side room where a clerk recorded each name again. At the lower yard, two more chain men were taken in the same manner, hands shown, doors held, names read, no haste and no raised voices. On the mountain road, at the pass checkpoint and the lower toll shelter, the reports listed three drivers and a toll keeper, each name spelled back over the radio until the depot clerk confirmed it. At the third position two bonded clerks were held, their keys surrendered into a sealed pouch. At the fourth, the last two names came in late and then again for certainty, a yard guard and a store keeper, eleven in all when the clerk underlined the total and read it back. Drivers and clerks and guards, held for their own services. The sealed boxes from the cabs went into the warehouse under seal without being opened in the yard. The route was closed at both ends, with officers at the pass and the lower gate until the member services arrived. The depot itself stayed sealed under Accord seal, warehouse doors, office, and store rooms along the corridor, the seal cord knotted and pressed and recorded.

Pell Anders sat in the side room with his hands in sight on his knees and a member guard at the door who did not touch him. He looked smaller without the road under him. Mira did not question him there. She wanted the order of the road before his account of it. His cab stood in the yard with its doors open, cold spilling from the metal box of it, the smell of old leather and lamp fuel and wet wool from a coat left over the seat back. She climbed up, set her hands to the wheel and the iron rail beside the door, and the weight of long repetition rose through both anchors at once. The road came back in reverse. Last bridge first, the long span above the river where water sound came up through the boards, then the wait at the pass checkpoint with the lamp swinging and cold working through the door seam while he counted and reported and waited to be waved on. Then the bridge below the pine stand, the bridge of dressed stone at the tight bend, and the short crossing where water ran fast under iron, each in its order undone as she held on. Waits between them laid themselves out in the metal as stretches of held breath. At one checkpoint, below the summit shelter, fear thickened in the wheel until her own hands tightened past comfort. Not fear of the cargo. He had not been trusted with cargo knowledge, or names, or destinations. Fear of the count itself, of saying the number wrong, of being worth only a number and failing at the single thing he was for. Nine bridges in the full run when the run ran true. Order, waits, and fear, laid down by repetition in cab metal and wheel, and no name anywhere in it, and no face, because weight never gave who a thing belonged to once the hands lifted away. She climbed down with the cold of that checkpoint deep in her chest and the long repetition aching across her shoulders. She asked the guard that Anders be given water and a blanket, and kept separate from the warehouse guard until their services sorted them.

The principal was not among the eleven. She said it to Leyden over the radio in the office doorway, the warm chair behind her, her voice at report level because Reni was listening on the same channel from the lower gate. Eleven taken. Route closed. Depot sealed. Principal absent. His last four hours readable in the room, his direction after the door not readable at all. He was warned, or he moved on his own order change when the bridge count changed from nine to eight and folded his own movement into the new order. The evidence supported both and settled neither, and she would not dress a guess as a finding. The chair was empty. That was the finding. The one trail that might have told her where he went, the old border crossing trail, had faded over three weeks into ordinary thinness that gave her nothing she could trust.

Reni Vosk waited at the edge of the lower yard office steps where the taken were being logged and where radio traffic had thinned to reliefs and confirmations. She was twenty one and she stood very straight, as if posture could answer for the night, her radio held correctly in both hands. Dust from the yard had lightened her boots. Her face held the particular stillness of a junior officer who believed she had missed the part of the night that mattered. Mira crossed the yard boards to her and stopped at speaking distance, close enough that what followed would belong to the two of them and not to the log. Reni had been unsupported for nineteen minutes. She had held alone at the lower gate approach while a chain car passed through her position, and she had done what her instructions required. She watched. She counted. She reported the car, its direction, and its passing time, and she did not engage. While she did that, the support officer who should have been within call of her position was covering the moved timing at the warehouse end, because the sting had been brought forward by six hours on Mira's reading of the changed bridge count.

She told her, plainly. The nineteen minutes were not the plan. No version of the plan Reni had been given had left her alone without support. The decision to move the sting was hers. Mira had read eight where nine was expected, had taken it as the run order changed, and had accepted the reassignment that opened the gap because the evidence said waiting would lose the warehouse and the road together. Hers, on her judgment, on her authority for the night. Reni listened with her whole attention, and the charter she believed in showed in the question she asked when Mira finished. She asked whether she should have engaged the car, wanting to know if holding to watch and report had been a failure of nerve dressed as obedience. Mira said no, and then she said why, so the no would hold later when Reni turned the night over alone. Engaging the car would have broken simultaneity before the warehouse take, warned every position on the route, and put a lone junior officer in front of men who might have run her down without slowing. Watching, counting, and reporting had placed that car for the pass officers who took its driver on the descent. She did the position exactly. The gap around the position was not hers to answer for. It belonged to the officer who moved the night, and that officer was standing in front of her taking the account onto her own name.

Reni nodded once. She did not ask for comfort and Mira did not offer any that would soften the fact. She asked if the nineteen minutes would go into the report as they had happened, unplanned and hers alone at the gate, and Mira said they would, with the decision that caused them written beside them under her own name. That settled something in the younger officer that praise would not have reached. She asked for her next instruction. Mira sent her to the depot clerk to give her count of the car and her times for the log, in her own words, before relief. The cost of the night was not only the cold carried out of an office and a cab. It was a name written beside a gap a junior officer had filled without knowing it was a gap, and Mira would carry that entry longer than she carried the principal's impatience. Sleep, when it came, would be thin. She watched Reni cross the yard toward the lamplit office steps, radio held correctly, and then she turned back to the sealed doors and the underlined list of eleven names.

## Still in Position

The corridor off the depot office held the cold that stone keeps after a door has stood open to a mountain yard. Mira smelled lamp oil, damp wool from coats on the pegs, and the iron smell of the warehouse below. Light from the office doorway fell across the floor and stopped short of the store room door. Beyond it the depot was quiet, boots passing at intervals, voices kept low, wind finding the loose pane in the corridor window.

She went back alone, before the depot was handed over and before the outer seals were read aloud and entered. The store room door was shut, its Office seal unbroken. She broke it herself, lifted the latch, went in, and pulled the door to behind her.

The room was smaller than the office and plainer. Shelving ran along two walls, dark wood fixed to stone, the boards bare except for dust and a stack of empty sacking folded flat. A narrow table stood against the third wall with nothing on it. The floor was stone, swept, brush marks faint in the dust. The air smelled of dry wood and cold stone and sacking, and of nothing else.

She put her palm flat to the stone beside the door. The office still held its weight in her shoulders, sustained intent and impatience and a decision made standing. The warehouse held fear and effort pressed into concrete and timber. The cab in the yard held the road in reverse, bridges and waits and a checkpoint fear carried in metal and in the wheel. Every working place in the depot read heavily.

In the store room there was nothing. She laid her hand to an upright, to a board, to the back wall where the stone was coldest. She crouched and touched the floor by the table leg, then stood and rested both hands on the table and waited. Nothing rose to meet her. No fear, no haste, no long repetition, no sustained intent. A room cleared in haste would have held the haste. A room used even once for waiting would have held the waiting. This room held no more than a room in Carrowmere had held, one she had found empty in the same flat way, and she let that thought sit without following it and without speaking it aloud. It cost her nothing to read it, and that was the fact itself. This one left her cold hands and tired eyes and an ache that belonged elsewhere.

She took her notebook from her inside pocket, her own book, not an Office issue one, its cover softened from being carried against her ribs. On the page after the last private entry she wrote in pencil the depot, the room off the office corridor, and that it had held nothing when she put her hands to its wall and shelving, in a depot where every other working room read heavily. She wrote no conclusion beneath it and closed the book. The note would not go into the case file, which would carry arrests and seals and the route closed, complete on its own terms. This page stayed in her own keeping, as the earlier page had stayed, unfiled and unreported and hers.

In the doorway she thought of the office chair she had found warm. The warmth had been a plain physical fact, as plain as the dust on these shelves. A man had sat there within the hour before the take and had gone before the warehouse was held. Someone had told him to go, or the changed bridge count he had ordered himself had told him the night had shifted. The Office was small, nine officers and a handful of desks, messages passed hand to hand along a corridor. Weight could give her the shape of a man deciding and standing, and nothing of the direction he took after the door. She left the question where the chair had left it, unanswered, latched the store room door, and set a fresh seal on it herself.

A clerk met her at the stair head with the handover list and told her the all clear had been given an hour before, and that every position had been stood down. Mira looked at the list. The mark against the fourth position was a tick and initials that were not at the lower yard gate. She did not send the clerk and she did not lift the radio from her belt. She went down the depot stairs herself, across the yard where lamps burned over the sealed warehouse doors, and out along the track to the lower gate approach, and there in the lamplight, with her radio at her shoulder and her eyes on the road, Reni Vosk was still in position, an hour after the all clear, because nobody had told her to stand down, and Mira went down the last stretch of track on foot to relieve her in person.

## The Case File

### Q: What are restricted practice materials under the Meridian Accord?

Restricted practice materials are controlled goods whose movement, storage and transfer require a bonded licence in every Accord member. The category covers binding cords, seal blanks, carrier anchor pairs, practice grade alloys and comparable items prepared for bonded practice. The restriction attaches to preparation and intended use rather than to raw substance alone. Unworked alloy in ordinary trade is one matter. The same alloy cut, paired and marked for practice use is another. Office guidance treats lists as illustrative and applies the purpose test when a consignment falls near a boundary. Possession without a licence is a customs matter in the first instance. Movement across a member boundary without a manifest converts the matter to an Office concern where practice use is shown.

### Q: How is a multi member rollup authorised?

A multi member rollup requires written authorisation from the Ninth Office directorate and countersigned consent from each member service whose territory will host an arrest position. The request states the positions, the officers assigned, the night proposed and the evidence linking the positions to a single chain. A member may refuse consent for its own territory without giving reasons, and refusal removes that position from the operation rather than delaying the whole. Authorisation lapses if the night changes materially, which includes a change of timing by more than two hours. In that event the moving officer must seek fresh consent or record the urgency ground in the operation log for review by every consenting service after the event.

### Q: What characterises the Teshkar mountain routes?

Teshkar routes cross high passes on stone built roads that serve depot towns in narrow valleys. Traffic moves in single file over long spans above rivers, and winter closures can isolate a district for weeks. Bonded traffic uses designated routes only, recorded on the manifest and checked at pass checkpoints. A driver who leaves the designated route places the consignment outside bonded protection until a member service restores it by inspection. Altitude, weather and limited passing places make timing estimates unreliable, and Office planning in Teshkar allows wider margins than lowland work. Local depot districts administer the checkpoints, issue toll chits and maintain the bridge registers on which route verification depends. Freight moves slowly by design, and a single delayed wagon can hold every vehicle behind it.

### Q: What does a bonded manifest prove?

A bonded manifest proves that a named consignment was presented, described and sealed at a named depot on a stated session day, and that duty was secured for its movement to a named destination. It proves presentation and description as recorded. It does not prove that the contents match the description, that the seal remained unbroken throughout the journey, or that the person named as consignee directed the movement. Those matters require inspection, seal comparison and separate evidence. The manifest is strongest on questions of paper: which office accepted the entry, which clerk recorded it, and which copy was carried with the goods. Office procedure treats any gap between copies as a question for the issuing depot first.

### Q: What is the field strength of the Ninth Office and why is it nine?

The Ninth Office maintains nine field officers. The number is fixed by the Accord charter and may be altered only by amendment agreed by all members. Nine was selected at founding to keep the service smaller than any single member delegation and large enough to staff simultaneous work in three members without borrowing local officers. Vacancies remain unfilled until a candidate completes bonded practice certification and customs service, which can leave the Office below strength for extended periods. Planning assumes nine as a ceiling rather than a guarantee. When fewer officers are free, operations are reduced in scope rather than supplemented with untrained staff. The charter treats the limit as a safeguard against expansion by gradual practice.

### Q: What is simultaneous arrest doctrine in Office operations?

Simultaneous arrest doctrine requires that linked positions in a rollup be taken within a narrow agreed window, with no position moving early on local opportunity. The reasoning is practical. An early arrest warns the remaining positions through ordinary channels, including drivers, clerks and household contacts, often faster than an Office radio net can close the gap. Each position therefore holds to the agreed signal even when its own target appears ready sooner. Exceptions require a direct order from the operation lead and are recorded with reasons. After action review examines timing variance before it examines individual conduct, because a sound plan executed out of sequence will often produce the appearance of separate failures at each delayed position.

### Q: How do toll chits and pass checkpoints work in Teshkar?

A toll chit is issued at a pass checkpoint when bonded traffic pays the district toll or presents an exemption seal. The chit records the vehicle description, the driver mark, the direction of travel and the checkpoint name in district shorthand. Drivers surrender chits at the destination depot, where clerks reconcile them against manifests and bridge registers. A missing chit indicates an unrecorded passage, a lost paper or a route deviation, and each explanation requires different follow up. Checkpoints keep duplicate stubs in bound books that are difficult to alter without visible damage. Office investigators value chit books for their dullness. Entries made as routine, before any inquiry was expected, carry fewer signs of arrangement than statements taken afterwards.

### Q: How can a bridge count serve as a signal?

A bridge count serves as a signal where drivers are instructed to count crossings on a designated route and report the number at a checkpoint, without written record. The receiving depot compares the reported number against the expected count for that route. Agreement confirms the expected run. Disagreement indicates a closed span, a detour, an added crossing or a deliberate change in run order. The method requires no equipment and leaves no paper, which explains its appeal to chains that avoid written instructions. Its weakness is ambiguity. The same changed number can arise from repair work, weather damage or a driver error at night. Office practice therefore treats a changed count as grounds for inquiry and preparation, not as proof of intent on its own.

### Q: Why do transcription errors matter in bonded paper?

Transcription errors matter because bonded paper circulates in copies, and each copy passes through clerks in different depots and members. Small errors arise in ordinary work and usually differ from copy to copy. When the same unusual error appears in sets of paper that claim separate origins, including a misspelled district name with the same wrong vowel and doubled consonant, or figures transposed in the same places, investigators treat the repetition as evidence of a common hand. The test is specificity. A common slip proves little. A rare slip repeated across hands that should be independent proves a great deal. Clerks preserve originals where possible, since copies can inherit an error innocently from a single shared source document.

### Q: What happens to a depot seal after a taking?

After a taking, the operation lead applies an Accord seal to the depot doors, store rooms and paper cabinets, and records each seal number in the operation log. The seal preserves the site for member service inspection and prevents removal or rearrangement of goods and records before inventory. Breaking an Accord seal without joint authorisation is an offence in every member, including by the depot operator or the local service acting alone. Seals remain in place until the inventory is complete and a handover minute is signed. Photographs are not used. Officers make measured drawings and written descriptions of each sealed space, noting existing damage so that later claims about forced entry can be tested against the record.

### Q: What are chain drivers told, and what are they withheld?

Drivers engaged by a chain are commonly told a route, a reporting procedure and a collection point, and little else. They may be instructed to count bridges, to avoid written notes and to surrender toll chits at destination. Names of principals, contents of sealed boxes and final destinations beyond the next depot are withheld as a matter of chain discipline. The arrangement protects the chain if a driver is questioned, and it protects no one else. A driver who knows only a number can report only a number. Office questioning therefore focuses on procedure, timing and waiting places, where a driver has genuine knowledge, rather than pressing for names the driver was never given and may invent under pressure.

### Q: How are yard gates and watch positions assigned?

Yard gates and watch positions are assigned by approach rather than by convenience. The officer holding a gate approach watches, counts and reports movement through that approach, and does not leave the position to investigate movement elsewhere. Instructions state the reporting interval, the signal for relief and the conduct required if a vehicle passes without stopping. A watch position is an observation commitment, not a lesser form of arrest work. Its value lies in continuity. A gap of minutes can remove the only reliable account of who used a gate during a taking. Positions are plotted on the depot plan before the night, and any reassignment during the operation is ordered by radio and logged with the time and reason.

### Q: How does the Office handle a junior officer on a first field position?

A junior officer on a first field position receives written instructions, a defined approach to hold, a radio check schedule and a named officer responsible for relief. The position selected should have clear sight lines, a single expected movement pattern and no requirement to engage. Briefing covers what to report, when to report and what to do if instructions and events diverge, which is to hold position and report unless life is at risk. Supervisors assess readiness before the night and may withdraw the officer without prejudice to record. After the operation, the junior officer gives an account before hearing the accounts of others, so that the record preserves an unshaped recollection of the position as held.

### Q: What governs support reassignment during an operation?

Support reassignment during an operation is governed by the operation lead, who alone may move a support officer from one position to another. The order states the position left, the position covered, the expected duration and the officer who accepts the added risk. Reassignment is logged by radio at the time, not reconstructed afterwards. Doctrine requires the lead to weigh the gain at the receiving position against the exposure created at the position left unsupported, and to state that weighing in the after action record. Where a timing change forces reassignment, the plan should name the thinnest position in advance. Naming it does not remove the exposure. It ensures that the exposure was chosen openly rather than discovered by the officer holding it.

### Q: How are sealed store rooms in depots treated during a taking?

Sealed store rooms in depots are treated as part of the site, whether or not the operation order lists their contents. Officers do not force a sealed store room during the active phase unless entry is required for safety or to prevent removal of goods. After the site is held, each room is entered under seal procedure, inventoried and resealed pending handover. An officer who reads a room as part of normal clearance records the result in the same form as any other room. Unusual results are recorded plainly, without speculation in the operation log. Questions about a room that appears unused or empty are referred to the depot inventory and the member service records, which should account for every sealed space in a working depot.

### Q: How is a taken depot handed back to a member service?

Handover of a taken depot requires a joint inventory, a signed minute and transfer of seals from the Ninth Office to the responsible member service. The inventory lists goods, paper cabinets and sealed rooms by seal number, and notes discrepancies found since the taking. The member service may dispute an entry at the time of signature, and the dispute is recorded rather than resolved on the spot. Once the minute is signed, custody and liability pass to the member service, and the Office retains copies of the inventory and the operation log. A depot may reopen under member authority after handover, although specific consignments named in the investigation remain held under separate bonded direction until released or condemned.

### Q: What happens to drivers held during a rollup?

Drivers held during a rollup are detained by the member service in whose territory they were stopped, under that service law, with the Office present as requesting party. Each driver is identified, searched for papers under local procedure and questioned first about route, timing and instructions received. Sealed boxes carried in the cab are opened under bonded inspection and listed separately from personal effects, which are returned unless retained as evidence by court order. Drivers are not held as a group. Statements are taken individually to prevent accounts aligning through discussion. Release, charge or transfer to another member follows local decision, and the Office records the outcome for each driver in the operation log, since drivers often become witnesses in later proceedings.

### Q: What is route closure procedure after a network is broken?

Route closure procedure begins with notice to each checkpoint and depot on the designated route that bonded protection for the named chain consignments is withdrawn. Checkpoints record the notice in their bound books and stop further traffic claiming the closed manifests. Member services inspect vehicles already in transit and divert lawful goods belonging to outside consignors to bonded storage at the nearest depot. The Office prepares a closure schedule listing each segment, the service responsible and the date of confirmation from that segment. A route is reported closed only when every segment has confirmed. Partial closure is reported as partial, with the open segments named, because traffic displaced from a closed segment will test any gap that remains.

### Q: What radio discipline applies on a rollup night?

Radio discipline on a rollup night limits traffic to position reports, movement reports and orders from the operation lead. Officers use assigned call forms, keep transmissions short and avoid names, cargo descriptions and place details beyond agreed references. A position that has nothing to report transmits the scheduled check and nothing more, since silence arranged in advance can be mistaken for failure and draw traffic the net does not need. Listening discipline matters equally. Each position acknowledges orders in full, repeating timing and position back to the sender. Transmissions are logged by the desk at Carrowmere where reception permits, and gaps in reception are marked as gaps rather than filled from later accounts. After the all clear, positions remain in place until individually stood down.

### Q: Why is a principal rarely present at a depot during a taking?

A principal rarely attends a depot in person because depot work is clerical and visible, and presence creates witnesses where paper alone would leave doubt. Instructions travel through clerks, drivers and intermediaries, each holding a portion of the procedure. Where a principal does attend, the visit is usually short, tied to a decision that could not be delegated, and timed away from deliveries. After action review therefore treats an empty office as an expected outcome rather than evidence of warning. A warm chair, a cleared desk or a missing ledger may indicate recent presence, yet each has ordinary explanations as well. Review separates the question of presence from the question of warning and examines each against records before drawing any conclusion about either.
