Hyperbole is the load-bearing figure of Romeo and Juliet, the exaggeration by which the lovers measure a feeling that every ordinary scale in Verona refuses to hold. A boy who has known a girl for an evening calls her the sun and asks the sun to murder the moon. The girl answers that her bounty is as boundless as the sea and her love as deep, and that the arithmetic of giving runs backwards in her hands, increasing what it spends. The feud speaks the same swollen dialect in reverse, hating words as it hates hell, boasting servants up against walls, a father announcing that the earth has swallowed all his hopes but one. No major speech in the tragedy is free of the figure, and the figure is never decoration. Hyperbole is how the play thinks about magnitude: what a feeling is worth, what a threat is worth, what a life is worth, all get priced in a currency of suns, seas, eternities and hells because the civic currency of Verona, dowries, alliances, banishments, pardons, keeps failing to price them at all.

![A boundless sea beneath a rising sun, the two measures the lovers reach for - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict argued through every section that follows is that the lovers' hyperbole is both things the long argument about it has wanted it to be, and that the two are the same fact seen at different distances. The exaggeration is sincere: it is the only accurate instrument the lovers own, because the feeling being measured genuinely has no proportion to the four days and the walled city that contain it, and understatement would be the real falsehood. The exaggeration is also the warning the Friar hears in it from the first, because a measure that admits no limit cannot register the limits the world is about to impose, and speech that has spent its superlatives by the second evening has nothing left in reserve for the catastrophe of the fifth day. Glory and danger are not rival readings of the figure. They are the figure's single property, boundlessness, experienced first as freedom and then as exposure. The play's own internal critic, Juliet, understands this before anyone: she keeps her lover's scale and disciplines his vehicles, barring the inconstant moon, refusing the oath machinery, demanding the deed instead of the declaration. Hyperbole survives her editing. What does not survive is the assumption that the largest possible words confer the largest possible safety.

## What Hyperbole Is, and Why This Play Cannot Speak Without It

### What does hyperbole claim that measurement cannot?

Hyperbole claims the truth of a feeling by surrendering the truth of a fact, asserting that the inner magnitude is real precisely because no outer magnitude suffices for it. When Juliet's bounty is boundless as the sea, the falsehood is cartographic and the accuracy is emotional: affection is being reported on the only scale that fits it.

Measurement, by contrast, reports the container. The figure therefore belongs wherever a speaker's subject outruns the available units, which in Verona is nearly everywhere that matters.

The rhetorical tradition the playwright inherited had a name and a warning ready for the figure. Greek and Latin teachers called it hyperbole, a throwing beyond, and classed it among the figures of amplification, the deliberate overshoot by which a speaker magnifies a subject past literal belief in order to land on felt proportion. George Puttenham, Englished the art of rhetoric for the playwright's own generation in The Arte of English Poesie (1589), and his name for the device is the most honest ever given it: the Loud Liar, or, in his fuller phrase, the figure that speaks hyperbolically and in the superlative degree, excused only when the exaggeration serves a vehemence the plain statement could not carry. Puttenham's treatment holds the whole later argument about the play in miniature. The figure is a lie. The figure is licensed. The licence depends on whether the matter warrants the overshoot, and the audience, not the speaker, grants or withholds it. Every hyperbole in Romeo and Juliet is therefore a small trial, in which the house must decide whether this speaker, on this subject, at this hour, has earned the right to be disbelieved beautifully.

The tragedy is engineered so that the trials keep succeeding and the costs keep mounting. Verona's official measures are all broken or corrupt: a prince whose edicts are pronounced in absolutes and enforced in compromises, a marriage market that prices daughters in dowries and alliances, a code of honour that prices a man's worth by the insults he cannot afford to hear, a church that will marry in secret what it dares not bless in public. Against that civic arithmetic the lovers set the only rival system available to the powerless, the rhetoric of the infinite. Their hyperboles are not escapes from measurement. They are a competing measurement, conducted in a unit, the boundless, chosen because every bounded unit in the city has been captured by the feud. When the Friar counsels measure, the counsel is wise and the counsel is also, in the strictest sense, a request that the lovers denominate their feeling in the currency of the world that is killing them. The play never resolves that exchange rate. It stages it, scene by scene, until the tomb settles the account in the only coin left.

One further property of the figure matters for everything that follows: hyperbole is inflationary. A superlative spent cannot be spent again at the same value. The speaker who has made the beloved the sun by the second act must find a larger denomination by the fifth, and the denominations run out: sun, angel, saint, god, eternity, the whole creation, and after the whole creation there is only negation, the defiance of the stars, the declaration that no world exists outside one city's walls. The lovers' verbal economy thus has a built-in terminus that mirrors the plot's. Speech that begins by enlarging the world ends by abolishing it, and the distance between those two gestures is exactly the distance the tragedy travels. Tracing that exchange, from the first reported raptures over Rosaline to the plain nine syllables of the final kiss, is the work of the sections ahead.

One distinction disciplines everything that follows. Metaphor, the play's other great engine, works by transfer: it carries a thing into a foreign country, a beloved into a shrine, a friar's herbs into a moral kingdom, and its success is measured by the aptness of the new address. Hyperbole works by degree alone: it changes no address, asserts no resemblance, and simply reports the subject at a size the world does not contain. The two figures intertwine constantly, since the lovers' metaphors are nearly always inflated past their aptness into enormity, a sun that must kill, a sea that must be infinite, but their logics differ, and the difference explains their different fates in the play. Metaphors can be corrected, exchanged, refined, and Juliet corrects them all evening. A degree cannot be corrected, only granted or refused. That is why the tragedy's argument about language is finally an argument about hyperbole: it is the one figure that asks its hearer for a verdict rather than an interpretation, and verdicts, in Verona, are the currency of life and death.

## The Frame Speaks First: The Prologue's Superlatives

Before any character has exaggerated anything, the play's frame has set the exchange rate. The Chorus that opens the tragedy speaks in totals: two households, both alike in dignity, an ancient grudge, a pair of star-crossed lovers who take their life, a fearful passage of death-marked love, the whole catastrophe announced as fact before a single scene has been played to test it. The Prologue's figures are not ornamental. "Star-crossed" assigns the lovers to the government of the heavens in a single compound word; "death-marked" stamps the ending onto the love at the moment of its definition; and the promise that all of this will occupy "the two hours' traffic of our stage" is itself a hyperbole of compression, a lifetime of consequence declared containable in an afternoon. The audience is being taught, before the evidence arrives, how to listen: magnitude will be asserted first and justified later, and the justifying will be done by events rather than by arguments. Puttenham's licence is negotiated in advance, wholesale, for the entire performance.

The second Chorus, standing between the feast and the balcony, extends the frame's contract in the direction of the new love. Old desire, it reports, lies on his deathbed, and young affection sits gazing to be his heir; Rosaline, loved with such extremity a day earlier, is already a discarded estate in an inheritance figure. The Chorus speaks of Romeo's feeling in the superlatives of succession because the Chorus has the play's whole shape in view and knows the exchange is total: one absolute is not moderated by the next but replaced by it, crowned and buried in the same gesture. Frame and characters thus share a single habit. The lovers' hyperbole is often treated as a youthful excess the play's mature voices correct, but the correction never comes from the frame. The frame exaggerates first, exaggerates structurally, and reserves its plainest diction for logistics, scene changes, apologies for the stage's limits. Magnitude is the house style of the telling itself.

This matters for the verdict because it redistributes the responsibility for the figure. If hyperbole were merely the lovers' private idiom, the tragedy could be read as the story of two speakers punished for their dialect. But the dialect is universal in the telling: Chorus, servants, nurse, friar, fathers, prince and lovers all issue absolutes, and the plot's engine is the collision of absolutes that cannot all be cashed. The feud's total claim on loyalty meets the love's total claim on the future inside a city whose ruler pronounces total sentences and enforces partial ones. Hyperbole is the tragedy's medium, not its symptom, and the balcony scene is simply the place where the medium is spoken most beautifully, by the two characters who alone attempt to build something with it rather than merely to threaten, mourn or boast in it. The frame's opening superlatives are the play's way of confessing this in advance: the story about to unfold is, by its teller's own account, the most extreme version of itself, a pair, a grudge, a love and a death, each stated at maximum size because no smaller statement would be true to what is coming.

## How the Figure Persuades: The Hearer's Discount

Hyperbole cannot be understood from the speaker's side alone, because the figure is completed by its hearer. Quintilian, whose Institutio Oratoria remained the period's deepest authority on rhetoric, treats hyperbole as a deliberate straining of the truth that may overshoot belief itself, elegant when the subject is genuinely large and absurd when the strain serves only the speaker's display. The distinction he draws is not between true and false statements but between magnitudes earned and magnitudes merely asserted, and the tribunal is the audience: an exaggeration persuades when the hearer, performing the discount, still finds a remainder worth honouring, and collapses when the discount consumes the whole claim and leaves only the speaker's vanity. Every hyperbole is therefore a wager. The speaker stakes credibility that the subject will survive the markdown, and the size of the overshoot is the size of the stake.

The play internalizes Quintilian's tribunal by building an auditor into nearly every scene of exaggeration, and the auditors grade the figures with remarkable consistency. Gregory deflates Sampson in asides, and the house learns that servants' absolutes are worth their markdown of nearly everything. Benvolio audits Romeo's Rosaline superlatives with a friend's patience and prescribes the empiricist's remedy, comparison against other faces, which the plot will administer at the feast. Juliet audits Romeo's balcony figures with a lover's rigour and a rhetorician's ear, striking the unsound vehicle and passing the sound one, so that the scene's hyperboles emerge from her audit with their value certified rather than merely asserted. The Friar audits professionally, in the dispensary's units, and his grades are the play's most quoted: violent delights, love moderately, wisely and slow. Even the audience in the theatre is assigned its auditor's seat by the Prologue, which issues the tragedy's largest claims in advance and spends two hours presenting the evidence on which they may be honoured.

The catastrophe, stated in these terms, is a failure of auditing rather than of speech. The lovers' figures are graded, scene by scene, by hearers equipped to grade them, and the grades are survivable. What kills is the migration of the figures into jurisdictions where the discount cannot be performed. The feud hears language at face value or not at all: Tybalt takes a thumb bitten in his direction as a total insult and a word, peace, as a total offence, because a code of honour is precisely a system that has abolished the discount, treating every figure at face value and every face value as a debt. Capulet hears his daughter's refusal as a total rebellion for the same reason; the household, like the feud, is a literalist regime, in which speech is either obedience or treason and no markdown for rhetoric is permitted. Romeo's banishment speech fails in the opposite jurisdiction, before an auditor, the Friar, who discounts so completely that nothing of the boy's magnitude survives the grading, and the boy reaches for a knife to restore by violence the proportion that rhetoric has denied him. Between the literalists who will not discount and the auditor who discounts to zero, the lovers' figures have no safe hearing, and a figure without a safe hearing is a fortune held in a currency no bank will change. The tomb is where the unchangeable currency is finally spent, at the only rate the city ever honoured in full, life for life, exact and without remainder.

## Romeo's First Hyperboles: Rosaline, the Unheard Beloved

Romeo enters the play already fluent in the figure, and the fluency is the problem his first audience on stage has with him. His Rosaline hyperboles are maximal in scale and minimal in effect, because they are addressed to a woman who never hears them and reported to friends who have heard them too often. Of the unseen Rosaline he pronounces the most sweeping comparative in the early acts: "The all-seeing sun / Ne'er saw her match since first the world begun" (1.2). The claim appoints the sun itself, the one witness who has inspected every face since creation, as the authority for a superlative about a girl the audience will never see. It is hyperbole in its purest inherited form, the Petrarchan lover's licensed overshoot, and Benvolio's response is the response of a man checking a familiar instrument: he proposes a controlled experiment, other beauties at the feast, comparison, evidence. The figure that claims to end all comparison is answered by a friend who insists on comparing. From the start, the play surrounds its largest speeches with auditors who audit.

The Rosaline hyperboles fail, and the play is precise about why. They fail not because the scale is wrong but because the circuit is open: hyperbole of this kind is a bid, an offer of magnitude that requires a respondent to complete it, and Rosaline returns nothing, no answer, no correction, no answering excess of her own. What Romeo speaks over her is therefore monologue in the strict sense, one voice inflating in a vacuum, and the vacuum shows. Mercutio's later mockery of the Rosaline manner, conjuring the absent mistress by her inventory of parts, lands because the manner had already exposed itself as recitation: the beloved as a list of superlatives is a beloved who could be anyone, and the proof is that within a day the identical instrument is picked up and played, transposed and transformed, over Juliet. The instrument is the same. The circuit closes. Everything about the figure changes when the woman on the balcony starts answering back, correcting the vehicles, returning larger coins than she receives. The tragedy of the language begins at the moment the language starts working.

A necessary fence belongs here. The inherited machinery that carries Romeo's early excess, the Petrarchan conceit with its fires, smokes, tear-floods and besieged fortresses of the heart, has its own full treatment elsewhere in this series, as does the oxymoron, the compressed contradiction in which the Rosaline speeches specialize. Those systems are the vehicles. The subject here is the overshoot itself, the magnitude claim that rides inside the vehicles and survives their exchange: when the conceits are traded in and the oxymorons fall silent, the hyperbole remains, grows, and migrates from the lover's set speeches into every register of the play. Romeo praising Rosaline to the sun is a boy reciting. Romeo, an hour into acquaintance, asking the heavens to reorganize themselves around a girl at a window is something else, and the difference is not the size of the claim. It is that the claim is now going to be answered, tested, edited and finally paid for.

## Hyperbole in the Balcony Scene: Sun, Moon and Sea

The balcony scene is the figure's central bank, the place where the play's hyperboles are issued, exchanged, audited and revalued in a single night. Romeo opens the bidding before Juliet knows he is present, in the window speech that makes the largest ontological claim in the tragedy: "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun. / Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon, / Who is already sick and pale with grief" (2.2). The exaggeration deserves slow parsing, because its structure is the structure of the lovers' whole rhetoric. Romeo does not compare Juliet to the sun; he reassigns the office. The east is redefined as wherever she appears, the moon is demoted to a sick rival, and the cosmos is instructed to complete the coup: arise, kill. Hyperbole here is not simile with the volume raised. It is a proposed reordering of the universe in which the beloved takes the central place and the previous centre is executed for envy. The violence inside the compliment is easy to miss and fatal to miss, because the same imagination that promotes Juliet by killing the moon will, three acts later, promote her again by killing Paris and then himself. The figure's grammar is already the plot's grammar: to exalt the beloved, something else must die.

### What does Romeo claim when he makes Juliet the sun?

Romeo claims a transfer of cosmic office, not a resemblance: the east is wherever Juliet appears, the day begins at her window, and the moon, heaven's previous governor of the night, is ordered killed for the envy of being outshone, the heavens instructed to complete the transfer.

The compliment therefore carries an execution inside it. To crown the beloved, the figure deposes and destroys the reigning light, so the largest praise in the play is also its first imagined murder, offered casually, in passing, as the natural price of a sunrise.

Juliet's answering hyperbole is larger than Romeo's and different in kind, and the difference is the scene's real discovery. Where Romeo's figure conquers space, rearranging the heavens, Juliet's conquers arithmetic. "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2). The sea is chosen with an economist's precision: it is the one magnitude in the experienced world that giving does not diminish, fed by every river and lowered by none. Juliet's hyperbole therefore asserts not merely size but a law, a physics of affection in which expenditure is increase, and the assertion is aimed point blank at the household economy she has been raised in, where a daughter is a finite asset, her value a dowry, her giving a transfer that leaves the giver's house poorer. Her figure nationalizes a rival economics. Critics divide over the speech, and the division is examined in the critics' section below; what no reading can remove is the speech's deliberateness. This is hyperbole as legislation, a considered counter-claim about how value behaves, delivered by the play's most precise thinker at the moment of her greatest exposure.

### What does Juliet claim in the sea speech?

Juliet claims a law of value, not a size: her bounty behaves like the sea, the one magnitude that expenditure cannot lower, so that giving increases the giver's store and love, like the bounty, is infinite in both directions at once.

The figure is aimed at her father's economy, in which a daughter is a finite asset spent once in marriage. Against that arithmetic she sets a physics in which the asset grows by being given, and she states it as fact about the world, not as rapture about a feeling.

Between the two great speeches sits the scene's most instructive small transaction, the oath by the moon, where hyperbole meets its editor. Romeo, reaching for the largest available guarantor, swears "by yonder blessèd moon... / That tips with silver all these fruit-tree tops" (2.2), and Juliet stops the ceremony cold: "O, swear not by the moon, the inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2). The objection is a rhetorician's, not a prude's. She does not refuse magnitude; she refuses the vehicle, on the technical ground that the chosen emblem contradicts the claim it is meant to underwrite. An oath sworn by mutability guarantees nothing, and a hyperbole built on the moon builds the beloved's constancy on the heavens' most visibly inconstant body. Romeo's response is to escalate the search for guarantors until Juliet cuts the whole procedure off and substitutes the deed for the declaration: if his bent of love is honourable and his purpose marriage, let a messenger come tomorrow. The scene's hyperboles thus pass through a quality inspection that the Rosaline speeches never faced. What survives inspection is the scale. What is discarded is the machinery of swearing by things. Juliet's editing is the play's own criticism of its figure, delivered from inside the figure's greatest scene, and it is why the balcony hyperboles persuade audiences who laugh at the Act 1 hyperboles: these have been audited by the beloved herself, and the audit is part of their text.

The scene's internal ladder deserves its own notice, because within a single conversation the figure climbs every rung it will ever reach. Romeo begins below the window with astronomy, the beloved as sun displacing the moon; overhearing her, he rises to angelology, the beloved as a winged messenger glorious above his head; drawn into dialogue, he is offered and accepts divinity itself, the beloved's lover installed as the god of her idolatry; and Juliet, capping the ascent, abandons rank altogether for magnitude, the sea, the infinite, the uncountable sum. The sequence is a compressed history of the trope: cosmic office, celestial being, godhead, boundlessness, each denomination larger than the last and each abandoned faster than the last, as if the speakers can feel the inflation running ahead of them and keep changing currency to stay honest. No other scene in the play attempts the whole ladder, and none needs to. The balcony is where the exchange rates are set; every later hyperbole, from the Friar's fire and powder to the Prince's superlative of woe, is priced in the denominations coined here, in a garden, at night, by two speakers who between them invented the scale on which their world would finally weigh them.

The scene also plants the two companion systems the figure will feed on for the rest of the play. The celestial machinery of the window speech, suns, moons, stars and envious heavens, belongs to a larger conceit system in which the lovers figure each other as lights, a system traced in full in the companion study of [the conceits of light that make the lovers stars and sun](/2025/08/15/conceits-of-light-celestial-imagery/); the hyperbole is the engine inside that machinery, the claim that the light in question outranks the heavens' own. And the scene's whole economy of brightness is staked against the dark, since the sun Romeo installs is a girl standing in the night, in a garden, in a feud's orchard, which is why the lovers' inverted cosmos, explored in the study of [day and night and the lovers' inverted world](/2024/02/02/day-and-night-imagery-romeo-juliet/), is less a backdrop than a casualty list: every enlargement of their private heaven is purchased by disordering the public one.

## Hyperbole Inside a Fixed Form: The Shared Sonnet at the Feast

The lovers' first exchange is the play's controlled experiment in what hyperbole does when it is given a strict container, and the experiment's result explains much of what goes wrong later. At the feast, Romeo approaches the unknown Juliet and the two of them, improvising together, build a sonnet between them, fourteen lines of fixed measure in which his devotion is figured at maximum extravagance: her hand is a holy shrine, his hand unworthy to profane it, his lips two blushing pilgrims ready to smooth the rough touch with a kiss. The hyperbole is double and inverted. The beloved is inflated to sanctity, a shrine, a saint, while the speaker is simultaneously deflated below worth, an unworthy hand, a rough touch, a sinner requiring purgation. Overshoot upward for her, undershoot downward for him: the two gestures are one figure, the measurement of a distance so great that it can only be stated by exaggerating both its ends. Devotional hyperbole of this kind was the small change of the sonnet tradition, and the lovers spend it fluently, but the scene's distinction is that the spending is shared. Quatrains and couplets pass between the speakers like a ball neither drops, so that the exaggeration is, from its first utterance in their story, a duet rather than a solo, audited in real time by its object.

Juliet's contribution to the sonnet is to regulate it from inside, and her regulation is the template for all her later editing. She corrects the pilgrim's theology with a wit's precision, granting that saints have hands and pilgrims touch them, that palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss, deflating Romeo's abasement by exactly the amount required to make the kiss permissible. The hyperbole is not refused; it is adjusted, tuned, brought within the tolerances the form allows, and the kiss that follows is the first figure the lovers cash in full. When Romeo, emboldened, presses for a second and figures her lips as the means by which sin is purged, her closing line brings the experiment to its verdict: "You kiss by the book" (1.5). The judgement is affectionate and exact. He has performed the inherited figures correctly, by the book, and the book is precisely what she has been quietly revising all scene. Form, the exchange demonstrates, is not hyperbole's enemy. A shared measure lets two speakers exaggerate safely, because each overshoot is answered, checked and returned within the count. The sonnet's fourteen lines are a little state in which magnitude has a constitution.

The balcony scene will keep the duet and lose the constitution. There the figures are improvised without a fixed form to close them, the speeches lengthen past every count, and the editing that Juliet performs, barring moons and cancelling oaths, is the work the sonnet's architecture once did automatically. Read against the feast, the tragedy of the language comes into focus: the lovers never lose the ability to exaggerate together, beautifully, in concert. What they lose, after the feast, is every structure that once told the exaggeration when to stop: the sonnet ends, the ball ends, the household dances on, and from that point forward the figures run in open country, measured by nothing but the speakers' own judgement, which is the first casualty of the feud's timetable. Hyperbole inside a fixed form is a game two people win. Hyperbole in the open, under time pressure, with a city listening through the orchard wall, is the same gift with the safety removed.

## The Friar's Measure: Proverbs Against the Flood

Friar Laurence is the play's appointed auditor of hyperbole, and his instruments are proverbs, measures and doses, the small change of a man who deals in quantities for a living. His counter-rhetoric is announced at his first entrance, in a speech of balanced pairs and husbandry: grace and rude will, virtue and vice, the plant's two opposed kings. When Romeo arrives inflated with the new love, the Friar's responses are units and limits. "Wisely and slow. They stumble that run fast" (2.3) compresses the whole ethic into a gait. The counsel joins this play's rhetoric of measure to its machinery of pace, the subject of the companion study of [time and haste in the tragedy](/2024/01/12/time-and-haste-romeo-and-juliet/): to speak within measure and to move within measure are, for the Friar, the same discipline, and the lovers fail both together.

The auditor, moreover, is not himself immune to the figure, and his entrance speech proves it before Romeo even arrives. Alone with his basket at dawn, the Friar pronounces the doctrine of the herbs in universals as sweeping as any lover's: nothing so vile on earth that it yields the earth no special good, nothing so good that, strained from its true use, it cannot revolt to abuse; virtue itself turning vice when misapplied, and vice dignified by action. The claims are totals, alls and nothings, the grammar of hyperbole wearing a herbalist's apron, and they are the foundation on which his whole practice stands, the potion included. The Friar's moderation is thus a discipline practised upon his own absolutes rather than an absence of them: he believes in total laws and administers them in measured doses, which is either wisdom or the tragedy's most dangerous combination, certainty about the system and confidence about the quantities. When the dosing fails by a letter's miscarriage, the totals collect their price with the same indifference as the feud's.

The confrontation comes to its head in the marriage scene itself, where the Friar, performing the ceremony the lovers' hyperbole has demanded, answers their scale in their presence: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6). The couplet is the tragedy's most quoted warning, and its rhetoric deserves notice: the Friar does not understate. He answers hyperbole with a counter-image of equal compression, fire and powder kissing, an emblem as violent as anything the lovers coin. Measure, in his mouth, is not smallness; it is magnitude with a terminus drawn around it, delight acknowledged and its end dated in the same breath. He follows it with the play's most famous piece of advice, "Therefore love moderately; long love doth so; / Too swift arrives as tardy as too slow" (2.6), where even the counsel is cast as a paradox of speeds. The Friar's moderation is a rival rhetoric, not an absence of rhetoric, and the contest of the middle acts is genuinely between two systems of figuration: the lovers' boundless, and the Friar's bounded emblem, each claiming to be the true description of what love is.

### How does the "violent delights" warning answer the lovers' scale?

The warning answers scale with terminus: it grants the lovers their magnitude, delights as violent as they claim, and attaches the one property their figure excludes, an end. Fire and powder meet in a kiss and consume in the meeting, so the Friar's counter-image accepts the lovers' premise that the feeling is elemental and corrects only its physics.

Where hyperbole asserts the boundless, his emblem asserts the explosive: same size, opposite duration. The scene's argument is therefore not between feeling and reason but between two forecasts.

Juliet, notably, outbids the auditor in his own scene. Brought to be married, she greets the Friar with the tragedy's most explicit statement of hyperbole's theory, addressed not to Romeo but to the priest: "But my true love is grown to such excess / I cannot sum up sum of half my wealth" (2.6). The vocabulary is accountancy invaded by the infinite. Love is wealth, wealth has sums, sums can be totalled, except that this sum defeats the totalling, half of it already past counting. The line is the sea speech translated into ledger language, and its setting is the point: at the very altar of measure, inside the ceremony meant to bound and license the feeling, the feeling files its declaration of boundlessness in the auditor's own dialect. The Friar marries them anyway. His whole tragedy is contained in that sequence: he hears the figure correctly, names its danger exactly, and proceeds, because his own counter-figure, the marriage that might turn households' rancour to pure love, is itself a hyperbole of social engineering, an overshoot about what one secret ceremony can accomplish. The auditor is a speculator too. Nobody in Verona speaks within measure about anything that matters to them, which is why the warning, examined in full in the companion study of [the Friar's "violent delights" warning and its violent ends](/2025/04/11/violent-delights-violent-ends-analysis/), functions less as the play's verdict than as its forecast: accurate, early, and useless to everyone who hears it.

Two features of the catalogue repay attention before the analysis moves on. The first is the direction of travel in the vehicles: the early figures borrow witnesses (the sun as inspector of faces), the middle figures seize offices (the sun's throne, the sea's physics, infinity's ledger), and the late figures turn against the cosmos itself (defiance of the stars, abolition of the world). Hyperbole begins by appealing to the universe as referee and ends by declaring war on it, because the universe, petitioned often enough, has started answering with corpses. The second feature is the distribution of the figure across the feud. Hatred's hyperboles are not smaller than love's; they are the same size, drawn from the same stock of hells, earths and absolutes. Verona does not have a rhetoric of love and a rhetoric of hate. It has one rhetoric of magnitude, and the households and the lovers draw on the same account, which is the deepest sense in which the two households are alike in dignity: alike in inflation.

A third feature is the figure's migration between speakers as the acts advance, which the catalogue's order makes visible. Act 1 distributes hyperbole democratically, servants, heir, father and lover all issuing it within a few scenes, as if inflation were simply Verona's weather. The middle acts concentrate it in the lovers and their auditor, the feud's speakers falling comparatively silent while the marriage is made and the duel fought in a prose of challenges and reports. Then, from the banishment onward, the figure deserts the lovers' mouths at the moments of their greatest need and reappears in the mouths of the bereaved and the authorities: a father's grief absolutes, a mother's curse, a prince's closing superlative. The lovers, who owned the figure when it was glorious, are progressively reduced to its plainest forms, defiance without vehicle, a kiss and nine syllables, a dagger addressed in eight words, while the world that audited them inherits their instrument and spends it on monuments. The migration is the tragedy's rhetoric completing its circuit: magnitude begins as the property of the powerless young, and ends as the official language of the powerful old, who discover its satisfactions only when there is nothing left to apply it to but the dead.

## Hyperbole in the Feud's Mouth: Boasts, Oaths and Rage

The feud speaks hyperbole as its native dialect, and the play establishes the fact in its first minutes, before any lover has exaggerated anything. Sampson, a serving-man, opens the action pricing himself in totals: he will take the wall of any man or maid of Montague's, he will be civil with the maids, he will cut off their heads, or their maidenheads; a dog of the house of Montague moves him. The boasts are comic because the boaster is a servant whose valour evaporates on schedule, but their grammar is the grammar of everything serious that follows. Sampson's hyperbole asserts worth as totality: not better than one rival, but against the whole house at once, on every axis, sexual, martial, civic. Gregory's running deflation, the friend's aside that punctures each boast as it inflates, is the feud's version of Benvolio's auditing, comedy's reminder that in this city every absolute is issued by someone who will shortly be running away. The brawl that follows, in which the whole town takes up arms over a bitten thumb, is the boast acted out: a city whose speech admits no proportions will reliably produce violence out of all proportion to its causes.

Tybalt's contribution to the same scene raises the register from boast to doctrine. Drawn against Benvolio, who has come to part the fray, Tybalt announces the feud's theory of language in the tragedy's most concentrated hyperbole of hatred: "What, drawn, and talk of peace? I hate the word / As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee" (1.1). The equation is exact and deranged. A word, peace, is hated with the hatred properly owed to hell; hell, an entire family, and one present man are set in a series as equivalents. Hyperbole here does what it does for the lovers, in mirror image: it abolishes proportion between the object and the response, and in abolishing proportion it abolishes negotiation, since a man who hates a word as he hates hell cannot be argued with in words. The lovers' exaggerations at least propose a world, a reordered heaven, a new physics of giving. Tybalt's proposes only an equivalence of targets. Yet the structural kinship is the play's point. Love and hate in Verona are the same figure pointed in opposite directions, each incapable of the middle register in which peace, contracts and survivals are negotiated, and the households' servants and the households' heirs catch the dialect from the air.

The fathers speak it too, in both directions of feeling. Capulet, courting Paris in the second scene, prices his daughter in the hyperbole of extinction: "The earth hath swallowed all my hopes but she; / She is the hopeful lady of my earth" (1.2). The figure is grief's arithmetic, many children buried, one hope surviving, and it does real work in the negotiation, assuring the suitor that the girl is the house's entire future and must therefore be courted, waited for, won. Three acts later the same father's hyperbole runs to abuse when the asset refuses the transfer: the rage speech of Act 3 unloads a superlative contempt, baggage, wretch, a mouthful of absolutes in which the daughter is revalued from the house's one hope to its disposable refuse, without the facts having changed. Hyperbole is the father's instrument in both scenes because hyperbole is how his world talks about value: a child is everything or nothing, the earth or the street, since the middle denominations, a daughter as a person with a partial claim on her own disposal, are not minted in his economy. The rage and the tenderness are one figure, and the girl who has learned to speak the sea's infinity at a window is, at home, spoken of in her father's infinities of worth and worthlessness.

Even the play's grief runs on the figure. Capulet over the supposedly dead Juliet in Act 4 pronounces death his son-in-law and his heir, a hyperbole that converts the wedding machinery wholesale into funeral machinery, while the lamentations of the household pile superlative on superlative in a set piece the play itself seems to watch with raised eyebrows, since the Friar must eventually cut the noise with measure: peace be to this house, the heavens do have all. The scene is the figure's reductio. Hyperbole of grief, spoken over a girl who is not dead, by the man whose hyperbole of rage drove her to the potion, in the hearing of the priest whose hyperbole of social engineering arranged the marriage: every absolute in the room is false in fact and true in feeling, and the audience, alone in possession of the truth, hears the figure exposed. Exaggeration that once crowned a beloved at a window now decorates a staged corpse, and the distance between the two scenes measures how far the rhetoric of magnitude has travelled from licence into liability.

## The Small World: Mercutio's Miniatures and the Nurse's Measures

Against the lovers' enlarging figure the play sets two comic counter-rhetorics, and understanding hyperbole in this tragedy requires both, because they define its borders. The first is Mercutio's miniaturization, hyperbole's exact inverse, the figure of meiosis raised to virtuosity. The Queen Mab speech shrinks the fairy world to the scale of an agate stone on an alderman's forefinger, a waggon no bigger than a hazelnut made by the joiner squirrel, couriers drawn by a team of atomies, galloping night by night through lovers' brains. Where Romeo inflates the beloved to the size of the sun, Mercutio deflates desire itself to the size of a nut, and the deflation is diagnostic: Mab delivers to each sleeper the precise fantasy his office craves, lawyers' fees, soldiers' throats to cut, a parson's benefice, so that the whole economy of human wanting is exhibited as a toy theatre of appetites. The speech is the play's coldest audit of its lovers. It proposes that the infinite longings Romeo is about to speak are, from a sufficient height of irony, very small things occurring in very small heads. The proposal is never answered in argument. It is answered by the plot, which makes the small things enormous: the boy who joked about dreams dies cursing both houses in the tragedy's most consequential speech, and the atomies turn out to govern the world after all.

Mercutio's death converts his miniatures into the feud's largest hyperbole, and the conversion is instructive. "A plague o' both your houses" is an absolute, a curse addressed to two entire families and to the city that contains them, and unlike the lovers' absolutes it is fulfilled to the letter: the houses are plagued, in their heirs, within three days. The play thus contains one hyperbole that heaven ratifies word for word, and it is a curse spoken by the play's chief deflater. The distribution is a judgment. Exaggerations of love are licensed fictions, believed by their speakers and pitied by their audience; the exaggeration of hatred, in a city built to enact it, is a prophecy. Verona's world is so arranged that curses cash out and blessings do not, which is as compact a definition of the tragedy as the play affords.

The Nurse supplies the second counter-rhetoric, hyperbole at ground level, the figure domesticated to bodies, cloth and teeth. Her praise of Paris is a sculptor's superlative, "a man of wax" (1.3), the suitor as a perfectly modelled figure, and her oath on Juliet's age is a gambler's hyperbole, laying fourteen of her teeth, of which she has but four, on the accuracy of her memory. Where the lovers reach for seas and suns, the Nurse reaches for the nearest stock of magnitudes her world contains: waxworks, wagers, the size of a girl she has measured with her own hands since weaning. Her exaggerations are never cosmic, and they are never quite lies either; they are the overshoot of a woman whose whole experience of magnitude is household scale. The contrast is not between honesty and inflation. It is between two currencies of the absolute, one drawn from the heavens and one from the pantry, and the play lets the pantry version make several of its shrewdest observations, since the Nurse, alone among the older generation, has actually watched the beloved grow and can price her in years, teeth and dresses rather than in dowries or stars. When she later counsels Juliet to take Paris as the available magnitude, Romeo being dead to her as a banished man, her hyperbole reverses into the meanest arithmetic in the play, and the betrayal is felt precisely as a collapse of scale: the woman of wax and wagers suddenly counting in single units.

### Why is excess the point of the figure rather than its defect?

Excess is the figure's mechanism, not its malfunction: hyperbole works by overshooting belief so that the hearer, discounting the overshoot, lands on the intended magnitude, which is always larger than any literal statement could have carried. An exaggeration that stayed within the plausible would be a comparison, and comparisons invite audit.

The lovers need the discounting process itself, the hearer's active conversion of the impossible figure into felt truth, because that conversion is the closest language comes to making another person feel a feeling at its actual size.

## Hyperbole Among the Women: A Sea, a Book, a Curse

The play distributes its figure by gender as carefully as by household, and the distribution contains one of its quietest judgements. The men of Verona exaggerate about honour, hatred, grief and girls; the women exaggerate, when they are permitted to, about the beloved and about the daughter, and the difference between Lady Capulet's rhetoric and her daughter's is the difference between the marriage market and the sea. Lady Capulet introduces Paris to Juliet in the play's most measured sales pitch, figuring the suitor as a book to be read and studied: "Read o'er the volume of young Paris' face, / And find delight writ there with beauty's pen" (1.3). The figure is a conceit of appraisal, the beloved as a text whose value a careful reader can verify line by line, and its scale is the scale of the library and the shop, not the heavens. A mother formed by the market speaks of a husband as an acquisition with legible contents, and her hyperbole, such as it is, runs to social inventory, the feast's splendour, the suitor's standing, magnitudes that can be checked. Against this book the daughter will set an ocean within the day, and the two figures, volume and sea, state the play's whole argument about how a woman may be valued: by being read, priced and shelved, or by declaring herself boundless and unpriceable in the same breath.

Lady Capulet's own absolutes arrive only under catastrophe, and they arrive, as Mercutio's did, in the form the city ratifies. In the crisis of Act 3, refused and raging at the daughter who will not take Paris, she exits on a wish that functions as a curse: "I would the fool were married to her grave" (3.5). It is the play's most quietly terrible hyperbole, a mother's exaggeration of contempt, spoken as a door closes, and the plot honours it with the literal-mindedness it reserves for curses: the fool is married to her grave, in the Capel monument, within the week. Her formal grief in Act 4 performs the figure in its social register, lamentation by repetition and superlative over a daughter who is not dead, the household's set piece of wailing that the Friar must eventually cap with measure. Between the book of Paris and the grave of the wish, Lady Capulet's rhetoric traces the market's full circuit: a daughter appraised in a volume, cursed in a sentence, mourned in a volume again, this time of noise. The sea was never in her vocabulary. She is the play's evidence that hyperbole's dangers are not the lovers' invention but the vernacular of the world that raised them all, spoken by a mother in a doorway with no idea she has pronounced a sentence.

Juliet's distinctness among the women is therefore not that she exaggerates while they measure. It is that she alone subjects the exaggeration to thought. The Nurse's wax and wagers are the figure as folk wisdom, unexamined and mostly harmless; her mother's book and curse are the figure as social instrument, examined only by the plot, which collects on the curse. Juliet's sea is the figure as philosophy, a claim about value argued for, defended against the oath's false guarantors, and revised under pressure until it can be stated in three plain words. The tragedy's rhetoric has often been described as a contest between the lovers' poetry and the world's prose. The women's scenes suggest the finer truth: the contest is between kinds of hyperbole, the kind that prices people and the kind that frees them, and it is fought in every register of the play, by speakers who share a city, a dialect and almost nothing else.

## When Hyperbole Fails: Banishment, Despair and the Road to Mantua

The figure's collapse is charted in Romeo's later speeches, where hyperbole survives but changes sign. In the Friar's cell, hearing his sentence, Romeo pronounces the banishment speech, whose central figure is a negation of the world: "There is no world without Verona walls, / But purgatory, torture, hell itself. / Hence-banishèd is banish'd from the world, / And world's exile is death" (3.3). The balcony hyperbole enlarged the beloved until she filled the heavens; the cell hyperbole shrinks existence until it fits inside one city's walls, with everything outside reclassified as hell. It is the same instrument, a total claim staked on a single term, and its bankruptcy is visible in the exchange it provokes. The Friar, the play's auditor, responds with the tragedy's plainest rebuke, calling the raving a kind of animal behaviour and offering, against the abolished world, an inventory of consolations counted on fingers: Juliet lives, Tybalt is dead who would have killed him, the prince's doom turned to mercy. Measure answers magnitude with a list, and the list is unanswerable except by a knife, which Romeo promptly reaches for. Hyperbole in despair has lost the quality that redeemed it in love, a respondent who shares the scale. Juliet answered the sun with the sea. The Friar answers the abolished world with arithmetic, and the boy on the floor cannot hear arithmetic as anything but mockery.

Juliet's later hyperboles undergo the same darkening in a different key. Her wedding-night invocation had requisitioned the sun's own horses to hurry the dark; in the potion speech her imagination runs to the vault's inventory of horrors, waking among ancestral bones, dashing out her brains with a kinsman's joint. The figure that once asserted bounty now assays terror, enlarging every feared particular to the size of the whole future. Hyperbole, the play keeps demonstrating, is a neutral engine of magnitude: it enlarges what the speaker dwells on, and as the play closes in, what the speakers dwell on is death. The instrument does not fail them. It obeys them, with increasing fidelity, in a world that has arranged for their dwelling places to be tombs.

The last of Romeo's enlargements is the smallest and the most famous. In Mantua, hearing from Balthasar that Juliet lies in the Capel monument, he answers with five words: "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1). The hyperbole is total and contentless. No vehicle is offered, no comparison drawn; a single gesture of defiance is aimed at the entire government of the heavens by a boy with no army, no plan and, within the hour, a purchased poison. As rhetoric it is the figure reduced to its essence, magnitude without mediation, the will asserting a scale it cannot support. As psychology it is exact: the speech of a mind that has lived for four days inside absolutes has no middle register in which to file a catastrophe, and so files it as a declaration of war. The stars do not answer. The apothecary does, and the play's final movement follows the hyperbole home to the tomb, where the figure's account is settled in the only terms Verona honours.

## Apostrophe: Hyperbole's Companion, Addressing What Cannot Hear

Hyperbole rarely travels alone in this play. Its constant companion is apostrophe, the figure of address turned toward a hearer who is absent, dead, inanimate or divine, and the companionship is structural: to exaggerate a subject's magnitude is already half to summon it, and to summon it is to claim a presence the world has not granted. Romeo's balcony speech begins, before Juliet appears, as apostrophe to an empty window and an unrisen sun. Juliet's wedding-night speech is apostrophe nearly entire, addressed to Phoebus's horses, to night, to love-performing night, to Romeo himself in his absence, a bride commanding the cosmos by name because the one name she wants is not yet in the room. The figure asserts, with each invocation, that the addressed power can hear and might obey, an exaggeration of presence laid over the plain fact of solitude. Where hyperbole inflates size, apostrophe inflates audience, and the lovers need both, since their world contains almost nobody cleared to hear what they have to say at its true volume.

The tragedy's arc can be traced in the changing fortunes of these addresses. In the early acts the apostrophes are answered, in effect if not in fact: Juliet appears at the window above the boy addressing the east; night does fall on the wedding speech, and Romeo does climb into the chamber it was summoned to darken. The fiction indulges its speakers, staging a world that seems to take orders from being named largely enough. After the banishment the answers stop. Romeo in Mantua addresses the stars and receives a poison-seller; Juliet in the vault addresses, in imagination, a whole senate of horrors and receives silence; Romeo in the tomb addresses death itself, figuring it as a lean abhorred monster and a paramour who keeps Juliet for embraces elsewhere, and death, alone among the play's addressed powers, keeps the appointment without speaking. The apostrophes grow more magnificent as their answering rate falls, which is the inflationary law of the figure working at the level of address: a summons unanswered must be issued again at higher volume, and the play's last acts are loud with unanswered summonses.

Juliet's final apostrophe is the shortest in the play and the only one addressed to an object that obeys. "O happy dagger, / This is thy sheath" (5.3) speaks to a knife as to a servant being given an order and a home, and the knife performs exactly what it is told. After a tragedy of addresses flung at suns, moons, nights, stars and death, the one perfectly answered apostrophe is addressed to the instrument of the speaker's own destruction. The economy of the figure closes there: magnitude of address, which began by crowning a beloved with the sun's office, ends by housing a blade in a breast it calls a sheath, and the rhetoric that promised the lovers a hearing universe leaves them, at the last, with the single listener who never failed to respond, the weapon in the hand.

## Plain Speech: The Moments the Play Puts the Figure Down

The strongest evidence for what hyperbole does in this play is the set of moments where it stops, because the stops are placed with surgical care and they land harder than any enlargement around them. The first occurs inside the balcony scene itself. After the seas and suns, after the barred moon and the refused oath, Juliet recalls the escaping Romeo to extract the scene's actual business, and her demand collapses the evening's whole inflation into a count: "Three words, dear Romeo, and good night indeed" (2.2). Three words: I love thee, or marry me tomorrow, the smallest denomination of the night, requested by the speaker of its largest. The plainness is not a rejection of the hyperbole that precedes it; it is its redemption, the conversion of declared magnitude into a performable act. Juliet, the play's best rhetorician, knows that a figure is worth what can be cashed from it, and she calls the loan.

Her plainness has a doctrine behind it, stated in the same scene in the play's most famous piece of anti-figuration: the argument that names are separable from things, that a rose by any other name would smell as sweet, that Romeo would retain his perfection divested of his title. The speech is usually read as philosophy of language, and it is also a lover's audit of her own rhetoric: having traded in absolutes all evening, Juliet pauses to note that the absolutes are labels, that the man exceeds every figure assigned to him, including the figure of the enemy's son. Plain speech in her mouth is not poverty of invention. It is the moment invention acknowledges its own limits and points past itself at the thing. That the pointing fails, that the name Montague turns out to be load-bearing in the world if not in the garden, is the tragedy's linguistic thesis in one line: Verona runs on names, and no private rhetoric, however boundless, can repeal them.

The tomb completes the pattern. Romeo's final speech contains genuine hyperbole still, the eyes looking their last, the arms taking their last embrace, death as a amorous paramour in the earlier raging, but it ends in the plainest sentence Shakespeare ever gave a dying lover: "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3). Nine syllables, no vehicle, no cosmos, a stage direction spoken aloud. After four days of suns and seas the figure is set down, and the setting down is the most devastating rhetoric in the play, because the audience has been trained by then to hear plainness as the sound of the account closing. Juliet's end matches it: a noise, a dagger identified in four words, "O happy dagger, / This is thy sheath", and the falling silence the hyperbole had been holding off since the balcony. The play's loudest figure thus frames its quietest lines, and the frame is the meaning. Exaggeration was the sound of the future being imagined. Plain speech is the sound of it arriving.

## The Economics of the Boundless: Dowries, Ducats and Infinite Wealth

Hyperbole in this play is shadowed everywhere by its opposite, the exact sum, and the collision between the two systems is clearest where money is concerned. Verona prices everything the lovers declare priceless. Juliet has a market value, assessed by Paris, negotiated by her father, expressed in the language of lands and alliances; Romeo has an income, a banishment price, and, in Mantua, exactly the poverty that makes an apothecary bribable. Against that ledger the lovers keep filing their declarations of the uncountable, and the declarations are not naive about the ledger. They are drafted in its vocabulary. Juliet's bounty is a treasury term before it is a sea; her love at the altar is wealth that defeats summing; her complaint in the parting scene is framed as satisfaction, a debt term, never to be satisfied with Romeo until she beholds him. The figure's whole career in her mouth is an argument with accountancy conducted inside accountancy's own words, which is why the sea speech is best read as a hostile takeover of the marriage market's language: the asset announces that it is not an asset, that its value obeys the opposite of market law, increasing under expenditure and refusing all totals.

Romeo's money hyperboles run the other way, toward damnation by gold. At the apothecary's shop he hands over forty ducats with the play's most concentrated piece of economic rhetoric, the declaration that gold is the worse poison, that men sell their souls for it while the drug he buys merely kills a body. The speech inverts the balcony: there, the beloved's value was inflated past the heavens; here, the world's value is inflated past the drug's, gold out-poisoning poison, and the speaker who once made a girl the sun now makes a coin the devil. The apothecary's answer is the tragedy's plainest sentence about measure, that his poverty and not his will consents, a man pricing his soul at forty ducats because the alternative price is starvation. No hyperbole survives in that shop. The transaction that seals the ending is conducted entirely in exact sums, weights and legal penalties recited by the seller, and the contrast is the play's bleak joke about rhetoric: the lovers' boundlessness could move the heavens rhetorically and Paris physically, but the ending is purchased in the feud's own currency, cash, at the going rate, from a man too poor to exaggerate.

The fathers close the account in the same two currencies. Over the bodies, Montague and Capulet compete one final time in the idiom each has always used, promising monuments: a statue in pure gold, a jointure matched by an equal statue, the daughter and the son converted at last into precious metal at a stated weight of civic expense. It is hyperbole literalized, the beloved made priceless in fact by being rendered in gold, and the play lets the gesture stand as both reparation and confession. The households that priced the living children too cheaply, in dowries and defiance, now price the dead ones at infinity, in the only infinite their world stocks. Juliet's sea had promised a wealth that grows by giving. The statues are wealth that can only be spent by keeping, frozen at the moment of valuation, and the difference between the sea and the statue is the difference between the love's economics and the feud's, stated by the ending without comment.

## Juliet's Arc: The Figure from Sea to Vault

Juliet's hyperbole has a biography, and tracing it exposes the play's design more cleanly than any single speech. She begins as the figure's editor, the auditor at the balcony who bars the moon and demands the deed; she becomes, within a day, its boldest legislator, filing the sea's law of value at the altar; and she ends as its darkest practitioner, her imagination enlarging not the beloved but the instruments of her own destruction. The scale never shrinks. What changes is the object, and the change of object is the tragedy told in rhetoric.

The wedding-night speech is the hinge. Alone, awaiting the bridegroom, Juliet requisitions the sun's own team to hurry the dark along, commanding Phoebus's steeds to gallop apace toward their lodging and night to arrive as a matron, sober-suited, to teach her how to lose a winning match. The hyperbole is cosmic in vehicle and shockingly practical in intent: the heavens are addressed as a transport system running late. Its confidence is total. The girl who edited Romeo's oath now issues orders to the machinery of day itself, and the orders are obeyed within the fiction of her expectation; night comes, Romeo comes, the marriage is consummated in the borrowed dark. Rhetorically it is the figure's high noon, the moment when the largest speech and the actual world briefly coincide. Everything after is the figure surviving the coincidence's collapse. In the parting at dawn she is already bargaining with omens, wishing the lark were the toad, hearing the nightingale on the pomegranate tree, and her language begins the slide from commanding magnitudes to misreading them, the hyperbole of a mind that has learned the world does not take orders.

The potion speech completes the inversion. Alone again, vial in hand, Juliet's imagination performs for terror exactly the amplification it once performed for love: the mixture may fail, the Friar may have poisoned her to hide his shame, she may wake before Romeo comes, suffocate in the vault's foul air, play with her forefathers' joints, dash out her desperate brains. Each fear is developed past probability into set-piece, a catalogue of horrors as formally accomplished as the balcony's catalogue of glories, and the resemblance is the horror: the same instrument, the same virtuosity, the object exchanged. Where the sea speech enlarged a giving until it became infinite, the vault speech enlarges a grave until it becomes the world. Critics who find the lovers' hyperbole merely decorative have to explain this scene away, because here the figure is plainly load-bearing psychology: a mind with no middle register, trained by love to think in totals, confronting fear and thinking in totals still. The play's rhetoric does not desert its heroine in the vault. It escorts her there, which is the most damning and the most moving thing that can be said about it.

## Eternity on Loan: Hyperbole and the Play's Clock

The lovers borrow eternity because the plot lends them no time, and the exchange is the figure's temporal form. The action occupies roughly four days, Sunday to Thursday, a span the play keeps audibly counting in messengers, appointed hours, a wedding moved forward by a day, a potion measured at forty-two hours. Against that stopwatch the lovers speak in the largest temporal denominations available: infinite, eternal, ever, the stars' fixed courses, a love as deep as the sea is old. The disproportion is usually read as youth's impatience, and it is more exact than that. Hyperbole is the rhetoric of speakers who cannot have duration and so claim magnitude instead, trading the length they are denied for an intensity they can assert. A love that cannot promise fifty years promises the infinite, because the infinite is the only quantity that cannot be checked against the calendar by Friday.

The feud, characteristically, borrows the same eternity for hatred: an ancient grudge, houses defined by an inherited quarrel whose origin nobody on stage can date, a tomb that has been filling with Capels for generations. Verona's hatred is old in the way the lovers' love is deep, by assertion, without documents. The play's two infinities, the feud's backward eternity of grievance and the lovers' forward eternity of devotion, meet in the vault, where both turn out to have been mortgages on the same four days. Even the Prologue speaks the temporal hyperbole before any character can: star-crossed, death-marked, a two hours' traffic that contains a lifetime. The audience is told at the outset that magnitude will substitute for duration here, that two hours must hold what years could not, and the whole performance is the working out of that exchange rate.

The audience's own experience ratifies the bargain nightly. Nobody leaves the theatre feeling that four days were too few for the love, because the hyperbole has done its temporal work on the house as surely as on the speakers: intensity, asserted at sufficient scale and paid for at sufficient cost, registers as duration in memory. The play is remembered as an epic of devotion rather than a long weekend of catastrophe, and the misremembering is the figure's final victory, the one exaggeration the lovers win outright.

## Staging the Overshoot: Hyperbole in Performance

Hyperbole is the figure most exposed by performance, because an audience in a theatre performs Puttenham's discounting operation collectively and audibly: pitch the overshoot a fraction too high and the house laughs where it should ache; pitch it too low and the lovers sound embarrassed by their own poetry, which is worse. Generations of actors have solved the problem in opposite directions, and the solutions map the criticism. The declamatory tradition, at home in the large nineteenth-century theatre, took the figures at full face, suns and seas delivered as oratory to the gallery, the balcony played as an aria in which magnitude was the meaning. Such playing honours Empson's side of the argument: the feeling is that size, and the actor's task is to have a voice and a conviction large enough to fill the claim rather than to apologize for it. Its risk is the one Benvolio embodies, the suspicion of recitation, of rapture by the book, beautifully spoken and internally unexamined.

David Garrick's celebrated eighteenth-century version of the play supplies the most instructive evidence in the whole performance record, because Garrick, the age's defining Romeo, kept the balcony's raptures and rewrote the tomb: in his alteration, Juliet wakes before Romeo dies, and the lovers are granted a final exchange in the vault. The change was popular for decades, and its logic is the answering thesis of this article stated in stagecraft. Garrick's audiences, saturated in the lovers' hyperbole, demanded that the hyperbole be answered in dialogue at the end as it had been answered at the balcony, that the play's greatest speech situation, one lover addressing another who cannot reply, be repaired into mutuality before the silence. The alteration is usually deplored as sentimentality, and it is better understood as a diagnosis: the culture that received the play most devotedly felt its ending as a rhetorical deprivation, the closing of the circuit through which all the play's magnitude had flowed. Restore the answering voice and the deaths, however briefly, rejoin the duet.

Modern playing tends to the opposite solution, conversational pitch, the figures tossed off at the speed of thought, Romeo and Juliet as two quick-witted teenagers whose poetry is simply how fast minds talk at night. This honours the Mahood side of the argument, hyperbole as cognition in progress, figures being found rather than monuments unveiled, and it has the great virtue of making Juliet's editing audible: her corrections land as interruptions in a live negotiation because there is a live negotiation to interrupt. Its corresponding risk is evaporation, the sea speech delivered as a pleasantry, magnitude ironed into charm. The productions that fail least, across the whole record, share one decision: they treat the figures as measurements the speakers are staking themselves on, neither inflated for the gallery nor deflated for plausibility, and they give the discounting work to the audience, where the text assigns it. Hyperbole in the theatre is a contract. The actors assert the size; the house converts the assertion into feeling; and when the conversion happens, the loudest lie in rhetoric becomes, for the length of a scene, the most accurate sentence in the play.

## The Critics on the Lovers' Exaggeration: Sincerity, Convention, Alarm

The argument about this play's hyperbole is one of the oldest in its criticism, because the figure sits exactly where interpretation divides: is the swollen speech the sound of a feeling too large for language, or the sound of a convention too large for the feeling, a borrowed grandeur the play itself exposes? The critics named here divide on that question, and their division is adjudicated at the end of the section rather than merely displayed, because the text supports a verdict.

William Empson, in Some Versions of Pastoral (1935), gives the sincerity case its most durable form. Reading the balcony scene's exchanges, Empson treats the lovers' absolutes as a version of the pastoral paradox he found everywhere in the period's writing: the infinite housed in the finite, the whole world claimed by two children in a garden, each becoming the other's universe precisely by surrendering to the surrender. On his account the hyperbole is not a failure of proportion but a discovery about mutuality, that love experienced from inside really does revalue the cosmos, demoting suns and promoting windows, and that the poetry's job is to make that inner revaluation credible rather than to apologize for it. The sea speech, for Empson's way of reading, is the paradox stated as economics: giving that increases the giver is absurd in the market and exact in the bedroom, and the figure's truth is the truth of the second location.

M. M. Mahood, in Shakespeare's Wordplay (1957), supplies the mechanism Empson leaves implicit, arguing across the play's verbal fabric that its wordplay and figuration are cognitive instruments, ways the characters think rather than ornaments on thoughts already formed. In the study's account of the lovers' heightened speech, hyperbole functions as a mode of measurement being invented under pressure: the lovers lack a language calibrated to what has happened to them, the inherited Petrarchan scale being built for unreturned devotion over years, and their overshoots are experiments, attempts to find the denomination in which the new fact can be stated. Mahood's reading explains a feature of the balcony scene the sincerity case needs: the figures are revised in real time, moon barred, oath cancelled, sea substituted, because the speakers are not reciting magnitudes but searching for them, and the search is the sincerity.

Susan Snyder, in The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies (1979), files the dissent that any honest verdict must answer. Snyder's structural account holds that the play is built on a comic matrix, the young lovers, the blocking fathers, the go-betweens, the secret marriage, which the tragic ending invades and breaks; and the lovers' rhetoric belongs, on her showing, to the comic world's equipment, the festive idiom of a form in which exaggeration is licensed because the form guarantees that nothing exaggerated will be finally harmed. Hyperbole is comedy's small change. When the matrix cracks at Mercutio's death, the idiom does not crack with it; the lovers go on speaking the festive scale inside a structure that has stopped protecting speakers, and the mismatch, Snyder argues, is a principal source of the peculiar pain of the last acts: the audience hears wedding language at funerals because the speakers own no other. On this reading the figure is neither sincere measurement nor empty convention but a generic inheritance spent in the wrong genre, beautiful and, in the strictest sense, inappropriate to the world it is spoken into.

Jill L. Levenson, editing the play for the Oxford Shakespeare (2000), reframes the dispute around Juliet's editing, the feature of the balcony scene the older debate underweights. Levenson's introduction and commentary stress the scene as a negotiation conducted in rhetoric, in which Juliet repeatedly takes up Romeo's figures and revises them, refusing the moon, redirecting the oath, converting declaration into arrangement; the hyperbole that emerges from the scene is therefore a co-authored text, tested by its addressee in a way no Petrarchan set piece ever is. Her editorial evidence matters as much as her argument: the scene's great speeches stand substantially stable across the early texts, the 1597 and 1599 quartos alike, which removes the suspicion that the excess is theatrical accretion and establishes it as design, revised by the playwright rather than inflated by the players.

René Weis, whose Arden Third Series edition (2012) supplies the text quoted throughout this article, adjudicates at the level of the line. Weis prints the fuller 1599 text of the balcony scene, glosses the sea speech's economics against the household bookkeeping it answers, and notes the care with which the scene distributes its figures between the speakers: Romeo assigns offices, Juliet states laws. The edition's cumulative effect is to show the hyperbole as engineered, each overshoot placed, balanced and answered, the work of a dramatist in control of his instrument rather than a young writer intoxicated by it. Where editors of an older school occasionally treated the lovers' flights as effusion to be indulged, the modern text presents them as architecture to be inspected.

The adjudication this article defends takes Empson and Mahood on the experience and Snyder on the structure, and finds them compatible at exactly one point: Juliet's editing. The hyperbole is sincere cognition, the lovers genuinely measuring a real magnitude with the only instrument that fits; it is also a comic inheritance, the festive scale of a world that guarantees its speakers safety; and the play knows both, which is why it builds its own critic into the balcony scene and lets her keep the scale while striking the guarantees. Juliet does not reduce the figures. She removes their insurance. What survives her editing, boundlessness about value, is the play's endorsed truth about the love. What she cancels, oaths by inconstant moons, magnitude as a substitute for arrangement, is the play's endorsed warning about it. Glory and alarm are therefore not rival critical camps to be chosen between. They are the two halves of the scene's own transaction, and criticism aligns with the text when it holds them together: the exaggeration is the love's accurate measure and the measure's known insufficiency, spoken in the same breath, by the same girl, on the same night.

## The Verdict: Glory and Warning in a Single Figure

The evidence assembled across the catalogue and the scenes supports a single conclusion about the lovers' hyperbole, and it can be stated without remainder. The exaggeration glorifies the love because it is the love's true scale, the one register in which two people with no power, no privacy and no future can state what has occurred between them at its actual size; every plainer statement available in Verona, the father's asset language, the Friar's doses, the Nurse's wages of wax and teeth, understates the case, and understatement about the central fact would be the play's only real falsehood. The same exaggeration endangers the love because a rhetoric without a middle register cannot negotiate, wait, or retreat, the three operations survival in Verona requires; speakers who have made the beloved the sun cannot file a partial concession, lovers who have declared their bounty infinite cannot hear a forty-two-hour delay as anything but annihilation, and a boy who has abolished the world outside one city's walls has nowhere to be banished to. The figure does not cause the catastrophe, the feud, the feud's timing and the feud's messengers do that, but it removes every linguistic shelter in which the catastrophe might have been waited out.

That is why the play's own last word on magnitude belongs to its coldest speaker. The Prince closes the tragedy with a hyperbole, "never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3), and the superlative is, for once, simply accurate: within the world of the play, no story is sadder. The state ratifies the lovers' scale at the moment it buries them, adopting their figure as its official measure of the event, and the adoption is the final adjudication the text offers. Hyperbole began the play as a servant's boast and a boy's recitation. It ends as the verdict of Verona itself, carved, in the fathers' answering contribution, into gold. The cliche that survives from all this, the greeting-card boundlessness, the sun-and-sea language worn smooth by quotation, preserves the glory and files off the warning, which is the one mutilation the play's rhetoric cannot survive intact: read whole, in sequence, the lovers' exaggeration is the sound of a magnitude that was real, that was fatal, and that its speakers, at their best and clearest, already knew was both.

Between the two critical temptations, worship of the figure as pure sincerity and dismissal of it as pure convention, the play itself occupies the ground its heroine built on the balcony: keep the scale, strike the guarantees. Juliet's sea is not retracted by her three plain words; it is underwritten by them, converted from declaration into covenant, and the conversion is the model for reading every hyperbole in the tragedy. Ask of each, not whether it is true, since none of them is, and not whether it is beautiful, since nearly all of them are, but what it costs the speaker to stand inside it when the bill arrives. The servants' boasts cost nothing and buy nothing. The fathers' absolutes cost their children. The lovers' boundlessness costs their lives, and buys, in exchange, the only statements about their love that the surviving world accepts as adequate, repeated by a prince over their bodies and by audiences ever since. Hyperbole is justified in this play exactly where it is paid for, and the lovers are its only speakers who never once ask for a discount on themselves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch hyperbole catalogue, and what does each of its entries record?

The InsightCrunch hyperbole catalogue is the table in this article listing the play's principal hyperboles in the order of their utterance, from Sampson's opening boasts to the Prince's closing superlative. Each entry records four things: the speaker and the moment, the exaggerated words themselves, the vehicle that carries the overshoot, whether a sun, a sea, a ledger or hell, and the plain proposition the figure asserts once translated out of its imagery. Read in sequence, the entries show the figure's career, rising from reported praise through cosmic office to laws of value, then collapsing into negation and plain speech at the tomb.

### Q: What is hyperbole, and how did Renaissance rhetoricians classify the figure?

Hyperbole is the figure of deliberate overshoot, an assertion that exceeds literal belief in order to land on felt proportion, called auxesis or amplification in the classical handbooks. Renaissance rhetoricians classed it among the figures that magnify, licensing it where vehemence warranted the excess and warning that the hearer always discounts the claim. George Puttenham, in The Arte of English Poesie (1589), gave it the frankest English name, the Loud Liar, holding the device excused only when the matter earns the superlative. The lovers' speeches are best read as small trials of that licence, each exaggeration asking its audience to grant or refuse the overshoot.

### Q: Why does Romeo's hyperbole change once the beloved can answer back?

Romeo's hyperbole changes because an answering beloved turns recitation into negotiation, and negotiation edits the figures. Over the absent Rosaline his superlatives circulate unchallenged among male friends, reported, conventional, addressed to no one who can test them, and they persuade nobody. At the window the same instrument meets a respondent who returns larger figures than she receives, bars the moon as an unsound guarantor, cancels the oath machinery and demands the deed behind the declaration. The scale survives her audit and grows under it, which is why the balcony speeches convince audiences who smile at the Act 1 speeches: the excess has been examined by the person it describes.

### Q: Why does Juliet reduce an evening of cosmic hyperbole to a demand for three plain words?

Juliet demands three plain words because she understands that a figure is worth only what can be cashed from it, and the evening's boundlessness needs converting into an act before the night ends. The request, "three words, dear Romeo, and good night indeed" (2.2), asks for the smallest denomination of the scene, the declaration of honourable intent and the promise of a messenger tomorrow, in place of further suns and seas. The plainness does not repudiate the hyperbole surrounding it. It redeems the hyperbole, proving that the largest speech in the play is anchored to the smallest promise, and that Juliet, its greatest speaker, is also its strictest accountant.

### Q: Why does Juliet tell the Friar that her love is grown to such excess she cannot sum up half her wealth?

Juliet tells the Friar this at the marriage ceremony because the sea speech's law of value applies in the auditor's own dialect, and she wants the declaration on record before the rite proceeds. Her love is wealth, wealth is normally summed, and this sum defeats the totalling at half its amount (2.6). The line translates boundlessness into ledger language at the very altar of measure, in the hearing of the man who has just counselled moderation. It also quietly warns him, and the audience, that a feeling which cannot be summed cannot be scheduled either, a truth his forty-two-hour potion plot will shortly test to destruction.

### Q: How does Mercutio's Queen Mab speech miniaturize the world the lovers keep enlarging?

The Queen Mab speech miniaturizes by shrinking desire itself to toy scale, a fairy no bigger than an agate stone, a waggon of hazelnut, couriers drawn by atomies, galloping through the brains of sleepers who dream only the appetites of their offices (1.4). Where Romeo enlarges one girl to the sun, Mercutio reduces all human wanting to a nutshell theatre, proposing that the grandest longings are small mechanisms in small heads. The lovers' hyperbole is never answered in argument, but Mab is its standing audit: the suspicion, voiced by the play's wittiest speaker, that magnitude is a trick of perspective, and that the infinite, viewed coldly, fits inside a nut.

### Q: Why is the Nurse's hyperbole confined to bodies, wax and wagers while the lovers' runs to seas and suns?

The Nurse's hyperbole is confined to household magnitudes because her whole experience of size is domestic: she has measured the beloved in years, teeth, dresses and weaning, not in heavens. Paris is a man of wax, a sculptor's superlative; her certainty about Juliet's age is staked on fourteen of her teeth. The lovers, educated in sonnets and shut out of every household decision, reach for the cosmos because the cosmos is the only stock of magnitudes nobody in Verona owns. The contrast is between two currencies of the absolute, the pantry's and the sky's, and the play lets the pantry version make some of its shrewdest observations.

### Q: Where does the feud speak in hyperbole, and how does exaggeration serve hatred as it serves love?

The feud speaks in hyperbole from the first scene onward, in Sampson's total boasts against the whole rival house, in Tybalt's equation of a word with hell and a family, in Capulet's extinction figure for his hopes and his superlative rage at a daughter's refusal. Exaggeration serves hatred exactly as it serves love, by abolishing proportion between object and response, which for hatred means abolishing negotiation: a word hated as hell is hated cannot be discussed. Verona holds one rhetoric of magnitude, not two, and households and lovers draw on the same account. The deepest sense in which the houses are alike is that both are alike in inflation.

### Q: Why does Romeo's last speech end not in hyperbole but in the plain sentence "Thus with a kiss I die"?

The last speech ends in plainness because the figure's work is finished: hyperbole was the sound of a future being imagined at maximum size, and in the tomb the future has arrived and fits inside nine syllables. Romeo's final speech still contains enlargements, the last embrace, death the paramour, but its closing sentence offers no vehicle, no cosmos, only an act and its consequence (5.3). After four days of suns and seas the setting down of the figure lands harder than any superlative could, because the audience has been trained to hear plain speech, in this play, as the sound of an account closing for good.

### Q: If both lovers know their figures outrun the facts, in what sense is the hyperbole sincere?

The hyperbole is sincere because its speakers are reporting an inner magnitude accurately by the only scale that fits it, and they know the difference between the scale and the fact. Juliet states the position herself: her bounty behaves as the sea behaves, a claim about how her giving works, not about hydrography, and she elsewhere dissects names and labels with a philosopher's detachment. Sincerity in figuration is not literal belief in the vehicle. It is fidelity to the size of the thing felt, tested, in this play, by whether the speaker will pay the figure's price. The lovers pay in full, which is the tragedy's certification of their arithmetic.

### Q: How should an essay use the hyperbole catalogue to argue that exaggeration is both the love's glory and its warning?

An essay should use the catalogue's claim column as its evidence chain, showing first that the lovers' propositions ascend in kind, from comparison to cosmic office to laws of value, which establishes the glory: the figures state the love's real scale as no plainer idiom in Verona can. The essay should then track the same entries forward, noting that a rhetoric without a middle register cannot negotiate, wait or retreat, and that the late entries collapse into negation, the abolished world and the defied stars. Holding both sequences together yields the thesis the play supports, that glory and warning are one property, boundlessness, at two distances.

### Q: How should an actor voice the balcony hyperbole so it sounds believed rather than performed?

An actor should voice the balcony hyperbole as measurement rather than declamation, delivering the suns and seas at the speed of thought, as figures being found for a feeling in real time instead of monuments being unveiled. The scene supplies its own direction: Juliet interrupts, corrects and answers, so Romeo's lines must leave room to be edited, and his wonder works best played as report, a boy describing a sky he is actually looking at. Juliet's sea speech lands when its economics sound considered, the conclusion of an argument she has been conducting all evening. Belief, in this scene, is the sound of thinking at full scale.

### Q: Why do the lovers borrow eternity and the whole heavens for a love the play measures in days?

The lovers borrow eternity because the plot lends them no duration, and hyperbole trades the length they are denied for an intensity they can assert. The action runs about four days, audibly counted in appointed hours, a wedding advanced by a day, a potion measured at forty-two hours. Against that stopwatch, infinite and eternal are the only denominations that cannot be checked against the calendar before Friday. The feud makes the mirror claim, an ancient grudge of undatable origin, so the play's two infinities, grievance backward and devotion forward, are both mortgages taken out on the same four days, and the vault forecloses on both.

### Q: Why does the Friar answer hyperbole with proverbs and measures instead of matching its scale?

The Friar answers with proverbs and measures because his authority is pharmaceutical: he deals in doses, gaits and seasons, and his rhetoric is the rhetoric of the dispensary, where the difference between medicine and poison is quantity. "Wisely and slow" and "love moderately" are prescriptions, magnitude with a terminus attached. Yet his counter-figures, fire and powder kissing, are as compressed as anything the lovers coin, and his own scheme, one secret marriage reconciling two houses, is a social hyperbole as bold as any sea. He does not oppose the lovers' scale from outside it. He is a fellow speculator auditing rivals, which is why his warnings are accurate and useless.

### Q: What does hyperbole do in Romeo's banishment speech that it never does in the balcony scene?

In the banishment speech hyperbole turns from enlargement to abolition, asserting not that the beloved fills the heavens but that the world outside one city's walls does not exist, only purgatory, torture and hell itself (3.3). The balcony figure added: a girl, plus the sun's office, plus the sea's physics. The cell figure subtracts: existence, minus Verona, equals nothing. It is the same instrument, a total claim staked on a single term, run in reverse, and the reversal marks the tragedy's midpoint. Love's hyperbole proposed a reordered universe; despair's hyperbole files for the universe's dissolution, and the Friar's finger-counted list of consolations cannot be heard inside it.

### Q: How does hyperbole differ from the conceit it so often rides inside?

Hyperbole differs from the conceit as magnitude differs from machinery: the conceit is an extended comparison, an ingenious vehicle developed at length, while hyperbole is the overshoot itself, the claim that the subject exceeds every measure offered for it. Romeo's speeches for Rosaline ride in Petrarchan conceits, fires, smokes and sieges, yet their hyperbole is detachable, the assertion that the sun never saw her match, which could survive translation into any vehicle. In the balcony scene the vehicles change, suns, seas, ledgers, while the overshoot not only persists but grows. The play's development can be tracked by the separation: the conceits are inherited and discarded, the hyperbole is personal and fatal.

### Q: Why does Juliet's hyperbole grow larger as her circumstances grow narrower?

Juliet's hyperbole grows as her circumstances narrow because the figure enlarges whatever its speaker dwells on, and her dwelling places contract from garden to chamber to vault. At the window, with a suitor below and a future conceivable, the overshoot crowns the beloved and legislates value. On the wedding night it requisitions the sun's horses. Alone with the vial, the same virtuosity enlarges the vault's horrors, ancestral bones, foul air, her own dashed brains, each fear developed to set-piece scale. The instrument never fails her and never changes; the world feeding it does. Her rhetoric is the tragedy's most faithful register of its closing walls.

### Q: Why does hyperbole cluster in the night scenes and thin out in the scenes of law and negotiation?

Hyperbole clusters at night because night is the lovers' jurisdiction, the hours when the feud's measures lapse and private magnitude can be asserted unchallenged, while daylight in Verona belongs to edicts, contracts and negotiations, registers that price and specify. The pattern is not absolute, since Capulet courts Paris by day in the hyperbole of extinction and the Prince sentences in absolutes, but the play's great enlargements are nocturnal: the orchard, the balcony, the wedding night, the tomb. Darkness licenses the boundless because darkness suspends audit. Dawn, throughout the tragedy, is when the figures are presented for payment, and they are always presented by someone else.

### Q: What distinguishes hyperbole a speaker believes from hyperbole a speaker uses?

Believed hyperbole stakes the speaker on the figure's size, while used hyperbole spends the figure on an audience. Juliet's sea is believed: she acts on its law, giving without reserve, and dies solvent in its terms. Capulet's extinction figure to Paris is used: it prices the daughter for a negotiation, one hope surviving, court her gently, and the same father revalues her to refuse in the superlatives of rage when the bargain changes. The Nurse moves between the modes, fond in her wax and wagers, calculating in her later counsel. The play judges the modes by their sequels: believed exaggeration is honoured in catastrophe, used exaggeration is exposed by it.

### Q: How did the lovers' hyperboles pass into the common stock of love-language, and what did the borrowing flatten?

The lovers' hyperboles passed into common stock because they are detachable: boundless as the sea, the beloved as the sun, love as the thing that grows by giving, all lift cleanly out of the feud that gives them their pressure, and centuries of quotation, song and valentine have done the lifting. The borrowing flattened the warning half of every figure. In the play, each absoluteness is answered, audited or foreclosed, by Juliet's editing, the Friar's terminus, the calendar itself; detached, the lines circulate as pure glory, magnitude without cost. Restoring the context restores the figure whole, an exaggeration whose beauty and whose danger are the same boundlessness.
