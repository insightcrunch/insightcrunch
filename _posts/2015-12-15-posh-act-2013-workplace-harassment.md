The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013, universally shortened to the POSH Act, rests on a single institutional bet. Parliament decided that the first forum for a complaint of workplace sexual harassment should not be a court, a police station or a government inspectorate, but a small committee inside or near the workplace itself, staffed substantially by the employer's own people, bound by statutory time limits and armed with the powers of a civil court for the purposes of its inquiry. Everything in the statute flows from that bet: the definitions that decide who may complain and where, the composition rules that try to make an internal body trustworthy, the clock that runs from complaint to report to action, the duties laid on employers, and the penalty that waits for an employer who never constitutes the committee at all. A reader who finishes this article should be able to state, without hesitation, exactly what the Act requires, who sits on each committee, what each time limit is, and what the employer must do at each stage.

The defining fact about the statute, however, is not in its text. It is in its record. A decade after the Act came into force, the Supreme Court of India was still issuing directions whose entire content was that the committees the Act requires should be constituted, that district officers should be appointed, that local committees should exist, and that governments should survey their own districts to find out whether any of this had happened. In May 2023, deciding an appeal about the fairness of one such committee's inquiry, the Court described the enforcement of the Act as a sorry state of affairs. It then kept the matter alive, and through orders in 2024 and 2025 it pressed states to appoint district officers, constitute local committees, survey public and private establishments and upload committee details to a government portal. This article is therefore two things at once: a statute pillar that states the law of the POSH Act completely and precisely, and an implementation account of the distance between the law Parliament wrote and the machinery that was actually built. It sits within a wider field of protective legislation whose map is drawn in [the guide to women's rights legislation in India](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/), and it deliberately leaves the judicial history before 2013 to the article that owns it, taking from that history only the headline record this statute cannot be understood without.

## The statute on one page: enactment, commencement and shape

The Act is Act 14 of 2013. Its long title states its purpose in the compressed form Indian drafting uses: an Act to provide protection against sexual harassment of women at workplace and for the prevention and redressal of complaints of sexual harassment and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto. It extends to the whole of India. It contains thirty-two sections arranged in eight chapters, moving from definitions through the constitution of the two committees, the complaint and inquiry procedure, the duties of employers and district officers, and the miscellaneous provisions on penalties, confidentiality, appeals and rule-making that hold the scheme together.

The legislative journey took more than two years. The Protection of Women against Sexual Harassment at Workplace Bill, 2010, Bill number 144 of 2010, was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 7 December 2010 by Krishna Tirath, the Minister of State for Women and Child Development. It was referred to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, which reported in December 2011 after taking evidence from women's organisations, employers' bodies and legal experts. The Lok Sabha passed the Bill on 3 September 2012. The Rajya Sabha passed it, with amendments, on 26 February 2013. The President gave assent on 22 April 2013. The Act did not commence on assent. It was brought into force, together with the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Rules, 2013 framed under it, on 9 December 2013. That gap between assent and commencement matters for the article's central theme: even the statute's own start was a two-stage affair in which the legal obligation existed on paper months before the machinery to operate it was notified.

Two features of the enacted text deserve notice at the outset because they recur throughout the analysis. The first is that the Act creates no new government department and no inspectorate of its own. Its working parts are the Internal Committee inside each qualifying workplace, the Local Committee at district level, the District Officer who constitutes and supervises the local machinery, and the appropriate Government, which in practice means the state government for most workplaces and the Central Government for its own establishments. The second is that the Act's own vocabulary changed after enactment. As passed in 2013, the two bodies were called the Internal Complaints Committee and the Local Complaints Committee. The Repealing and Amending Act, 2016, Act 23 of 2016, substituted the shorter names, Internal Committee and Local Committee, with effect from 6 May 2016. Much of the commentary, and most institutional memory, still uses the original names and the abbreviations ICC and LCC. This article uses the statutory names as they stand after the 2016 substitution, because precision about what a body is called is the beginning of precision about what it must do.

The commencement date carries one further consequence. Conduct before 9 December 2013 is governed by the regime that preceded the Act, and complaints arising in the long interval between 1997 and 2013 were handled under judge-made guidelines rather than a statute. The Act is the third act of a story whose first two acts were written by the Supreme Court, and the pillar that owns that story tells it in full. What this article needs from it is only the outline, given in the next section, because the outline explains several choices in the statutory text that otherwise look arbitrary: why the definition of sexual harassment reads as it does, why the presiding officer must be a woman, and why the statute assumes a committee rather than a court.

## Passage: from Bill number 144 of 2010 to Act number 14 of 2013

The legislative passage took two years and four months, and its chronology explains several of the Act's final features. The Protection of Women Against Sexual Harassment at Workplace Bill, numbered Bill 144 of 2010, was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 7 December 2010 by Krishna Tirath, the Minister of State with independent charge of the Women and Child Development Ministry. The Bill was referred to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, which reported in December 2011, after taking evidence from women's organisations, employers' bodies and legal experts on the questions that had dogged the proposal since its first drafts in the preceding decade: whether the definition should reach the unorganised sector and domestic workers, whether conciliation had any place in the scheme, whether a provision on false complaints would deter genuine ones, and whether the committee model could work in small establishments at all.

The Lok Sabha passed the Bill on 3 September 2012. Between its passage in the lower House and its consideration in the upper, the ground shifted. The Delhi gang rape of December 2012 produced the committee headed by Justice J. S. Verma, the former Chief Justice who had authored the Vishaka judgment sixteen years earlier, and its report of January 2013 dealt directly with this Bill: it recommended that the committee system be replaced by an employment tribunal, and that the provision penalising false complaints be reconsidered. The Rajya Sabha passed the Bill on 26 February 2013 without adopting the tribunal model, retaining the internal and local committees as the forum and retaining Section 14 in its qualified form. The Bill thus survived the strongest institutional criticism it received, delivered by the one committee whose authority on the subject no member could discount, and the statute that emerged is Parliament's considered rejection of the external tribunal in favour of the internal forum with external membership. The President assented on 22 April 2013. In a final act of legislative sequencing, the Act was not brought into force on assent: it commenced on 9 December 2013, together with the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Rules, 2013, so that the duties it imposed on millions of employers began on the day the machinery for discharging them was also published.

Three features of the passage matter for reading the Act. The first is the title's own history: the Bill spoke of protection of women against sexual harassment, and the enacted title speaks of sexual harassment of women at the workplace, prevention, prohibition and redressal, the triple formulation signalling that the statute understood itself as a code of workplace governance rather than a single remedy. The second is what the debates settled by decision rather than by silence: domestic workers stayed in, against the administrative objection that households cannot be policed, because exclusion would have removed the statute's most exposed constituency; conciliation stayed in, confined to the complainant's request and stripped of money; and the false complaint provision stayed in, confined by its proviso. The third is the part of the passage that lies outside Parliament. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, enacted in the same session-season out of the same public moment, inserted the offence of sexual harassment into the Penal Code, so that the civil-disciplinary track and the criminal track were built contemporaneously, by the same legislature, in full view of each other. The POSH Act's Section 28 saving of other laws is therefore not boilerplate: it is the joint between two halves of one legislative response.

## Before the statute: sixteen years of judge-made law

Workplace sexual harassment had no statutory home in India before 2013. The Indian Penal Code punished assault or criminal force to a woman with intent to outrage her modesty, and words or gestures intended to insult the modesty of a woman, but it contained no offence or remedy addressed to harassment as a condition of working life, and it offered nothing to a woman whose complaint was about her working environment rather than a discrete criminal act. Service rules in government employment treated sexual harassment as misconduct only after the Court intervened. The gap was filled by the Supreme Court in Vishaka v State of Rajasthan, decided on 13 August 1997 and reported at (1997) 6 SCC 241. The petition grew out of the gang rape of Bhanwari Devi, a grassroots worker in a Rajasthan government programme, attacked in retaliation for her work against child marriage. Women's organisations, Vishaka among them, asked the Court to enforce the fundamental rights of working women against a form of violence the legislature had not addressed.

A bench presided over by Chief Justice J. S. Verma held that sexual harassment at the workplace violates Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 of the Constitution, and, reading those provisions with the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, laid down guidelines that were to be treated as the law declared by the Court under Article 141 until Parliament legislated. The guidelines defined sexual harassment, placed a duty on employers to prevent it, and required a complaints committee headed by a woman, with not less than half its members women and a third-party representative from a non-governmental organisation to guard against the pressure that an internal body invites. The definition the guidelines framed, and the committee they designed, are the direct ancestors of Sections 2(n) and 4 of the Act. The full judicial history, including how guidelines framed in a public interest petition could bind as law and what later cases added to them, belongs to [the account of the road from the Vishaka guidelines to the POSH Act](/2016/01/01/vishaka-guidelines-to-posh-act/), and this article does not retell it.

What happened between 1997 and 2013 is, for this statute's purposes, a warning about implementation that the Act's own record would repeat. In Medha Kotwal Lele v Union of India, decided in 2012 and reported at (2013) 1 SCC 297, the Supreme Court reviewed what the states had done with the Vishaka directions fifteen years on and found the compliance patchy in the specific and elementary respects that matter: several states had not amended their conduct rules to treat sexual harassment as misconduct, complaints committees had not been constituted across public bodies, and the third-party member the guidelines required was frequently missing. The Court issued directions to close those gaps. The sequence is worth holding in mind because it is the statute's biography in miniature. A court frames an obligation. Governments comply unevenly and late. A court returns, years later, to supervise the existence of the machinery rather than the merits of any complaint. The POSH Act was enacted to replace the first episode. Its record produced the second and third all over again, and the sections on implementation below trace exactly how.

One more piece of the pre-statute landscape matters because it explains the Act's deliberate narrowness. The Justice Verma Committee, appointed after the Delhi gang rape of December 2012 and reporting in January 2013, reviewed the criminal law on sexual offences and also addressed the pending harassment Bill. The Committee recommended structural changes to the committee model and, separately, urged that the Bill's provision permitting action on a complaint found to be false should be reconsidered, on the ground that the fear of counter-action would suppress reporting. Parliament enacted the Act within months of that report but retained both the committee model and the false complaint provision, in the qualified form examined below. The statute is therefore not a judicial draft transcribed; it is a legislative choice made against stated alternatives, and the criticisms it attracts are criticisms of choices its drafters knew they were making.

## From guidelines to statute: what changed on 9 December 2013

A reader who knows only Vishaka can miss how much the Act altered while appearing to codify. The guidelines had defined sexual harassment in the five forms Section 2(n) reproduces, and had required every employer to notify, publish and circulate the prohibition, to include it in service rules and standing orders, to constitute a complaint committee headed by a woman with not less than half its members women and one outside member from a non-governmental organisation familiar with the issue, and to run awareness programmes. Against that baseline, the statute's changes can be grouped under four heads.

First, scope. The guidelines spoke to employers and their employees; the Act defines its protected person, its respondent, its workplace and its employment relationship independently of the contract of employment, reaching visitors and applicants, contract and agency labour, trainees and volunteers, client sites and employer transport, and, through the dwelling place limb, the household. The unorganised sector, invisible in the guidelines' institutional imagination, is named and given a forum. Second, process. The guidelines prescribed no limitation period, no inquiry deadline, no reporting or action clocks and no compensation mechanism; the Act supplies all of them, and the clock table in this article is, in effect, a list of things the guidelines never contained. Third, consequence. Breach of the guidelines exposed an employer to contempt or to service-law embarrassment; the Act adds a penal section with a fine and a licence consequence on repetition, an employer's duty list enforceable as law, and a compensation power collected through the payroll. Fourth, the state. The guidelines addressed employers and, through the Court's continuing directions, governments; the Act builds a district administration of its own, District Officers, nodal officers, Local Committees, annual reports flowing upward, and a monitoring power in the appropriate government armed with inspection.

The case that measures the guidelines' limits, and that the statute's supporters cited throughout its passage, is Medha Kotwal Lele v Union of India, (2013) 1 SCC 297. The petition documented, state by state, the Vishaka directions unimplemented fifteen years on: state governments had not amended their conduct rules to treat sexual harassment as misconduct, complaint committees had not been constituted in large classes of institutions, and the committee heads contemplated by the guidelines did not exist in the private sector in any systematic way. The judgment recorded this non-compliance and pressed the states to complete the rule amendments and constitutions the guidelines required. Its significance for the Act is chronological and argumentative at once: decided in 2012, while the Bill was between the Houses, it demonstrated that the failure of the pre-statutory regime was not a failure of norms but of obligation, since the norms had been binding law under Article 141 for fifteen years. Parliament's answer was to convert guidance into duty with penalties, reporting and a district spine. The irony recorded in this article's verdict is that the same demonstration had to be made again, a decade later, about the statute itself.

One continuity deserves equal emphasis, because Article 145 owns the lineage and this pillar must not distort it: the Act did not disown the guidelines. It adopted their definition, their committee architecture and their external member, and it left open, through Section 28, every remedy the Vishaka framework had generated. What commenced on 9 December 2013 was not a repudiation of the judge-made regime but its enactment, with the additions that only a legislature could make: penalties, clocks, compensation and a district administration.

## What counts as sexual harassment: the definition and its surrounding circumstances

Section 2(n) defines sexual harassment to include any one or more of the following unwelcome acts or behaviour, whether directly or by implication: physical contact and advances; a demand or request for sexual favours; making sexually coloured remarks; showing pornography; and any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature. Section 3(1) then states the prohibition in a single sentence: no woman shall be subjected to sexual harassment at any workplace. The definition is inherited almost word for word from the Vishaka guidelines, and its architecture repays attention because nearly every dispute about the Act's reach is a dispute about one of its three moving parts: the catalogue of acts, the requirement that the conduct be unwelcome, and the open-ended final clause.

The catalogue is illustrative at its end and specific at its start. The first four limbs name conduct that legislatures and courts had repeatedly encountered: touching and advances; the quid pro quo demand; sexualised speech; and the display of pornography, a form of harassment that needs no physical proximity at all. The fifth limb, any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature, is the provision that does the long-term work. It captures the gesture, the message, the image circulated on a phone, the persistent comment about appearance, and forms of conduct mediated by technologies the 2013 Parliament could describe only in general words. A definition confined to the four named limbs would have dated within a parliamentary term. The generality of the fifth limb is the drafters' answer, and it is why argument under the Act so often turns on characterisation of conduct rather than on whether a named category exists.

The word that governs the whole definition is unwelcome. Harassment under the Act is not defined by the actor's intention but by the character of the conduct from the standpoint of the woman subjected to it. Conduct that is welcomed is outside the definition; conduct that continues after its unwelcome character is apparent sits squarely inside it. That single word converts a range of workplace behaviour that organisations once treated as culture, humour or courtship into a statutory wrong, and it does so without requiring the complainant to prove injury, a hostile motive, or repetition. A single act, if it falls within a named limb and is unwelcome, can constitute sexual harassment under the Act, although the gravity of the response the committee recommends will naturally reflect the gravity and pattern of what is proved.

Section 3(2) adds a second layer that is frequently misread as part of the definition. It provides that certain circumstances, if present in relation to or connected with any act or behaviour of sexual harassment, may amount to sexual harassment: an implied or explicit promise of preferential treatment in employment; an implied or explicit threat of detrimental treatment in employment; an implied or explicit threat about present or future employment status; interference with work, or the creation of an intimidating or offensive or hostile work environment; and humiliating treatment likely to affect health or safety. The subsection does not create five further offences. It directs the committee's attention to the setting in which sexual conduct occurs at work, where power does the harm: the supervisor whose favour controls a posting, the appraisal that follows a refusal, the roster changed after a complaint, the meeting from which a woman is frozen out. Read together, Sections 2(n) and 3(2) describe two distinct wrongs that real cases usually combine, the sexualised act itself and the employment consequence that gives it coercive force.

The definition's limits are as deliberate as its breadth. The Act is concerned with sexual harassment of women. Conduct that is bullying, discriminatory or oppressive without a sexual character falls to service law, to the general criminal law where it crosses those thresholds, or to no legal remedy at all, a gap the gender debate below takes up. The Act also requires the conduct to be connected with a workplace, a term the next section shows to be very wide but not unlimited. And the Act's scheme is civil and disciplinary rather than criminal: a finding by a committee is a finding of misconduct for service purposes, with a compensation mechanism attached, while criminal liability for the same conduct travels by a separate route under the penal law, examined later in this article. Confusing those two tracks, expecting a committee to convict or a criminal court to conduct the workplace inquiry, is the most common structural misunderstanding the statute attracts, and the case law on what a committee is, addressed in the section on judicial treatment, returns to it.

## Who may complain, and where: the definitions that fix the Act's reach

The Act's protective reach is set by four definitions in Section 2, and each was drafted wider than the employment model a reader might assume. The first is aggrieved woman, defined in Section 2(a) in two limbs. In relation to a workplace, an aggrieved woman is a woman of any age, whether employed or not, who alleges that she has been subjected to an act of sexual harassment by the respondent. In relation to a dwelling place or a house, she is a woman of any age who is employed in that dwelling place or house. The first limb's words whether employed or not do heavy work. A job applicant interviewed on the premises, a client visiting a supplier's office, a consultant, a vendor's employee, a student in an institution covered by the Act: each can be an aggrieved woman in relation to that workplace without any contract of employment with it. The respondent, defined in Section 2(m), is simply the person against whom the aggrieved woman has made a complaint, and the respondent need not be her employer or even an employee of the same establishment for the committee's jurisdiction to be engaged, although the remedies available differ where the respondent is a stranger to the organisation, as the discussion of the employer's duties explains.

The second definition is employee, in Section 2(f), and it is drafted to defeat the contractual evasions that labour law litigates endlessly. An employee is a person employed at a workplace for any work on a regular, temporary, ad hoc or daily wage basis, either directly or through an agent, including a contractor, with or without the knowledge of the principal employer, whether for remuneration or not, or working on a voluntary basis or otherwise, whether the terms of employment are express or implied, and includes a co-worker, a contract worker, a probationer, a trainee, an apprentice, or a person called by any other name. The definition is a list of the routes by which organisations obtain work without admitting an employment relationship, and it forecloses each of them for the purposes of this Act. That breadth matters beyond the POSH statute itself, because the same drafting contest, between the reality of work and the paper form of engagement, runs through the modern labour codes, as [the treatment of the employment relationship in the Industrial Relations Code](/2013/08/01/industrial-relations-code-2020-explained/) shows from the other side of the argument.

The third definition is workplace, in Section 2(o), and it is the widest of all. It includes any department, organisation, undertaking, establishment, enterprise, institution, office, branch or unit established, owned, controlled or wholly or substantially financed by the appropriate Government or a local authority. It includes any private sector organisation, venture, undertaking, enterprise, institution, establishment, society, trust, non-governmental organisation, unit or service provider carrying on commercial, professional, vocational, educational, entertainment, industrial, health services or financial activities. It includes hospitals and nursing homes. It includes any sports institute, stadium, sports complex or competition or games venue, whether residential or not, used for training in sports or other activities. It includes, crucially, any place visited by the employee arising out of or during the course of employment, including transportation provided by the employer for undertaking such a journey. And it includes a dwelling place or a house. The fifth limb detaches the workplace from the premises: the client site, the conference hotel, the official vehicle, the field visit are workplaces for the Act's purposes when the visit arises out of employment. The sixth limb brings the household inside the statute, which connects directly to the fourth definition.

That fourth definition is domestic worker, in Section 2(d): a woman employed to do household work in any household for remuneration, whether in cash or kind, either directly or through an agency, on a temporary, permanent, part-time or full-time basis, but not including any member of the family of the employer. Allied to it is the definition of the unorganised sector in Section 2(p), an enterprise owned by individuals or self-employed workers and engaged in the production or sale of goods or the provision of any kind of service, where the enterprise employs fewer than ten workers. The architecture these definitions create is deliberate. The organised sector, with its offices and human resources departments, gets the internal committee. The household, the small shop, the tiny enterprise, where no internal committee is possible, gets the district-level machinery described below. The statute's coverage on paper is therefore close to universal for women at work in India. Its practical coverage is another matter, and the difference between the two is the implementation story this article keeps returning to: a definition can be perfectly wide while the committee it points to does not exist.

Two boundary questions recur in practice and the definitions answer both. The first is whether harassment that occurs away from the office, at a work dinner, on an official trip, in messages exchanged outside working hours, falls within the Act. The definition of workplace, with its visited-place limb, answers with reference to the connection with employment rather than geography: conduct arising out of or during the course of employment is within the statute wherever it occurs, and electronic conduct directed at a colleague is conduct of a verbal or non-verbal character for Section 2(n) purposes. The second is whether a woman who has left employment can complain about conduct during it. The definition of aggrieved woman turns on the allegation and the workplace relation, not on subsisting employment, although the three-month limitation period examined below disciplines how long after the events a complaint can be brought, and a committee's remedial reach over a respondent who has also departed is a practical rather than jurisdictional problem.

## The Internal Committee: composition as a safeguard

Section 4(1) obliges every employer of a workplace, by an order in writing, to constitute a committee to be known as the Internal Committee, and its proviso adds that where the offices or administrative units of the workplace are located at different places or divided into sub-units, the committee shall be constituted at all administrative units or offices. The obligation is the employer's, it is discharged unit by unit rather than by a single headquarters committee, and it exists whether or not any complaint has ever been made. The question of the threshold at which the obligation bites is answered in the next section: Section 6 routes complaints from establishments that have no Internal Committee because they have fewer than ten workers to the Local Committee, and the working rule across the statute, confirmed by the Supreme Court in its compliance orders, is that a workplace with ten or more workers must have its own Internal Committee, while smaller workplaces are served from the district.

Section 4(2) prescribes the composition, and every element of it has generated a compliance failure somewhere in the record. The Presiding Officer must be a woman employed at a senior level at the workplace, drawn from among the employees. Two provisos manage scarcity: if no senior woman employee is available at that workplace, the Presiding Officer is nominated from another office or administrative unit of the same employer, and if none is available there either, from any other workplace of the same employer or another department or organisation. Next, the committee must include not less than two members from among the employees, preferably committed to the cause of women, or who have experience in social work, or who have legal knowledge. Then comes the provision whose omission has invalidated more inquiries than any other: one member must be drawn from among non-governmental organisations or associations committed to the cause of women, or be a person familiar with the issues relating to sexual harassment. Finally, a proviso requires that at least one-half of the total members nominated be women. Under Section 4(3), the Presiding Officer and members hold office for a term not exceeding three years from the date of nomination. Under Section 4(4), the external member from an organisation is entitled to allowances or fees as prescribed, payable by the employer, a small provision with a large purpose: the external member's independence is meant to be structural, and an unpaid outsider dependent on the employer's goodwill for the role itself is independence of a thinner kind.

### Why does the Act insist on an external member and a majority of women?

The external member exists because every other member is the employer's own nominee and usually the respondent's colleague: the seat reserved for a person from an organisation committed to the cause of women, or familiar with harassment issues, guarantees one person in the room who owes the employer nothing.

That answer deserves its fuller form. The composition rules are the Vishaka guidelines carried into statute, and they encode a judgment about where internal inquiries fail. They fail through deference: junior members unwilling to find against a senior respondent, colleagues closing ranks, human resources staff treating the complaint as a reputational problem for the organisation rather than a wrong to the complainant. The senior woman Presiding Officer answers the hierarchy problem from one direction, by placing the committee's chair at a level from which a respondent's seniority is less intimidating. The external member answers it from the other, by importing a person whose standing does not depend on the employer at all. The majority-of-women rule answers a different concern, that a complainant describing sexual conduct to a mostly male panel faces a second ordeal inside the remedy itself. When the Supreme Court's compliance exercise began examining committees in 2023, the defects it encountered were precisely composition defects: committees without external members, committees with the wrong presiding officer, committees that existed as a line in a policy document and nowhere else. A committee defectively composed is not a technicality, because its report is the foundation of disciplinary action, and action built on a report by a body the statute did not authorise is vulnerable in every forum that later examines it.

The committee is also, by Section 4(5), removable in its individual membership: where a member contravenes the confidentiality provision, or has been convicted of an offence or has an inquiry pending under the Act, or is found guilty in disciplinary proceedings, or has abused the position so as to render continuance prejudicial to the public interest, the employer must remove the member and fill the vacancy by fresh nomination. The provision is rarely discussed and worth noticing, because it treats committee membership as a position of trust with its own standards, not as an honorary assignment.

What Section 4 does not do is equally instructive. It does not require the committee to be trained, certified or advised, although the employer's duties in Section 19 include organising orientation for members, and the Supreme Court's directions later pressed training hard. It does not prescribe a secretariat, a budget or a meeting rhythm in quiet times. And it does not, by itself, create any machinery for the woman whose workplace has no committee in fact: her route is the District Officer and the Local Committee, which is why the district machinery is not a footnote to this statute but half of it.

Composition is only the beginning of a committee's integrity, and Section 4 legislates for its decay as well. The term discipline comes first: the Presiding Officer and every member hold office for the period specified by the employer in the constituting order, not exceeding three years from the date of nomination, so a committee is a renewable body rather than a permanent fixture, and an employer that never reconstitutes its committee is, after three years, relying on a panel whose mandate has expired. Section 4(5) then provides for removal during the term. Where a member, including the Presiding Officer or the external member, contravenes the confidentiality command of Section 16, or has been convicted of an offence, or an inquiry into an offence under any law in force is pending against that member, or has been found guilty in disciplinary proceedings, or has so abused the member's position as to render continuance in office prejudicial to the public interest, the employer shall remove the member from the committee. The grounds are worth reading slowly. The first converts the confidentiality duty from an ethic into a tenure condition: a member who leaks does not merely commit an offence against Section 17, the member loses the seat. The middle grounds import character and pending-process standards from public employment law. The last, abuse of position prejudicial to the public interest, is the catch-all aimed at the member who uses committee membership itself as an instrument, to warn respondents, to protect colleagues or to trade in information. Where a vacancy arises, whether by removal, resignation or expiry, the employer fills it by fresh nomination in accordance with the composition rules, and proceedings before the committee continue from the stage at which they stood when the vacancy occurred, so that a change of membership cannot be used to restart, or to strand, a live inquiry. The Local Committee carries parallel provisions in Section 7, with the District Officer in the employer's place as the constituting and removing authority. Together these clauses show the legislature anticipating the failure modes of small standing bodies, capture, leakage, expiry and drift, and pricing each of them in advance. They also complete the answer to the structural criticism recorded in the debate section: the committee's dependence on its constituting authority is real, but it is a dependence the statute surrounds with tenure limits, removal grounds and continuity rules, rather than the unregulated patronage the criticism sometimes assumes.

## The Local Committee and the District Officer: the district half of the statute

Section 5 requires the appropriate Government to notify, for every district, a District Officer, who is the District Magistrate or Additional District Magistrate, or the Collector or Deputy Collector. Section 6(1) directs the District Officer to constitute, in the district, a committee to be known as the Local Committee, to receive complaints of sexual harassment from establishments where the Internal Committee has not been constituted due to having less than ten workers, or where the complaint is against the employer himself. The two triggers deserve separation. The first is size: the sub-ten workplace, the household employing a domestic worker, the small enterprise in the unorganised sector as Section 2(p) defines it. The second is conflict of interest at its most acute: a complaint against the employer cannot be inquired into by a committee the employer constitutes, pays and can dissolve, so the statute lifts it out of the workplace altogether. Any honest account of the Act must record that the second route, for all its structural elegance, depends entirely on the Local Committee existing, and for years after commencement, in district after district, it did not.

Section 7 composes the Local Committee differently from the internal body, because its members cannot be drawn from a single employer's staff. The Chairperson is nominated from among eminent women in the field of social work who are committed to the cause of women. One member is nominated from among the women working in the block, taluka, tehsil, ward or municipality in the district. Two members, of whom at least one is a woman, are nominated from among non-governmental organisations or associations committed to the cause of women, or persons familiar with issues relating to sexual harassment, provided that at least one is, as far as practicable, from the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, the Other Backward Classes or a minority community. One officer of the district's Social Welfare or Women and Child Development department sits ex officio. Members hold office for a period not exceeding three years as specified by the District Officer. Section 6(3) adds the intake layer: the District Officer designates one nodal officer in every block, taluka and tehsil in rural or tribal areas, and in every ward or municipality in urban areas, to receive complaints and forward them to the Local Committee within seven days. Section 8 provides for grants by the appropriate Government toward the Local Committee's payments. Section 20 loads the District Officer with continuing duties: to monitor the timely submission of reports by Internal Committees, to take necessary measures for engaging non-governmental organisations in awareness creation, and to carry the reporting chain upward that the section on annual reports describes.

The design is coherent on paper: a district officer of the senior civil service, a committee with a social-work chair and community membership, nodal officers within reach of every complainant, and a forwarding duty measured in days. The recorded reality, established by the Supreme Court's supervision described below, is that this was the half of the statute most often left unbuilt. District Officers went unnotified in some states for years. Local Committees existed in some districts as a notification and in others not at all. Nodal officers were designated on paper in places where the designee had never heard of the designation. The complainant the district machinery exists for, the domestic worker, the woman in a nine-person tailoring unit, the agricultural or home-based worker drawn in by the definitions, is also the complainant least placed to discover that a Local Committee was supposed to exist and did not. When the Court in December 2024 set dates for the appointment of District Officers and the constitution of Local Committees, it was legislating administratively for the statute's missing half, a point this article's assessment returns to.

A word belongs here to the appropriate Government, because its identity decides who answers for all of this. For workplaces under the Central Government, the Centre is the appropriate Government; for state government establishments and for the generality of private workplaces within a state, it is the state government. The Act's supervision, monitoring and rule-making powers are therefore distributed across the same federal pattern that the series encounters in statute after statute: a central text, state-level machinery, and a compliance record that varies by state in ways national statements flatten. The Supreme Court's orders in the compliance proceedings were addressed to all states and union territories precisely because the failures were distributed the same way.

## The district half in operation: grants, nodal officers and the Local Committee's year

The district half of the Act runs on money and personnel the statute arranges with unusual specificity, and both explain its uneven record. Section 8 provides for grants: the Central Government may, after due appropriation made by Parliament by law in that behalf, make grants to the State Government for the purpose of paying the fees or allowances to the members of the Local Committee. The provision recognises that Local Committee members, unlike Internal Committee members, are not salaried by an employer with a compliance interest; they are public functionaries of a kind the statute creates without creating a service, dependent on budgeted grants reaching the district and being released. A Local Committee whose members' fees are unpaid, whose meetings have no secretarial support and whose Chairperson serves without staff is a committee in the same position as the unproctored Internal Committee in a small firm: formally constituted, practically inert. The Act's framers saw the point and legislated the grant; the appropriation and release chain is where the intention has repeatedly thinned.

The nodal officer network is the second piece of district plumbing. Section 6(3) obliges the District Officer to designate nodal officers in every block, taluka and tehsil in a district, and in a ward or municipality in an urban area, to receive complaints and forward them to the Local Committee within seven days of receipt. The design is an intake system: complaints in the unorganised sector rarely begin at the committee, they begin where the complainant already goes, the block office, the ward office, the panchayat functionary, and the nodal officer is the statute's attempt to make every such door the right door. Where nodal officers are notified, named and trained, the district system has a front end. Where the notification exists only in a government order that field offices have not operationalised, the complainant's first journey ends at a desk that does not know its own designation. The Supreme Court's December 2024 direction fixing a deadline for the constitution of Local Committees, and its insistence through 2025 on district surveys, were aimed at exactly this layer: not the Act's ideas, which were complete in 2013, but the notifications, appointments and releases that make a committee a place a woman can physically reach.

The Local Committee's own working year mirrors the Internal Committee's at district scale, with one addition that changes its character. It receives its complaints from three sources: directly, through nodal officers, and, since its registration function matured, through the She-Box portal's routing. It inquires with the same civil court powers under Section 11(3), reports within the same ten days to the District Officer, and watches the same sixty-day action clock, with the District Officer rather than an employer as the acting authority. Its annual report under Section 22 travels through the District Officer to the State Government, making it the only point in the system where unorganised-sector complaints are counted as a class. And it carries the domestic worker jurisdiction described earlier, the Section 11 route that connects the committee to the police where a prima facie penal case exists. District Officers who treat the committee as a functioning institution, reviewing its reports, pressing nodal officers and reporting upward under Section 20, produce districts where the district half works. The statute gives the District Officer the monitoring of timely submission of reports by committees within the district, which makes the officer, in law, the supervisor of both halves of the system in the district, the Internal Committees of its workplaces and the Local Committee of its unorganised sector alike.

## The POSH clock: complaint, inquiry, report, action

No feature of the statute is misstated more often, in compliance manuals and in practice, than its time limits, and no feature matters more to the people inside a live complaint. The Act runs on a clock with five distinct periods, and the findable artifact of this article sets them out in sequence. Section 9(1) requires the aggrieved woman to make a written complaint to the Internal Committee, if constituted, or otherwise to the Local Committee, within three months from the date of the incident, and where there is a series of incidents, within three months from the date of the last incident. The period is extendable: the committee may, for reasons recorded in writing, extend it by a further period not exceeding three months, if satisfied that the circumstances prevented the woman from filing within the initial period. The limitation is therefore three months plus a discretionary three, not six months as a matter of right, and not the open-ended period some guides describe. Section 9 also answers who may complain where the woman cannot: if she is unable to make a complaint on account of physical or mental incapacity, or death, or otherwise, her legal heir or such other person as may be prescribed may make the complaint on her behalf.

What follows is procedural discipline of a kind Indian statutes often promise and rarely specify. The committee may, before initiating an inquiry and at the request of the aggrieved woman, take steps to settle the matter through conciliation, examined in the next section. The inquiry itself, under Section 11(4), must be completed within ninety days. Interim relief is available during its pendency under Section 12. On completion, Section 13(1) requires the committee to provide a report of its findings to the employer, or to the District Officer where the Local Committee inquired, within ten days of the completion of the inquiry, and the report must be made available to both parties. Section 13(3) then obliges the employer or the District Officer to act upon the committee's recommendations within sixty days of receiving the report. Section 18 gives any person aggrieved by a recommendation, or by its non-implementation, an appeal to the court or tribunal in accordance with the applicable service rules, to be preferred within ninety days of the recommendation. The general position described in this section is the statute's framework rather than advice on an individual case, and anyone inside a live complaint should take advice on its facts, because limitation periods and forum rules under this Act are short and unforgiving.

The table compresses; the prose must add what the table cannot. The complaint period's extension is discretionary and reasoned, which means a late complaint should always be accompanied by the explanation the committee needs to record. The ninety days for inquiry run against the committee, and the Rules framed under the Act fill in the working detail the statute leaves out: the respondent must file a reply to the complaint, with documents and a list of witnesses, within ten working days of receiving the complaint copy; the inquiry proceeds in accordance with the principles of natural justice; neither party may be represented by a legal practitioner before the committee; and a minimum of three members, including the Presiding Officer or Chairperson, must be present for the inquiry to proceed. The provision for absence is stern: a member who absents from three consecutive meetings without reasonable cause, after notice, faces termination of membership. These rules are where the statute's promise of a fair but informal process is actually kept or lost, and the leading case on them is taken up in the inquiry section below.

## The 2013 Rules: the statute's operating manual

The Act is a framework; the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Rules, 2013, which commenced with it on 9 December 2013, are where a committee learns how to sit. Four groups of rules carry the operational weight. The first governs who may complain when the woman cannot. Building on Section 9, the Rules prescribe the circle: where the aggrieved woman is unable to make a complaint, it may be made by her relative or friend, by a co-worker, by an officer of the National Commission for Women or the State Commission for Women, or by any person who has knowledge of the incident, with her written consent; and where the inability arises from mental incapacity, the prescribed circle extends to a relative or friend, a special educator, a qualified psychiatrist or psychologist, or the guardian or authority under whose care she is receiving treatment or care. The detail matters because the woman least able to write a complaint is frequently the woman most exposed, and the Rules answer the standing question before it can be used against her.

The second group governs the inquiry's conduct in Rule 7, and it is the text committees actually work from. The complaint is supplied to the respondent, who must file a written reply, with documents and the names and addresses of witnesses, within ten working days. The committee then proceeds in accordance with the principles of natural justice: both parties are heard, documents are shared, and the inquiry is a fact-finding process rather than an adversarial trial. Two provisions keep it that way. Neither the aggrieved woman nor the respondent is permitted to bring a legal practitioner to represent them before the committee at any stage. And the inquiry requires a quorum of three members, including the Presiding Officer or the Chairperson, so that no finding issues from a rump. Membership discipline mirrors the duty: a member who absents from three consecutive meetings without prior intimation and sufficient grounds faces termination of membership, a rule aimed at the commonest form of committee failure, which is not corruption but drift.

The third group supplies the consequences catalogue. Where the respondent is an employee and the allegation is proved, Rule 9 lists the actions the service rules or the committee's recommendation may deploy: written apology, reprimand, warning, censure, withholding of promotion, withholding of pay rise or increments, termination of service, counselling, and community service, alongside the compensation deduction Section 13 authorises. The spread of that list is itself a policy statement. Termination is present but not mandatory, and the presence of counselling and community service among the outcomes shows the Rules contemplating correction for conduct at the lower end of the definition, proportionate response being a condition of the system's legitimacy with the workforce that must use it. Rule 8, on interim relief, adds the administrative protections described in the compensation section: the catalogue of measures a committee may recommend while the inquiry runs, including protection against the respondent's exercise of supervisory or reporting power over the complainant.

The fourth group is financial and administrative housekeeping: the fees and allowances payable to the external member of the Internal Committee and to Local Committee members, borne within the scheme the Act designs, and the manner of preparing and forwarding the annual reports Sections 21 and 22 require. Housekeeping is the correct note on which to end this section, because it is the level at which the Act most often silently fails: a committee whose external member is never paid, whose meetings lack quorum discipline and whose annual report is never filed is a committee that exists for the website disclosure and not for the complainant, and the Rules are the standard against which that failure is measurable.

## Conciliation, interim relief and compensation: the three middle mechanisms

Section 10 is the most argued-about short provision in the Act. It permits the committee, before initiating an inquiry and at the request of the aggrieved woman, to take steps to settle the matter between her and the respondent through conciliation, subject to a proviso that has to be quoted to be believed accurately: no monetary settlement shall be made as a basis of conciliation. If a settlement is arrived at, the committee records it, forwards it to the employer or District Officer for action as specified, gives copies to both parties, and conducts no further inquiry. If any term of the settlement is not complied with, the committee proceeds with the inquiry. Three features of the design are deliberate. The initiative belongs to the woman alone; neither the respondent nor the employer nor the committee can press conciliation on her. It is available only before the inquiry, as an alternative to it, not as an exit from an inquiry going badly. And the exclusion of money from its basis is the legislature's judgment that a settlement bought with a payment, in a relationship of unequal power, is presumptively not a settlement at all. The criticism of the provision, stated at full strength in the debate section, is that conciliation of any kind sits uneasily with the wrong the Act addresses, and that in real workplaces the request for conciliation is often the employer's preference ventriloquised. The defence is that the choice is the complainant's by the statute's express words, and that for some women a swift, recorded resolution that changes their working conditions is worth more than a ninety-day inquiry. The Act takes no side beyond structuring the choice; this article follows it in that.

Section 12 addresses the interval problem: an inquiry takes weeks, and the complainant typically continues to work beside the respondent while it runs. On a written request from the aggrieved woman, the committee may recommend, and the employer or District Officer shall act on, interim measures: transfer of the aggrieved woman or the respondent to another workplace; leave to the aggrieved woman for a period up to three months, which is in addition to leave she is otherwise entitled to; and such other relief as may be prescribed. The Rules add texture to the third head, including restraining the respondent from reporting on the work allocation or performance appraisal of the complainant where he holds that power over her, a measure aimed at the most common form of retaliation, which is administrative rather than physical. Interim relief under the Act is recommendatory in form and obligatory in effect, since the employer's duty to act on the committee's recommendations within the statutory scheme is not a courtesy. It is also, in practice, the provision complainants value first and committees use last, because transfer and leave are visible acts in a small workplace and every party can read their meaning; the statute accepts that cost as the price of not leaving the woman unprotected during the very process meant to protect her.

Compensation is handled inside Section 13 rather than in a separate damages chapter, and its mechanism is administrative. Where the allegations are proved, the committee recommends action against the respondent for misconduct in accordance with the service rules, and it may direct the employer to deduct from the respondent's salary or wages such sum as the committee determines is payable to the aggrieved woman. Section 15 lists the factors that govern that determination: the mental trauma, pain, suffering and emotional distress caused to the woman; the loss in career opportunity she suffered; the medical expenses she incurred for physical or psychiatric treatment; the income and financial status of the respondent; and the feasibility of payment in a lump sum or in instalments. Where the respondent has ceased to be employed, or the deduction cannot be made, the committee may direct the respondent to pay directly, and on default the matter goes to the District Officer for recovery as an arrear of land revenue, a revenue recovery route that gives the order an enforcement spine most internal disciplinary outcomes lack. The design is worth pausing on. Parliament gave the committee a compensatory power keyed to the respondent's means and the woman's losses, collected through the payroll, inside a disciplinary process, rather than leaving compensation to a separate civil suit the complainant would have to fund and wait years to conclude. Whatever else the record shows, that choice was a considered attempt to make the remedy arrive with the finding.

## The inquiry and natural justice: what Aureliano Fernandes decided

Section 11 routes the inquiry. Where the respondent is an employee, the committee inquires in accordance with the service rules applicable to the respondent, and where no service rules exist, in the prescribed manner. For a domestic worker, the route differs: the Local Committee, if a prima facie case exists, forwards the complaint to the police within seven days for registration of a case under the Indian Penal Code, and the inquiry and compensation machinery proceeds alongside. Section 11(3) equips the committee, for the purposes of the inquiry, with the powers vested in a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure when trying a suit: summoning and enforcing the attendance of any person and examining that person on oath, requiring the discovery and production of documents, and any other prescribed matter. The committee is thus a hybrid: an internal body with coercive evidence powers, conducting an inquiry rather than a trial, under service rules where they exist and natural justice everywhere.

### What powers does the Internal Committee have while conducting an inquiry?

For the inquiry, Section 11(3) gives the committee the powers of a civil court trying a suit: it can summon and enforce the attendance of any person, examine witnesses on oath, and require the discovery and production of documents, alongside any further prescribed matter, which makes its evidence-gathering authority statutory rather than dependent on the employer's cooperation.

The holding that defines this territory came, fittingly, from a case about an inquiry that went wrong before the Act existed. Aureliano Fernandes was a professor and head of the Political Science department at Goa University. Allegations of sexual harassment were made against him in 2009, and the university's standing committee, operating under the Vishaka framework, inquired and reported against him; the university's Executive Council terminated his services and disqualified him from future employment. The Bombay High Court at Goa dismissed his challenge. On 12 May 2023, a Supreme Court bench of Justices A. S. Bopanna and Hima Kohli allowed his appeal in Civil Appeal number 2482 of 2014. The Court found that the committee's proceedings in May 2009 had been conducted in a tearing hurry, that the appellant had not been supplied the material he needed or given a reasonable opportunity to defend himself, and that the proceedings fell short of the standard the applicable rules prescribed and defied the principles of natural justice. The termination could not stand. The Court quashed the High Court's judgment, remanded the matter to the committee to take the inquiry up from the position it had reached on 5 May 2009, directed that the appellant be given adequate opportunity to defend himself, set a three-month period for the whole process from the first hearing, and recorded that he would not be entitled to immediate reinstatement or back wages until the inquiry concluded and a decision was taken.

The substantive importance of the judgment for respondents is plain, and this article states it as the Court did: a committee under this framework is not relieved of fairness by the seriousness of the allegation or the sympathy the complainant deserves. The copy of the complaint, the statements relied on, the chance to answer, the chance to test evidence, time that is reasonable rather than theatrical: these are not courtesies extended to the accused but conditions of a report the legal system will act on. Committees across the country had treated speed as a virtue and informality as a licence. After May 2023, a defective inquiry is not merely unfair to the respondent; it is the surest way to destroy the complainant's remedy, because the report and every act built on it fall together. The same judgment then turned from the case before it to the system, in the passage that made it the Act's second founding document. It addressed implementation in terms quoted in the section below, and the directions it issued began the continuing supervision that the implementation record describes.

### Is the Internal Committee a court?

The committee is not a court, and its proceedings are an inquiry, not a trial: it borrows a civil court's powers for summoning witnesses and documents, follows the respondent's service rules where they exist, and reports findings and recommendations to the employer, whose disciplinary act, not the committee's report itself, is the operative decision a court later reviews.

The distinction disciplines what judicial review of committee work looks like. High Courts examining committee reports have consistently treated the report as a fact-finding input to the employer's disciplinary decision, tested for procedural fairness, perversity and proportionality rather than re-heard as an appeal on evidence, while reserving the power to intervene where the inquiry was no inquiry at all: no notice, no material supplied, no opportunity given, or a committee the statute never authorised. Section 18's appeal is the route the statute itself provides, to the court or tribunal that the service rules designate, within ninety days. The result is a two-layer legality: the committee's work must satisfy natural justice to survive the employer's reliance on it, and the employer's action must satisfy service law and constitutional standards where the employer is the state. Aureliano Fernandes is best understood as the Supreme Court enforcing the first layer with unusual strictness precisely because the second layer had, in that case, converted a defective report into a career-ending order.

## A complaint, end to end: the statute applied

The sections so far have taken the Act apart provision by provision. This section puts it back together, following two complaints through the machinery, as an illustration of how the parts interlock. The accounts are composites constructed from the statute's own steps, not reports of particular cases.

Take first a woman employed in the regional office of a company large enough to have an Internal Committee at that office. Her complaint concerns a senior colleague whose conduct, unwanted physical contact and a stream of sexually coloured remarks, falls within two limbs of Section 2(n), and whose behaviour after she objected, adverse appraisals and exclusion from assignments, engages the circumstances in Section 3(2). She complains in writing to the committee within the three-month period, so no extension question arises. The committee supplies the complaint to the respondent, who files his reply within the ten working days Rule 7 allows. She does not request conciliation, so Section 10 falls away. She does request interim relief in writing: because the respondent appraises her work, the committee recommends that his reporting power over her be suspended for the inquiry's duration and that she be moved to a different reporting line without loss of grade, measures within Section 12 and Rule 8. The inquiry proceeds without legal practitioners, on documents and witnesses, with the quorum the Rules require, and completes within ninety days. The committee finds the allegations proved in part. Its report reaches the employer within ten days, recommending action for misconduct under the service rules, a written censure and withholding of increment drawn from the Rule 9 catalogue, and a compensation sum under Section 15 calculated on the trauma, the career loss evidenced by the appraisals, and the respondent's income, to be deducted from his salary in instalments. The employer acts within sixty days. Either party could have appealed within ninety days; neither does. The case enters the committee's annual report as a number, the employer's filings as a statistic, and its identities remain sealed under Section 16. Every step in that sequence corresponds to a provision already examined; the illustration's point is that the statute, when its machinery exists, is a complete procedural universe with no gap at which the complainant is handed back to her own resources.

The second complaint shows the other universe. A domestic worker in a city household is harassed by the householder. There is no employer committee; her workplace is a dwelling place within Section 2(o), and she is an aggrieved woman within Section 2(a) and a domestic worker within Section 2(d). Her route runs to the district: a nodal officer or a women's organisation helps her reach the Local Committee, or she registers on the She-Box portal and the district machinery is set in motion. The Local Committee receives the complaint. Because a prima facie case of a penal offence exists, Section 11's domestic worker route requires the committee to forward the complaint to the police within seven days for registration under the penal law, while the committee's own process addresses the redressal side. Interim protection in a household cannot mean transfer or leave in the office sense, which is precisely why the penal track and the District Officer's oversight carry more weight here than in any office case, and why the Act's defenders and critics argue about this constituency hardest: the text gives her the fullest definitional inclusion and the thinnest practical apparatus, and both halves of that sentence are true at once.

Between the two illustrations lies the whole implementation question of this statute. The first universe functions in thousands of workplaces and its failures are failures of fairness, correctable by the disciplines Aureliano Fernandes enforced. The second universe's failures are failures of existence, correctable only by the constitution and activation of district machinery, which is what the Supreme Court's supervision has been compelling since 2023. A reader assessing any real workplace under this Act should begin by asking which universe it belongs to: whether the committee exists, is correctly composed, is reachable and keeps its clocks. That diagnostic, not the definition of harassment, is where compliance with this statute is decided.

## False complaints and confidentiality: the two provisions everyone quotes and few quote whole

Section 14 is the provision around which the loudest argument about the Act revolves, and it is two provisions wearing one number. Section 14(1) provides that where the committee arrives at a conclusion, after the inquiry, that the allegation against the respondent is malicious, or that the aggrieved woman or any other person making the complaint has made it knowing it to be false, or has produced forged or misleading documents, the committee may recommend to the employer or the District Officer that action be taken against the person who made the complaint, in accordance with the service rules or in the prescribed manner. Section 14(2) applies the same logic to a witness who gives false evidence or produces forged or misleading documents. Then comes the proviso, which is the part that disappears in hostile quotation: a mere inability to substantiate a complaint or provide adequate proof need not attract action against the complainant under this section. A further explanation requires that malicious intent be established after an inquiry in accordance with the prescribed procedure before action follows.

The legal content is therefore narrower than its reputation. An unproved complaint is not a false complaint. A complaint that fails because witnesses will not speak, documents do not exist or memories differ is protected by the proviso in terms. Action requires a positive finding, reached after inquiry, that the complaint was malicious or knowingly false or supported by fabricated documents, a standard closer to a finding of bad faith than to a costs order against an unsuccessful litigant. Whether the provision chills reporting despite that drafting is a question of workplace behaviour rather than statutory text, and it is taken up, with its defenders and critics named, in the debate section. What belongs here is the mechanical point that committees themselves sometimes miss: the proviso is not a discretion to be merciful but a statutory bar on treating non-substantiation as misconduct, and a committee that recommends action merely because a complaint was not proved has acted outside Section 14.

Confidentiality is the other half of the design. Section 16 provides that, notwithstanding anything contained in the Right to Information Act, 2005, the contents of the complaint, the identity and addresses of the aggrieved woman, the respondent and the witnesses, any information relating to conciliation and inquiry proceedings, the recommendations of the committee and the action taken by the employer or District Officer shall not be published, communicated or made known to the public, the press or the media in any manner. Section 17 attaches a penalty: a person entrusted with the care or custody of the complaint or the inquiry documents who contravenes Section 16 is liable to a penalty of five thousand rupees, recoverable in the prescribed manner, including by deduction from salary or payment where the person is within the employer's reach, in addition to action under the service rules. The bar is comprehensive and deliberately overrides the transparency statute, reflecting a legislative judgment that in this subject the ordinary default of disclosure destroys the remedy: women do not complain into publicity, respondents are ruined by accusation if names circulate before findings, and witnesses speak only under cover of the section. The tension with open justice and with an employer's disclosure obligations elsewhere in company law is real, and the disclosure section below shows how the system has chosen to handle it: numbers and compliance statements are public, while identities and proceedings are sealed.

## The employer's duties and the penalty for doing nothing

Section 19 converts the employer from a bystander into the statute's principal duty-bearer, and its clauses should be read as a compliance list because a court or a District Officer will read them that way. The employer must provide a safe working environment at the workplace, which includes safety from the persons coming into contact at the workplace. The employer must display, at a conspicuous place in the workplace, the penal consequences of sexual harassment and the order constituting the Internal Committee. The employer must organise workshops and awareness programmes at regular intervals for sensitising employees, and orientation programmes for the members of the Internal Committee. The employer must provide the facilities the committee needs for dealing with the complaint and conducting the inquiry. The employer must assist in securing the attendance of the respondent and witnesses before the committee, and make available such information as the committee requires. The employer must provide assistance to the woman if she chooses to file a complaint under the Indian Penal Code or any other law in relation to the offence, and must cause action to be initiated under the penal law against a perpetrator who is not an employee of the workplace, where the woman so informs the committee in writing. The employer must treat sexual harassment as a misconduct under the service rules and initiate action for such misconduct. And the employer must monitor the timely submission of reports by the Internal Committee. Read with Section 4, the picture is of an employer who must build the forum, publicise it, train its members, feed it evidence, protect the complainant's parallel remedies, and discipline on its findings, all as continuing duties rather than as a response to any particular complaint.

Section 26 prices the failure. Where the employer fails to constitute an Internal Committee under Section 4, or fails to take action under Sections 13, 14 and 22, or contravenes or attempts to contravene or abets the contravention of any other provision of the Act or the Rules, the employer is punishable with a fine which may extend to fifty thousand rupees. On a subsequent conviction for the same offence, Section 26(2) imposes twice the punishment that might have been imposed on a first conviction, subject to the maximum provided, and adds the consequence that gives the section its teeth in the commercial world: cancellation of the licence, or withdrawal or non-renewal of the approval, or cancellation of the registration, by the Government or local authority, required for carrying on the employer's business or activity. Section 27 closes the procedural loop by providing that no court shall take cognizance of an offence under the Act except on a complaint by the aggrieved woman or a person authorised by the committee, and that no court inferior to a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class shall try such an offence. Section 28 preserves every other law: the Act is in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law in force, so a complainant's criminal, service-law and constitutional remedies survive her statutory complaint untouched.

The honest commentary on Section 26 is that its first-offence fine is trivial against the budgets of the organisations most often in default, and that prosecutions have been rare relative to defaults, because cognizance requires a complaint by the aggrieved woman or an authorised person and the woman whose complaint is about a missing committee is usually trying to get a remedy, not to prosecute her employer. The section's real force has proved to be reputational and supervisory: the Supreme Court's compliance orders repeatedly pointed establishments and governments toward Section 26 as the consequence awaiting employers who failed the district surveys, converting an under-used penal clause into the stated sanction behind an administrative campaign. The repeat-offence limb, with licence cancellation attached, is where the legislature put the serious deterrent, and it was aimed at the employer who treats the first fine as a fee.

## Reports, disclosures and the paper trail

The Act's reporting chain is the mechanism by which an internal process becomes visible to the state without becoming public in its details. Section 21 requires the Internal Committee to prepare, in each calendar year, an annual report of the number of cases filed, if any, and their disposal under the Act, and to forward it to the employer and to the District Officer; the employer must include the number of cases filed and disposed of in the organisation's annual report, or, where no annual report is prepared, inform the District Officer of those numbers. Section 22 requires the Local Committee to prepare an annual report on the same pattern for the District Officer, who forwards a brief report to the State Government. Section 23 obliges the appropriate Government to monitor the implementation of the Act and maintain data, and Section 25 arms it with the power to call for information from any employer or District Officer and to authorise inspection of records and the workplace. Records of this sensitivity, complaints, statements, medical and personal documents gathered by a committee, are exactly the material that must be kept in an encrypted and access-controlled file rather than on shared drives and open inboxes, and the discipline described in [VaultBook's account of how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) is the closest working model of that discipline: a single encrypted workspace, controlled by the person responsible for the file, in which confidential material is assembled without scattering copies across systems that leak.

Company law then adds a second, public layer. The Companies (Accounts) Rules were amended in 2018 to require the Board's report of a company to include a statement that the company has complied with the provisions relating to the constitution of an Internal Committee under this Act. That made committee constitution a matter on which directors made a signed public representation, testable by shareholders, auditors and regulators. The Companies (Accounts) Second Amendment Rules, 2025 tightened the requirement from a bare statement of constitution to numbers: companies must disclose in the Board's report the number of complaints of sexual harassment received in the year, the number disposed of, and the number pending for more than ninety days. The direction of travel across a decade is consistent: the state's chosen instrument for this statute, after the committee itself, is compelled visibility, because the defaults it kept finding were defaults of existence that only reporting could expose.

What the numbers show, when they finally began to be assembled, is treated with care in this article, because complaint counts under a harassment statute are ambiguous evidence: a rise can mean more harassment or more confidence in complaining, and a zero can mean a safe workplace or a dead committee. A 2024 study by the Centre for Economic Data and Analysis at Ashoka University, examining disclosures by three hundred companies listed on the National Stock Exchange across eleven years, found that of 1,160 complaints reported in the financial year 2022-23, the great majority came from only eighty-one companies, while two hundred and nineteen companies reported none at all. The study's own caution mirrors this article's: concentration of that kind is evidence about reporting systems before it is evidence about workplaces. The figure is used here for the one proposition it safely supports, which is that formal compliance and lived compliance diverge widely among large, regulated, disclosure-bound employers, the very employers best placed to comply.

## Reading the complaint data: what the filings do and do not show

Because this pillar declines to invent statistics, it owes the reader an account of how to read the statistics that exist. Complaint numbers under this Act are generated by three different systems that must not be merged. The first is the statutory reporting chain: Internal Committees report cases filed and disposed of to employers and District Officers, employers carry numbers into annual reports, Local Committees report through District Officers to state governments, and appropriate governments are charged with maintaining data under Section 23. The second is company disclosure: the Board's report statement of committee constitution from 2018, and the complaint, disposal and pendency numbers required from 2025, which cover the listed and larger company universe in auditable form. The third is research assembly of the second, of which the Ashoka University centre's study of three hundred listed companies is the worked example used in this article. Each system counts a different universe with different incentives, and a figure from one cannot be set against a figure from another without the merger error the series rules forbid.

The interpretive discipline follows from the incentives. A company reporting zero complaints for a decade has disclosed a fact about its reporting system before it has disclosed a fact about its workplace: in a workforce of thousands, the base rate of conduct falling somewhere within Section 2(n) makes a permanent zero more plausibly a measure of distrust, ignorance of the committee, or fear of Section 14's reputation than of universal propriety. Conversely, a company whose reported complaints rise after a credible committee is constituted and publicised has usually improved, not deteriorated, since the rise measures complaints reaching the forum the Act built for them. Pendency is the least ambiguous figure in the set, which is why the 2025 disclosure amendment's choice to require the count of cases pending beyond ninety days is analytically the sharpest of the three numbers: it measures the institution against the statute's own clock, on a metric no interpretation can flatter. The district data carry the opposite warning. A district whose Local Committee reports no complaints in a year has, on the structure of the unorganised sector, almost certainly reported an intake failure rather than an absence of harassment, and the Court's survey directions treated nil-returning districts accordingly, as objects of verification rather than as successes. Read with those disciplines, the data support the conclusion this article has drawn from the case law and the compliance orders independently: the machinery works where it has been built and trusted, and the map of its working is a map of institutional capacity, not of the geography of the wrong.

## The implementation record: a decade of directions

### Why did the Supreme Court keep supervising the Act after deciding the appeal?

Because the May 2023 judgment recorded that the failure was not in the statute's text but in the existence of its machinery: committees unconstituted or wrongly composed a decade after commencement, which led the Court to direct a time-bound verification across governments and institutions and then to retain the matter through successive compliance orders rather than close it.

The fuller record runs as follows. The directions in Aureliano Fernandes on 12 May 2023 were addressed to the Union of India, all state governments and union territories, and required a time-bound exercise to verify whether ministries, departments, government organisations, authorities, public sector undertakings, institutions and bodies had constituted Internal Committees and Local Committees strictly in terms of the Act, and whether their composition matched the statute. Statutory professional bodies, universities and colleges, training centres, and government and private hospitals were directed to run the same exercise. The Court directed that the constitution and composition of committees, the contact details of designated persons, the procedure for online complaints and the relevant rules be made readily available on the websites of the organisations concerned, and it asked governments and the National Commission for Women and state commissions to carry the Act's requirements into public awareness. The trigger for this breadth sits inside the judgment itself: the Court noticed a newspaper survey reporting that sixteen of thirty national sports federations did not have a duly constituted Internal Committee, some having no committee at all and others lacking the stipulated membership or the external member, and it called the position a sorry state of affairs reflecting poorly on every state functionary and institution duty bound to implement the Act in letter and spirit.

Supervision did not end with the judgment. The matter was taken up again in proceedings in which the Court, by an order of 22 October 2024 from a bench of Justices B. V. Nagarathna and N. Kotiswar Singh, directed the National Legal Services Authority and the state and district legal services authorities to assist women in approaching the committees and the government's She-Box portal, reasoning that harassment must not be allowed to push a woman out of the employment that Articles 19(1)(g) and 21 protect. By an order of 3 December 2024 the Court moved from exhortation to dates, directing the appointment of District Officers and the constitution of Local Committees within stated deadlines running to the end of January 2025, and a survey of establishments to establish whether committees existed. In 2025 the pattern continued: an order in May 2025 recorded the amicus curiae's status report, noted that District Officers had been appointed across the states but that the district-level survey of private workplaces remained incomplete in several jurisdictions, and pressed for its completion within weeks; and on 12 August 2025 a bench of Justices B. V. Nagarathna and K. V. Viswanathan directed a district-wise survey, to be carried out through Labour Commissioners and District Officers, to verify whether organisations in both sectors had constituted committees, with the data to be uploaded on the She-Box portal maintained by the Ministry of Women and Child Development and with the Section 26 penalties pointed out to defaulters. State governments responded with their own instruments in the same period: labour departments issued circulars directing field officers to verify committee constitution in commercial establishments, and city administrations, including Delhi's in June 2025, directed organisations to register their committee details on the She-Box portal. The proceedings remained on foot, with the matter listed for further hearing, as this line of orders continued.

Two analytical points complete the record. The first is about what kind of judicial activity this is. The Court is not, in these orders, interpreting the Act; there is nothing left to interpret in a direction that a committee exist. It is administering the statute's preconditions, in a continuing mandamus that keeps the case open and the reporting obligations alive until the machinery demonstrably exists. That remedial pattern, a court supervising the construction of institutions over years because the executive default is structural rather than episodic, is one the series has met in the environmental field, where continuing supervision of statutory machinery is most fully documented in [the account of environmental jurisprudence in the Supreme Court](/2014/12/15/environmental-jurisprudence-supreme-court/), and its appearance in an employment statute shows how far the pattern has travelled. The second point is about the She-Box portal, which became the supervision's chosen instrument. The portal began as a complaint-filing facility of the Ministry of Women and Child Development, and the Ministry relaunched it in 2024 with provision for registering the details of Internal Committees, Local Committees and nodal officers, so that the existence of a committee became a matter of central record rather than local assertion. The Court's insistence on registration and survey is an admission, in remedial form, that the state had legislated machinery for a decade without maintaining a list of whether the machinery existed.

Set against this record, the places where the Act does work deserve their due, because the verdict is not uniform failure. In large companies, public sector undertakings and educational institutions with functioning committees, the statute has normalised a channel that did not exist before Vishaka and was voluntary in effect before 2013: complaints are received, inquired and closed within the statutory clock in substantial numbers, the disclosure data themselves being proof of operation. Committee members in such workplaces accumulate a practical expertise the Vishaka era never built. The failure the Court documented is concentrated where the supervision was always going to be hardest: small establishments, the unorganised sector, district machinery, and institutions, like the sports federations the judgment named, whose governance cultures treated the committee as an embarrassment to be minimised. The statute's text reaches all of them. Its machinery, for its first decade, reached them last or not at all.

## The criticisms at full strength

Three criticisms of the Act are serious enough to require statement in their strongest form, with the answer each attracts, because a pillar that reports only the statute's intentions misleads its readers about the law they actually live under.

The first is the gender gap, and it is not a drafting oversight but a boundary the Act draws in terms. The statute protects women. A man sexually harassed at his workplace, or a person of any other gender, has no complaint under this Act, no committee with jurisdiction over his case, and no statutory clock running in his favour; his remedies are his employer's internal policies, his service rules where they exist, and the general law. Critics of the boundary make two distinct points. The principled point is that a wrong defined by conduct does not change its character with the victim's gender, and that comparable jurisdictions have legislated accordingly: Title VII of the United States Civil Rights Act has always been written in gender-neutral terms, and in Oncale v Sundowner Offshore Services in 1998 the United States Supreme Court applied it to harassment between men; the United Kingdom's Equality Act 2010 likewise protects every sex. The practical point is that male complainants and complainants of other genders exist, are concentrated in the same hierarchical workplaces, and are served by nothing in the statute that polices everyone else's working conditions. The defenders of the boundary answer that the Act is a remedial statute aimed at a documented, asymmetric pattern: the Vishaka petition, the committee it created and the constituency the statute serves all arose from the specific exposure of women to sexual coercion at work, and protective legislation for women is constitutionally contemplated by Article 15(3), which permits special provision for women. They add that nothing prevents an employer from running a gender-neutral internal policy alongside the statutory committee, and many large employers do, so the gap is one of statutory entitlement rather than of all remedy. The honest statement of the position is that Parliament chose a women-specific statute in full knowledge of the neutral alternative, the choice was constitutionally available to it, and the cost of the choice falls on a class of complainants the statute does not see.

The second criticism concerns Section 14 and conciliation together, as twin deterrents. Women's organisations, among them the All India Democratic Women's Association, objected during the Bill's long passage that conciliation has no legitimate place in sexual harassment, since what is conciliated between unequals is usually the complaint itself, and that a provision for action against complainants, however qualified, would be read in workplaces as a warning that complaining carries personal risk. The Justice Verma Committee's recommendation that the false complaint provision be reconsidered gave the objection institutional weight. The defenders answer from the text: conciliation happens only at the woman's request, money cannot be its basis, and Section 14 requires a positive finding of malice or knowing falsehood after inquiry, with the proviso expressly protecting the complainant who merely fails to substantiate. They add a respondent-side fairness claim: a statute that can end careers must contain some answer to the demonstrably fabricated complaint, and the alternative to a qualified provision is not the absence of consequences for fabrication but their invention by employers without statutory discipline. The factual dispute underneath is about behaviour: whether the proviso's protection is known to the women deciding whether to complain. On the record assembled by the compliance proceedings, where the recurring problem is under-reporting rather than fabricated reporting, the chilling concern has the better of the evidence about practice, while the defenders have the better of the argument about the text.

The third criticism is structural, and it is the deepest: the committee cannot be independent of the employer who constitutes it. Its members are the employer's nominees, its presiding officer is the employer's senior employee, its facilities, information and secretarial support come from the employer, and the employer is the authority who acts on its report. Where the respondent is senior, well connected or the employer himself in substance, the committee is asked to find facts against the source of its own existence. The external member is the statute's only structural counterweight, and one member among four or more, dependent on the employer for the appointment and the fee, is a thin counterweight. The Justice Verma Committee's proposal to replace the committees with an employment tribunal, an external adjudicatory body, was the fully worked version of this criticism, and Parliament considered and declined it. The defence of the internal model is functional: harassment is a workplace condition best corrected inside the workplace, swiftly, by people who understand its hierarchies and can change rosters, reporting lines and working arrangements at once; an external tribunal for every complaint would be slower, more legalistic and, on Indian tribunal waiting times, a remedy delivered after the working relationship it was meant to save had ended. Aureliano Fernandes cuts both ways in this argument and both sides cite it: it proves an internal committee can destroy a career on a procedurally rotten inquiry, and it proves the appellate structure can catch exactly that failure and order the inquiry done properly. What the case does not supply is a case in which the machinery protected a complainant against a powerful respondent inside her own organisation with equal drama, and the asymmetry of the celebrated examples is itself data about the structural criticism.

A further criticism runs in the opposite direction from the first three, and it is owed the same full-strength statement, because a pillar that hears only complainant-side objections will misdescribe the law's legitimacy conditions. Respondent-side critics, among them service lawyers and the line of High Court decisions quashing committee reports on natural justice grounds, argue that the committee process concentrates accusation, investigation and recommendation in one employer-appointed body, sitting without legal representation for either side, under a confidentiality rule that prevents the respondent from answering public damage to his reputation, and with consequences up to termination and compensation deducted from salary. On that view, the process is defended as informal precisely where its outcomes are most formal, and the respondent's protection depends entirely on the committee's self-restraint. The supporters of the design answer that the informality is symmetrical, that legal representation historically converts workplace inquiries into litigation the complainant cannot sustain, that the committee recommends rather than decides, and that the employer who acts remains answerable in appeal and judicial review for the fairness of the whole. Aureliano Fernandes is the case both sides must reckon with: it vindicated a respondent destroyed by a hurried and unfair inquiry, in a judgment that simultaneously hardened the compliance obligations owed to complainants, which suggests the Act's fairness problem is not a design preference for one side but a discipline problem on both. The standard of proof point completes the respondent-side case and should be recorded accurately: a committee inquiry is a disciplinary fact-finding, decided on the preponderance of probabilities rather than proof beyond reasonable doubt, which is the correct standard for its purpose and also the reason its procedures must be scrupulous, since career consequences follow from a civil standard of persuasion.

A fourth, quieter criticism completes the set, aimed at the unorganised sector the definitions so carefully include. The Local Committee model asks the complainant with the least power, the domestic worker against her employing household, the woman in a nine-worker unit against its owner, to travel to a district body she has never heard of, in a system whose nodal officers and committees were, in many districts, unconstituted for years. Coverage without machinery is the statute's standing reproach, and it is not answered by pointing to the definitions, because the definitions were never the missing part.

## The parallel criminal law and the other doors

Section 28's saving means the statutory complaint is one door among several, and a reader should know the shape of the others. The same conduct that grounds a committee complaint may constitute offences under the penal law. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 inserted Section 354A into the Indian Penal Code, making sexual harassment itself an offence and punishing its graver forms, physical contact and advances involving unwelcome and explicit sexual overtures and demands or requests for sexual favours, with rigorous imprisonment of one to three years, and the lesser forms, sexually coloured remarks and showing pornography, with imprisonment of up to one year; Section 509 separately punishes words, gestures or acts intended to insult the modesty of a woman, and Section 354 punishes assault or criminal force with intent to outrage modesty. The successor penal code, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, carries the harassment offence forward in its own numbering. The relationship between the two tracks, and the wider comparison of the criminal law amendments directed at offences against women, is mapped in [the comparison of the criminal law amendments on women](/2016/03/15/criminal-law-amendments-women-compared/); for this statute, the operative points are three. The tracks are independent: a woman may complain to the committee, to the police, or to both, and neither forum's finding binds the other. The standards differ: the committee decides misconduct on the disciplinary standard for service action, the criminal court decides guilt beyond reasonable doubt for imprisonment. And the employer stands in both: Section 19 obliges assistance to the woman who chooses the criminal route, including initiating action against a non-employee perpetrator on her written information to the committee.

Beyond the two tracks lie the service-law route for government employees, for whom sexual harassment is misconduct under the conduct rules independently of the Act; the constitutional route against state employers; and the labour-law environment in which harassment intersects with victimisation, wrongful transfer and constructive dismissal. The Act does not displace any of them, and its committees frequently operate in the shadow of parallel proceedings, which is one reason the confidentiality and natural justice disciplines matter: a record created carelessly in the internal inquiry will be read later by forums with stricter rules.

## The Act in special settings: campuses, hospitals and sport

The general scheme bends, in three settings, under pressures the drafter could foresee only in part, and each has generated its own layer of law or litigation. Higher education is the first. A university or college is a workplace for its employees and something more complicated for its students, who are not employees at all. The Act reaches the campus through the workplace definition, and a student harassed in relation to the institution is an aggrieved woman within Section 2(a) because the definition does not require employment. The University Grants Commission then built on the Act with the University Grants Commission (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal of Sexual Harassment of Women Employees and Students in Higher Educational Institutions) Regulations, 2015, which require every higher educational institution to maintain an Internal Committee on the statutory model, expressly frame the institution's responsibility toward women employees and students, and provide for student representation within the committee structure. The campus problem the regulations answer is distinctively academic: the respondent is frequently the person who examines, supervises or recommends the complainant, so the power imbalance Section 3(2) describes is not incidental to the setting but built into its degree structure. Aureliano Fernandes itself was a university case, and its insistence on procedural regularity in a campus inquiry, where the committee is drawn from the respondent's own institution and the complainant may be a student or junior colleague, is the campus regime's governing precedent.

Hospitals and nursing homes are named in the workplace definition itself, and they present the inverse problem: the risk runs in several directions at once, staff harassed by patients and attendants, patients harassed by staff, and large numbers of women working night shifts in institutions that never close. The Act's answer is structural rather than specific: the hospital is a workplace, the employer duties apply, and the persons coming into contact at the workplace are expressly part of the safe environment the employer must provide under Section 19, a phrase that reads as though drafted for institutions whose workplaces admit the public. Sport is the third setting, and it enters the statute through the definition's inclusion of sports institutes, stadiums and venues where sporting activities are held. It enters the case law through the embarrassment recorded in Aureliano Fernandes: the newspaper survey the Court noticed found a majority of national sports federations without a properly constituted committee, in a sector where selection power over athletes is concentrated, travel is constant, and complainants are often minors or young athletes dependent on the federation for their careers. The Court's directions treated federations as ordinary duty-bearers under the Act, which, as a matter of law, they are; the survey's findings showed how far ordinary duty-bearing was from ordinary practice in that sector.

A fourth setting deserves mention because the definitions create it: government offices and public sector undertakings, where the respondent is a government servant and the inquiry runs on the conduct rules. There the Act and the service rules interlock: the committee inquires, its report has the character of an inquiry report within the disciplinary process, and the standard constitutional protections for civil servants in disciplinary proceedings surround the outcome. The interlock produces the most heavily litigated POSH cases, because a government respondent commands both the Section 18 appeal and the writ jurisdiction, and because the penalty imposed on the committee's findings must survive the proportionality review the courts apply to punishments in service law.

## The small employer and the threshold problem

The ten-employee line is the Act's most consequential piece of arithmetic, and it works differently from the way it is usually summarised. The duty to constitute an Internal Committee attaches to every employer of a workplace, and Section 4 requires the committee to be constituted at all administrative units or offices of the workplace; the ten-worker figure operates in Section 6, which routes complaints to the Local Committee from establishments where an Internal Committee has not been constituted due to their having less than ten workers. The threshold therefore decides the forum, not the coverage: a woman in a nine-worker establishment is fully protected, but her forum is the district committee rather than an internal one. Employers near the line face genuine counting questions the Act answers only in part. The definition of employee is wide, sweeping in contract, agency, temporary and daily wage workers, trainees and volunteers, so an establishment that imagines itself below ten on its payroll alone may be above it on the statutory count. Multi-unit employers face the opposite temptation, to treat each small unit as a separate workplace below the threshold, against a section that contemplates committees at each administrative unit of one workplace and a district backstop designed for genuinely small establishments, not for fragmentation.

The staffing industry tests the scheme hardest. A woman supplied by a contractor to a client site is, on the definitions, an employee in relation to the workplace where she works, engaged through an agent or contractor with or without the knowledge of the principal employer, and the client establishment's committee is the forum the definitions point to, since the workplace and the respondent are ordinarily there. In practice, responsibility between contractor and principal employer is the point on which complaints stall, each organisation directing the complainant to the other's committee. The statute's drafting does not authorise that stall: the workplace-based definitions fix the forum by where the work is done and where the respondent sits, and the employer's duties under Section 19 attach to the organisation that runs the workplace. The honest difficulty is not legal but administrative, since the committee of the client site must inquire into a respondent who may be the contractor's employee, and compel cooperation across a commercial contract. The Act's answer is the one it gives everywhere: it fixes the duty and leaves the cooperation to be enforced.

## What compliance looks like from inside: the committee's working year

It is worth describing, once, the annual reality of a functioning committee, because the distance between the statute and its reputation is usually a distance of administration rather than of law. The year begins with constitution: a written order naming the Presiding Officer at the senior level the section requires, the employee members, and the external member, whose sourcing is the first practical problem, since organisations committed to the cause of women with members willing to sit, be trained and be paid the prescribed fees are unevenly distributed outside the large cities. The order must be displayed, with the penal consequences of harassment, at a conspicuous place, and the committee's existence communicated to the workforce in terms employees actually receive, which in practice means induction material, the employee handbook and the intranet rather than the notice board alone. Orientation for members and awareness sessions for employees follow under Section 19, and the quality of the training is the single variable most predictive of whether the committee will be trusted: members who have never been taught the difference between an inquiry and a trial, or the content of the Section 14 proviso, will reinvent both under pressure.

The working year then turns on readiness rather than volume. A well-run committee maintains a confidential register and secure custody of records, because Section 16's confidentiality is an operational discipline before it is a legal one: complaint papers in shared mailboxes or open files are a breach waiting for a recipient. It keeps its quorum discipline, rotates no member mid-inquiry, and understands that its most-used powers will be interim, the transfer, the changed reporting line, the leave letter, deployed within days of a complaint rather than at its end. It prepares the annual report the Act requires, counts cases filed and disposed of, and forwards the numbers that will surface in the employer's annual report and, for companies, in the Board's report disclosures. And it rehearses, in effect, for the two stresses the statute predicts: the complaint against a senior person, where its independence will be tested in fact, and the complaint from outside the employment relation, the client, the applicant, the vendor's staff, where its jurisdiction will be questioned by colleagues who have not read Section 2(a). None of this appears in the statute's text. All of it is what the text is for.

## The constitutional footing and the machinery of the state

The Act's constitutional position was built before it was enacted, and it explains why the statute has never faced a serious existential challenge. Vishaka grounded the obligation in Articles 14 and 15, equality before the law and non-discrimination on grounds of sex, in Article 19(1)(g), the freedom to practise a profession or carry on an occupation, which a workplace made hostile by harassment burdens in fact, and in Article 21, life and personal liberty read to include dignity. A women-specific remedial statute is additionally sheltered by Article 15(3), which expressly permits the state to make special provision for women, so the Act's gender boundary, criticised as policy in the debate section, is not a constitutional vulnerability. The international layer Vishaka drew on, the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, whose Articles 11 and 24 the judgment used to fill the domestic silence, receded once the legislation occupied the field, its work done: the statute is the form in which the treaty obligation was finally domesticated.

On the state's side, the Act distributes power with care for its federal setting. Labour and welfare subjects sit across the Union and State lists, and the Act's operative concept is the appropriate government: the Central Government for workplaces it controls and for the central sphere, the State Government for the rest, each empowered to monitor implementation and maintain data under Section 23, and each armed under Section 25 with the power to call for information from any employer or District Officer and to authorise an officer to inspect records and the workplace. Rule-making sits with the appropriate government under Section 29, and the Union's 2013 Rules supply the model the states work from; Section 30 gives the Central Government a time-limited power to remove difficulties in giving effect to the Act. Two features of this machinery deserve emphasis. The inspection power is the provision that converts the reporting chain from self-description into verified fact, and its sparing use in the Act's first decade is part of why the Supreme Court's supervision proceeded by survey rather than by the statute's own instruments. And the data duty in Section 23 is the provision under which the long absence of reliable national numbers on committees and complaints is itself a statutory shortfall, since a government that must maintain data on implementation cannot plead the absence of the data as a neutral condition.

## Assessment: a statute that replaced supervision, and then required it again

The namable claim of this pillar can be stated in its full form. The POSH Act was enacted to end the era of judicial supervision of workplace harassment, replacing guidelines a court had framed and a court had monitored for sixteen years with a complete legislative scheme. Within a decade, the Supreme Court was supervising the field again, more intrusively than before, down to deadlines for appointing district officers and orders for district-wise surveys. What changed is what the Court supervises. Under Vishaka it supervised the content of the obligation, writing the norms itself. Under the Act it supervises the existence of the machinery, because the norms are enacted and uncontested while the committees, officers and reports that give them effect were, in large parts of the country, never built. Legislation did not remove the judiciary from this field; it moved the judiciary from author to auditor.

On the text, the Act deserves the standing it has. Its definitions are wide where width protects and specific where specificity disciplines. Its clock is complete, complaint to report to action to appeal, and its middle mechanisms, interim relief and payroll-deducted compensation, show a legislature thinking about the interval between wrong and remedy rather than about liability alone. Its two most criticised provisions are more carefully qualified than their reputations. Its genuine design weaknesses are the ones its critics name: the gender boundary, the structural dependence of the committee on the employer, and a first-offence penalty too small to price compliance for a large defaulter, mitigated only by the licence consequence on repetition.

On the record, the verdict the evidence supports is narrower and harder. The statute works where institutions were already capable of working it, and failed, for its first decade, precisely in the territories its definitions most proudly reach: the small workplace, the household, the district. The Supreme Court's supervision has begun to close that gap by the only method available to it, compelled existence, and the registration and disclosure architecture built since 2024 gives the next decade a factual baseline the first decade never had. Whether compelled existence matures into functioning committees is the question the continuing proceedings will answer. The statute's own test was always the committee in the room. Everything else, this article included, is commentary on whether the room exists and what happens inside it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the POSH Act require an employer to do?

The employer must constitute an Internal Committee by written order at every administrative unit or office of the workplace, display the penal consequences of sexual harassment and the committee's order at a conspicuous place, and provide a safe working environment. The employer must organise awareness workshops for employees and orientation for committee members, give the committee the facilities, information and attendance assistance it needs for an inquiry, treat sexual harassment as misconduct under the service rules, act on the committee's recommendations within sixty days, assist a woman who chooses to file a police complaint, and monitor the committee's timely reporting. These duties under Section 19 are continuing obligations, owed whether or not a complaint has been filed.

### Q: Who must be on an Internal Committee?

Section 4 requires a Presiding Officer who is a woman employed at a senior level at the workplace, or, if none is available there, a senior woman nominated from another unit of the employer or from another workplace or organisation. There must be at least two members from among the employees, preferably persons committed to the cause of women or with experience in social work or legal knowledge. One member must come from outside the organisation: a person from a non-governmental organisation or association committed to the cause of women, or a person familiar with issues relating to sexual harassment. At least half of the members must be women. Members, including the Presiding Officer, hold office for a term not exceeding three years, and the external member is paid fees or allowances by the employer.

### Q: What is a Local Committee and whom does it serve?

The Local Committee is the district-level committee constituted by the District Officer under Section 6. It receives complaints of sexual harassment from establishments where the Internal Committee has not been constituted because the workplace has fewer than ten workers, and complaints where the respondent is the employer himself, which an employer-appointed committee cannot impartially hear. It is chaired by an eminent woman in the field of social work committed to the cause of women, and includes a woman working in the local administration, two members drawn from non-governmental organisations or persons familiar with sexual harassment issues, and an officer of the district's Social Welfare or Women and Child Development department. It serves the unorganised sector, small workplaces and domestic workers, for whom no internal committee exists.

### Q: What is the time limit for a POSH complaint?

Under Section 9, the complaint must be made in writing to the Internal Committee, or to the Local Committee where there is no internal committee, within three months from the date of the incident. Where the complaint concerns a series of incidents, the three months run from the date of the last incident. The committee may extend the period by up to three more months, but only where it is satisfied that circumstances prevented the woman from filing in time, and only for reasons recorded in writing. The limit is therefore three months with a discretionary, reasoned extension of three months, not six months as a matter of right. A complaint filed beyond even the extended period is time-barred, which is why the explanation for any delay should accompany a late complaint.

### Q: What happens if a POSH complaint is found to be false?

Section 14 permits the committee to recommend action against the complainant only where, after an inquiry, it concludes that the allegation was malicious, or that the complaint was made knowing it to be false, or that forged or misleading documents were produced; the same applies to a witness who gives false evidence. The action is taken under the service rules or in the prescribed manner. The critical qualification is the proviso: a mere inability to substantiate a complaint or provide adequate proof need not attract any action against the complainant. An unproved complaint is therefore not a false complaint in law, and a committee cannot recommend action simply because the allegation was not established. Malicious intent must itself be established through an inquiry before any action follows.

### Q: What penalty does an employer face for POSH non compliance?

Under Section 26, an employer who fails to constitute an Internal Committee, fails to act on the committee's recommendations, or contravenes any other provision of the Act or the Rules is punishable, on conviction, with a fine which may extend to fifty thousand rupees. If the employer is convicted again of the same offence, the punishment doubles, subject to the maximum provided for the offence, and the government or local authority may cancel the employer's licence, or withdraw, not renew or cancel the approval or registration required for carrying on its business or activity. Courts can take cognizance of these offences only on a complaint by the aggrieved woman or a person authorised by the committee, and trial lies before a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class.

### Q: Are domestic workers and the unorganised sector covered?

Yes, on the face of the Act. A domestic worker is expressly defined in Section 2(d) as a woman employed to do household work in any household for remuneration in cash or kind, directly or through an agency, on any basis from temporary to full-time, excluding members of the employer's family. A dwelling place or house is included in the definition of workplace, and the unorganised sector, defined as enterprises employing fewer than ten workers, is within the Act. Because a household or small enterprise cannot have an Internal Committee, the domestic worker's complaint goes to the district Local Committee, and where a prima facie case exists the committee forwards the complaint to the police within seven days for registration of a case under the penal law. The coverage is complete in text; its weakness is the district machinery's uneven existence.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court direct about POSH compliance?

In Aureliano Fernandes v State of Goa, decided on 12 May 2023, the Supreme Court described the enforcement of the Act a decade after commencement as a sorry state of affairs and directed the Union, all states and union territories to undertake a time-bound exercise verifying that ministries, departments, public bodies, statutory professional bodies, universities, hospitals and institutions had constituted Internal Committees and Local Committees with the composition the Act requires, and to publish committee details and complaint procedures on their websites. In later orders in the same proceedings, in October and December 2024 and through 2025, the Court directed the appointment of District Officers and the constitution of Local Committees against stated deadlines, legal services authorities' assistance to complainants, district-wise surveys of public and private establishments, and the uploading of committee data on the She-Box portal, keeping the matter under continuing supervision.

### Q: Who can file a complaint under the POSH Act?

The complaint is made by the aggrieved woman herself, in writing, to the Internal Committee of the workplace or, where no such committee exists or the complaint is against the employer, to the Local Committee. The definition of aggrieved woman covers a woman of any age who alleges sexual harassment at a workplace, whether or not she is employed there, so visitors, applicants, clients and trainees are not excluded by status. Where the woman is unable to complain because of physical or mental incapacity, or where she has died, or in other circumstances covered by Section 9, her legal heir or such other person as the Rules prescribe may make the complaint on her behalf. The complaint must be written; committees and the Rules then regulate how copies are supplied to the respondent and how the process begins.

### Q: What counts as a workplace under the POSH Act?

Section 2(o) defines workplace to include government departments, organisations, undertakings, offices and units; private sector organisations, enterprises, institutions, societies, trusts and service providers carrying on commercial, professional, educational, entertainment, industrial, health or financial activities; hospitals and nursing homes; and sports institutes, stadiums and competition venues. Two limbs widen it decisively. Any place visited by the employee arising out of or during the course of employment, including transportation provided by the employer for the journey, is a workplace, so client sites, field visits, conferences and official travel are covered. A dwelling place or a house is also a workplace, which is what brings domestic work inside the Act. The test is the connection with employment, not the ownership or geography of the premises.

### Q: Does the POSH Act protect women who are not employees of the workplace?

Yes. In relation to a workplace, the Act defines an aggrieved woman as a woman of any age, whether employed or not, who alleges that she has been subjected to sexual harassment by the respondent. The definition of employee in Section 2(f) is itself very wide, covering regular, temporary, ad hoc and daily wage workers, persons engaged directly or through an agent or contractor, workers without remuneration and volunteers, probationers, trainees and apprentices. Beyond that, a woman with no employment relationship at all, such as a job applicant, a client, a customer's representative or a visitor, may complain about conduct in relation to that workplace. The practical difference lies in the remedy: disciplinary action under service rules does not reach a respondent who is not an employee, so the committee's findings then feed into the employer's other powers, including initiating action under the penal law against a non-employee perpetrator under Section 19.

### Q: Can a man file a complaint under the POSH Act?

No. The Act protects women, and only a woman can be an aggrieved woman under Section 2(a). A man who is sexually harassed at work, or a person of another gender, has no complaint under this statute and no access to its committees or timelines. His remedies lie in his employer's internal policies, in service rules where they treat such conduct as misconduct, and in the general criminal law, which since the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita has its own gendered structure for sexual offences. Many employers operate gender-neutral internal policies alongside the statutory committee, but that protection rests on policy, not on the Act. The boundary was a deliberate legislative choice, and whether Parliament should replace it with a gender-neutral statute, as the United Kingdom and the United States have, remains a live legislative debate.

### Q: What relief can a complainant get while an inquiry is pending?

Section 12 provides interim relief for the period between complaint and report, on the written request of the aggrieved woman. The committee may recommend the transfer of the aggrieved woman or of the respondent to another workplace, or leave for the aggrieved woman for a period of up to three months, which is granted in addition to the leave she is otherwise entitled to, or such other relief as the Rules prescribe. The prescribed reliefs address administrative retaliation, including restraining a respondent who holds power over the complainant from reporting on her work allocation or appraising her performance during the inquiry. The employer or District Officer acts on the recommendation, so the protection takes effect while the inquiry runs rather than after it concludes. Interim relief is not a finding on the merits and creates no inference against the respondent.

### Q: Can a POSH complaint be settled through conciliation?

Only in a narrow and controlled way. Section 10 allows the committee, before it begins an inquiry and only at the request of the aggrieved woman, to take steps to settle the matter through conciliation. The statute attaches a strict condition: no monetary settlement shall be made as a basis of conciliation. If a settlement is reached, it is recorded, sent to the employer or District Officer for action as specified, copies are given to both parties, and no further inquiry is held. If any term of the settlement is not complied with, the committee proceeds to inquire into the complaint. Conciliation cannot be imposed by the employer or the committee, cannot be traded for money, and cannot be used after the inquiry has begun; a woman who does not want it is entitled to the full inquiry instead.

### Q: How long does an inquiry under the POSH Act take?

Section 11(4) requires the inquiry to be completed within ninety days. The clock then continues: under Section 13(1) the committee must give its report of findings to the employer or the District Officer within ten days of the completion of the inquiry, and a copy is made available to both parties; and under Section 13 the employer or District Officer must act on the committee's recommendations within sixty days of receiving the report. A party aggrieved by a recommendation, or by its non-implementation, may appeal to the court or tribunal designated by the service rules within ninety days of the recommendation. In working terms, therefore, the statutory design runs from complaint to implemented action in about five months at the outside, provided each period is honoured; the delays that occur in practice are failures of those periods, not gaps in them.

### Q: How is compensation calculated under the POSH Act?

Compensation is decided by the committee itself as part of its recommendations under Section 13, and Section 15 fixes the factors: the mental trauma, pain, suffering and emotional distress caused to the aggrieved woman; the loss in career opportunity she suffered because of the incident; the medical expenses she incurred for physical or psychiatric treatment; the income and financial status of the respondent; and the feasibility of paying the sum in a lump sum or in instalments. The committee directs the employer to deduct the determined sum from the respondent's salary or wages and pay it to the woman. If the respondent is no longer employed or deduction is not possible, he is directed to pay directly, and if he defaults, the committee forwards the order to the District Officer for recovery as an arrear of land revenue.

### Q: Are POSH complaints and inquiry proceedings confidential?

Yes, comprehensively. Section 16 provides that, notwithstanding anything in the Right to Information Act, 2005, the contents of the complaint, the identity and addresses of the aggrieved woman, the respondent and the witnesses, any information relating to conciliation and inquiry proceedings, the committee's recommendations and the action taken shall not be published, communicated or made known to the public, the press or the media in any manner. A person entrusted with handling the complaint or the inquiry proceedings who breaches Section 16 faces a penalty of five thousand rupees under Section 17, in addition to action under the applicable service rules. What is public is aggregate and institutional: case numbers and disposal figures move upward through the annual reporting chain, while identities and proceedings remain sealed.

### Q: What does the District Officer do under the POSH Act?

The District Officer, who is the District Magistrate, Additional District Magistrate, Collector or Deputy Collector notified for the district under Section 5, is the Act's district-level anchor. The officer constitutes the Local Committee and designates nodal officers in every block, taluka, tehsil, ward and municipality to receive complaints and forward them to the Local Committee within seven days. The officer receives the annual reports of Internal Committees and of the Local Committee, forwards a brief report on the Local Committee to the State Government, monitors the timely submission of reports, receives committee reports in complaints handled by the Local Committee and must act on their recommendations within sixty days, promotes awareness through non-governmental organisations, and serves as the recovery authority when a compensation order must be enforced as an arrear of land revenue.

### Q: Can a complaint be filed through the SHe-Box portal instead of the committee?

The SHe-Box portal, maintained by the Ministry of Women and Child Development, lets a woman register a complaint of workplace sexual harassment online and routes it to the committee or authority concerned with the workplace; the Ministry relaunched the portal with facilities for registering the details of Internal Committees, Local Committees and nodal officers, and the Supreme Court's compliance orders pressed states to upload committee data to it. The portal is a channel of access, not a substitute forum: it does not itself inquire, conciliate or decide. A complaint registered there must still travel to the Internal Committee or Local Committee, which conducts the inquiry under the Act's procedure and timelines. Its practical value is discovery and record: a complainant who does not know whether her workplace has a committee, or who it is, can find the registered details or set the district machinery in motion without first confronting her own organisation.

### Q: What must a company disclose about POSH compliance in its annual reporting?

At two levels. Under the Act itself, the Internal Committee prepares an annual report of the number of cases filed and their disposal, forwards it to the employer and the District Officer, and the employer includes the number of cases filed and disposed of in the organisation's annual report or reports those numbers to the District Officer. Under company law, the Board's report has carried, since an amendment to the Companies (Accounts) Rules in 2018, a statement that the company has complied with the provisions on constituting an Internal Committee under the Act; the Companies (Accounts) Second Amendment Rules, 2025 went further and require disclosure of the number of complaints received during the year, the number disposed of, and the number pending for more than ninety days. Identities and proceedings remain confidential; what shareholders and regulators see is constitution and case numbers.
