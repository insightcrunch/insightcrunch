The prosody of "Gallop apace" is the sharpest evidence in Romeo and Juliet that verse music can carry a meaning the words only half state. Juliet's soliloquy at the opening of Act 3 Scene 2, the thirty-three lines she speaks alone while waiting for night and for Romeo, is usually read for its desire: a bride commanding the sun's horses to hurry so that darkness will bring her husband to her chamber. That reading is true and insufficient. The speech's longing is not merely described by its language; the longing is performed by its metre. The lines strain against the iambic pentameter that contains them, crowding extra syllables into the measure, front-loading their stresses into commands, spilling their syntax over line endings, and then checking that speed with spondaic brakes and heavy monosyllables, so that the verse alternately gallops and pulls up short exactly as impatience itself alternates between urging time forward and enduring its refusal to move. Sound and sense do not run in parallel in this speech. Sound is the sense, arriving first.

![Juliet alone at her window urging the sun's horses toward nightfall - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-109.webp)

The verdict of this close reading can be stated plainly at the outset: the gallop in "Gallop apace" is real, it is measurable, and it is built. It is not a metaphor pasted onto the speech by enthusiastic readers, and it is not an accident of performance that a gifted actor might supply or withhold. The scansion shows a line-by-line engineering of tempo: trochaic openings that strike before the expected beat, anapaestic ripples that triple the pace inside a duple measure, feminine endings that let lines overshoot their measure the way a runner overshoots a stride, and enjambments that refuse the ear any resting place. Against that forward pressure the speech sets its brakes: the spondee packed into "close curtain", the hammered monosyllables of "Come, night, come, Romeo", the checked and heavy movement around "Hood my unmanned blood". Desire in this speech has a tempo curve, rising, racing, braking, rising again, and the curve is written into the verse before any actor touches it. To scan the speech is to watch impatience think.

The placement of the speech sharpens the effect. The solo opens a scene that will reverse everything in it: within two hundred lines the same voice that commands the sun's horses will be keening over Tybalt's corpse and Romeo's banishment in the broken music of the news scene. Shakespeare sets the play's most complete rhythmic achievement at the top of the slope, at the last moment when Juliet's world is still arranged as she wishes it, married, expectant, the night pledged to her. The verse does not know what the audience knows, that the gallop is racing toward a wreck, but the form carries a related knowledge in its bones: a tempo this strained, a measure this overshot, a breath this extended cannot be a resting state, in verse any more than in a life. The prosody is the tragedy's clock rendered audible, and it is already ticking faster than the world it measures can survive.

That claim needs the evidence set out in full, because prosodic claims are the easiest in criticism to assert and the hardest to prove. The sections that follow therefore proceed from the marked lines outward: first a scansion of the opening movement, laid out in the artifact table and read closely; then the mechanics of triple time inside a duple metre; then the imperatives and the apostrophe to Phoebus that load the verse with command; then the enjambment that lets syntax outrun the line; then the brakes that keep the gallop from becoming mere hurry; then a working method for hearing all of this silently, on the page; then the genuine critical disagreement about whether such music lives in the text or in the ear that receives it, adjudicated; and finally the verdict on how sound and sense fuse. The desire the speech expresses has its own full treatment elsewhere in this series, and the scene the speech opens has its scene-level reading as well. The territory here is narrower and, for this speech, deeper: the speech as sound.

Scansion is given in the plainest notation available: stressed syllables stand in capitals, and the line is allowed to keep the eleven syllables that several of these lines actually contain. The table is the article's evidence base. Every claim made later about tempo, pressure, and braking can be traced back to a row of it.

Three facts stand out from the marked lines before any interpretation begins. The first is how rarely the verse sits still in its iambic ground during this opening movement. Of the eight lines, three open with a trochee rather than an iamb, two end on feminine endings that add an eleventh syllable, one carries a spondee, one is enjambed so hard that its grammar does not complete until the following line, and one contains a word, "immediately", whose five syllables sprawl across the space the metre allots to roughly two feet. A reader counting only deviations might call the passage irregular. A reader counting their distribution notices something more precise: the deviations cluster at the points of greatest urgency, at the commands, at the naming of the horses, at the wish for the sentence to arrive before the line does. The irregularity has an address. It lives where the impatience lives.

One row of the table conceals a small drama of tempo that the eye passes over. The second line imagines the sun's driver as "such a wagoner" as Phaethon would make, and a wagoner is a plodder by trade, the slowest professional on the road, his team walking its freight to market. Juliet's fantasy performs a substitution of drivers: the plodding wagoner is to be replaced, in the whip-hand, by the boy who lost control. The verse scores the substitution. The wagoner's line is the opening movement's most relaxed, its pyrrhic "such a" skimming two light syllables, its feminine ending trailing off on the driver's three syllables; the very next line quickens as Phaethon's name ripples through it and the whip is named. Plod and gallop stand in adjacent lines, assigned to the two drivers between whom the speech is choosing, and the choice the words describe, reckless speed over professional pace, is executed by the metre before the sentence that describes it has even finished. Details of this order are the reason the scansion table, and not the paraphrase, is the article's evidence: no summary of the wish mentions drivers at all, yet the verse has cast the wish as a contest of drivers and settled it in rhythm.

The second fact is the feminine endings. Lines 1, 2, and 4 all overshoot the pentameter's ten-syllable frame, closing on an unstressed syllable after the final stress: "steeds", "wagoner", "immediately". In the abstract, a feminine ending is a small relaxation of the measure, a dying fall. In this passage the effect is nearly the opposite. Each overshoot arrives attached to a vehicle of speed: the horses themselves, the wagoner who drives them, and the adverb that demands instant arrival. The line cannot quite contain the thing it names, and the excess syllable is the formal trace of that failure to contain. The measure is being asked to hold more motion than it was built for, and the seams show exactly where the motion presses hardest.

The third fact is the sixth line. "That runaways' eyes may wink; and Romeo" is not a sentence, not even a clause; it is the front half of a construction whose verb, "Leap", waits in the next line. The ear, following the syntax, is denied the small satisfaction of a completed unit at the line ending and is pulled forward into "Leap to these arms" before any pause can register. Enjambment of this strength is the verse equivalent of a horse refusing to check at a fence. It will be treated at length in its own section below; here it is enough to mark its position, inside the first eight lines, as proof that the forward pressure governs the speech from its opening breath and not merely in its famous later phrases.

### What does a scansion mark that a paraphrase leaves out?

A scansion marks the distribution of weight and time inside the line: which syllables carry stress, where the measure is strained or slackened, and where the syntax refuses the line's boundary. A paraphrase keeps only the meaning. The scansion therefore preserves the one thing the speech is finally about, the experience of duration, which paraphrase discards entirely.

René Weis's edition matters to this table in a way worth stating once. The early texts of the play, the First Quarto of 1597 and the Second Quarto of 1599 on which Weis's text rests, differ in punctuation and in small readings throughout the speech, and punctuation in a verse text is a tempo instruction: every comma an editor prints or removes lengthens or shortens the pause a reading voice takes. The scansion above follows Weis's comparatively light pointing, which lets the lines run. An edition that pointed the opening movement more heavily would slow the gallop without changing a single word, a fact that will matter when the critics' disagreement is reached. Prosody is never quite separable from editing, and the honest close reading says so.

The opening movement, then, in eight lines, establishes the speech's whole rhythmic argument in miniature: strike early, overshoot the measure, refuse the stop, brake hard once at the curtain, and go again. What remains is to take the mechanisms one at a time and show how each works, how far each extends through the full thirty-three lines, and what each contributes to the fusion of sound and sense that the verdict claims.

## Why the Verse Gallops: Triple Time Inside Iambic Pentameter

A horse's gallop is a three-beat gait with a moment of suspension, and the oldest trick in English prosody for rendering it is to let triple rhythms surface inside a duple measure. Iambic pentameter moves in twos: five alternations of slack and stress, a walking pace, the natural gait of English formal verse. The speech's subject is a team of horses being whipped toward their stable, and Shakespeare scores that subject by repeatedly disturbing the walking measure with three-beat figures, so the ear keeps hearing a faster gait break through the slower one that officially governs the line. The effect does not depend on any single substitution. It depends on accumulation, and the accumulation begins in the first line.

"Gallop apace" itself is the first exhibit. The line opens with a trochee, GAL-lop, a stressed syllable followed by an unstressed one, reversing the iambic expectation at the very moment the command to gallop is given. A trochaic opening is not a triple rhythm, but it does something the argument needs: it puts the stress ahead of the beat, so the line starts by lunging. The ear, braced for the iamb's rise, receives the weight early, and that prematurity is experienced as eagerness. The verse cannot wait for its own downbeat. Two syllables later the line supplies its true triple figure in "fiery-footed": FIER-y-FOOT-ed, a stressed syllable trailed by an unstressed one, twice over, so the compound epithet rocks in a dactylic tumble across the line's second half. Horses in this line have fiery feet, and the feet of the verse oblige by multiplying: where the pentameter promises five stresses, the line crowds in the extra syllabic material of speed, eleven syllables sounding against a ten-syllable contract.

The triple pressure continues through the mythological machinery of the next three lines. "Phaethon", three syllables with the stress on the first, ripples across the middle of its line like a ripple across water, and "immediately" closes the fourth line with five syllables where the measure has room for a falling pair. None of these figures is an anapaest in the textbook sense of two light syllables and a stress, though the speech supplies those too as it proceeds; the point is broader and more audible. Again and again the verse fits three syllables into the span the iambic contract prices at two, and the surplus is felt as acceleration. A measure can absorb one such substitution as ornament. A passage that stacks them, line after line, at the exact moments when the speaker demands more speed, has made substitution into statement.

The system being strained here is the play's blank verse itself, the unrhymed pentameter that carries nearly all of the tragedy's serious speech, and [the full account of how that system works across the play](/2024/12/06/blank-verse-romeo-and-juliet/) shows how disciplined the norm is elsewhere: shared lines fitted to the measure, caesuras placed like joints, the metre a reliable floor under the drama. Juliet's speech derives its meaning from that reliability. Because the audience's ear has been trained by two acts of verse to expect the walking measure, every overshoot registers. In a looser versification the gallop would be invisible. Shakespeare first builds a metronome and then writes a passage that keeps outrunning it, and the outrunning is legible only against the tick.

George T. Wright, whose Shakespeare's Metrical Art remains the standard account of this interplay, gives the phenomenon its precise frame. For Wright, the music of Shakespeare's verse is the product of a continuing negotiation between the abstract metrical pattern, which the audience absorbs within minutes and thereafter expects, and the actual stresses of the spoken line, which comply with the pattern, lean against it, or briefly override it. Meaning lives in the negotiation. A substitution is not a blemish on the metre or a licence ignoring it; the substitution is heard against the metre, and its expressive force is exactly the distance between what was expected and what arrived. On Wright's account the opening of "Gallop apace" is a compact lesson in the method: the trochee is impatient because the iamb was expected, the feminine endings overshoot because the measure was counted, and the enjambment hurries because the line ending promised rest. Remove the norm and the effects evaporate. The gallop is a relation, not a property, and it is measurable because the norm is stable enough to measure against.

### Why does the gallop need the regular metre it keeps breaking?

The gallop needs the regular metre because rhythmic expression in verse is relational: a hurried line is hurried only against an expected pace. Shakespeare's pentameter supplies that expectation, a steady walking measure the audience absorbs early. Every substitution in the speech, trochee, anapaest, feminine ending, registers as speed precisely because the underlying beat stays audible beneath the disturbance.

One further mechanic deserves naming before the imperatives are reached, because it operates quietly through the whole speech: the pyrrhic, a foot of two unstressed syllables, as in "such a" in the second line and "to the" in the third. A pyrrhic does not accelerate a line by adding material; it accelerates by removing weight, letting the voice skim across two syllables in the time of one beat and land harder on the stress that follows. Skim and strike, skim and strike: the alternation is the rhythm of hooves leaving and hitting ground. The speech's speed is engineered as much by these lightened passages as by its celebrated crowded ones, and a scansion that marks only stresses will miss half the machinery. The full pattern is a collaboration between surplus and lightness, lines that carry too many syllables and feet that carry too little weight, both conspiring to keep the verse off balance in the forward direction.

It is worth pausing on how unusual the concentration is. Speeches of erotic longing elsewhere in the play tend toward metrical richness of a different kind: the balcony scene trades in shared lines and sonnet echoes, forms of reciprocity. The wedding-night speech is a solo, and its prosody is the prosody of a single will pressing against a resistant medium, time itself. The triple rhythms are not decoration laid over that pressure. They are the pressure, transcribed into the only notation the verse has: more syllables than the measure wants, arriving sooner than the beat allows.

## The Stress Budget: Substitutions Counted Across Thirty-Three Lines

Impressions of tempo become evidence when the features are counted across the whole speech, and the count, on the scansion defended in this article, tells a structured story. The method is simple: for each line, mark the presence of the speech's four disturbance types, trochaic or imperative openings, surplus syllables by feminine ending or internal crowding, pyrrhic lightening, and enjambment strong enough to defer a clause's completion. Tallied by movement, the distribution is anything but even. The opening movement, the eight lines of the gallop scansion, carries disturbances in nearly every line, several lines carrying two at once: the speech begins at maximum rhythmic pressure. The middle movement, the summons to night, redistributes the budget: enjambment persists, the surplus syllables recede, and stress-density rises in their place, the spondee and the hammered monosyllables spending the budget on weight instead of speed. The great sentence of the stars spends almost nothing within its lines, which run nearly regular, and everything across its boundaries, its four deferred endings the whole investment. The final movement, the mansion and the simile, is the speech's most regular verse by a wide margin, its few disturbances gentle, its lines pattern-true. The budget declines across the speech in a long decrescendo of strain, from the whipping of the horses to the calendar patience of the child, and the decline is itself the expressive fact: the verse spends its energy exactly as the speaker spends hers, in a blaze of command that exhausts itself into reckoning and simile.

Two lessons follow from the tally. The first concerns the word "irregular". A passage in which a third of the lines depart from the pattern in some marked way might be called irregular by a census of single features, and Tarlinskaja's caution, addressed in the critics' section, draws part of its force from such censuses. But the census cannot see what the distribution shows: the departures are sorted by sense with a consistency no random process produces. Disturbance types are not scattered; they are assigned, speed-devices to the driving, weight-devices to the covering, overflow to the imagining, regularity to the reckoning. Prosodic analysis that stops at counting features has measured the speech's weather and missed its climate. The second lesson concerns the regular lines, which a deviation-hunting method treats as neutral filler. In this speech the regular lines are load-bearing: the pattern-true lines of the simile and the star sentence carry the prophecy and the patience, the two moments when the verse's calm is the meaning. A full prosodic reading budgets the regularities as carefully as the disturbances, because the speech's argument is conducted by their alternation.

One category in the budget sits between disturbance and regularity and deserves its line in the ledger: the hovering accent, Wright's suspended beat, of which the speech's clearest instance is the spondee at "close curtain". Hovering is neither strain nor calm but a momentary thickening of the beat itself, two stresses sharing one metrical position, and its expressive assignment in the speech is consistently to the objects of handling, the curtain, the mansion, the robes, the tangible furniture of the wedding night that hands can touch. Where the feet crowd with syllables, the speech is reaching; where the beat hovers, the speech is grasping. The taxonomy matters because it keeps the analysis honest: not every marked event in the passage is a speed event, and a reading that heard only acceleration would miss the counter-system by which the verse keeps registering the physical world the desire wants to possess. The budget, fully kept, shows a speech spending on reach and on grasp in alternation, which is to say a speech whose rhythm has the structure of desire itself rather than the structure of a metronome set fast.

The decrescendo also answers a question the speech's fame tends to obscure: why the ending of the solo, the child and the robes, lands with such disproportionate melancholy. Endings gather force from contrast, and the simile's smoothness is the terminal point of a thirty-line process the ear has been following without needing to name it. The verse has been slowing, gear by gear, since the horses; the simile is where the slowing arrives. Grief in this speech does not interrupt the music. Grief is the music's destination, reached by decelerations so gradual that the listener discovers the arrival only in retrospect, which is, not incidentally, the temporal structure of the whole tragedy in miniature.

## Imperatives and Apostrophe: Commanding the Sky at Speed

Count the speech's openings and a second mechanism comes into focus. "Gallop apace". "Spread thy close curtain". "Leap to these arms". "Come, civil night". "Hood my unmanned blood". "Come, night, come, Romeo". "Give me my Romeo". The speech is built from commands, and commands in English verse have a prosodic signature: the imperative verb carries its stress at the front, so a line or clause that opens with an imperative opens with weight, GAL-lop, SPREAD, LEAP, COME, HOOD, GIVE. The trochaic openings catalogued in the scansion are not scattered ornaments. They are the grammatical shape of commanding, made audible. Juliet is ordering the cosmos, and the verse puts the stress where the order falls, at the head of the utterance, again and again, until the rhythm of the speech becomes the rhythm of repeated insistence.

Apostrophe, the rhetorical figure of address to an absent or inanimate hearer, is the speech's enabling device: the steeds, Phoebus, night, and the absent Romeo are all summoned by name and spoken to as if present and obedient. The figure has its own full treatment in this series, in [the study of apostrophe and address in the soliloquies](/2025/02/14/apostrophe-soliloquy-romeo-juliet/), and the present article takes from that ground only what the prosody needs. What the prosody needs is this: apostrophe licenses the imperative. A speaker can command only what the fiction of address has made present, and so the speech's summonses and its orders are one gesture, the naming and the driving fused. Each new address restarts the engine. Night is apostrophized three times under three descriptions, civil night, the sober-suited matron, gentle and loving and black-browed night, and each fresh naming carries a fresh imperative stress at its head, so the verse's pulse of command never has to sustain itself across a long period; it is re-struck, like a bell, at every turn.

The Phoebus address concentrates the mechanism into the speech's first movement and repays exact attention, because the mythology is doing prosodic work. Phoebus Apollo drives the sun's chariot across the sky; his steeds are the horses of the day. Juliet's prayer is that those horses be driven home at reckless speed, "such a wagoner / As Phaethon would whip you to the west". Phaethon is the son who took the chariot and could not hold the horses, who nearly burned the world and fell for it. The allusion is famous for its recklessness as desire: Juliet wants the day ended at the pace of a fatal joyride, consequences excluded from the wish. Its prosodic dimension is less often marked. The name Phaethon is the line's triple ripple, the three syllables that quicken the centre of the third line, so the boy who drove too fast is present in the verse as a burst of speed in the metre itself. The mythology is not illustrated by the rhythm from outside. The rhythm pronounces the mythology, syllable by syllable, at the moment of reference. Verification of the reference, which the brief for this article rightly flags, cuts in the prosody's favour: the steeds are Phoebus's, the whip-hand imagined is Phaethon's, and the line that names the whip-hand is the line whose syllables crowd and hurry.

The imperatives also govern the speech's largest rhythm, its alternation of urging and pleading. Commands to the steeds and to night are maximal, cosmic, addressed to powers that cannot comply; the speech's later commands shade toward entreaty, "Give me my Romeo", where the imperative form survives but the power relation has collapsed into begging. The stress pattern tracks the shading. The early imperatives strike at line openings with trochaic force. The later ones arrive mid-line and mid-sentence, carried along by enjambment, their initial stress absorbed into a longer period, so the verse's commanding pulse weakens into a flowing wish at exactly the point where the content passes from driving the sun to imagining the husband. Form grades the rhetoric. A reading that attends only to the words will register confidence giving way to longing; a reading that scans registers the same curve as stress migrating from the line's head to its middle, command dissolving into current.

A negative fact about the imperatives completes their portrait: the speech contains no prohibition. Every command in the thirty-three lines is an injunction to do, to gallop, spread, leap, come, hood, give, take; nowhere does Juliet command anything to stop, refrain, or be still. The verse of the play elsewhere is rich in prohibition, the Prince's edicts, the Friar's cautions, old Capulet's furious forbiddings, and prohibition has its own prosody, the checked line, the negative particle striking like a bar. By withholding that mode entirely, the soliloquy keeps its commanding energy single in kind: pure instigation, a will that can accelerate the world and cover it but cannot conceive of ordering it to halt, because halting is the one thing the speaker is suffering from. Even the wish for darkness is phrased as an arrival to be hastened, night commanded to come, never the day commanded to stop. The stress system follows the grammar: prohibitions brake lines wherever they occur in the play, and a speech without prohibitions is a speech whose imperative engine has no reverse gear, which is a formal definition of the impatience the whole article has been tracking.

### How does the apostrophe to Phoebus change the verse's speed?

The apostrophe changes the speed by installing the speech's triple rhythms at its point of reference: the name Phaethon ripples across its line, the steeds are addressed as a present team to be whipped, and the imperative mode the address licenses front-loads stress into line openings, so the mythology of reckless driving is enacted by the metre.

There is a counter-current, and honesty requires it before the enjambment section. Not every imperative in the speech accelerates. "Hood my unmanned blood" opens a passage whose movement checks and darkens, and "Spread thy close curtain" contains the speech's heaviest spondee. Command as such is neutral; the verse decides, command by command, whether the order speeds the line or brakes it, and the braking commands are those whose object is concealment: the curtain drawn, the blood hooded, the eyes of runaways closed. Where Juliet orders the world to hurry, the verse hurries. Where she orders it to hide, the verse thickens. The imperative system is thus a map of the speech's double wish, for speed and for cover, and the prosody draws the map without a word of commentary. That double wish is the doorway into the brakes, by way of the force that most resists all braking, the speech's enjambment.

## Enjambment: Syntax That Outruns the Line

Enjambment, the running of a sentence across a line ending without pause, is the speech's second engine, and it works by withholding the one rest the verse form regularly offers. A line of pentameter is a unit of breath and of expectation; when the grammar completes at the line's end, the ear collects a small payment of closure. When the grammar refuses to complete, the payment is deferred and the listener is carried forward on credit. "Gallop apace" defers payment at its most charged moments. The sixth line ends on "and Romeo", the beloved's name suspended at the very edge of the measure with its verb still to come: the name itself is made to hang in the air for the duration of a line break before "Leap to these arms" releases it. The beloved arrives in the syntax the way he is to arrive in the chamber, delayed by a held breath and then suddenly. No paraphrase can keep that. A prose summary of the wish, that Romeo should leap to her arms unseen, contains the content and loses the event, which is a name made to wait at a line's end for its action.

The speech's longest sentence is its grandest enjambment structure and the climax of the mechanism. "Give me my Romeo; and when I shall die, / Take him and cut him out in little stars, / And he will make the face of heaven so fine / That all the world will be in love with night / And pay no worship to the garish sun." The sentence runs across four line endings, gathering subordinate clauses the way the earlier injunctions gathered commands, and its verse music differs from the opening gallop in a way the scansion makes audible: the lines here are more nearly regular, the stresses falling into the iambic pattern with fewer violent substitutions, yet the passage feels swifter, not slower. The paradox resolves once the two engines are distinguished. The opening movement accelerates by disturbing the measure. This passage accelerates by refusing to stop at the measure's boundaries. Disturbance is speed within the line; enjambment is speed across lines, the long-breathed momentum of a period that will not break. Juliet's desire has two tempi, the staccato of command and the legato of imagining, and the verse supplies both, the second more dangerous than the first because it carries her furthest, from the wedding night to her own death and Romeo's apotheosis among the stars, in one unbroken outpouring.

The textual crux buried in that sentence belongs to the editor's section of the argument but must be flagged where the lines are quoted: the early texts divide between "when he shall die" and "when I shall die", and the difference is not decorative. On the second reading, which the note in Weis's edition weighs, Juliet imagines her own death as the occasion of Romeo's transformation into stars, a lover's extravagance that also happens to be the plot's exact prophecy. The prosody is untouched by the choice, the same smooth enjambed flow carrying either reading, which is itself a critical datum: at the speech's most prophetic moment the verse music has settled into its most regular gait, as if the future, alone among the speech's subjects, did not need to be hurried because it was certain.

The breath arithmetic can be stated exactly, and it is startling. The thirty-three lines contain eight sentences. Eight of the lines end a sentence outright; the remaining twenty-five run on into their successors in some degree, and the average sentence spans a little over four lines. A speaking voice must therefore plan the speech not line by line but period by period, husbanding breath across spans of twenty, thirty, forty syllables without the rest that a full stop provides, and the planning itself is expressive: long-breathed syntax is the physiology of urgency in a speaker who cannot afford to pause because pausing is waiting. Set beside the scene that follows, where the Nurse's news arrives in shattered fragments and Juliet's responses contract to cries and questions, the solo's great periods measure what is about to be lost: the last sustained, self-governed rhythm in Juliet's play, whole sentences built like bridges across the line endings, before the world's events begin breaking her verse into pieces. The enjambment is thus elegiac in retrospect. It is the sound of a mind still able to hold a four-line arc of intention intact, and the tragedy will spend three acts taking that ability away.

Caesura placement completes the picture. Where the speech does pause inside its lines, the pauses are rhetorical pivots, not rests: the semicolon after "lodging", the comma after "curtain", the halted address "Come, civil night" before the description dilates. The mid-line pauses in this speech function like the gathering of a horse before a fence, a collected instant that makes the ensuing movement faster by contrast. A voice performing the speech discovers that the text permits almost no full stops of the breath in its first twenty-five lines except at the ends of its great periods, and that the periods end not with closure but with images: night, stars, the garish sun. Breath, syntax, and line are three systems in this speech, deliberately set out of phase, so that at any given moment at least one of them is mid-stride. The ear never finds all three at rest together until the speech turns toward the Nurse and the waiting breaks. That out-of-phase engineering is the deepest sense in which the verse gallops: not in any single line, but in the refusal of the systems ever to settle simultaneously.

### Where is the speech's strongest enjambment, and what does it hold back?

The strongest enjambment falls at the sixth line, where the sentence breaks after "and Romeo" and the verb "Leap" waits in the next line. The beloved's name is suspended at the measure's edge for the length of a line break, so the syntax makes the name itself wait, enacting the delay the whole speech exists to protest.

Set against the whole play, the density is the distinction. Enjambment is everywhere in Shakespeare, but its expressive assignment varies: in the Friar's speeches it serves meditative overflow, thought exceeding the line because thought is copious; in Romeo's early speeches it serves the spilling of a borrowed rhetoric. In Juliet's wedding-night speech it serves one thing only, the refusal of delay. Every deferred closure is a small formal protest against the interval between desire and its object. The speech's grammar behaves like its speaker: it will not wait for the line to finish before reaching for what it wants. That is the fusion thesis in its syntactic form, and it prepares the necessary correction, because a speech that only accelerated would be a monotone of hurry. The verse also brakes, and the braking is where its maturity lies.

## The Brakes: Spondees, Monosyllables, and the Checked Gallop

The fifth line of the scansion table holds the counter-evidence: "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night". Its centre is a spondee, CLOSE CUR-tain, two stressed syllables jammed together where the iambic pattern prices a slack and a stress. A spondee does not speed a line; it loads it. The curtain Juliet asks night to spread is heavy, and the verse makes the request heavy to say, the tongue striking two consecutive weights. Around that centre the line crowds six strong stresses into ten syllables, the densest stress-packing in the opening movement. This is the speech's first brake, and its position is the argument: at the very moment the gallop is established, the verse demonstrates that it can also thicken, darken, and slow. The wish for concealment, night's curtain drawn over the marriage bed, arrives in the prosody as increased gravity. Speed and cover are the speech's two desires, and the metre serves each with opposite means, surplus syllables for the one, surplus stress for the other.

The monosyllables work the same side of the street. "Come, night, come, Romeo, come, thou day in night" is built almost entirely from single syllables, and monosyllabic writing in pentameter has a double nature that the speech exploits: the words are short, but because nearly every one can bear a stress, the line becomes a row of separate weights, each to be placed rather than skimmed. The effect is not hurry but hammering, insistence slowed into blows. The threefold "come" is the speech's most famous sound, and its music is the music of a hand striking a door repeatedly, not of a horse at speed. Impatience, the speech insists at the level of sound, is not only the wish for time to move faster. It is also the experience of time refusing, and the verse gives that experience its own rhythm, the stopped rhythm of blows on an unyielding surface. The day-and-night patterning that the line's central paradox names, Romeo as day lodged in night, belongs to [the play's wider geography of darkness and light](/2024/02/02/day-and-night-imagery-romeo-juliet/); the concern here is only that the paradox is delivered in the speech's heaviest syllables, the oxymoron weighted like a stone.

The passage around "Hood my unmanned blood, bating in my cheeks" completes the braking system. Its verse movement is the speech's most checked: the falconry imperative opens the period, and then the syntax subordinates and qualifies, "till strange love grow bold, / Think true love acted simple modesty", the lines enjambing gently, the stresses distributing themselves without the earlier crowding, as if the verse had drawn breath and lowered its voice for the confession of modesty that the passage performs. A speaker who has been commanding suns and steeds now asks to be hooded like a hawk, hidden until boldness comes, and the prosody lowers with the request. This modulation is the strongest internal evidence against a crude version of the fusion thesis. If the verse simply raced whenever Juliet desired, the passage of concealment and maidenly reserve would race too, since the desire has not abated. Instead the verse distinguishes among the desire's phases: urging, hammering, hiding. The music is not a tempo marking stamped on the speech. It is a continuously responsive medium, and its responsiveness is what makes the racing passages legible as racing: they are racing relative to the speech's own calms.

Harley Granville-Barker, writing in the Prefaces to Shakespeare from a lifetime of production, heard this responsiveness and made tempo the key to the play in performance: the tragedy, in his account, must move, its scenes pressed forward, its verse spoken with a speed that respects the verse's own momentum rather than a ponderous reverence. The wedding-night speech is the test case his argument implies. Played at uniform reverence, the speech dies: the gallop passages need their lift, the imperatives their strike, and equally the hooding passage needs its checked and lowered movement, or the return of speed afterward has nothing to return from. Granville-Barker's practical wisdom and the scansion agree: the speech's music is a curve, and the curve is the meaning. A performance that flattens the curve into either breathlessness or solemnity has edited the text as surely as a cut.

The brakes also answer, in advance, the sceptical objection that galloping verse is a critic's hallucination. Hallucinations do not usually come equipped with their own counterweights, systematically placed. The spondee falls on the curtain, the monosyllabic hammering on the summons, the checked period on the hooding: three distinct slowing devices, each at the passage where the sense turns from speed to cover, from command to plea, from boldness to modesty. A pattern that predicts its own exceptions, and places them semantically, is design. The design is the verdict's foundation, and the remaining questions are how a reader with no performance to attend can gain access to it, and whether the critics are right to locate it in the text at all.

## Seven Line-Endings on "Night": The Tolling Refrain

One statistic governs the speech's sound from beneath, and it is available to any reader with a pencil: of the thirty-three lines Juliet speaks alone, seven end on the same word, "night". The word closes the fifth line, the ninth, the tenth, the seventeenth, the eighteenth, the twentieth, and the twenty-fourth, a tolling recurrence at the position of maximum emphasis that verse affords, the line's final stress. No other word in the speech is given anything like this placement. "Romeo" ends a line once, suspended at the great enjambment; "stars" and "sun" close the star sentence's lines in their turn; but night returns and returns, the refrain the whole solo beats against. Prosodically, the word is built for the office: a monosyllable with a long vowel and a closing consonant that can be struck and left ringing, a bell-note of a word, heavy enough to bear a line's final stress and short enough to leave a silence after it. Each recurrence lands at a different tempo, in the gallop's overshoot, in the hammered summons, in the flowing sentence, and the sameness of the note against the variety of the approaches is the speech's ground-bass, the fixed point by which all its accelerations and brakings are measured.

The refrain's distribution sketches the speech's emotional map in a single column. In the opening movement night is the destination of the horses, the thing the gallop exists to reach. In the middle movement night is the addressee, summoned, dressed, pleaded with, the word tolling three times in four lines as the summons intensifies. In the star sentence night becomes the medium of Romeo's glory, the face of heaven's rival, worshipped in the sun's place. Destination, person, element: the word's grammatical roles ascend while its sound stays identical, and the verse music holds the identity so the ear can measure the ascent. A prose paraphrase, which would vary the word for elegance, destroys the instrument; the speech insists on the one syllable because the tolling is the meaning, time's bell counting down the day in identical strokes while everything else in the verse strains, crowds, and hurries. Impatience needs something that will not hurry, and the refrain supplies it: night itself, arriving at its own pace, named at the line's end again and again like a clock that desire cannot bully.

The tolling word has a partner, and the partnership completes the speech's percussion section. Where "night" tolls at line endings, "come" strikes at openings and outbursts: "Come, civil night", "Come, night, come, Romeo, come", "Come, gentle night, come, loving, black-browed night". The verb of arrival is the speech's downbeat as the noun of darkness is its resonance, attack and decay, the two halves of a bell's note distributed across the solo. The pattern is audible even inattentively, which is the mark of a refrain system rather than a local figure: an audience that retained nothing else would retain the alternation of summons and nightfall, command and answer, struck through the speech like a pulse. The counts cooperate with the design. The summons is struck most densely in the middle movement, where the addresses multiply, while the tolling word spreads evenly across the whole solo, as if the hour were being marked at regular intervals beneath the accelerating pleas. Form could hardly state the situation more economically: the pleas speed up; the hour does not.

This is the deepest of the speech's fusions, and the simplest. The verse's most conspicuous sound-event is a word that means the speech's object; the refrain is a prosodic fact and a semantic fact in a single syllable, repeated seven times at the strongest position the form possesses. Analysts of the speech who count its images will find night catalogued among them, and analysts who count its stresses will find the same word at the line ends, and the two counts are one count. The gallop has been the article's subject, but the tolling is its ground: the horses race, the curtain falls heavy, the sentence flows, and under all of it, at the end of line after line, the bell of the single syllable sounds the hour the speaker is waiting for.

## Hearing Verse Music on the Page: A Working Method

The brief for any prosodic reading carries a standing embarrassment, and this article's brief names it honestly: verse music happens in time, in a voice, and the page is silent. How is the gallop to be heard by a reader holding a book? The embarrassment is real but answerable, and the answer is a method rather than a mystery. Scansion is a score. Like a musical score, it does not contain the sound; it contains instructions from which a competent performer, including the silent reader performing inwardly, can reconstruct the sound with considerable fidelity. The method has four operations, each simple, and their combination recovers nearly everything the preceding sections have claimed.

The first operation is to mark the stresses, as the gallop scansion does, without worrying at first about feet. Stress in English is not a subtle quantity: a reader speaking the line aloud, even under the breath, will land on GAL-lop, PACE, FIER, FOOT, STEEDS without instruction, because the language's own phonology puts the weight there. The second operation is to count syllables against the pentameter's expectation of ten, and to note every line that carries eleven or crowds three syllables into a foot's span, since surplus is the speech's primary signature of hurry. The third operation is to mark the line endings where the sentence refuses to stop, the enjambments, and to read through them without the pause that print invites, so the deferred closures accumulate their pressure. The fourth operation is the one silent reading most often skips: to attend to the weight of single syllables in the monosyllabic runs, placing each "come" as a separate blow rather than sliding across the words as print encourages. Readers who keep a working copy of the speech beside a notebook of markings, the kind of study record [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) exists to hold, discover that four passes through thirty-three lines permanently change the inner ear's hearing of them: the music, once marked, stays heard.

A single worked line shows the four operations cooperating. Take the fifth line: "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night". The stresses, marked first, fall on SPREAD, CLOSE, CUR, LOVE, FORM, NIGHT, six weights where the pattern prices five. The syllable count comes next: ten syllables, no surplus, so the line's disturbance is not crowding but packing, the discovery that directs attention to the spondee. The line ending is then tested: the sentence completes there, but the completion is of a command, and the ear, denied an enjambment's pull, feels the stop as a door closing, the curtain itself. The monosyllabic weigh-in completes the pass: "Spread thy close" is three successive strikes before the line's first run of lighter syllables, and the inner voice, placing those strikes honestly, has already slowed to the tempo of drawing a heavy curtain across a stage. Four operations, perhaps ninety seconds, and the line's music has been recovered from print with nothing but attention and a pencil. What works for the fifth line works, with the emphases redistributed, for all thirty-three.

The method rests on a claim about reading that should be made explicit, because the critics' disagreement turns on it. Silent reading of verse is not the absence of performance; it is performance conducted by the inner voice at reduced volume, with the same stresses, groupings, and tempi executed in imagination. Readers differ in the skill of that inner performance as orchestral players differ, but the score constrains all of them. No competent inner performance of "Gallop apace" can make its first line plod, because the trochee, the crowded epithet, and the eleventh syllable forbid plodding from any voice that honours the stresses the language assigns. The page is silent the way a score is silent: conditionally, pending a reader, and with the sound specified to a degree that makes most realizations recognizably the same event.

Two practical corollaries follow for the study of this speech. The first is that tempo markings belong in the margin: where the verse crowds, the inner voice should be allowed its natural acceleration, and where the spondees and monosyllables fall, the voice should feel the check as a physical event, a hand on the rein. Reading the speech at a deliberately uniform pace, as metronomic recitation does, is not neutrality; it is a performance decision that erases the text's most deliberate effects. The second corollary concerns editions. Punctuation is the editor's tempo marking, and a reader comparing Weis's light pointing of the opening movement with a more heavily pointed edition is comparing two performances fixed in print. The words are identical; the music differs. Prosodic literacy includes knowing that the score has editors as well as a composer, and that the gallop's exact gait, though not its existence, is partly an editorial fact.

### Does silent reading lose the verse music beyond recovery?

Silent reading loses nothing that scansion cannot restore, because scansion converts the line's timing and stress into visible marks from which the inner voice reconstructs the performance. What silent reading genuinely loses is only the social fact of a shared tempo in a theatre. The music's structure, its accelerations and brakes, survives the page intact.

This is also the place to concede the method's limit, since the verdict's credibility depends on conceding it cleanly. Scansion can demonstrate where a line crowds, slackens, strikes early, or refuses to stop. It cannot demonstrate that any historical audience, or any individual modern reader, felt impatience at those points; feeling is not a metrical quantity. What the method establishes is narrower and stronger: that the verse is engineered with a consistency that matches, feature for feature, the emotional curve the words describe, and that the match is too systematic, across too many independent parameters, to be coincidence. The gallop is in the engineering. Whether a given ear receives it is a question about ears. The engineering is a question about the text, and the text answers it.

## Wright Against Attridge: Is the Gallop in the Verse or in the Ear?

The disagreement this article must adjudicate is genuine and consequential, because the fusion thesis stands or falls with it. On one side stands George T. Wright's account, already drawn on above: verse music is a real transaction between an abstract metrical pattern, which the poem establishes and the audience internalizes, and the line's actual rhythmic life, which fulfils, strains, or resists the pattern. On Wright's view the gallop is in the verse in the only sense that matters: the substitutions are objectively present, the norm against which they register is objectively established by the play's own practice, and the expressive values, eagerness in the trochee, overshoot in the feminine ending, are reliable consequences of that objective relation. Wright adds the refinement of the hovering accent, his term for the moment when two adjacent syllables approach equal weight and the stress hovers between them, as at the crowded centre of "close curtain": the verse can suspend its own decision about where the beat falls, and the suspension is itself an expressive event, a held instant in the gallop.

On the other side stands Derek Attridge, whose work on poetic rhythm argues that rhythm is not a property a text possesses but an event a reader or performer enacts. On Attridge's account, the beat of English verse is a psychological pulse that a competent performance realizes, and the same line can sustain more than one rhythmic realization; scansion describes not the line's rhythm but one possible performance of it, hardened into notation. Applied to "Gallop apace", the objection runs: the gallop is not in the verse; the gallop is what a reader, primed by the title and the horses, chooses to enact, and a different reader could voice the identical lines in a measured, processional tempo without violating any stress the language fixes. The feminine endings and trochees are facts, but their conversion into speed is an act of interpretation, not a discovery. Marina Tarlinskaja's statistical work lends the sceptical side a different kind of support: her counts across the canon show the verse of this early play to be, by her measures, relatively regular in its stress profile compared with the later tragedies, which warns against building large expressive claims on deviations that are, statistically, the play's normal weather.

René Weis's edition supplies the material evidence on which the dispute can be tried, and the trial goes as follows. First, the sceptical position is correct about the single line: almost any isolated line of the speech could be performed against its grain, slowed or flattened, because performance is free at the margin. Second, the position fails at the level of the passage, for a reason Attridge's own framework supplies: rhythmic realizations are constrained by the distribution of the features that invite them. A text in which trochaic imperative openings, triple-syllable crowding, feminine endings, pyrrhic skim, and violent enjambment cluster at the commands and relax at the concealments, while spondees and monosyllabic hammering fall exactly on the brakes, does not merely permit a galloping performance; it makes the contrary performance progressively more perverse as the passage proceeds, a reading against more and more of the text's grain at once. Third, Tarlinskaja's caution, properly heard, refines rather than refutes the claim: the speech is not the play's most irregular passage by raw deviation count, and the fusion thesis does not need it to be. The claim was never that the verse is wild. The claim is that its deviations and its regularities are distributed by sense, hurry here, check there, and distribution, unlike raw frequency, is exactly what the statistics of isolated features cannot see and what scansion of the whole speech displays.

The adjudication, then: Wright wins on the existence and location of the music, Attridge wins on its mode of being, and the gallop is real the way a score's allegro is real, as a strongly specified potential that competent performance realizes and incompetent or wilful performance can betray but not refute. Weis's text is the arbiter of the evidence: the features on which the verdict rests stand in the early texts and are not an editor's invention, surviving collation between the quartos even where the pointing differs. The editor's role confines itself to tempo at the margins. The composer built the gallop; the editor tunes it; the reader rides it or refuses the ride, and refusal is a fact about the reader, not about the horse.

Granville-Barker's production wisdom closes the critical triangle from the practical side. A director who must decide, tonight, how fast the speech goes cannot wait for the philosophy of rhythm to settle, and the Prefaces' insistence on the play's speed is the practitioner's version of the verdict: the verse tells the company how it moves, and companies ignore the instruction at the production's peril. Between Wright's analysis, Attridge's caution, Tarlinskaja's statistics, Weis's text, and Granville-Barker's stage, the scansion reading emerges not as one impression among many but as the account that explains the most evidence with the least strain. That is the standard this series applies to critical disagreements, and the fusion thesis meets it.

## The Verdict: How Sound and Sense Fuse in "Gallop Apace"

The verdict can now be given its full form. In the gallop-apace speech, sound and sense fuse because the verse's rhythmic behaviour and the speech's emotional argument are the same curve drawn in two notations. The curve has three phases, and the scansion has tracked each. In the first phase, the address to the steeds, the verse gallops: trochaic strikes, triple crowding, feminine overshoot, the whip-hand of Phaethon rippling through the metre. In the second phase, the summons to night, the verse thickens and hammers: spondee at the curtain, monosyllables at the repeated "come", the checked period of the hooding, desire discovering that its object is not speed alone but arrival, concealment, possession. In the third phase, the great enjambed sentence of the stars, the verse flows: regularity restored within the line, momentum generated across lines, the long breath of imagining that carries Juliet from the marriage bed to the heavens in a single period. Urging, enduring, imagining: the speech's senses, and simultaneously its tempi. A listener who heard only the music, in a language not understood, would receive the emotional plot entire. A reader who reads only the sense receives the same plot denuded of its body. The fusion is not a critic's compliment. It is the speech's construction.

The fuller reading of the speech as a statement of desire, its place in Juliet's arc and the scene's tragedy, belongs to [the companion close reading of the soliloquy as desire](/2022/07/15/juliet-gallop-apace-soliloquy-desire/), and the present verdict is deliberately confined to the prosody. But the confinement itself yields the article's last and largest claim. Critics have sometimes treated the speech's desire as its content and its music as its clothing, the one essential, the other a grace. The scansion reverses the priority. Strip the speech of its prosodic behaviour, render it in prose at the same length, and the desire that remains is generic, a bride's wish for night, indistinguishable from a hundred stage longings. The specific desire of this speech, its impatience with the structure of time itself, its alternation of command and plea, its prophecy uttered in the calmest verse of the passage, exists only in the music. The cliche about the speech, that it is the play's great aria of longing, is true for a reason the cliche never states: the longing is not what the speech is about. The longing is what the speech does, in the ear, in real time, thirty-three lines long, at a gallop that keeps braking against the coming dark.

That is the standard the close reading sets for the play's other celebrated speeches, and it is the reason this speech, of all the soliloquies, most rewards the labour of the marked page. The metre was the meaning all along, waiting for a reader willing to count.

The consequences reach past this one speech. Editors choosing punctuation for the passage are choosing tempi, and the choice deserves to be made as musicianship rather than housekeeping; performers deciding the speech's pace are deciding its argument, and the text has already made most of the decision for any company willing to scan; teachers assigning the speech as a set text on desire assign, whether the syllabus says so or not, a set text on rhythm, since the desire has no other complete existence. The series thesis that governs these close readings holds that the cliche about a passage usually preserves a truth whose mechanism has been forgotten. The cliche calls this speech a song of longing, and the mechanism, recovered line by line, is a pentameter ridden at a gallop, braked at a curtain, floated into prophecy, and slowed at last into the patient metre of a child before a festival, while one syllable tolls at the line ends like a bell for the coming dark. The song is the mechanism. To hear the one is to have scanned the other.

## The Middle Movement: Night Under Three Names

Between the gallop and the great sentence stands the speech's middle movement, the triple summons to night, and its prosody deserves a section of its own because it performs the most delicate operation in the speech: it slows the verse without ever letting the urgency drop. The mechanism is dilation. Each address to night opens with the imperative strike already examined, "Come, civil night", and then, instead of proceeding to the next command, the verse expands into description: "Thou sober-suited matron all in black". The epithets accumulate, sober-suited, all in black, and the line fills with adjectives the way a figure is dressed, garment by garment. Adjectival lines in pentameter are naturally slower than verbal lines, because adjectives invite the voice to dwell where verbs invite it to advance, and Shakespeare exploits the contrast systematically: command, dilation, command, dilation, the verse breathing in great waves, each wave cresting on an imperative and subsiding into epithet.

The content of the epithets is the other articles' ground; the prosodic fact is that night is named three times in this movement and renamed each time, and each renaming restarts the rhythm. Civil night, the matron, gentle and loving and black-browed night: the descriptions lengthen as the movement proceeds, the third address carrying a pile of adjectives, "gentle night, come, loving, black-browed night", where the stresses themselves begin to crowd in the manner of the opening gallop, as though the accumulated tenderness were re-accelerating the verse from inside. The movement is therefore not a plateau between two speeds but a slow swell, description gathering momentum until it breaks back into command and then into the great enjambed sentence. Critics who call the speech uniformly breathless have usually quoted its opening and ignored this architecture; the middle movement is where the speech proves its range, a soloist demonstrating that the tempo can be governed downward as precisely as upward.

Two lines in the movement repay the scanner's closest attention. The first is the paradox line, "For thou wilt lie upon the wings of night / Whiter than new snow upon a raven's back". Its music is a rocking comparative structure: WHI-ter than NEW SNOW up-ON a RA-ven's BACK, the stresses arranged in a rising pattern that lifts through "new snow" and settles on the raven, brightness carried on darkness in the syntax exactly as in the sense. The line is metrically smooth, nearly regular, and its smoothness is a decision: the paradox that in another register might be forced with a spondee is here floated, the verse declining to underline its own wit. The second line is the gambling line, "Played for a pair of stainless maidenhoods", closing the match metaphor that "learn me how to lose a winning match" opened. Its stresses fall on the wager's terms, PLAYED, PAIR, STAIN-less, MAID-en-hoods, and its feminine ending lets the stake itself overshoot the measure, the one place in the middle movement where the opening gallop's signature reappears. The overshoot is placed like a signature on the contract: at the moment the speech names what is wagered, the verse again exceeds its frame.

One line in the movement concentrates its sound to the point of proverb: "And learn me how to lose a winning match". Set aside what the phrase means, which has its own treatment in the desire reading of the speech, and hear only its placement of weight. LEARN, LOSE, WIN, MATCH: the line's four content stresses fall on four monosyllables in a row, verb, verb, participle, noun, each struck separately, with the unstressed syllables reduced to a minimum of connective tissue. The line is the middle movement's equivalent of the curtain spondee, a place where the verse stops flowing and starts hammering, and the hammering fits the figure the line carries, a contest decided blow by blow. Around it the lines dilate and flow; inside it the verse is all knuckles. Scansion that averages the movement's tempo misses the line entirely, which is why this article has insisted on reading at the resolution of the single line: the speech's meanings are filed at that resolution, one hammered line at a time.

Heard whole, the middle movement explains why the speech's tempo curve persuades. A merely fast passage and a merely slow one, juxtaposed, would produce incoherence; what the summons to night produces is continuity under transformation, the same imperative engine driving at different gears. The prosodic unity is the unity of the will behind the commands, and it is why the speech can bear its final modulations, toward commerce and toward simile, without ever sounding like a different poem.

## The Last Movement: Bought, Sold, and the Long Simile

The speech's final movement, from "O, I have bought the mansion of a love" to the Nurse's approach, changes the prosodic subject from time to tenure, and the verse changes with it. The famous triad is built on balanced negation: bought but not possessed, sold but not enjoyed, a rhetoric of antithesis whose verse music is correspondingly measured, the stresses distributing themselves in matched pairs across the clauses, POS-sessed against EN-joyed, the semicolons falling like the strokes of an account being ruled. Where the opening movement's verse strained forward, this verse stands still and reckons. The mansion is the speech's largest and stillest image, architecture after horses, and the metre behaves architecturally: regular lines, clear caesuras, the balance of a ledger read aloud. The impatience has not lessened; it has changed its figure, from the rider whipping horses to the purchaser locked out of a purchased house, and the prosody registers the change as a shift from acceleration to suspension, speed converted into the ache of an interval that will not close.

Then comes the simile, the child with the new robes, and with it the smoothest verse in the speech: "So tedious is this day / As is the night before some festival / To an impatient child that hath new robes / And may not wear them." Four lines of nearly perfect iambic regularity carry the comparison, the enjambments gentle, the stresses falling where the pattern predicts, the whole passage singable in a way the opening gallop is not. The regularity is the expressiveness. A child waiting for a festival is not whipping horses; the child's time is the slow time of a calendar, and the verse adopts calendar time, line succeeding line in unhurried order. After thirty lines of strained measure, the speech ends its solo career in the metre's most obedient form, and the effect is devastating in context: the formal verse of patience, made to describe the one thing the speaker cannot perform. The gallop has spent itself against the fact that the day will last exactly as long as days last. Prosody here is irony at the deepest level, the verse enacting the tediousness it names by finally, perfectly, behaving.

The solo's last two lines deserve their own hearing, because they perform the whole speech in miniature one final time. "And she brings news; and every tongue that speaks / But Romeo's name speaks heavenly eloquence" runs the beloved's name over a line ending once more, the final enjambment of the speech, gentler than the great suspension of the sixth line but of the same family: the name will not stay inside the measure, and the sentence must cross into the next line to complete itself around him. The verse then closes on "eloquence", a feminine ending, the measure overshooting softly one last time, the speech ending not on a struck stress but on a dying syllable that trails into the Nurse's entrance. Gallop, tolling, flow, and now this valedictory overshoot: the solo's final gesture repeats its first, the line exceeding its frame, except that the energy has inverted, the opening's overshoot an act of propulsion and the close's an act of release, the hand opening. A reader tracking only the argument may register the lines as a transition. A reader tracking the prosody hears the speech sign its name.

The closing lines turn outward, "O, here comes my Nurse", and the verse's solo system begins to dissolve into the scene's. Even this transition is prosodically marked: the apostrophic mode, which has sustained the speech by addressing absent powers, gives way to address of a present person, and the long periods shorten into the approach of dialogue. The music does not conclude; it is discontinued, a further formal truth about waiting, which ends not with a cadence but with an interruption from the world of fact. The Nurse will bring the news that reverses the speech's whole errand, and the verse's failure to round itself off, its openness at the moment of breaking, reads in retrospect as the form's own foreboding: this music was never going to be allowed to finish.

## The Speech Among the Play's Other Tempi

A speech's tempo means most when set against the tempi around it, and the close reading closes its evidence by placing the gallop in the play's rhythmic landscape, briefly, by pointer rather than by re-derivation. The Prologue's sonnet moves in the closed regularity of its form, fourteen lines of prophecy delivered at the pace of a rite; Juliet's speech shares the sonnet's pentameter ground but treats the ground as something to strain against, prophecy's calm inverted into desire's strain. The balcony scene's music is reciprocal: shared lines, traded speeches, a duet in which the measure itself is completed by the other voice, the prosody of two speakers fitting one another. The wedding-night speech is that duet's dark twin, a solo in which every completion must be supplied by the speaker alone, the enjambments doing the work the partner's voice did in the orchard, carrying the line over its ending because no answering voice arrives to carry it. The aubade at dawn will reverse the gallop's errand in the play's other famous tempo contest, the lark against the nightingale, where the verse slows into the dragging music of parting; the wedding night's hurry and the dawn's reluctance are one argument about time conducted in two tempi, and the later scene's drag is legible because this scene's gallop has taught the ear what Juliet sounds like when time is her enemy in the opposite direction.

The comparison that matters most is the least obvious: the Friar's dawn soliloquy in Act 2, the play's other great solo of natural philosophy, moves in meditative couplets, its enjambments serving the overflow of copious thought, its pace the pace of a man with all morning. Juliet's solo answers it across the play's structure, night against dawn, the pupil of haste against the teacher of patience, and the prosodies embody the philosophies before the philosophies are stated: his verse digests the world at leisure, hers races it. When the Friar later counsels that violent delights have violent ends and advises loving moderately, the audience has already heard, in the wedding-night speech, the tempo his moderation is warning against, rendered not as folly but as music. The tragedy is partly a war of tempi, and the gallop-apace speech is haste's finest hour, the moment the war's losing side is given the play's most beautiful rhythmic argument.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch gallop scansion, and what does it reveal about the opening lines?

The InsightCrunch gallop scansion is the marked scansion of the speech's first eight lines printed in this article, setting out each line's stresses, syllable count, and metrical events in a single table. It reveals that the opening movement disturbs its pentameter in patterned ways: trochaic openings on the commands, feminine endings that overshoot the measure on the horses, the wagoner, and the word "immediately", a spondee at the curtain, and one violent enjambment that suspends Romeo's name at a line ending. The pattern is the revelation: the disturbances cluster where the impatience is greatest, which converts an impression of speed into evidence of design.

### Q: Why does the opening line of "Gallop apace" end on an unstressed syllable?

The opening line ends on an unstressed syllable because its last word, "steeds", completes a feminine ending: the eleventh syllable of the line follows the final stress and lets the line overshoot its measure. The effect is forward spill rather than rest. A masculine ending closes a line with a struck stress, a full stop of sound; the feminine ending lets the verse tip past its own boundary, still moving. Shakespeare places that overshoot on the horses themselves, the agents of speed, and repeats the device on "wagoner" and "immediately" within four lines, so the measure keeps failing to contain exactly the things that carry or demand haste.

### Q: What makes the first lines of the speech sound like a horse at the gallop?

The gallop effect comes from triple rhythms surfacing inside a duple measure. Iambic pentameter walks in twos, but the opening lines keep fitting three syllables into the span the measure prices at two: the dactylic tumble of "fiery-footed", the three syllables of "Phaethon" rippling across their line, the five syllables of "immediately" sprawling at a line's end. A galloping gait is itself three beats and a suspension, and the ear recognizes the figure without instruction. The effect is cumulative rather than local; no single substitution gallops, but their concentration at the commands, laid over the audible tick of the regular metre beneath, builds the gait within four lines.

### Q: Why do commands dominate the speech, and how do the imperatives reshape its stress pattern?

Commands dominate because the speech is an act of will against time: Juliet orders the sun's team, night, and the absent Romeo as if address could compel arrival. The imperatives reshape the stress pattern by front-loading weight. An English imperative carries its stress at its head, so "Gallop", "Spread", "Leap", "Come", "Hood", and "Give" strike at the openings of lines and clauses, reversing the iambic rise into a succession of trochaic blows. The verse's pulse of command is therefore grammatical before it is decorative. Each new apostrophe re-strikes the beat, and the speech's rhythm becomes the rhythm of insistence repeated against an unanswering sky.

### Q: Where does the verse overflow its line endings in the speech, and what does the overflow enact?

The overflow is strongest at the sixth line, which ends on "and Romeo" with the verb "Leap" withheld into the next line, and broadest in the great sentence that begins "Give me my Romeo", which runs across four line endings to the garish sun. Enjambment defers the small closure a line ending promises, carrying the listener forward on unfinished grammar. What the overflow enacts is the refusal of delay: the syntax will not wait for the measure to finish before reaching for its object. At the sixth line the beloved's very name is made to wait at the edge of the verse for its action, held for the length of a breath, then released.

### Q: How does the tempo change when Juliet turns from the sun's horses to night itself?

The tempo checks and thickens. The address to the steeds is the speech's fastest music, all crowding, overshoot, and imperative strike; the summons to night, beginning with "Spread thy close curtain", replaces surplus syllables with surplus stress. A spondee jams "close curtain", the repeated "come" hammers in monosyllables, and the periods lengthen and darken. The change tracks the wish: driving the day away is a matter of speed, but drawing night's curtain is a matter of weight and cover, and the verse exchanges the one currency for the other. The speech's curve, gallop into gravity, is the clearest sign that its tempo is responsive rather than uniform.

### Q: Why does the verse's movement check and slow at "Hood my unmanned blood"?

The movement checks because the wish changes from speed to concealment. Juliet asks night to hood the falcon-flush in her cheeks until love grows bold, a request for hiding rather than haste, and the verse answers with its most restrained passage: stresses distribute evenly, the syntax subordinates and qualifies, the enjambment gentles, and no syllabic crowding disturbs the measure. The slowing is semantic before it is metrical, modesty lowering the voice of the verse as it lowers the speaker's demand. The passage also serves the whole speech's legibility: without such calms, the galloping passages would have no baseline of restraint against which their hurry could register as hurry.

### Q: What work do the repeated openings on "Come" do in the middle of the speech?

The repeated "come" converts speed into insistence. Each summons, "Come, civil night", "Come, night, come, Romeo", "Come, gentle night", restrikes the imperative beat at the head of a fresh address, so the verse's pulse is renewed rather than sustained. Because the words are monosyllables that each bear a full stress, the line becomes a row of separate weights, blows placed one by one rather than a stream skimmed at pace. The music is that of a hand striking a door: impatience experienced not as motion but as time's refusal to move. The anaphora therefore supplies the speech's second tempo, the stopped rhythm against which the galloping passages define themselves.

### Q: How does the long sentence that begins "Give me my Romeo" hold together metrically?

The sentence holds together by exchanging disturbance for flow. Its lines run across four endings without a full stop, yet within the lines the stresses fall into the iambic pattern with unusual regularity, the verse settling into its ground beat while the syntax supplies the momentum. The two engines of the speech divide the labour: enjambment carries the speed, the regular measure carries the steadiness of a mind contemplating rather than commanding. Clausular weight gathers at the image nouns, stars, heaven, night, sun, each landing at a line's end like a toll. The sentence feels swifter than the opening gallop and calmer at once, because its haste is in its syntax, not its syllables.

### Q: What is a hovering accent, and where does one fall in the opening lines?

A hovering accent, in George T. Wright's term, is the suspension of stress between two adjacent syllables of nearly equal weight, so the beat hovers instead of landing cleanly on one side. One falls at the centre of the fifth line, in "close curtain", where the adjective and the noun press against each other with comparable force and the scansion cannot honestly demote either. The hover matters expressively: at the instant night is asked to draw its curtain, the verse itself hesitates between two weights, a held instant of maximum density. Wright's concept names what the ear registers, the gallop's single most loaded footfall, a beat that lands twice and moves on neither time.

### Q: Why does the speech largely refuse rhyme, and what would a closing couplet do to it?

The speech refuses rhyme because its business is unfulfilment deferred, and rhyme is the sound of completion. A closing couplet clicks a passage shut, delivering the paired chime of expectation answered; the sonnet the lovers exchange at the feast can afford that click because the form's completion mirrors their mutual arrival. Juliet alone, addressing powers that do not answer, speaks blank verse whose lines end on horses, night, stars, and sun without the consolation of chiming pairs. The one near-couplet effect arrives disguised, in the repeated "night" line endings that toll rather than rhyme. Rhyme would promise her the ending she is begging time to provide; the verse withholds both together.

### Q: How does the verse enact waiting even while it urges speed?

The verse enacts waiting by building delay into its own procedures. Enjambment makes the beloved's name wait at a line ending for its verb. Feminine endings make the measure overshoot and fall, arrival always one syllable past the count. The great sentence postpones its main clause's completion across four lines of subordination. Even the imperatives enact waiting, since a command repeated is a command unanswered: "come" struck three times is the sound of nothing yet arriving. Speed in the speech is the wish; waiting is the structure. The prosody holds both simultaneously, which is why the speech hurts as it exhilarates: every device that hurries the verse also demonstrates, formally, that the verse has not yet arrived.

### Q: Why does the Nurse's arrival break the speech's music rather than complete it?

The Nurse's arrival breaks the music because the solo's tempo system depends on a single will pressing against silence, and dialogue dissolves that condition. The soliloquy's long periods, its apostrophes to unanswering powers, its alternation of gallop and brake, all require an empty stage: the verse is timed by one breath and one impatience. The Nurse brings news, errands, and the prose of the world, and the scene's verse contracts into exchange, question, and the shattered tidings of the duel. Nothing in the speech's curve prepares a cadence; the music is interrupted mid-phrase by fact. The abruptness is itself the play's point, private tempo colliding with public event, and it lands because the solo's music was so completely established.

### Q: How can a silent reader hear the verse music of the speech on the page?

A silent reader hears the music by treating scansion as a score and performing the poem inwardly. Four operations recover the sound: mark the stresses the language assigns, count syllables against the pentameter's ten and note every overshoot, read through the enjambed endings without pausing, and place the monosyllables of the "come" passages as separate weights. Silent reading is performance at reduced volume, not its absence, and the score constrains the inner voice as firmly as print constrains the eye: no competent inward performance of the first line can make it plod. What the page cannot supply is the shared tempo of a theatre; the music's structure survives entire.

### Q: What does the scansion of the speech show about Juliet's impatience that the words alone do not?

The scansion shows the impatience distributed, phased, and embodied rather than merely declared. The words say that Juliet longs for night; the scansion shows the longing operating as a tempo curve with three distinct phases, the gallop of the commands to the steeds, the hammered gravity of the summons to night, the long enjambed flow of the imagining, and it shows the curve turning at exactly the lines where the wish changes its object. The words also cannot show what the measure reveals: that the impatience is disciplined, riding a strict pentameter it strains against but never abandons. Scansion converts a reported emotion into a demonstrated behaviour of the verse itself.

### Q: Why are so many of the speech's stressed syllables verbs of motion?

So many stressed syllables are verbs of motion because the speech's grammar of command places verbs at the heads of lines and clauses, where the stresses fall. "Gallop", "Spread", "Leap", "Come", "Give", "Take" are the load-bearing words of their lines, and each names a movement: horses running, a curtain drawn, a leap into arms, an arrival, a star-cutting. The prosodic fact and the semantic fact are one fact viewed twice; the verse stresses what the syntax commands, and what the syntax commands is motion. The distribution also explains the speech's peculiar physicality in performance, where the actor's breath is spent on verbs, the body of the line propelled by actions rather than descriptions.

### Q: Why does Juliet's most urgent speech keep so regular a measure underneath its disturbances?

The measure stays regular because the expression of the disturbances depends on it. A hurried line registers as hurried only against an expected pace, and Shakespeare is careful never to let the pentameter's ground beat disappear for long: between the crowded openings, lines settle into clean iambic movement, and the great sentence of the stars is metrically the calmest passage in the speech. The discipline is also characterization at the level of form. Juliet's urgency is not hysteria; the girl who will manage the Friar, the potion, and the tomb speaks a verse whose passion rides a measure it fully commands. The gallop is thrilling precisely because the rider never loses the reins.

### Q: How do the sounds of "love-performing night" slow the line that carries them?

The phrase slows its line through density of stress and consonant. "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night" packs six strong stresses into ten syllables, with the spondee "close curtain" at its centre and the compound "love-performing" adding a secondary stress that thickens the line's second half. The consonants cooperate: the clusters in "spread", "close", and "curtain" require the tongue to strike and hold rather than glide, and the long vowels of "night" extend the line's final syllable. Where the galloping lines favour open syllables and surplus lightness, this line is built from weight and friction, night's curtain drawn in the phonetics as well as the sense.

### Q: What goes wrong in performance when the speech is delivered at a single, unvarying pace?

Uniform pace erases the speech's argument. Delivered all at a rush, the performance loses the brakes: the spondee at the curtain, the hammered monosyllables, and the checked modesty of the hooding passage are flattened into the gallop, and the return of speed afterward has no calm to depart from. Delivered all at reverent solemnity, the imperatives lose their strike, the overshooting lines are trimmed to a decorum the text refuses, and impatience becomes meditation. Harley Granville-Barker's production writings insist the play lives by tempo rightly judged, and this speech is the doctrine's test case: its meaning is a curve, and any single pace, fast or slow, performs only one point on the curve and calls it the whole.

### Q: Which single line best shows sound and sense fusing in the speech, and why?

The first line is the strongest candidate, because its fusion is total and immediate. The command names the gallop and the verse performs it in the same breath: a trochee lunges ahead of the beat, the dactylic compound "fiery-footed" rocks like hooves across the line's second half, and an eleventh syllable overshoots the measure as the horses overshoot the day. Nothing in the line is illustration added to sense; remove the rhythmic behaviour and the command remains but the gallop vanishes, which proves the gallop was in the sound. Other lines fuse locally, the spondee's curtain, the name suspended at the enjambment, but the first line fuses completely, announcing the speech's whole method in ten syllables and one to spare.
