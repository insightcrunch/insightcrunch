Election law in India defeats most readers at the same point, and the point is not ignorance of the rules. A voter whose name has vanished from the roll, a candidate whose nomination paper has been rejected, a party worker who watches money change hands outside a polling station and a legislator accused of crossing the floor are all holding election problems, and each of them instinctively carries the problem to the institution that seems largest and nearest. The instinct is understandable and it is usually wrong. The roll complaint taken to a court fails because the courts are barred from interrupting an election in progress. The nomination complaint taken to the Election Commission after the result fails because the statute reserves that question to an election petition. The defection complaint taken anywhere except the presiding officer of the House fails because the Tenth Schedule gives the decision to that officer alone. Indian election law is not one body of law administered by one powerful commission. It is a field divided among a constitutional commission, two principal statutes, a set of rules and orders made under them, the presiding officers of legislatures, the President and the Governors acting on advice they are bound to take, and the High Courts and the Supreme Court sitting in carefully separated compartments. The reader who can see the division can navigate the field. The reader who cannot will keep knocking on doors that do not open.

The division has a clean shape, and stating it plainly is the single most useful thing a guide to this field can do. Election law in India divides into three domains. The first domain is who may vote: the constitutional guarantee of adult suffrage, the preparation and revision of electoral rolls, the qualifications and disqualifications for registration, and the machinery of officers who maintain the roll between elections. The second domain is who may stand: the qualifications for membership of Parliament and the state legislatures, the disqualifications that bar a person from contesting or from continuing to sit, the nomination process and its scrutiny, and the deposits, affidavits and disclosures that accompany a candidature. The third domain is how the contest is run: the notification that starts an election, the campaign and the code that governs it, the polling and counting machinery, the offences that police the process, the declaration of the result, and the narrow route by which a declared result may afterwards be undone. Every election question a citizen, a candidate or a court will ever meet belongs to one of these three domains, and each domain has its own governing statute, its own deciding authority and its own forum. Confuse the domains and the complaint goes to the wrong door. Keep them separate and the whole field becomes a map rather than a maze.

That map has an unusual feature, and the feature is the article's central claim. The largest single source of power in Indian election law sits in none of the statutes. It sits in one constitutional phrase: the superintendence, direction and control of elections, vested in the Election Commission by Article 324 of the Constitution. That phrase is what allows the Commission to enforce a Model Code of Conduct that Parliament never enacted, to postpone or countermand a poll where the process has been vitiated, to deploy observers and direct the transfer of officials, and to act in the many places where the statutes are silent. The statutes supply the detailed law of rolls, candidatures and polling. The constitutional phrase supplies the residuary authority that holds the system together when the detailed law runs out, and the extent of that authority has been settled case by case in the Supreme Court rather than by legislation. A field with two principal statutes and one constitutional phrase behaves, in practice, as though the phrase were the statute. Identifying where authority actually comes from, provision by provision and question by question, is what makes the field navigable, and it is the work this guide performs.

## The constitutional layer of election law in India

Every statute in this field is subordinate legislation in the deepest sense, because the Constitution itself settles the questions that matter most and leaves the remainder to Parliament and the state legislatures. Part Fifteen of the Constitution, Articles 324 to 329, is the electoral chapter, and a reader who fixes those six provisions in mind holds the skeleton of the entire subject. Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, all elections to Parliament, to the legislatures of the states and to the offices of President and Vice-President in the Election Commission. Article 325 provides that there shall be one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency and that no person shall be ineligible for inclusion in such a roll on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex. Article 326 provides that elections to the House of the People and to the Legislative Assembly of every state shall be on the basis of adult suffrage. Article 327 empowers Parliament to make provision with respect to all matters relating to elections to Parliament and the state legislatures, including the preparation of electoral rolls and the delimitation of constituencies. Article 328 gives the legislature of a state a corresponding power to the extent that Parliament has not occupied the ground. Article 329 bars interference by courts in electoral matters, providing that the validity of a law relating to delimitation shall not be called in question in any court and that no election shall be called in question except by an election petition presented to such authority and in such manner as may be provided for by or under law.

Two features of that chapter deserve emphasis before any statute is opened. The first is that the Constitution distributes the field instead of monopolising it. The Commission receives the conduct of elections. Parliament receives the widest legislative power over the subject, extending even to the rolls and to delimitation. The states receive a residual legislative space. The courts receive an exclusion, softened only by the election petition route that a statute must supply. Nothing in the chapter makes the Commission a legislature, and nothing makes Parliament the conductor of elections, and a large share of the litigation in this field consists of one institution straying, or being accused of straying, across a boundary the chapter drew in 1950. The second feature is the deliberate asymmetry between the roll and the contest. Article 325 commands a single general roll and forbids exclusion from it on the four named grounds, which is a guarantee about the composition of the electorate itself. No equivalent guarantee polices who may stand, beyond the qualifications and disqualifications the Constitution and the statutes separately prescribe. The franchise is constitutionalised. The candidature is largely statutory. That asymmetry explains why arguments about the roll carry a constitutional weight that arguments about nomination procedure usually do not.

### What are the three domains into which election law divides?

Election law divides into who may vote, governed by the Constitution's suffrage provisions and the Representation of the People Act, 1950; who may stand, governed by the qualifications and disqualifications in the Constitution and the Representation of the People Act, 1951; and how the contest is run, governed by the 1951 Act, its rules and the Commission's power of superintendence.

The suffrage provision repays a closer reading, because its history is the history of the Indian electorate. Article 326 as adopted required a voter to be a citizen of India, not less than twenty-one years of age on a qualifying date fixed by law, and not disqualified under the Constitution or a law on grounds such as non-residence, unsoundness of mind, crime or corrupt or illegal practice. The Constitution (Sixty-first Amendment) Act, 1988 substituted eighteen years for twenty-one years, and the amendment came into force on 28 March 1989, enlarging the electorate by tens of millions of young citizens at a stroke. The decision was a political judgment dressed as a franchise rule: the government of the day argued that young citizens were literate, politically conscious and entitled to a voice in choosing the governments whose policies would govern their working lives, and the amendment passed with broad support. What the text did not change is equally instructive. The right conferred is a right to be registered and to vote subject to statutory disqualifications, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly described the right to vote as a statutory right rather than a fundamental right, while holding in the judgment that introduced the none of the above option that the act of voting, including a negative vote, is a form of expression protected by Article 19(1)(a). The two propositions coexist. The entitlement to be on the roll comes from the statute book. The freedom expressed at the moment of voting carries constitutional protection. Much loose commentary collapses the distinction and claims a fundamental right to vote, which the case law does not support.

The disqualification provisions sit outside Part Fifteen and must be gathered from the chapters on the Union and the states. Article 102 lists the disqualifications for membership of Parliament: holding an office of profit under the government, being of unsound mind as declared by a competent court, being an undischarged insolvent, ceasing to be a citizen or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign state, and disqualification under a law made by Parliament, which is the hook on which the Representation of the People Act, 1951 hangs its own list. Article 191 performs the same work for state legislatures. Article 103 then provides the decision route for a question whether a member of Parliament has become subject to a disqualification: the question is referred for the decision of the President, who obtains the opinion of the Election Commission and acts according to that opinion. Article 192 mirrors the arrangement for state legislatures with the Governor in place of the President. The design is distinctive. The constitutional head decides in form, the Commission decides in substance, and the courts have treated the Commission's opinion as effectively binding on the President or Governor who sought it. Alongside these provisions stands the Tenth Schedule, inserted by the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985, which disqualifies a member who defects from the political party that set the member up as a candidate, and which assigns the decision to the Speaker or Chairman of the House rather than to the President or Governor. Defection is therefore a disqualification that travels by a different road from every other disqualification, a point the routing table at the end of this guide makes visible and a subject the cluster's own articles own in depth.

## Article 324 and the power that no statute confers

Article 324 is drafted in two movements. The first clause vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of all elections to Parliament, the state legislatures and the two highest offices in an Election Commission consisting of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners as the President may from time to time fix. The second clause provides for appointment by the President, subject to any law made by Parliament, a qualification that lay dormant as a legislative invitation for more than seven decades and that produced the appointment controversy examined later in this guide. Later clauses protect the Chief Election Commissioner with a removal process modelled on that of a Supreme Court judge, protect an Election Commissioner from removal except on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner, reserve the conditions of service from disadvantageous variation after appointment, and oblige the President and the Governors to make available to the Commission such staff as it may require for the discharge of its functions.

The operative words are the three verbs of the first clause. Superintendence, direction and control is a phrase of the widest amplitude, and the Supreme Court read it that way early and has never retreated. In Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, decided in 1978, the Court held that Article 324 is a plenary provision that operates in areas left unoccupied by legislation, that the Commission may exercise its reserved power to meet a situation the statutes do not cover, and that the power is not unlimited, because the Commission must act in conformity with the Constitution, the statutes and the rule of law, and its orders remain open to judicial review on the ordinary grounds. The companion proposition came from the earlier line of authority on Article 329. In N. P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer, decided in 1952 before the first general election had concluded, the Court held that the word election in Article 329 covers the entire process from notification to declaration of result, that a nomination dispute could not be raised in a writ petition midway through that process, and that the remedy for a wrong done during the process is the election petition the statute provides after the process ends. Read together, the two decisions construct the constitutional settlement of this field. During an election, the Commission governs and the courts stand back. After an election, the courts govern through the statutory petition and the Commission's work is examinable in that forum. The narrow exception the case law recognises is an act wholly without jurisdiction, where intervention does not obstruct the process but corrects an authority that never had power to act at all.

### Where does the power to act when the statutes are silent come from?

It comes from Article 324 of the Constitution, which vests the superintendence, direction and control of electoral rolls and of the conduct of elections in the Election Commission. The Supreme Court reads that vesting as a plenary reservoir of power for situations legislation has not occupied, exercisable in conformity with the Constitution, the statutes and the rule of law.

The practical reach of the reservoir is best seen in the things the Commission does for which no section of any Act can be produced. It enforces the Model Code of Conduct, a document created by political consensus and administrative practice rather than by Parliament. It orders the transfer of district officials whose neutrality it doubts during an election, drawing on the staff clause and the superintendence clause together. It deploys general, police and expenditure observers drawn from the civil services. It adjourns a poll at a station where violence or booth capturing has destroyed the freedom of the vote, and it countermands an election where the process as a whole has been vitiated, in each case under statutory provisions for the polling station and under the constitutional power for the larger judgment. It decides disputes between rival groups of a recognised political party over which group is the party entitled to its reserved symbol. None of these powers is trivial, and several of them decide elections in substance. The honest account of Indian election law therefore begins with a paradox that the rest of this guide keeps in view: the institution's most consequential powers are the ones a reader cannot find by reading the two Representation of the People Acts from cover to cover, because those powers were never enacted. They were vested.

## The two Representation of the People Acts and the division between them

Parliament filled the constitutional skeleton with two statutes whose similar names cause more confusion than any other feature of this field, and separating them is the second piece of orientation a reader needs. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 is Act 43 of 1950. The Representation of the People Act, 1951 is Act 43 of 1951. The coincidence of number is genuine and has misled generations of students: each Act is the forty-third enactment of its own year, and a citation that gives the number without the year is worthless. Their subjects divide along the first two domains of the map. The 1950 Act governs who may vote. It provides for the allocation of seats in the House of the People and in the state legislative assemblies and councils, the delimitation of parliamentary and assembly constituencies, the officers charged with the electoral machinery at the roll stage, the qualifications and disqualifications for registration, and the preparation and revision of electoral rolls. The 1951 Act governs who may stand and how the contest is run. It provides for the qualifications and disqualifications for membership of Parliament and the state legislatures, the notification and conduct of elections, the registration of political parties, the corrupt practices and electoral offences that police the contest, and the machinery for deciding disputes, including the election petition. A complaint about a missing name belongs to the world of the 1950 Act. A complaint about a corrupt practice belongs to the world of the 1951 Act. The detailed treatment of each statute belongs to its own pillar in this series, and the reader who needs the roll law in depth should follow [the pillar on the Representation of the People Act, 1950](/2018/01/01/representation-of-people-act-1950/), while the candidature and conduct law is treated in [the pillar on the Representation of the People Act, 1951](/2017/12/15/representation-of-people-act-1951/). This guide keeps both at the level of their place in the map, which is where a thematic account earns its keep.

The division was a deliberate piece of legislative engineering by the provisional Parliament that enacted both statutes in the run up to the first general elections of 1951 to 1952. The roll had to exist before a single nomination could be filed, and the work of building it was a continuing administrative function that would repeat every year for as long as the republic lasted, while the conduct of a contest was an episodic event governed by a timetable. Separating the standing machinery from the episodic machinery allowed the roll law to develop its own officer hierarchy, its own claims and objections procedure and its own rhythm of annual revision, undisturbed by the heat of any particular election. The cost of the design is the confusion of titles. The benefit, visible across seven decades, is that a dispute about the roll never has to wait for an election to be resolved, and a dispute about an election never reopens the composition of the roll except through the one door the law leaves ajar, which is the rule that the roll as it stands when nominations close is the roll on which the election is fought.

Around the two principal Acts sits a penumbra of other instruments, and a complete map names them. The Delimitation Acts, enacted after censuses to constitute a Delimitation Commission and translate population into constituency boundaries, govern the shape of the constituencies themselves, and the subject is treated in depth in [the guide to the Delimitation Acts](/2018/04/01/delimitation-acts-india-explained/). The Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952, Act 31 of 1952, regulates the elections to the two offices that Article 324 places within the Commission's charge but that the Representation of the People Acts do not cover. The statute governing the appointment and service conditions of the Commission's own members, enacted in 2023, completes the statutory layer and is examined with the appointment question below. Beneath the statutes lies the subordinate layer: the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960, which govern the form and revision of the roll; the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961, which govern nominations, polling, counting and the declaration of results in operational detail; the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, an order issued by the Commission under its own authority, which governs the reservation of symbols for recognised parties, their allotment to candidates and the classification of parties as national or state parties; and the Model Code of Conduct, which is not subordinate legislation at all, being neither a statute nor a rule nor an order, but a code evolved by agreement among political parties and enforced by the Commission under Article 324. A reader who can place a rule in the correct layer of that stack already understands more about its force than most commentary conveys, because the layer determines what happens when the rule is broken. Breach of a statute may be an offence or a corrupt practice. Breach of the Symbols Order may cost a party its symbol. Breach of the Model Code draws a censure, a direction or a temporary bar on campaigning, and no prosecution at all.

## The first domain: who may vote

The law of the roll begins with the single general roll commanded by Article 325 and constructed by the 1950 Act. For each parliamentary and assembly constituency there is one roll, used for both levels of election, and the unity of the roll is a substantive choice rather than an administrative convenience. A citizen does not register separately for Parliament and for the Assembly, and a name struck off the roll disappears for every election the roll serves. The 1950 Act lays down who may be registered: a person who is a citizen of India, who is not less than eighteen years of age on the qualifying date, who is ordinarily resident in the constituency, and who is not disqualified under the Act, which excludes a person of unsound mind so declared, a person disqualified for a corrupt practice, and a person serving a sentence of imprisonment in defined circumstances, though a person in lawful custody or preventive detention retains a route to vote that the courts have had to work out in separate litigation. Ordinary residence is the concept on which most roll litigation turns, because it separates the student, the soldier, the migrant worker and the person with two homes from the rolls of constituencies they have left, and the Act surrounds it with deeming provisions and explanations that the roll pillar examines closely.

The machinery that keeps the roll is a standing hierarchy worth naming, because the routing of a roll complaint depends on knowing which officer holds the pen. The Election Commission superintends the whole. In each state a Chief Electoral Officer, appointed by the Commission in consultation with the state government, supervises the work. In each district a District Election Officer coordinates it. For each constituency an Electoral Registration Officer prepares and revises the roll, assisted by Assistant Electoral Registration Officers, and beneath them the booth level officers who verify entries house by house. Revision takes two principal forms. A summary revision is the annual exercise, conducted against a qualifying date, in which claims for inclusion, objections to entries and applications for correction or transposition are invited and decided. An intensive revision rebuilds the roll afresh and is ordered where the Commission judges that the existing roll cannot be repaired by amendment. Between revisions, continuous updation admits claims and objections through the year, subject to the decisive cut off that the roll is frozen for an election once the last date for making nominations has passed, so that the electorate for a contest cannot change while the contest is being fought. A citizen whose name is missing has a remedy at every stage of that cycle, and the remedy is administrative, addressed to the Electoral Registration Officer with an appeal upward within the machinery. It is not a writ petition, and it is not an election petition, and the citizen who chooses either of those doors discovers that the first is premature and the second is about a different subject altogether.

Two features of roll law have generated the heaviest controversy in the field and deserve a thematic statement here, with the depth reserved to the roll pillar. The first is the power to require information and the treatment of citizenship. Registration is confined to citizens, and the registration officer must be satisfied on that question where a doubt is raised, which places an administrative officer in the position of making findings that shade into citizenship adjudication. The second is the scale of deletion. A revision that removes names in large numbers will always be attacked as exclusion and defended as purification, and the legal answer to the argument lies in the procedure the Act prescribes: notice to the person affected, an opportunity to be heard, a reasoned order and an appeal. Where those steps are followed, the deletion is the Act working as designed. Where they are compressed or skipped, the deletion is vulnerable, and the vulnerability is procedural rather than political. That distinction, between attacking an outcome and attacking the absence of the prescribed process, is the one that decides roll cases, and it is the same distinction that decides nomination cases in the second domain.

## The second domain: who may stand

The law of candidature divides into qualifications, which a person must possess, and disqualifications, which a person must not carry, and the two lists live in different places. The qualifications for membership are constitutional and statutory together: citizenship, a minimum age of thirty years for the Council of States and twenty-five years for the House of the People and the state assemblies, and the additional qualifications the 1951 Act prescribes, centrally that the candidate be an elector, for Parliament in the case of the two Houses, with a further requirement of community membership where a seat is reserved for the Scheduled Castes or the Scheduled Tribes. The disqualifications are principally statutory. The 1951 Act disqualifies a person convicted of specified offences, or sentenced to imprisonment for not less than two years for other offences, for the period of imprisonment and a further six years from release; a person found guilty of a corrupt practice; a person dismissed from government service for corruption or disloyalty, for five years; a person interested in government contracts in defined ways; a person holding specified offices in government companies; and a person who fails to lodge an account of election expenses within the time and in the manner required, where the Commission's disqualification for that failure runs for three years. The constitutional disqualifications in Articles 102 and 191 run in parallel, and the governing detail of each ground, its duration and its exceptions belongs to the pillar on the Representation of the People Act, 1951 already linked for the division of the Acts, which owns that ground in this series.

One episode in the disqualification story matters at the thematic level because it changed the incentives of every sitting legislator in the country. The 1951 Act originally protected a sitting member who was convicted: the disqualification did not take effect until three months had passed, and if the member appealed within that period it remained suspended until the appeal was decided. In Lily Thomas v Union of India, decided in 2013, the Supreme Court struck that protection down, holding that Parliament had no power to defer a disqualification that Articles 102 and 191 impose from the date of conviction, because the same provisions that authorise Parliament to define disqualifications do not authorise it to postpone their operation for sitting members alone. The consequence is that conviction disqualifies a sitting member at once, subject to the appellate court's power to stay the conviction itself, which is a materially different and harder order to obtain than a stay of the sentence. The judgment is the clearest modern illustration of this guide's organising point. The question looked like a question about the 1951 Act. It was decided by reading the Constitution's disqualification articles as a complete code on timing, and the statute yielded.

The gateway into a contest is the nomination, and its law illustrates the routing principle in miniature. A candidate files nomination papers before the Returning Officer within the window the election notification opens, accompanied by a deposit, an oath or affirmation, the affidavit of criminal antecedents, assets and liabilities that the Supreme Court's disclosure judgments require, and, for a party candidate, the authorisation that connects the candidate to the party and its symbol. The Returning Officer scrutinises the papers on the appointed day and may reject a nomination for defects of a substantial character, including that the candidate is not qualified or is disqualified, that the proposer is not an elector of the constituency, or that the paper was not delivered in time or in the prescribed manner. A defect that is not substantial cannot ground rejection. The critical routing fact is the remedy. The Returning Officer's decision on a nomination, whether accepting or rejecting, is not appealable to the Commission and is not challengeable in a writ petition while the election proceeds, on the strength of Article 329 and the Ponnuswami line. It becomes a ground of challenge only inside an election petition after the result, where the statute treats improper rejection of a nomination as a defect that voids the election altogether, and improper acceptance as a defect that voids it where the result was materially affected. A candidate whose paper is wrongly rejected therefore has no immediate forum at all, which is a deliberate constitutional choice in favour of completing elections over correcting them in mid course, and it is the choice litigants find hardest to accept every cycle.

## The third domain: how the contest is run

An election begins in law when the President or the Governor, acting on the recommendation of the Election Commission, issues the notification calling upon the constituencies to elect, and the Commission publishes the programme that gives the notification its dates: the last date for nominations, the day of scrutiny, the last date for withdrawal, the days of poll and the day of counting. From that moment the machinery of the 1951 Act and the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961 takes over, and the Model Code of Conduct, which sleeps between elections, wakes and binds the conduct of governments, parties and candidates until the process closes. The sequence matters legally and not merely administratively. The bar in Article 329 descends with the notification and lifts with the declaration of the result. The Code's restraints on the use of official machinery, on announcements of new schemes and on transfers of officials run for the same window. The campaign offences and corrupt practices of the 1951 Act are judged by reference to acts done within the contest the notification created. A reader who wants to know from when a rule applies in this field is almost always asking when the notification issued, and the habit of checking that date resolves a surprising number of confused arguments.

The corrupt practices listed in the 1951 Act are the moral law of the contest, and they must be distinguished from the electoral offences that accompany them, because the consequences of the two categories run to different persons. A corrupt practice, such as bribery, undue influence, appeal to religion or caste or the symbols of either, the promotion of enmity, the publication of false statements about a candidate's personal character, or the incurring of expenditure beyond the authorised limit, is a defect of the election itself. Proved against a returned candidate or with the returned candidate's consent, it voids the election in an election petition and may lead to disqualification. An electoral offence, such as personation, voting more than once, removing a ballot or its electronic equivalent, disorder at a polling station or a breach of the secrecy of voting, is a crime committed by an individual, prosecuted in the ordinary criminal courts and punished with imprisonment or fine, and it does not by itself undo a result. The citizen who reports bribery at a polling station is therefore setting two different machines in motion at once, a prosecution against the giver and receiver and a potential ground of petition against the candidate who benefited, and the two machines have different forums, different burdens of proof and different clocks. Election petitions are decided on standards the courts describe as quasi criminal where a corrupt practice is alleged, which is one reason so many petitions fail on proof even where the underlying conduct was notorious.

Expenditure is the third domain's most contested territory, and its law is a study in the gap between a ceiling on paper and the money a contest absorbs. The 1951 Act requires every candidate to keep a separate and correct account of election expenditure between the date of nomination and the date of declaration, to lodge the account with the District Election Officer within thirty days of the declaration, and to stay within the ceiling the Conduct of Elections Rules prescribe for the constituency, a ceiling revised upward from time to time by rule amendment. The Commission polices the account through expenditure observers, shadow registers and notices to candidates whose reported spending appears understated, and the sanction for a failure to lodge the account as required is the disqualification already noted. What the ceiling does not reach is the spending of the political party and of supporters, which the Act largely excludes from the candidate's account except in defined circumstances, and that exclusion is the structural reason the expenditure debate never settles: the regulated figure is the candidate's figure, the contested figure is the campaign's figure, and the two are not the same number. The funding of parties beyond the constituency account, including the electoral bond scheme that the Supreme Court struck down in February 2024 in the challenge brought by the Association for Democratic Reforms, belongs to the funding articles of this cluster, and the thematic point for this guide is narrower. Money enters election law at three separate doors, the candidate's account, the party's disclosure obligations under the tax and companies framework, and the law of donations, and a reform aimed at only one door leaves the building open.

Polling itself is conducted by a hierarchy that mirrors the roll machinery and then extends below it. The Returning Officer for the constituency, ordinarily the district magistrate or a senior officer of the district, conducts the election under the superintendence of the District Election Officer and the Chief Electoral Officer, and presiding officers and polling officers staff each polling station on the day. Voting is by electronic voting machine, whose use the 1951 Act authorises and whose adoption the courts upheld against challenges that it was vulnerable and unverifiable. The voter verifiable paper audit trail unit attached to the machine prints a slip bearing the symbol and name of the candidate chosen, visible to the voter through a window for a few seconds before it falls into a sealed box, and the slips of a sample of polling stations in each constituency are counted against the machine totals under rules and directions the Supreme Court has adjusted over time. The none of the above button, placed last on the ballot unit, records a voter's rejection of every candidate, following the 2013 judgment in the case brought by the People's Union for Civil Liberties, which reasoned from the secrecy and expressive character of the vote. A none of the above tally larger than every candidate's tally does not void the election or order a fresh poll under the law governing Parliament and the assemblies: the candidate with the largest number of votes among the candidates is still declared elected, a position that reform proposals have repeatedly sought to change and that remains the governing position for these elections. Counting follows the poll under the Returning Officer's direction, postal ballots are counted with the machine votes, and the Returning Officer declares the candidate with the largest number of valid votes elected and grants the certificate of election, which is the act that closes the process and opens the petition window.

### How can a code with no statutory force change behaviour during a campaign?

The Model Code binds because the Election Commission enforces it under Article 324, because parties consented to its terms across successive revisions, and because the sanctions it deploys, censure, a direction to stop a practice, a temporary bar on a campaigner, and the reputational cost of a public notice, operate inside the short campaign window where delay itself is punishment.

## The subordinate layer and the Model Code

The Model Code deserves a section of its own because it is the most misunderstood instrument in Indian public law. It began as a code of conduct adopted for the Kerala assembly elections of 1960 and grew, through adoption by parties at the national level and revision by the Commission in consultation with them, into a detailed code governing speeches, processions, meetings, polling day conduct, the behaviour of the party in power, and the use of official transport, aircraft, rest houses and publicity. Its provisions on the ruling party are its sharpest edge: ministers may not combine official visits with election work, government machinery and personnel may not be used for campaign purposes, and announcements of grants, projects and appointments that could influence the voter are restrained from the day the election is announced. The Code is not a statute. It was never passed by Parliament, and it is not a rule made under a statute, so a breach of it is not an offence and no court will punish it as one. Its enforcement is the Commission's, under the superintendence power, and its sanctions are the notice, the censure, the direction and, in cases the Commission judges grave, instructions that a campaigner stay off the trail for a stated period or that a star campaigner's privileges be withdrawn. Statutory provisions overlap the Code at certain points, which complicates the picture usefully. The forty-eight hour silence period before the close of poll is statutory, in the 1951 Act. The prohibition on exit poll publication during the polling window is statutory. Restrictions on liquor sales, on the display of arms and on canvassing near a polling station are statutory or rest on other penal and excise law. A campaigner may therefore break the Code and the law in the same act, break the Code alone, or break the law alone, and the forum that answers differs in each case.

The case for leaving the Code non-statutory is a serious one and is made by serious people, including former Commissioners who enforced it. A code that must be revised between elections to meet new campaign practices, from paid news to social media manipulation, can be revised by consultation in weeks, while a statute takes a Parliament that may not sit until the election is over. A code that binds by consent carries a legitimacy that parties helped to write, and its breach is judged politically as well as administratively. Above all, codification would hand every Code dispute to the courts in the middle of a campaign, and Article 329 exists precisely to keep the campaign out of the courtroom: a statutory code would manufacture the very mid-election litigation the Constitution forbids in substance, dressed as enforcement of the Code itself. The case for statutory backing is equally serious. A code enforced by advisories and censures against parties and candidates who face no legal consequence is a weak instrument against a determined offender, and its weakness is not evenly distributed. A censure that a small party fears may be absorbed as a cost of campaigning by a large one, and the perception that enforcement is uneven corrodes the authority on which the whole consensual structure rests. Proposals for codification, including recommendations associated with parliamentary committees and the Law Commission's work on electoral reform, have therefore recurred for decades, and the Commission's own position has shifted across time between defending the consensual model and seeking statutory teeth for parts of it. This guide adjudicates neither side. What the record supports is the narrower proposition that the Code's force is real but borrowed: it is borrowed from Article 324, from party consent and from publicity, and every argument about codifying it is an argument about whether borrowed force is enough.

Beneath the Code, the formal subordinate instruments do quieter work. The Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 prescribe the forms on which a citizen claims inclusion, objects to an entry, seeks a correction or asks for a transposition, the manner of the registration officer's inquiry and the appeal that lies from the officer's order. The Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961 prescribe the form of the nomination paper, the conduct of the poll, the counting of votes, the custody of election papers and the form of the account of election expenses. The Symbols Order of 1968, which is the Commission's own instrument issued under Article 324 read with the conduct rules, classifies political parties as national or state parties by electoral performance, reserves symbols for recognised parties so that a party's symbol is not allotted to another candidate, allots free symbols to the candidates of unrecognised parties and independents, and provides in its paragraph on rival groups the mechanism by which the Commission decides which of two groups claiming to be a party is entitled to its name and symbol, applying tests drawn from the party's constitution, its organisational elections and its support among legislators and office bearers. The decision in such a dispute, first worked out in the litigation that followed the split in the Communist Party and the decision in Sadiq Ali v Election Commission, is administrative in form and profound in effect, because the group that loses the symbol usually loses the party's public identity with it. Registration of a party with the Commission under the 1951 Act, which requires a memorandum, a commitment to the Constitution and a fee, is a separate and lesser thing than recognition under the Symbols Order: registration makes a body a political party in law, recognition gives it a reserved symbol, broadcast time and other advantages, and the confusion of the two is another small routing error this field specialises in.

## The appointment question: who chooses the choosers

The Commission's authority is constitutional, but the Commission's members arrive by appointment, and for most of the republic's history the manner of their arrival was the least examined part of the structure. Article 324 provides that the Chief Election Commissioner and the Election Commissioners shall be appointed by the President subject to the provisions of any law made in that behalf by Parliament. Parliament made no such law for over seven decades. In its absence, appointments were made by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers, which in practice meant that the government of the day selected the persons who would conduct the elections at which that government would seek a renewed mandate. The arrangement was not a secret and not a scandal in itself; it was the ordinary executive method applied to an office whose defining requirement is visible independence from the executive. Reform bodies returned to the point across the decades. The committee on electoral reforms chaired by Dinesh Goswami in 1990 recommended a consultative appointment process. The Law Commission of India, in its two hundred and fifty-fifth report on electoral reforms submitted in 2015, recommended appointment by the President on the recommendation of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition and the Chief Justice of India, with a secretariat for the Commission and expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund. The recommendation was not enacted, and the vacuum the Constitution had contemplated filling remained filled by executive practice.

The vacuum was filled by a court instead. In Anoop Baranwal v Union of India, a Constitution Bench of five judges of the Supreme Court delivered judgment on 2 March 2023. The Bench held that the absence of a law under Article 324 had left the appointment of the persons charged with conducting free and fair elections entirely in the hands of the executive, that free and fair elections form part of the basic structure of the Constitution, and that the appointment process had to be insulated from executive exclusivity until Parliament legislated. The Court directed that, until a law was made, the Chief Election Commissioner and the Election Commissioners be appointed by the President on the advice of a committee consisting of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the House of the People, or the leader of the single largest opposition party in that House where there is no recognised Leader of the Opposition, and the Chief Justice of India. The direction was expressly interim. It governed only until Parliament exercised the power Article 324 had always given it, and the judgment said so in terms. The composition the Court chose was the composition the reform bodies had long proposed, and its logic was triangular: the head of the government, the head of the opposition and the head of the judiciary, so that no single interest controlled the choice.

Parliament then legislated within the year. The Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023, Act 49 of 2023, received the assent of the President on 28 December 2023 and repealed and replaced the Election Commission (Conditions of Service of Election Commissioners and Transaction of Business) Act, 1991, which had governed service conditions without touching appointments. The 2023 Act constitutes a Search Committee, headed by the Minister of Law and Justice with two members not below the rank of Secretary to the Government of India, which prepares a panel of five names. It constitutes a Selection Committee consisting of the Prime Minister as chairperson, the Leader of the Opposition in the House of the People as a member, and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister as a member, and it provides that the appointment is made by the President on the Selection Committee's recommendation, that the Committee may consider persons beyond the panel, and that an appointment is not invalidated by a vacancy in the Committee. The Act fixes a term of six years or until the age of sixty-five, whichever is earlier, bars reappointment, sets eligibility at persons who hold or have held a post equivalent to Secretary to the Government of India and who are persons of integrity with knowledge of and experience in the management and conduct of elections, and equates salary and conditions with those of the Cabinet Secretary. The composition is the point of controversy, and it must be stated exactly: the Chief Justice of India, whom the Constitution Bench had placed on the interim committee, is not on the statutory committee, and the third seat belongs to a minister of the government nominated by the Prime Minister, so that the government of the day commands two of the three voices in the room.

The two positions on that composition deserve statement at full strength, in the terms their proponents use, because this guide's charter requires it and because the matter is pending before the Supreme Court. The case for the statutory composition runs as follows. Article 324 commits the appointment law to Parliament, and a court direction issued to fill a vacuum cannot bind the content of the law that fills it, or the interim arrangement would become a permanent amendment of the Constitution by judgment. The inclusion of the Chief Justice in an executive appointment draws the judiciary into a political process, exposes the head of the judiciary to lobbying and to later litigation about the appointees the Chief Justice helped to select, and sits uneasily with the separation of powers that the same judgment invoked. Democratic accountability points the other way: the persons who conduct elections should be chosen by persons answerable to the electorate, and a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition and a senior minister is such a body, with the opposition's presence guaranteeing that the choice cannot be made in secret or without a dissenting voice in the room. The case against the statutory composition runs as follows. The concern that produced the litigation was not secrecy but independence: an institution that must restrain the government during a campaign cannot be selected by a committee in which the government holds a majority, because the appointee knows to whom the appointment is owed and the public knows it too, and the appearance of dependence damages the Commission even where an individual Commissioner acts with complete integrity. The interim committee's design answered that concern by giving the third vote to an office outside electoral politics, and replacing that vote with a second government voice reproduces, in statutory form, the executive predominance the Constitution Bench held incompatible with free and fair elections. On this view the Act complies with the letter of the judgment, which permitted Parliament to legislate, while defeating its reasoning, which was that selection must not rest with the executive alone in substance.

The Act has been challenged, and the challenge is pending. The Supreme Court declined to stay the operation of the Act when interim relief was sought, so the statutory committee has made appointments while the constitutional question remains undecided, and appointments made under a law later struck down would raise questions the Court has not yet had to answer. No outcome is predicted here, and the reader should treat any account that describes the challenge as decided, in either direction, as unreliable. The structural point that survives whatever the Court holds is the one with which this section began. The Constitution contemplated a law on appointments from 1950. The law arrived in 2023, by way of a judgment that governed for the interval between March and December of that year. For the whole period in which the Commission built its authority, its members were chosen by the method the Constitution had left unregulated, and the institution's independence during that period rested on the tenure protections of Article 324, on the removal shield for the Chief Election Commissioner, on the convention of seniority in succession, and on the personal conduct of the persons appointed, rather than on the process that chose them.

### What makes the appointment of Commissioners a constitutional question at all?

It is constitutional because Article 324 itself contemplates a parliamentary law on appointment and none existed for over seven decades, because free and fair elections are part of the basic structure, and because the body being appointed is the body that conducts the elections at which the appointing government seeks re-election.

The tenure protections complete the institutional picture. Article 324 provides that the Chief Election Commissioner shall not be removed from office except in like manner and on the like grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court, which means an address by each House of Parliament supported by the special majority the Constitution prescribes for judicial removal, on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity. An Election Commissioner may not be removed except on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner, a shield that protects the Commissioners from the executive while subordinating them, for this purpose, to the head of their own institution. The conditions of service of neither may be varied to disadvantage after appointment. The Commission decides by majority where its members differ, under the transaction of business provisions that the 2023 Act re-enacts from the 1991 Act, and the Chief Election Commissioner holds a primacy of office rather than a veto. Two gaps in the protective architecture are regularly noted by the reform literature and belong in an honest map. The Commission's expenditure is voted by Parliament rather than charged on the Consolidated Fund, as the Law Commission recommended it should be, so the institution depends on the annual budget process of the government it regulates. And the constitutional shield against removal is at its strongest for the Chief Election Commissioner and derivative for the Election Commissioners, an asymmetry the Anoop Baranwal litigation noted and the 2023 Act did not alter.

## The courts: the bar, the exception and the petition

The judicial compartment of election law is defined by exclusion first and intervention second. Article 329 provides that no election to either House of Parliament or to a House of a state legislature shall be called in question except by an election petition presented to such authority and in such manner as may be provided for by or under any law made by the appropriate legislature. The 1951 Act makes the provision concrete. An election petition may be presented by a candidate at the election or by an elector, to the High Court of the state concerned, within forty-five days from the date of election of the returned candidate. The petition must plead material facts, it is tried by the High Court sitting as an election court under a procedure that follows the civil procedure code subject to the Act, and an appeal lies to the Supreme Court. The grounds on which the High Court may declare an election void are set out in the Act: that the returned candidate was not qualified or was disqualified, that a corrupt practice was committed by the returned candidate or the election agent or with consent, that a nomination was improperly rejected, that the result was materially affected by improper acceptance of a nomination, by a corrupt practice committed in the interests of the returned candidate by another person with consent, by improper reception or refusal of votes, or by non-compliance with the Constitution, the Act or the rules. The court may declare the petitioner or another candidate duly elected in the cases the Act permits. The companion bar in Article 329 also protects delimitation: the validity of a law relating to the delimitation of constituencies or the allotment of seats shall not be called in question in any court, and the Supreme Court has read the orders of a Delimitation Commission as carrying a comparable finality, subject to the narrowest exceptions.

The settlement these provisions create is severe and deliberate. A voter who believes a nomination was wrongly accepted, a candidate who believes the count was corrupted, a party that believes the campaign was bought, all must wait until the result is declared and then proceed by petition, with the returned candidate continuing to sit and vote while the petition is tried. Petitions are expected to be tried expeditiously, and the Act directs day to day trial, yet the lived pattern across decades is that many petitions outlast a substantial part of the term they challenge, which hollows the remedy without formally denying it. The reasons are structural rather than mysterious: the pleading standard is strict and generates preliminary litigation, the proof standard for corrupt practices approaches the criminal standard, the trial requires evidence from a constituency at scale, and the appellate route adds a second full hearing. The detailed treatment of that record, its grounds and its time limits belongs to [the article on election petitions and disqualification](/2018/03/15/election-petitions-disqualification-india/), and the thematic conclusion for this guide is the one the routing table demonstrates: the petition is a powerful remedy pointed at a narrow target, the declared result of a completed election, and it answers almost none of the questions an election generates while the election is still alive.

The narrow exception to the bar completes the doctrine. Where the Commission or an election officer acts wholly without jurisdiction, or where the challenge is not to an election at all but to a law or a provision under which elections are held, the courts retain their ordinary constitutional jurisdiction. Challenges to the validity of the election statutes themselves, to the constitutional amendments that frame the subject and to provisions such as the appointment law of 2023 are therefore heard on their merits and are not election petitions in disguise. The line is between questioning an election, which must wait, and questioning the law under which elections are conducted, which need not. Litigants test the line in every cycle, and the courts police it by asking whether allowing the petition would interrupt the process the Constitution ordered to run uninterrupted from notification to declaration. That question, rather than the sympathy a case excites, is the one that decides whether a mid-election writ lives or dies.

## Disqualification after election: three doors that look alike

A member who is said to have become disqualified after election stands at a junction where three different doors open, and choosing the wrong one wastes the challenge entirely, which is why this guide separates them with care. The first door is Articles 103 and 192 of the Constitution. A question whether a sitting member has become subject to a disqualification under the constitutional list or under the law Parliament has made is referred to the President for a member of Parliament, or to the Governor for a member of a state legislature, and the President or Governor obtains the opinion of the Election Commission and acts according to that opinion. The courts have held the opinion to be binding in substance, the constitutional head's order to follow it, and the Commission's inquiry in such a reference to be a proceeding in which the member is entitled to be heard. This door covers conviction, office of profit, insolvency and the other standing disqualifications. It does not cover the question whether the member's election was valid, which belongs to the petition, and it does not cover defection, which belongs to the third door.

The second door is the Commission's own disqualification power in the specific territory the 1951 Act assigns to it, the clearest example being the failure to lodge the account of election expenses, where the Commission inquires and declares the person disqualified for three years from the date of its order. The third door is the Tenth Schedule, which governs defection alone: the question whether a member has become subject to disqualification on the ground of defection is decided by the Speaker or the Chairman of the House, whose decision the Schedule originally sought to make final and beyond judicial question. The Constitution Bench in Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu, decided in 1992, upheld the Schedule while striking down the paragraph that ousted the jurisdiction of the courts, holding that the presiding officer deciding a defection question acts as a tribunal and that the decision is open to judicial review on the ordinary grounds, though review ordinarily follows the decision rather than preceding it. The defection ground itself, the voluntary giving up of party membership, the defiance of a whip, the surviving merger exception and the record of presiding officers deciding slowly or not at all belong to [the article on the anti-defection law](/2018/01/15/anti-defection-law-india-explained/), which owns the Tenth Schedule in this series. The map level point is the contrast the three doors display. For ordinary disqualification the Constitution trusts the Election Commission's opinion and binds the constitutional head to it. For defection it trusts the presiding officer of the House and lets the courts in only afterwards. The difference is a deliberate allocation of trust, made in 1985, between an external constitutional body and the internal authority of the legislature, and almost every controversy about defection decisions since is an argument about whether that allocation was wise.

### Why does one Commission conduct both parliamentary and assembly elections?

Because Article 324 vests the conduct of elections to Parliament and to the legislatures of the states in the same Election Commission, working through a single machinery of Chief Electoral Officers, District Election Officers and Returning Officers drawn largely from the state administrations and placed under the Commission's direction for election work.

## Delimitation, presidential elections and the state commissions

Delimitation is the drawing of the map on which everything else is played, and its law belongs to the constitutional layer as much as to any statute. Articles 82 and 170 direct that the allocation of seats and the division of states into constituencies be readjusted after each census by such authority and in such manner as Parliament may by law determine. Parliament's instrument is the Delimitation Act of the relevant cycle, constituting a Delimitation Commission whose orders have the force of law and are laid before the legislatures without power of modification. The constitutional amendments layered on that foundation are the politics of the subject: the allocation of seats among the states was frozen on the 1971 census by the Forty-second Amendment and the freeze was extended by the Eighty-fourth Amendment until the first census taken after the year 2026, in order that states which had controlled population growth should not lose representation for having done so, while the Eighty-seventh Amendment permitted the boundaries and the reservation of seats within each state to be readjusted on the 2001 census without altering the number of seats a state holds. The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, 2023, which reserves seats for women in the House of the People and the state assemblies, is expressly tied to a delimitation undertaken after the relevant census, which binds the largest pending change in the composition of Indian legislatures to the same event that ends the freeze. The detail of the Acts, the commissions and the contests over the freeze belongs to the guide to the Delimitation Acts already linked in this article. The thematic point is that delimitation is the one part of election law where the Constitution itself postpones the reckoning, and the postponement, rather than any statute, is what every argument in the field is waiting on.

The elections to the offices of President and Vice-President stand apart from the general election machinery and a complete map marks their separateness. The electoral college for the President consists of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and of the state legislative assemblies, voting by secret ballot under proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, with the value of votes weighted to secure uniformity among the states and parity between the states and the Union under Article 55. The Vice-President is elected by the members of both Houses of Parliament by the same system. The Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952 supplies the conduct law, and Article 71 assigns all doubts and disputes arising out of such an election to the Supreme Court, whose decision is final, while protecting acts done by a President or Vice-President whose election is later declared void. The contrast with parliamentary elections is exact. A dispute about a member's election goes to a High Court by petition. A dispute about the head of state's election goes to the Supreme Court alone, because the Constitution treats the office as one whose occupancy cannot be left in suspense before a forum of first instance scattered across the states.

Below the state legislatures lies a further electoral tier that Article 324 does not reach, and naming it prevents a common overstatement of the Commission's empire. Elections to Panchayats and Municipalities are vested by Articles 243K and 243ZA in State Election Commissions, separate constitutional bodies appointed under state law, which prepare the rolls and conduct the polls for local bodies. The Election Commission of India does not conduct a municipal election, does not prepare the panchayat roll, and does not decide a local election dispute, which travels by the petition route state law provides. The two families of commissions coexist in every state capital, occasionally sharing officers and always sharing public confusion, and the citizen with a panchayat roll complaint who writes to the Election Commission of India has made the routing error with which this guide began, in its most everyday form. Cooperative society elections, university elections and the internal elections of political parties stand outside even that structure, governed by their own statutes, rules and party constitutions, with the Commission's role in party affairs confined to registration, recognition and symbol disputes under the instruments already described.

## The historical sequence: how the field acquired its shape

The field was built in a founding burst and then altered at identifiable moments, and the sequence explains features that a purely structural account leaves mysterious. The Constitution came into force in January 1950 with the electoral chapter complete, and the Commission was constituted at once, with Sukumar Sen, a member of the Indian Civil Service, as the first Chief Election Commissioner. The provisional Parliament enacted the two Representation of the People Acts in 1950 and 1951, and the first general elections were held across the winter of 1951 to 1952 on a roll prepared for a largely illiterate electorate, using separate ballot boxes for each candidate before the marking ballot and then the electronic machine simplified the act of voting. The founding choices set the pattern that has never been displaced: a single constitutional commission, a universal adult franchise adopted at a stroke rather than expanded in stages, first past the post elections for the directly elected Houses, and a roll maintained as a permanent administrative function. Each later change has been an amendment of that settlement rather than a replacement of it, which is why the field reads as one continuous structure rather than a succession of regimes.

The second phase was judicial. The courts defined the boundaries of the Commission's power and of their own abstention in the line of cases this guide has described, from the abstention of Ponnuswami through the plenary reading of Article 324 in Mohinder Singh Gill to the finality of delimitation and the reviewability of defection decisions in Kihoto Hollohan. The same phase settled the Commission's internal constitution. The Commission began as a single member body. Two Election Commissioners were appointed in 1989 shortly before a general election and the posts were abolished soon after, and in 1993 the Commission was made permanently multi member by ordinance and then by the service conditions legislation. When the serving Chief Election Commissioner, T. N. Seshan, whose tenure had transformed the public understanding of the office through aggressive enforcement of the Code and of electoral law, challenged the parity of the Election Commissioners, the Supreme Court held in 1995 that the Commission decides as a body, that the Chief Election Commissioner holds a primacy in the removal protection and in administration but no superiority of vote, and that the multi member design was constitutional. The institution a reader meets in the appointment debate of the present structure is therefore a three member body that decides by majority, a design settled by litigation rather than by the constitutional text, which leaves the number of Commissioners to the President's determination.

The third phase was the disclosure phase, and it was driven by litigation brought by civil society organisations rather than by Parliament. In Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms, decided in 2002, the Supreme Court held that a voter has a right to know the antecedents of a candidate, deriving the right from the freedom of expression, and directed the Commission to require affidavits disclosing criminal cases, assets, liabilities and educational qualifications. When Parliament legislated to dilute the direction, the Court struck the dilution down in 2003 and the disclosure regime survived in the form the Commission's orders and the amended conduct rules give it. The affidavit a candidate files at nomination is therefore a judicial creation administered by statute and rules, and its contents feed the public databases on which much electoral journalism rests. A false affidavit became, in turn, a ground on which elections and candidatures are attacked, and the failure to disclose became a contested species of corrupt practice and of undue influence in the case law that followed. The fourth phase, overlapping the third, was technological: the statutory authorisation of voting machines, their phased adoption until paper ballots disappeared from parliamentary and assembly elections, the judicial acceptance of the machines coupled with the demand for a paper trail, the introduction of the voter verifiable paper audit trail, and the litigation over how many stations' slips must be counted to make the trail a real audit rather than a ritual. The fifth phase is the appointment phase already narrated, in which the composition of the Commission itself became the contested question. A field whose phases arrive by judgment as often as by legislation is a field in which the case law is not commentary on the law. It is a large part of the law, and the reader who studies only the statutes will miss the disclosure regime, the paper trail's counting rules and the interim appointment arrangement alike.

## Overlaps, gaps and the questions the map cannot answer

A thematic guide earns its place by naming the overlaps and gaps that single statute accounts pass over, and this field has four of each worth recording. The first overlap is between the roll and citizenship. Registration requires citizenship, the registration officer must decide disputed claims, and the same person's citizenship may be in issue in other legal settings governed by other statutes and authorities, so that an electoral finding and a citizenship finding can diverge with the person a voter in one record and a doubtful citizen in another. The second overlap is between the Model Code and the penal law, already described: the same speech may attract a Code censure, a prosecution for promoting enmity and a corrupt practice charge in a petition, in three forums on three clocks, with no mechanism coordinating them. The third overlap is between the Commission and the state governments whose officers conduct the election. The District Election Officer, the Returning Officer and the polling staff are state officials placed at the Commission's disposal, and the Commission's power to direct their transfer during an election sits on top of a service relationship that belongs to the state, a duality that works because both sides accept it and strains whenever they do not. The fourth overlap is between the expenditure account of the candidate and the finances of the party, where the regulated unit and the spending unit are different persons in law.

The gaps are more instructive. There is no law governing the internal democracy of political parties: the Commission registers parties and requires a party constitution professing democratic organisation, but it has no general power to compel a party to hold internal elections, to supervise them, or to deregister a party for failing to hold them, a limitation the courts have underlined. There is no state funding of elections, despite recommendations in its favour running from the Goswami Committee through the Law Commission's one hundred and seventieth report of 1999 on reform of the election laws to the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution. There is no general law of political finance beyond the disclosure and donation provisions scattered across the representation, companies and tax statutes, which is why a single instrument such as the electoral bond could rearrange the field and why its striking down rearranged it back. And there is the standing proposal for simultaneous elections to the House of the People and the state assemblies, which a high level committee chaired by the former President, Ram Nath Kovind, examined and reported on in favour, and which was carried into amendment bills introduced in Parliament and referred to a joint parliamentary committee, where the proposal rests as a pending legislative question whose constitutional path, requiring amendment of the term provisions of the Houses and ratification questions the committee debated, this guide describes without forecasting. Each gap is a place where the map runs out and politics continues without a legal route, and naming them protects the reader from the opposite error of assuming that because a question is important, some authority must hold power over it.

## The state level in operation

Because the same Commission conducts parliamentary and assembly elections through machinery borrowed from the states, the state level deserves a closer operational account than the constitutional sections gave it. The Chief Electoral Officer of a state is ordinarily a senior officer of the state administration, appointed by the Commission in consultation with the state government, and the office becomes, during an election, the Commission's nerve centre in the state: the roll revision is supervised there, the programme is administered there, complaints travel upward through it, and the Commission's directions are translated into district orders there. The District Election Officer, ordinarily the district magistrate, carries the corresponding responsibility for the district, and the Returning Officer for each constituency answers for that constituency's nomination, polling, counting and declaration. Over this standing structure the Commission lays a temporary one for each election. General observers, drawn from the Indian Administrative Service of other states, watch the conduct of the poll and the count. Police observers watch the deployment of force. Expenditure observers, drawn from the revenue services, watch the money. The observers report to the Commission and not to the government of the state in which they serve, and the Commission's power to act on their reports, by ordering a repoll at a station, transferring an officer or requiring a fresh scrutiny of accounts, is Article 324 in its daily dress. Central armed police forces are deployed on the Commission's assessment of a constituency's vulnerability, a judgment that weighs the history of violence, the sensitivity of the contest and the requests of the candidates, and the phased conduct of a general election across several polling days is principally a device for moving that force and the polling staff from one region to the next. The scale is administered without a single election services cadre, a design choice the founding generation made in favour of the district administration and that every proposal for a dedicated election service has so far failed to displace.

Elections to the Council of States and to the state legislative councils follow a different logic and expose the limits of the direct election model that dominates public understanding. Members of the Council of States are elected by the elected members of the state legislative assemblies by proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, in accordance with the system of preferences marked on an open ballot, the secrecy of the ballot having been displaced for these elections by an amendment upheld in Kuldip Nayar v Union of India, where the Supreme Court sustained the open ballot and the removal of the domicile requirement against challenges founded on the federal character of the chamber and the secrecy principle. Legislative council elections divide among graduates, teachers, local authority and assembly constituencies, each with its own roll and its own electorate defined by the 1950 Act. The thematic lesson is that Indian election law contains two electoral systems living side by side, plurality in single member constituencies for the directly elected Houses and proportional representation by transferable vote for the indirect ones, together with the reserved constituencies through which the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes are returned to the direct Houses on joint electorates, a settlement that rejected separate electorates and that the delimitation process operationalises each cycle. A guide that treated the field as a single system would miss the extent to which the Constitution chooses the electoral system afresh for each chamber it creates.

One further class of elector tests the machinery from the opposite direction, the elector who by law or by circumstance cannot attend the polling station of the constituency at all. The 1951 Act has long provided postal ballots for defined classes, among them the service voter, the member of the armed forces or of a force to which the Army Act applies, the government servant posted abroad and the spouse residing with such a person, together with electors under preventive detention, whose statutory right to vote the Act preserves while the machinery of the detention order carries the ballot to them. The electronically transmitted postal ballot system moved the service voter's ballot from a paper journey measured in weeks to a transmission measured in minutes, with the marked ballot returning by post for counting, and the reform closed one of the oldest gaps between the formal universality of the franchise and its practical exercise by the persons posted farthest from their constituencies. Facilitated voting at home, extended by rule and direction to electors of advanced age and to electors with disabilities who cannot travel to a station, applies the same reasoning inside the constituency: the polling team attends the elector, secrecy is preserved by the prescribed procedure, and the vote enters the count with the postal ballots. Each extension is a small statutory or subordinate act, and together they mark the direction of the franchise law's unfinished business, which is no longer the definition of who may vote but the logistics of a vote that distance, service, age and disability place out of reach of the ordinary polling station.

## The reform debate: repair, consolidation and the pending questions

Reform of this field is a permanent industry, and a thematic guide should organise its proposals by the layer of the map each proposal would change, because a proposal's layer predicts its difficulty. Proposals that change administrative practice are the easiest: the Commission itself has adopted many without legislation, from the standardisation of the candidate affidavit to the counting protocol for paper trail slips. Proposals that change the subordinate layer are next: expenditure ceilings are revised by rule, the Symbols Order is the Commission's to amend. Proposals that change the statutes require Parliament, and the record there is mixed. The disqualification for conviction was transformed by a judgment rather than by an amendment. The protection for sitting members that the judgment struck down had itself been a statutory choice. The bar on persons in lawful custody voting, the disqualification of a person whose name is on the roll of a constituency in which the person is not ordinarily resident for council elections, and the many smaller rules of the conduct framework are all statutory choices Parliament could revisit in an ordinary session if the will existed.

Three statutory proposals dominate the debate and each illustrates a different obstacle. The first is the decriminalisation of candidature: proposals, supported at different times by the Commission and examined by the Law Commission in its report on electoral disqualifications, to bar a person from contesting where a court has framed charges for a serious offence, rather than waiting for conviction. The obstacle is the presumption of innocence and the demonstrated risk that charges can be engineered against a rival, so the proposal has repeatedly been commended in principle and withheld in practice, leaving the disclosure regime as the operative answer: the voter is told, and the voter decides. The second is the voting rights of electors who cannot reach the polling station of their constituency, including overseas electors registered under the provision the 1950 Act inserted for citizens absent from India on account of employment, education or otherwise, who must at present vote in person at their constituency. A bill to permit proxy voting for such electors was passed by the House of the People in 2018 and lapsed on the dissolution of that House without passing the Council of States, and postal and facilitated voting has since expanded for service voters and for the oldest electors and electors with disabilities at home, leaving the overseas elector as the conspicuous remainder. The third is the proposal for simultaneous elections, which sits one layer higher still, because the terms of the Houses are constitutional text: aligning them requires amendment of the duration provisions and of the dissolution powers, and the argument turns on whether the savings and governance benefits claimed for a fixed cycle outweigh the federal cost of truncating or extending the mandate of state assemblies whose electors chose them for a different period. A reader who sorts the pending questions by layer will not be surprised by which of them move and which of them wait.

Above the statutes sits the constitutional layer of reform, where the appointment question already decided provisionally by judgment and provisionally by statute awaits final adjudication, and where two older proposals recur. One is to charge the Commission's expenditure on the Consolidated Fund of India, removing the institution from the annual voted budget, a change the Law Commission recommended and that requires no amendment to the Commission's powers at all, only to the financial procedure that sustains it. The other is to give the Commission an independent secretariat of its own, on the model of the secretariats of Parliament, rather than a staff drawn on deputation from the government of the day. Both proposals share a premise the appointment debate also rests on: that independence is a property of systems and budgets no less than of persons, and that a commission selected impeccably can still be weakened by dependence in its money and its staff. The honest summary of the reform record is that the field changes fastest where a court supplies the rule, next fastest where the Commission supplies it, and slowest where Parliament must, and that this ordering, rather than any disagreement about the merits, explains the shape of the law a reader actually encounters.

## Working the map: four grievances traced to their doors

The routing table that follows compresses the map into a single view, and this section slows the same material down into narrative, because the commonest failure in this field is a failure of sequence. Consider first the voter who reaches the polling station and finds the name missing. The election cannot help that voter. The roll was frozen for the constituency when the last date for nominations passed, the presiding officer has no power to add a name, the Commission will not direct an addition mid poll, and a writ petition will meet Article 329. The voter's remedy belonged to the revision that preceded the election: a claim for inclusion before the Electoral Registration Officer, an appeal within the machinery, and, after the final publication of the roll, vigilance during the continuous updation window that runs until the freeze. The lesson the case teaches is that roll rights are exercised in the quiet season or not at all, and every campaign to register voters before an election is an acknowledgment of that legal fact.

Consider the candidate whose nomination the Returning Officer rejects on scrutiny day for a defect in the affidavit. The candidate cannot appeal to the Commission, cannot obtain a writ that interrupts the election, and cannot be restored to the ballot by any order operating in that contest. If the rejection was improper and the defect was not of a substantial character, the rejection is a ground on which the election of the returned candidate may be declared void in an election petition, because the statute treats improper rejection as vitiating the election without proof of material effect on the result. The candidate's real assets are therefore preventive: a nomination filed early enough to cure defects, in more than one set of papers where the rules permit, with the affidavit complete and the deposit proved. Consider next the legislator who crosses the floor and joins the party the legislator was elected against. No election petition lies, the Commission has no disqualification reference to answer, and the Governor and the President are not in the picture at all. The petition travels to the Speaker or the Chairman, who decides as a tribunal, at a pace no statute fixes, and the courts enter only after the decision, on review, or, in the exceptional line of cases, where the presiding officer's failure to decide has itself become the grievance and the Court has indicated that a decision should ordinarily come within a reasonable period. Consider finally the campaign speech that promotes enmity between communities. Three proceedings may follow from the same words: a complaint to the Commission under the Model Code, answered by a notice and possibly a censure or a campaign restraint within days; a prosecution under the penal law and the electoral offences, answered by a criminal court over years; and, if the speaker is elected, a corrupt practice charge inside an election petition, answered by the High Court after the result. The complainant who understands the map files in all three places for three different purposes. The complainant who does not files in one place and waits for a remedy that forum was never built to give.

### Where does the Constitution itself speak about elections, and where does it fall silent?

The Constitution speaks on the Commission, the franchise, the single roll, legislative competence, the bar on court interference, disqualification and defection. It falls silent on campaign finance, on the internal life of parties, on the Model Code and on the conduct of the poll, and those silences are where the statutes, the orders and the case law carry the weight.

## The limit of the reservoir: when the statute speaks first

The plenary reading of Article 324 has a boundary, and the boundary case is as important as the power case. In A. C. Jose v Sivan Pillai, decided in 1984, the Supreme Court considered the Commission's direction to use voting machines at a bye election at a time when the 1951 Act and the Conduct of Elections Rules prescribed the ballot paper and the ballot box and made no provision for machines. The Court set the direction aside, holding that the power under Article 324 does not extend to overriding an express statutory provision, that the reservoir operates only where the law is silent, and that a change in the very method of voting required legislation. Parliament responded by amending the 1951 Act to authorise voting by machine, and the electronic era of Indian elections therefore began with a statute passed because the Court had refused to let the constitutional phrase do the work of one. The two decisions together state the doctrine completely. Where the statute book is silent, the Commission may act and its action has constitutional force. Where the statute book speaks, it governs, and the Commission's remedy is to ask Parliament to change it. A reader testing any contested act of the Commission should ask the Jose question first: is there a provision on this point, and if there is, what does it say? Half the public argument about the Commission's powers dissolves once that question is put in the right order, because acts denounced as usurpation frequently turn out to be exercises of the reservoir in genuinely unoccupied ground, and acts defended as housekeeping occasionally turn out to contradict a rule.

The Commission's lesser instruments fill the remaining space between statute and event. The handbooks issued for Returning Officers, Presiding Officers and counting staff translate the Acts and rules into operational instruction and are revised for each cycle of elections. The compendium of instructions gathers the Commission's standing directions on subjects from the custody of machines to the treatment of tendered votes. Directions issued under specific provisions, such as the direction requiring the candidate affidavit, have the force the parent provision gives them, and directions issued under Article 324 in unoccupied ground have the force the Mohinder Singh Gill doctrine gives them, subject to the Jose limit. None of this material is enacted, and a lawyer advising on an election problem who reads only the two Acts and the two sets of rules will repeatedly miss the instrument that actually governs the point. The thematic lesson generalises beyond elections. Indian public law is administered through layers of instruction beneath its statutes, and the skill this series exists to teach, in this cluster as in every other, is the skill of identifying which layer a given rule lives in and what follows from its address.

## Reserved seats and the joint electorate

The composition of the directly elected Houses carries one further constitutional settlement that a complete map records. Articles 330 and 332 reserve seats in the House of the People and in the state legislative assemblies for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, in proportion, as nearly as may be, to their population in the state, within constituencies that are otherwise ordinary territorial constituencies in which every elector votes. The design is the joint electorate: the candidate must belong to the reserved community, the electorate belongs to everyone. The settlement displaced the separate electorates of the colonial period after the confrontation of 1932 between the award of separate electorates to the Depressed Classes and the fast undertaken in protest against it, which produced the Poona agreement for reserved seats within joint electorates, and the Constituent Assembly carried the design into the Constitution as a temporary provision that has been extended by amendment at each decade's turn. The Constitution (One Hundred and Fourth Amendment) Act, 2019 extended the reservation for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes until 2030 while ending the reserved nomination of Anglo-Indian members to the House and the assemblies, closing a smaller and older chapter of communal representation in the same stroke. Delimitation gives the settlement its geography, designating which constituencies are reserved in each cycle by population and rotation, which is why the delimitation section of this guide and this section belong to the same conversation. The electoral consequence a thematic account should not miss is that reservation in this design disciplines candidates rather than electorates: a party contesting a reserved constituency must find its candidate within the community, while it must persuade an electorate of all communities, a combination that shapes candidate selection in a large fraction of the constituencies of every general election.

## The campaign beyond the Code: paid news, platforms and the silence period

The statutory and consensual instruments described in this guide were drafted for a campaign of rallies, posters and print, and the strain of the digital campaign on that inheritance is the field's most active frontier of improvisation. Paid news, the publication of favourable coverage for consideration disguised as reporting, is not an offence under the 1951 Act in terms, and the Commission's response has been to treat the value of such coverage as election expenditure of the candidate where the connection is established, pursued through the media certification and monitoring committees that also pre-certify political advertisements on electronic media. The committees themselves are an administrative creation, resting on the Cable Television Networks law for the certification requirement and on the Commission's directions for the rest. For the general elections of 2019 the Commission and the Internet and Mobile Association of India announced a voluntary code of ethics under which the major platforms undertook to act on Code violations reported through a dedicated channel, to carry voter education and to observe the silence period, an arrangement that extends the consensual technique of the Model Code to companies that are not parties, are not candidates and are not Indian public bodies at all. The silence period itself, the forty-eight hours ending with the close of poll during which the 1951 Act forbids public meetings, processions and the display of election matter on electronic media, was drafted before a campaign could continue on a telephone in every voter's pocket, and its application to digital content is worked out through the voluntary code and the Commission's directions rather than through the section's own text. The pattern across all three examples is the pattern of the whole field in accelerated form: a gap appears, the reservoir power and consent fill it provisionally, litigation or legislation follows at a distance, and the interim arrangement hardens into the practice everyone treats as law. A reader who has grasped the Model Code's borrowed force has grasped the mechanism by which the digital campaign is governed as well.

## Parties on the register: recognition and its limits

The political party is the central actor of every election and the least regulated institution in the field, and the precise extent of the Commission's authority over parties is a subject on which confident error flourishes. Registration under the 1951 Act is obtained by an association that submits its memorandum and constitution, undertakes to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and to the principles of socialism, secularism and democracy, and undertakes to uphold the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. Registration confers legal identity as a political party and access to the allotment of a symbol, and it is the gateway to the income tax exemption that party finance law provides. What registration does not confer is any general supervisory jurisdiction in the Commission over the party's internal affairs. In Indian National Congress (I) v Institute of Social Welfare, decided in 2002, the Supreme Court held that the Commission has no general power to deregister a party, and that deregistration is possible only in narrow exceptional situations, such as registration obtained by fraud or a party's own communication that it has changed in a manner that takes it outside the registration provision. The Commission cannot compel a registered party to hold internal elections on a schedule the Commission approves, cannot annul a party election, and cannot strike a party off the register for inactivity, however long the party goes without contesting an election. Proposals to confer such powers have been made by the Commission itself and have not been enacted, and the gap is deliberate in effect if not in design: the law treats the party as a voluntary association whose internal life the Constitution's freedom of association protects, and regulates the party only at the points where it meets the election, in registration, in symbols, in expenditure disclosure and in the affidavits of its candidates.

Recognition is the second and more valuable status, and it is governed by the Symbols Order rather than by the registration provision. A party is recognised as a state party in a state, or as a national party, by satisfying performance criteria the Order prescribes, measured in votes polled and seats won at general elections to the House of the People or the state assembly, and reviewed after elections on the results as they stand. Recognition carries the reserved symbol, which no other candidate in the territory of recognition may use, together with advantages in broadcast and telecast time on the public media, in the supply of electoral rolls and in the number of proposers a candidate requires. The reserved symbol is the party's most important campaign asset in an electorate where the symbol on the ballot unit is the element every voter can identify, and the Commission's adjudication between rival groups claiming to be the recognised party is correspondingly consequential. The test the Commission applies in such a dispute, settled in the line of decisions beginning with Sadiq Ali, weighs the aims and objects of the party constitution, the support each group commands in the organisational and legislative wings, and the group's adherence to the party's constitution, and the decision determines which group keeps the name and the symbol while the other must register afresh and take a free symbol. Elections within the party, the selection of its candidates and the expulsion of its members remain outside the Commission's reach throughout, and the member expelled or denied a ticket has remedies, if any, in the party's own constitution and the ordinary courts, and not in the election law this guide maps. The boundary is worth stating as a rule of the field: the Commission regulates the contest between parties and the conduct of candidates, and it does not govern the parties themselves.

## Vacancies, bye elections and the calendar of the field

Between general elections the field keeps its own rhythm, and the law of vacancies is where the rhythm is set. A seat falls vacant by death, by resignation accepted by the presiding officer, by disqualification taking effect, by the election being declared void, by expulsion under the rules of the House, or by a member being chosen to both Houses or to a House and a state legislature and failing to resign one seat within the period the law allows. The 1951 Act requires a bye election to fill a vacancy, and its provision on timing directs that the bye election be held within six months of the vacancy, subject to exceptions the Act states: where the remainder of the term of the member whose seat fell vacant is less than one year, or where the Commission, in consultation with the Central Government, certifies that it is difficult to hold the bye election within the period. The exceptions matter in practice, because a vacancy arising near the end of a House's life is routinely left unfilled, and the composition of the House for its final months is allowed to differ from the composition its electorate chose. The term provisions themselves sit in the Constitution: the House of the People continues for five years from the date of its first meeting unless sooner dissolved, the state assemblies likewise, and the Council of States and the legislative councils are permanent bodies retiring by thirds. A general election is therefore not an event the government of the day schedules at pleasure within the Indian design; it falls due by the calendar, and the Commission's programme is built backward from the date by which the new House must be constituted, which is the deeper reason the Commission, and not the government, announces the schedule and defends its length.

The death of a candidate during a contest tests the same machinery at its most sensitive joint, and the law's treatment of it changed in a way that repays attention. As originally enacted, the death of any candidate before the poll required the Returning Officer to countermand the election and start the process again. The amendment of 1996 confined that consequence to the death of a candidate set up by a recognised political party. The poll proceeds where an independent candidate or a candidate of an unrecognised party dies, while the recognised party whose candidate has died is given time to nominate a substitute, and the election is adjourned and resumed with the substituted name on the ballot. The reasoning is a judgment about what an election is: where the contest is between parties, the death removes one side of the contest and the electorate is entitled to the contest restated; where the candidate stands alone in the relevant sense, the remaining contest is real and delay punishes every other candidate and voter. Critics of the distinction note that it writes the hierarchy of parties into the law of the poll itself, and defenders answer that a rule requiring a fresh election on the death of any of the many independent candidates in a large constituency would make every general election hostage to mortality. The provision is a small, exact example of the field's habits: a practical problem, a statutory answer calibrated to party realities, and an argument about equality that the answer does not pretend to satisfy.

## Three domains, three routes: the routing table

Every section of this guide converges on the table below, which is the artifact the article exists to provide. It begins where a reader actually begins, with a grievance rather than with a statute, and it routes the grievance to the instrument that governs it, the authority that holds power over it, the forum in which it is pursued, the time within which it must be pursued and the remedy that forum can give. The general position described in this guide is legal information and not advice on any individual matter, and limitation periods and forum rules in election cases are unforgiving, so a reader carrying a live grievance should treat the table as a map of where advice must be taken rather than as a substitute for taking it, and should note that several of these routes close while an election is still in progress. The companion study tools that support revision of this map are addressed at the close of the guide.

Read the table by its pattern rather than by its rows and the whole argument of this guide is visible at once. Roll grievances stay inside the election machinery and die at the freeze. Nomination grievances sleep until the result and wake inside a petition. Campaign grievances run to the Commission and are answered in days with remedies that expire with the campaign. Result grievances belong to the High Court and to no one else. Disqualification grievances divide by their ground, to the constitutional head acting on the Commission's opinion, to the Commission itself, or to the presiding officer, and the citizen who cannot tell a defection from a conviction will address the wrong desk with complete confidence and lose the remedy to the delay. The table also exposes the field's deliberate cruelty, which is the price of its central choice. Almost every route that matters is short, and almost every short route closes before the grievance is fully understood by the person holding it. Indian election law prefers a completed election to a corrected one at every joint where the two values conflict, and it has preferred that outcome consistently since Ponnuswami. Whether the preference is right is a question about the purpose of elections themselves: whether the process exists to produce a legislature on a date certain, with errors corrected afterwards where correction remains possible, or to produce the correct result in each constituency whenever correctness can be established. The Constitution chose the first answer. The statutes, the Code and the case law are the working out of that choice, and a reader who sees the choice can predict the answer to a new election problem with surprising reliability by asking a single question: would deciding this question interrupt the process? If it would, the answer is that it waits.

## The Commission and the governments: the working relationship

The machinery sections of this guide describe officers who serve two masters, and the legal device that makes the arrangement work deserves a statement of its own, because it is the device on which the conduct of every large election silently depends. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 provides that the returning officers, assistant returning officers, presiding officers, polling officers and other officers appointed for the preparation of rolls and the conduct of elections, and the police officers designated for the purpose, shall be deemed to be on deputation to the Election Commission for the period during which they are employed on election work, and shall during that period be subject to the control, superintendence and discipline of the Commission. Article 324 adds the obligation of the President and of the Governor of a state, when so requested, to make available to the Commission such staff as may be necessary for the discharge of its functions. Between the deputation device and the staff clause, the Commission acquires for the election period a disciplinary authority over the district administration that no other constitutional body possesses over the executive of a state, and it uses that authority to post observers over the administration's head, to transfer officers whose impartiality it doubts and to direct the deployment of police and central forces. The arrangement's legitimacy rests on its temporariness and its purpose limitation: the authority begins with the election work, ends with it, and may be exercised only for it, and the courts have intervened where election directions travelled beyond the conduct of the election into the ordinary government of a state.

The friction points of the relationship are as instructive as its design. A state government whose senior officers are transferred in the middle of a campaign experiences the transfer as a judgment on its neutrality, and says so. A Commission that must request forces and staff from the governments it regulates experiences the request as a dependence, and the reform proposals for an independent secretariat and charged expenditure are the institutional form of that experience. Candidates experience the relationship from below, as the difference between a district administration that answers to the Commission's calendar and one that answers to the habits of the place. The field's mature position, visible across the case law and the practice of successive Commissions, is that the deputation device is the settlement: the officers remain the state's, the discipline for election work is the Commission's, and the boundary is policed by the requirement that every direction be traceable to the conduct of a free and fair election. Election law in India is, in this respect, a federalism of borrowed institutions, and it works for the same reason the single Commission works, because the Constitution said the conduct of elections is one function and assigned it to one body, while leaving the instruments of that conduct distributed across the Union and the states.

## What the Commission cannot do

A map of power is incomplete without the coastline where power ends, and the Commission's limits are as much a part of election law as its reservoir. The Commission cannot extend the life of a House. The duration of the House of the People and of the state assemblies is fixed by the Constitution, and the Commission's duty runs the other way: the election must be completed so that the new House can be constituted when the old one expires or after a dissolution, and in the case of a dissolution the Commission works to the calendar the Constitution and the practice of the institution set for it. The Commission cannot create a disqualification. The grounds on which a person is barred from contesting or from sitting are those the Constitution and the statutes state, and proposals to add a ground, such as the framing of charges for a serious offence, require legislation the Commission may recommend but cannot enact by direction. It cannot bar a candidate for a false affidavit by its own order; the affidavit's falsity is tested in the fora the statutes provide. It cannot deregister a party at large, cannot compel a party to hold internal elections, cannot dictate a party's choice of candidate and cannot review a presiding officer's defection decision, which is reviewable by courts alone after it is made. It cannot add a name to a frozen roll for a constituency whose nominations have closed, cannot order a general repoll of an election it considers unsatisfactory in the large, as distinct from repolls at stations where the poll was vitiated, and cannot postpone an announced election indefinitely, its power of adjournment and countermanding being tied to the breakdown of the process in identifiable places and circumstances. It cannot prosecute offenders itself; prosecution belongs to the state under the penal and electoral law, subject to such sanction requirements as the statutes impose. It cannot spend money Parliament has not voted to it. Each limit is the obverse of a power located somewhere else in the map, in Parliament, in the courts, in the presiding officers or in the constitutional heads, and the discipline of asking, whenever the Commission is demanded to act, whether the power demanded is in the reservoir, in a statute, or in someone else's hands altogether is the single most reliable habit a reader of this field can form. The controversies that recur each cycle are, on inspection, very often controversies about a limit mistaken for a failure of will.

## Closing verdict: a navigable field

The verdict this guide defends is that Indian election law is more coherent than its reputation and less complete than its map suggests. Its coherence comes from the founding division of labour: a Constitution that settles the franchise, the Commission and the exclusion of the courts; two statutes that divide the roll from the contest between them without overlap of consequence; a subordinate layer that handles operations; and a residuary constitutional power, bounded by the rule that it cannot override the statutes, which absorbs the shocks the drafters did not foresee. Its incompleteness is equally structural. The internal life of parties, the finance of campaigns beyond the candidate's account, the pace of presiding officers deciding defection petitions and of High Courts deciding election petitions, and the appointment of the Commissioners themselves are the four territories where the law is thinnest, and they are not marginal territories. A reader who has absorbed the three domains, the layers and the routing table holds the field as its makers built it, and holds with it the ability to say, of almost any election controversy in the news or in a courtroom, which statute governs it, which authority decides it, in which forum, on which clock, and with what remedy, and to say which parts of the controversy no authority has been given power to decide at all. For the aspirant preparing this subject for examination, the same structure converts directly into revision: the three-domain map, the six constitutional provisions, the two Acts and their division, and the appointment sequence are the retrievable spine, and a reader organising revision material into slides will find [the online PowerPoint viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) a practical way to keep the routing table and the domain map in front of the eye while the detail is committed to memory. The field rewards the reader who navigates it over the reader who merely surveys it, and navigation, in the end, is a matter of taking each question to its own door.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws govern elections in India?

Elections in India are governed by a layered body of law rather than by a single code. Part Fifteen of the Constitution, Articles 324 to 329, establishes the Election Commission, the single electoral roll, adult suffrage, legislative competence and the bar on court interference. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 governs seats, delimitation, roll officers and electoral rolls. The Representation of the People Act, 1951 governs qualifications, disqualifications, conduct of elections, corrupt practices, offences and disputes. The Registration of Electors Rules, 1960, the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961 and the Election Symbols Order, 1968 supply the operational detail, and the Model Code of Conduct governs campaign behaviour without being a statute.

### Q: What does Article 324 give the Election Commission?

Article 324 vests in the Election Commission the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, all elections to Parliament, to the state legislatures and to the offices of President and Vice-President. The Supreme Court reads that vesting as a plenary reservoir of power for situations the statutes have not covered, which is the source of the Commission's authority to enforce the Model Code, deploy observers, direct transfers of officials and meet contingencies no Act foresaw. The power has a boundary: it cannot override an express provision of the election statutes.

### Q: How are Election Commissioners appointed?

The Chief Election Commissioner and the Election Commissioners are appointed by the President. For over seven decades no statute governed the process and appointments rested on executive advice. In Anoop Baranwal v Union of India, decided on 2 March 2023, a Constitution Bench directed that appointments be made on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the House of the People and the Chief Justice of India until Parliament legislated. Parliament then enacted the Act of 2023, under which a Selection Committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister recommends the appointment. The Act stands challenged, and the challenge is pending.

### Q: Is the Model Code of Conduct legally binding?

The Model Code of Conduct is not a statute, not a rule made under a statute and not a court enforceable code, so a breach of it is not an offence and no prosecution lies for the breach alone. It is a code evolved by agreement among political parties and enforced by the Election Commission under Article 324 of the Constitution. Its sanctions are administrative and reputational: a notice, a censure, a direction to stop a practice and, in grave cases, restraint of a campaigner. Some conduct the Code forbids is separately punishable under the Representation of the People Act, 1951 or the penal law, and that liability is statutory rather than codal.

### Q: Can a court intervene while an election is under way?

As a general rule, no. Article 329 of the Constitution provides that no election shall be called in question except by an election petition presented in the manner a statute provides, and the Supreme Court reads the word election to cover the entire process from notification to the declaration of the result. A grievance about a nomination, the roll or the conduct of the poll must therefore wait and be raised, where the statute allows, in an election petition after the result. The recognised exception is narrow: intervention where an authority acts wholly without jurisdiction, and challenges to the validity of the election law itself rather than to a particular election.

### Q: Which election matters are decided by the Commission and which by courts?

The Commission decides the matters of process: the schedule, the conduct of nominations and polling through its officers, the enforcement of the Model Code, symbol disputes between rival groups of a party, recognition of parties, and the disqualification that follows a failure to lodge election expenses. It also gives the binding opinion on which the President or a Governor decides a sitting member's disqualification. Courts decide the validity of a declared election, through an election petition tried by the High Court, with an appeal to the Supreme Court, and presidential and vice-presidential election disputes, which the Supreme Court alone decides under Article 71. Defection decisions belong to neither: the presiding officer of the House decides them.

### Q: What is the difference between the two Representation of the People Acts?

The two Acts divide the field between them and are told apart by subject rather than by number, because each is Act 43 of its own year. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 governs who may vote: allocation of seats, delimitation of constituencies, the officers of the roll machinery, qualifications and disqualifications for registration, and the preparation and revision of electoral rolls. The Representation of the People Act, 1951 governs who may stand and how the contest is run: qualifications and disqualifications for membership, notification and conduct of elections, registration of parties, corrupt practices, electoral offences and the resolution of disputes by election petition.

### Q: Who conducts elections at the state level?

Elections to a state legislative assembly and to Parliament from the state are conducted by the Election Commission of India, acting through the Chief Electoral Officer of the state, the District Election Officers and the Returning Officers for the constituencies, who are officers of the state administration placed under the Commission's control, superintendence and discipline for election work. General, police and expenditure observers appointed by the Commission oversee the process. Elections to Panchayats and Municipalities are a different charge: they are conducted by the State Election Commission constituted under Articles 243K and 243ZA of the Constitution, and not by the Election Commission of India.

### Q: What is the Election Symbols Order and what does it decide?

The Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968 is an instrument issued by the Election Commission under Article 324 read with the Conduct of Elections Rules. It classifies political parties as national parties and state parties by their electoral performance, reserves a symbol for each recognised party so that no other candidate in that territory may use it, allots free symbols to candidates of unrecognised parties and to independents, and decides which of two rival groups claiming to be a party is entitled to the party's name and reserved symbol. The Commission applies tests drawn from the party's constitution, its organisational strength and its support among legislators.

### Q: What is NOTA and what happens if it receives the most votes?

NOTA, expanded as none of the above, is the last option on the ballot unit, introduced after the Supreme Court held in the People's Union for Civil Liberties case in 2013 that a voter must be able to record a rejection of every candidate while preserving the secrecy of the vote. A NOTA vote is counted and reported like any other vote. In elections to Parliament and the state assemblies, a NOTA tally larger than the tally of every candidate does not void the election and does not produce a fresh poll: the candidate who has received the largest number of votes among the candidates is declared elected.

### Q: What is VVPAT and how does a voter verify the vote cast?

VVPAT is the voter verifiable paper audit trail, a unit attached to the electronic voting machine. When a vote is cast, the unit prints a slip bearing the serial number, name and symbol of the candidate chosen and displays it behind a window to the voter for a few seconds, after which the slip falls into a sealed compartment of the unit. The voter therefore verifies that the machine recorded the intended choice before the slip is deposited. The slips are paper records independent of the electronic count, and the slips of a sample of polling stations in each constituency are counted and compared with the machine totals under the applicable rules and directions.

### Q: When can a poll be adjourned or an election countermanded?

A poll at a polling station may be adjourned where violence, destruction of voting machines, booth capturing or a natural disruption destroys the freedom or the possibility of voting, and a fresh poll is held for that station. The Election Commission may countermand an election in a constituency where the process as a whole has been vitiated, drawing on the statute for the station level situations and on Article 324 for the larger judgment. The death of a candidate set up by a recognised political party before the poll also adjourns the election while the party nominates a substitute; the death of an independent candidate does not stop the poll.

### Q: Who are election observers and what can they do?

Observers are senior officers, drawn principally from the Indian Administrative Service, the police services and the revenue services, whom the Election Commission appoints for constituencies during an election. General observers watch the conduct of the poll and the count, police observers watch the deployment of force and the law and order arrangements, and expenditure observers watch the spending of candidates against the statutory ceiling. Observers serve outside their home state, report directly to the Commission and not to the state government, and their reports ground the Commission's orders for repolls, transfers of officials and action on expenditure. An observer holds no independent power to decide a grievance: the authority to order a remedy remains the Commission's.

### Q: What is the silence period before polling day?

The silence period is the interval of forty-eight hours ending with the hour fixed for the conclusion of the poll, during which the Representation of the People Act, 1951 prohibits public meetings and processions in connection with the election and the display or propagation of election matter by cinematograph, television or similar apparatus in the constituency. The prohibition is statutory and punishable, unlike much of the Model Code of Conduct, which restates a corresponding restraint as a matter of campaign discipline. A related statutory restriction bars the publication of exit poll results during the period the Commission notifies while polling is spread across phases.

### Q: How are elections to the Rajya Sabha different from elections to the Lok Sabha?

The House of the People is elected directly by the voters of territorial constituencies, each returning one member by plurality of votes. The Council of States is elected indirectly: its members are chosen by the elected members of the state legislative assemblies through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, in which each elector marks preferences and candidates are elected on reaching a quota. Voting in a Council of States election is by open ballot, a change the Supreme Court upheld in Kuldip Nayar v Union of India, so a party elector's ballot may be inspected by the party's authorised representative. The Council is a permanent body, with a third of its members retiring every two years.

### Q: What does a Returning Officer do in an election?

The Returning Officer conducts the election for a constituency under the superintendence of the District Election Officer and the Election Commission. The officer receives the nomination papers, scrutinises them on the appointed day and accepts or rejects each paper for the reasons the statute permits, allots symbols to candidates, arranges the polling stations and the polling staff with the district machinery, supervises the poll, conducts the counting of votes, postal ballots first and then the machine votes, and declares the candidate with the largest number of valid votes elected, granting the certificate of election. The officer's decisions during the process are final for the conduct of that election and are examinable afterwards only in an election petition.

### Q: When must a bye election be held after a seat falls vacant?

A bye election fills a casual vacancy in Parliament or a state legislature arising from death, resignation, disqualification or the voiding of an election. The Representation of the People Act, 1951 directs that the bye election be held within six months of the date the vacancy occurs. Two exceptions qualify the direction: no bye election is required where the remainder of the term of the member whose place fell vacant is less than one year, and none is held within the period where the Election Commission, in consultation with the Central Government, certifies that it is difficult to hold the bye election within that period. The House continues with the seat empty until the bye election or the general election fills it.

### Q: Why do Parliament and assembly elections use the same electoral roll?

Because the Constitution commands it. Article 325 provides that there shall be one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency for elections to Parliament and to the state legislature, and forbids ineligibility for inclusion on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 builds that single roll, constituency by constituency, and both levels of election are fought on it. The unity is substantive as well as administrative: a citizen registers once, a correction or deletion operates for every election the roll serves, and the roll that is frozen when nominations close for an assembly election is the same roll a parliamentary election in the same constituency would use.

### Q: What is a recognised political party and what does recognition bring?

A recognised party is a political party, registered with the Election Commission under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, that the Commission has classified as a national party or a state party under the Election Symbols Order, 1968 by satisfying performance criteria measured in votes polled and seats won at general elections. Registration alone makes a body a party in law; recognition is the further and more valuable status. It brings a symbol reserved for the party's exclusive use in the territory of recognition, free broadcast and telecast time on the public media, free copies of the electoral roll, a reduced number of proposers for its candidates and the star campaigner facilities the Commission's directions provide.

### Q: Who notifies an election and who announces its schedule?

The two acts are distinct and belong to different authorities. The election is called into being by a notification issued by the President for elections to Parliament and by the Governor for elections to a state assembly, acting on the recommendation of the Election Commission, which fixes the constituency or constituencies called upon to elect. The Election Commission then publishes the programme for the election, fixing the last date for making nominations, the date for scrutiny, the last date for withdrawal of candidatures, the dates of poll where polling extends over more than one day, and the date of counting. The notification creates the election in law; the programme gives it the timetable within which every later step must fall.
