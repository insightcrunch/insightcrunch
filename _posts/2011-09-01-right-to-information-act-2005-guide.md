The question that decides whether a citizen learns anything from the state is not, in the first instance, a question about paper or about procedure. It is a question about who carries the burden of justification. Under a regime of official secrecy, the citizen carries that burden. The citizen must explain why a file should be opened, why a decision should be explained and why a record that public money created and public authority holds should be seen by the public at all. The Right to Information Act 2005 reversed the direction of that burden in Indian law. Under its scheme, the citizen asks, and the state answers, or the state refuses and must ground the refusal in the statute itself. That reversal sounds simple when stated in one sentence. Its consequences run through every part of the Act, because a reversal of burden changes who waits, who reasons, who appeals and who must prove that a denial was justified when the matter reaches a Commission.

![The Right to Information Act 2005 decision structure, from public authority to disclosure - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-37.webp)

Section 6(2) gives the reversal its first and plainest form by providing that an applicant shall not be required to give any reason for requesting information or any other personal details except those that may be necessary for contacting him. The provision removes curiosity as a ground of suspicion. A request does not improve because its motive is respectable, and it does not weaken because its motive is inconvenient, political, commercial, personal or obscure. The public authority is not invited to grade the citizen before it searches the record. It is directed to the record itself, to the exemptions that Parliament enacted and to the time limits that govern its response. Section 19(5) completes the same movement at the appellate stage by placing the onus to prove that a denial was justified on the Public Information Officer who denied the request. Between those two provisions lies the moral centre of the legislation. At the point of asking, no reason is demanded. At the point of refusal, a reason is compulsory, and the officer who refuses carries the burden of defending it.

That is why the Act should not be read as a modest administrative reform that merely speeded up access to documents that departments were already free to release. It displaced a legal culture. In the older culture, disclosure was an act of grace, secrecy was the settled posture and the file belonged, in practical terms, to the office that created it. In the order created in 2005, information held by a public authority is subject to a statutory right conferred on all citizens by Section 3, and the right defined in Section 2(j) reaches inspection of work, documents and records, the taking of notes, extracts and certified copies, the taking of certified samples of material and the obtaining of information in electronic form. The definition in Section 2(f) is equally wide, covering records, documents, memos, e-mails, opinions, advices, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models and data material held in any electronic form, together with information relating to a private body which can be accessed by a public authority under another law in force. Breadth at the definition stage matters because a narrow definition would have allowed the old default to return through classification. A department that could relabel a record as an opinion, a noting, a sample or electronic data outside the Act would not need to refuse disclosure openly. It could argue that the thing sought was never information at all.

The scale on which the machinery operates gives the reversal its social weight. Published estimates cited by the Satark Nagrik Sangathan report cards and by the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative place applications between four and six million a year across the Centre and the States, and there is no single consolidated official count that turns that estimate into an exact figure. Even as an estimate, the range tells a reader what kind of statute this is. It is not a remedy used by a small circle of lawyers in exceptional disputes. It is a routine instrument through which citizens seek ration records, pension papers, examination material, land documents, contract details, inspection reports and reasons for decisions that affect their lives. The core machinery of request, response, first appeal and second appeal has carried that volume for two decades, and the Act remains intelligible to applicants who have no legal training because its opening promise is uncomplicated: a citizen may ask without explaining why.

Against that working core stands a perimeter that has been narrowed at several points, and the tension between the core and the perimeter is the tension that any honest account of the Act must hold together. Organisations placed in the Second Schedule are taken outside the Act by Section 24, subject to the carve-outs for allegations of corruption and human rights violations. The substituted Section 8(1)(j), in force since 13 November 2025, reads in full as information which relates to personal information, and the substitution removed the public activity test, the unwarranted invasion test, the larger public interest justification inside the clause and the proviso that information which cannot be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature shall not be denied to any person. The 2019 amendment altered the position of the Information Commissions themselves by replacing a statutory term of five years or until the age of sixty five with a term prescribed by the Central Government, and by making salaries, allowances and other terms and conditions of service matters for prescription rather than matters fixed in the parent Act by equivalence with the Election Commission. None of those changes repeals the central right in Section 3. Each of them changes the conditions under which the right is exercised, defended or refused, and that is precisely why perimeter changes deserve close attention rather than dismissal as technical adjustments.

The claim that organises this article can be named at the outset, and it is a claim about Section 22. Section 22 provides that the provisions of the Act shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained in the Official Secrets Act, 1923 and any other law for the time being in force or in any instrument having effect by virtue of any law other than the Act. That overriding clause is the whole Act in miniature. Without it, every other secrecy law would have survived intact beside the new right, and each public authority faced with an uncomfortable request could have searched the wider statute book for an inconsistent duty, a confidentiality clause or an older prohibition and treated that provision as the governing answer. The right would have been ornamental because it would have applied only where no other law stood in its way, which is to say it would have applied where disclosure was already easy and failed where disclosure mattered. With Section 22 in force, inconsistency is resolved in favour of the Act. The practical strength of the right at any given point is therefore measured by how much of that override remains intact, by how widely its exceptions are drawn around it and by whether institutions charged with enforcing it retain the independence to give the override effect against the executive whose records are sought.

## The default the Act displaced: official secrecy

The Official Secrets Act, 1923 was the inherited default against which the 2005 Act had to work. It was a colonial statute, and its organising presumption ran in the opposite direction from the presumption that a democratic citizen would choose. Official information was treated as closed unless its release was authorised. The file was not a public record temporarily held by an office. It was official material guarded by law, and unauthorised disclosure was the danger against which the statute directed its force. A legal order built on that presumption does not need to announce that citizens have no right to know. It achieves the same result by making secrecy the ordinary condition and disclosure the exception that an official must be willing to defend inside his own hierarchy. The applicant outside the office has no standing in that internal calculation. The risk falls on the official who releases, while delay, silence and refusal carry no comparable institutional cost.

Understanding that default matters because statutes do not enter an empty field. By 2005, generations of administrators had been trained inside a system in which withholding was safe and disclosure was exposed. Rules, manuals, filing practices and habits of noting all carried the same assumption. A new right written on paper would have achieved little if every older instruction retained equal authority whenever the two pulled apart. The draftsman therefore had to answer a conflict question, not merely a rights question. It was not enough to declare that citizens may seek information. The Act had to say what happens when its provisions meet the Official Secrets Act, 1923, when they meet a confidentiality obligation in another statute, and when they meet an instrument that derives its force from another law. Section 22 answers that question directly, and its language names the Official Secrets Act first before extending to any other law in force and to instruments having effect by virtue of another law. The order of the language is instructive. The oldest and most symbolic obstacle is identified, and then the answer is generalised so that the result does not depend on the ingenuity of the authority in finding a different obstacle of the same kind.

The path to that answer passed through a predecessor that demonstrated the weakness of a disclosure law without commencement, without repeal of the older order and without the political will to bring its own provisions into operation. The Freedom of Information Act, 2002 was enacted as an earlier statutory attempt, yet it was never brought into force. A statute that is never commenced creates no enforceable right, appoints no functioning machinery that a citizen can use and imposes no operative duty that an official can breach. It occupies the statute book while leaving daily practice untouched. Section 31 of the 2005 Act repealed the Freedom of Information Act, 2002, and that repeal should be read as more than tidying. It cleared away a dormant text that could otherwise have been cited as evidence that Parliament had already dealt with the subject. The 2005 settlement was therefore a replacement in substance as well as in name: a commenced Act, with institutions, time limits, appeals and penalties, taking the place of an uncommenced promise.

It would nevertheless be a mistake to describe the 2005 Act as having repealed official secrecy itself. It did not repeal the Official Secrets Act, 1923. That statute remains part of Indian law, and its continued existence is the clearest proof that the displacement achieved in 2005 was a displacement of priority rather than a removal of the older text. Where the two Acts conflict, Section 22 gives the later transparency statute effect notwithstanding the inconsistency. Where no inconsistency arises, the older law continues to operate in its own field. The distinction is subtle and consequential. Repeal would have settled the matter by subtraction. Override settles it by a rule of precedence that must be applied case by case, record by record and exemption by exemption. Every dispute therefore asks whether the provision relied upon to refuse disclosure is genuinely inconsistent with the Act, whether an exemption in Section 8 covers the material and whether the public interest override in Section 8(2) permits access in any event. Section 8(2) reinforces Section 22 from inside the exemptions chapter by providing that, notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act, 1923 or any exemption in Section 8(1), a public authority may allow access if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests. Secrecy after 2005 is therefore not a self executing answer. It is an argument that must survive the structure of the Act.

That structure also explains why the mere survival of secrecy language elsewhere in the statute book does not settle a request. An authority that points to a restrictive provision in another law has identified the beginning of the legal question, not its end. Section 22 requires the decision maker to confront the inconsistency and to apply the Act in spite of it. A departmental instruction, a contract term or an instrument deriving force from another law cannot quietly restore the pre 2005 default, because the override reaches instruments as well as statutes. The citizen, for his part, does not have to prove that the other law has been repealed. He has to bring his request within the Act, and the authority has to find its refusal within the Act. The movement from the older default is captured in that allocation of work. Before commencement, the citizen had to overcome secrecy. After commencement, secrecy has to justify itself through exemptions that Parliament specified, through the balancing that Section 8(2) preserves and through appeal proceedings in which Section 19(5) places the onus on the officer who denied the request.

The limits of the displacement are part of the same story and should not be softened. Section 24 removes the intelligence and security organisations specified in the Second Schedule from the reach of the Act, together with information furnished by those organisations to the Central Government, while preserving access to information pertaining to allegations of corruption and, with Commission approval and a forty five day period, human rights violations. Section 8 protects interests that include the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security, strategic, scientific or economic interests of the State, relations with a foreign State and material likely to lead to incitement of an offence, alongside cabinet papers, fiduciary material, commercial confidence and other specified categories. The 2005 Act therefore did not declare that all official information must be disclosed. It declared that closure requires a statutory home. A refusal that cannot be placed inside an exemption, a schedule exclusion or another provision of the Act cannot borrow authority from the general atmosphere of the Official Secrets Act. The old default lost its status as an atmosphere. It survived as a statute, but it ceased to govern the answer whenever the Act spoke inconsistently with it, and that loss of governing status is the precise measure of the change.

There is a practical lesson in that design for anyone who uses or administers the Act. Arguments framed as general appeals to confidentiality, administrative convenience or the sensitivity of government as a whole belong to the displaced order. Arguments framed under the 2005 Act are narrower, harder and more useful. They identify the record, identify the clause, identify the harm that the clause protects against and then face the public interest question that the Act refuses to abandon. The discipline cuts both ways. An applicant gains nothing by invoking transparency as a slogan when a specific exemption plainly covers the material and no outweighing public interest is shown. An authority gains nothing by invoking secrecy as a slogan when the clause does not cover the record or when Section 8(2) points the other way. The statute replaced competing slogans with competing reasons, and it placed the older secrecy law in a subordinate position whenever reasons under the two regimes could not stand together. That subordination, enacted in a single sentence in Section 22, is the hinge on which the rest of the Act turns.


## A right recognised before it was enacted

Long before Parliament enacted a disclosure statute, the Supreme Court had placed the citizen's right to know inside the freedom of speech and expression guaranteed by Article 19(1)(a). In State of Uttar Pradesh v Raj Narain, decided in 1975, the Court treated the citizen's right to know about public acts as implicit in that freedom. The reasoning carries a force that a purely statutory account misses. Speech about government is hollow if the facts of government are lawfully hidden from the speaker. A voter, a journalist or an ordinary citizen cannot form or express a meaningful judgment on public action while the record of that action remains closed by default. The right recognised in Raj Narain was therefore not a favour extended by the executive. It was an element of a freedom that the Constitution had already conferred and that the executive was bound to respect.

S. P. Gupta v Union of India, decided in 1981, carried the line further by affirming open government and holding that disclosure of information regarding the functioning of government must be the rule and secrecy an exception justified by the strictest standards of public interest. That formulation matters because it reverses the presumption of the Official Secrets Act within constitutional reasoning itself. Secrecy is not condemned in every circumstance, but it is made to answer a demanding test, and the interest that supports it must be a public interest rather than an institutional preference for quiet administration. In Secretary, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting v Cricket Association of Bengal, decided in 1995, the Court located the right to impart and receive information in Article 19(1)(a), joining the citizen as receiver to the citizen as speaker. The later electoral cases gave the doctrine a concrete democratic application. In Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms, decided in 2002, and People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India, decided in 2003, the voter's right to know the antecedents of candidates, covering criminal history, assets and education, was held to flow from Article 19(1)(a) and to support mandatory disclosure declarations by candidates. A vote cast without knowledge of the person seeking office is a formal act stripped of much of its substance, and the Court treated access to that knowledge as part of the freedom that makes the vote meaningful.

### Did the Right to Information Act create the right to information?

No. The Supreme Court had located the right in Article 19(1)(a) decades before 2005, so the Act codified a recognised right rather than creating one from nothing. Its contribution was machinery: public authorities, information officers, time limits, appeals, Commissions and penalties that made an existing constitutional right enforceable in daily administration.

The distinction between recognition and enactment explains both the strength and the vulnerability of the settlement of 2005. Because the right has a constitutional source, it cannot be described as a concession that the executive grants on sufferance and withdraws at pleasure. Because the enforceable machinery is statutory, its daily reach still depends on definitions, exemptions, institutions and amendments enacted by Parliament. The campaign and the drafting of the Bill through which that machinery took shape are traced in [the legislative history of the RTI Act](/2011/09/15/rti-act-legislative-history/), and the text that emerged was the Right to Information Act, 2005, Act 22 of 2005. The Lok Sabha passed it on 11 May 2005 and the Rajya Sabha passed it on 12 May 2005, and President A. P. J. Abdul Kalam gave assent on 15 June 2005. Sections 4(1), 5(1), 5(2), 12, 13, 15, 16, 24, 27 and 28, the provisions that set the institutional frame in motion, came into force at once on assent. The rest of the Act came into force on the one hundred and twentieth day, 12 October 2005. The Act that resulted contains thirty one sections in six chapters and two schedules, moving from preliminary provisions and definitions through the right and the obligations of public authorities, the Central and State Information Commissions, the powers of those Commissions with appeals and penalties, and the miscellaneous provisions in which Sections 22 and 31 stand. Codification, on this account, was not a lesser achievement than creation. It was the step that converted a principle declared in constitutional cases into a procedure that a citizen could invoke at a counter, within a time limit, and on appeal when the counter refused.

## The shape of the Right to Information Act 2005

The Right to Information Act 2005 contains thirty one sections arranged in six chapters, together with two schedules. That compactness is a design choice. The Act does not attempt to restate the whole law of secrecy, privilege, privacy or copyright in a single code. It lays down a right, duties that make the right workable, institutions to supervise its working, an enforcement ladder, and closing provisions that settle its relationship with other laws. Each chapter performs a distinct function, and the force of the statute comes from the way those functions lock together, so that a failure at one stage engages a remedy at the next.

Chapter I, comprising Sections 1 to 2, is preliminary. Section 1 gives the short title, extent and commencement, while Section 2 supplies the definitions on which the entire scheme turns. Information, record, public authority and right to information are not decorative preliminaries. They fix the outer boundaries of the right and the inner content of the duty. A request may look straightforward under the asking machinery and yet fail at the threshold if the body approached is not a public authority, or if the material sought does not fall within information or record as defined. Chapter I is therefore the gate through which every request must pass.

Chapter II, comprising Sections 3 to 11, is the heart of the statute. It confers the right in Section 3 and builds the obligations of public authorities around that conferral. The sequence matters. A right stated without machinery would be a declaration. Machinery stated without a right would be procedure in search of a purpose. Chapter II avoids both errors by placing the entitlement first and the duties after it, so that publication, designation of officers, the form of a request, the time for a response, severance, third party procedure and the exemptions appear as elaborations of a single entitlement. The exemptions belong here conceptually as well, because they define the limits of the right at the point where the right is created.

Chapter III, comprising Sections 12 to 14, establishes the Central Information Commission, and Chapter IV, comprising Sections 15 to 17, establishes the State Information Commissions on a parallel pattern. Two tiers reflect the federal distribution of public authorities. An authority under the Central Government and an authority under a State Government do not answer to a single undifferentiated supervisor. Each tier has its own commission, constituted through its own appointment process, and the parallelism is deliberate. Constitution, appointment and removal are treated separately for each tier, even where the pattern repeats, so that the institutional home for an appeal or complaint can be identified without ambiguity.

Chapter V, comprising Sections 18 to 20, carries the powers and functions of the Commissions, appeals and penalties. If Chapter II creates the right and Chapters III and IV create the institutions, Chapter V gives those institutions their teeth and gives the applicant a route when the machinery has failed. A complaint brings a failure to the Commission. An appeal tests a decision, or a deemed decision, through a structured hierarchy. A penalty attaches personal consequence to specified defaults by the Public Information Officer. Enforcement is therefore not an afterthought. It is a distinct stage with its own powers, burdens and sanctions.

Chapter VI, comprising Sections 21 to 31, is miscellaneous, and the label should not be mistaken for unimportant. This chapter carries the overrides and protections that determine how the Act sits within the wider legal order. It contains protection for action taken in good faith under Section 21, the overriding effect given by Section 22 over inconsistent laws including the Official Secrets Act 1923, the bar in Section 23 on courts entertaining proceedings otherwise than by way of appeal under the Act, and provisions on annual reports, educational programmes, rule making and removal of difficulties. It also contains Section 31, which repealed the Freedom of Information Act 2002, a statute that had never been brought into force.

The two schedules complete the architecture, the Second Schedule carrying the list for the special regime that Section 24 applies to intelligence and security organisations.

This pillar treats the provisions grouped by function rather than in numerical order. Numerical order is easy to check against the printed text, but a question under the Act rarely arrives as a question about a single section in isolation. It arrives as a question about whether a citizen can obtain a record from a particular body, whether a refusal was lawful, whether time has run out, or whether a commission can intervene and with what consequence. Each question cuts across the sequence. The definition in Section 2 controls the entitlement in Section 3, the entitlement controls the duties that follow, and a breach engages Section 18 or Section 19, with a penalty under Section 20 depending on findings in that process. Grouping by function follows the life of a request rather than the pagination of the statute book, prevents the exemptions from appearing to be the centre of the Act, and allows the common pattern across the central and state tiers to be stated once.

## Who holds the right and what it reaches

Section 3 confers the right to information on all citizens. Two decisions are compressed into that short conferral. Within the class of citizens, the right is general, without an added qualification that the citizen must show a personal interest in the subject matter, a connection with the record, or a need that an officer considers sufficient. At the boundary of the class, the line is firm, because the right is conferred on citizens and not on persons at large. An account of Section 3 must hold both points together. Generality without the boundary misstates the statute, and the boundary without generality invites officers to impose tests that Section 3 does not impose.

Companies and firms illustrate the boundary. Companies and firms are not citizens and cannot themselves be applicants. The settled administrative position is that an application must be made by a citizen in an individual capacity. The point matters because information seeking often has a commercial or organisational context. The statutory question is not whose work the information will assist. The question is who the applicant is. Where the applicant is presented as a company or a firm as such, the threshold in Section 3 is not met. Where a citizen applies in an individual capacity, the threshold is met and the inquiry moves to the body approached and the material sought.

What the right reaches is answered first by Section 2(f), which defines information as any material in any form, including records, documents, memos, e-mails, opinions, advices, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models, data material held in any electronic form, and information relating to any private body which can be accessed by a public authority under any other law for the time being in force. The opening words do the principal work, and the inclusive list prevents a reading confined to paper files or formal orders. An opinion is not excluded because it is not a decision. Advice is not excluded because it was tendered internally. A logbook or contract is not excluded because it records operations or obligations rather than reasoning. Samples and models are included even though they are material objects or representations rather than prose. Data material held in any electronic form sits beside memos and logbooks because the definition is indifferent to the technology of storage.

The final limb of Section 2(f) extends a request beyond material that originated with the public authority. It does not declare that a private body becomes a public authority, and it does not give a citizen a direct right against a private body under this Act. It provides that where a public authority can access information relating to a private body under another law, that information is not outside Section 2(f) merely because it concerns a private body. The route runs through the public authority and through its powers under the other law, and the limb creates no power of access where none exists.

Section 2(i) defines record, and its function overlaps with Section 2(f) without collapsing into it. Record includes any document, manuscript and file, any microfilm and facsimile copy of a document, any reproduction of an image or images embodied in microfilm, and any other material produced by a computer or by any other device. Its point is to secure the carriers of information against an argument that a copy in a particular medium, or material generated through a device, falls outside the Act because it is not an original paper document. Information describes content, including opinions, advices and data. Record describes the material forms in which content is embodied and preserved. Read together, the two definitions stop a request from being defeated through a mismatch of labels.

Section 2(j) defines the right to information by the modes of its exercise. The right includes inspection of work, documents and records, taking notes, extracts or certified copies of documents or records, taking certified samples of material, and obtaining information in electronic form, described in the Act through diskettes, floppies, tapes, video cassettes or any other electronic mode or through printouts where information is stored in a computer or in any other device. Access is therefore not exhausted by a photocopy chosen entirely at the discretion of the authority. Inspection is a mode in its own right, valuable where volume is large, where the relevant portion must first be identified, or where the character of a work or record forms part of what is to be understood. Inspection of work extends the gaze beyond the office file to execution on the ground. Certified copies and certified samples give the material supplied an authenticated character, and notes and extracts recognise that an applicant may need only part of a larger record.

Against whom the right may be exercised is answered by Section 2(h), the definition of public authority, and the structure of that definition must be taken apart carefully. Its first movement is constitutive. A public authority means any authority or body or institution of self government established or constituted by or under the Constitution, by any other law made by Parliament, by any other law made by a State Legislature, or by notification issued or order made by the appropriate Government. Those four routes identify bodies whose public character flows from the manner of their creation, without a further inquiry into finances in the ordinary case. The constitutional route captures bodies rooted in the constitutional text or under its authority. The parliamentary and state legislative routes capture the large class of statutory bodies, varied in function. The notification or order route captures bodies owing their existence to an executive instrument, and ensures that a body cannot escape the definition merely because the Government chose a notification rather than a statute as the instrument of creation.

The second movement of Section 2(h) is inclusive. The definition includes any body owned, controlled or substantially financed by funds provided by the appropriate Government, and any non Government organisation substantially financed, directly or indirectly, by funds provided by the appropriate Government. A body may not have been created through any of the four constitutive routes and yet be owned, controlled or substantially financed to a degree that treating it as wholly private for disclosure purposes would defeat the purpose of the Act. The inclusive limbs look to relationship and dependence rather than to origin. Ownership is one form of that relationship, control is another, and substantial financing is a third, with financing for a non Government organisation capable of being direct or indirect.

Litigation has concentrated on those inclusive limbs, and that litigation is reserved for a later section of this article. A body created by statute rarely requires a lengthy argument to show that it is a public authority. Dispute has gathered at the margin, over bodies that perform public functions, receive public money or operate under public regulation but were not established through a constitutive route in any straightforward sense. In that territory the meaning of control, the meaning of substantial financing, the treatment of indirect financing and the effect of mere registration, regulation or supervision have all been pressed. Three structural points belong here. The inclusive limbs are part of the definition itself, not an exception to it. The two limbs are distinct. A dispute under them is necessarily factual, turning on accounts, grant patterns, governance documents and control mechanisms in a way that has no parallel where a statute itself establishes the body.

## The asking machinery in outline

A right that depends on asking must specify whom to ask, how to ask, and how long an answer may take. Chapter II supplies that specification through Sections 5 to 7, beginning before any request is made. Under Sections 5(1) and 5(2), every public authority was to designate Public Information Officers in all administrative units or offices under it within one hundred days of enactment, and Assistant Public Information Officers at each sub divisional or sub district level. The officer must exist, so that a request has a destination and an identified office bears the duty to respond. The Public Information Officer is the pivot of the machinery, taking the decision on disclosure in the first instance, intimating fees and issuing rejections with reasons. The Assistant Public Information Officer provides a local receiving point, widening access without converting the assistant into the decision maker for all purposes.

Two allied provisions prevent designation from becoming a shell. Section 5(3) provides that the Public Information Officer shall render reasonable assistance to persons requesting information. Reasonable assistance is a duty, not a courtesy, and it recognises that a machinery designed for citizens at large cannot presuppose legal training or administrative fluency in every applicant. Section 5(5) addresses the position inside the authority. An officer whose assistance is sought by the Public Information Officer shall render that assistance and shall be treated as a Public Information Officer for the purposes of contravention of the provisions of the Act. Such an officer is commonly described as a deemed PIO. The device prevents a designated officer from answering that the record lay elsewhere in the authority with an officer who declined to assist. Responsibility follows the request through the authority.

The request itself is governed by Section 6. Under Section 6(1), a person who desires to obtain information shall make a request in writing or through electronic means in English or Hindi or in the official language of the area in which the application is being made, accompanied by such fee as may be prescribed. The language position reflects the same accessibility judgment as the duty of assistance. A right conferred on all citizens would be narrowed in practice if it could be exercised only in English. Where an applicant cannot make a written request, the Public Information Officer shall render all reasonable assistance to the person making the request orally to reduce the same in writing. Inability to write therefore does not become inability to ask. The oral approach is converted, with assistance, into the written form the machinery requires for receipt, dating, transfer and time calculation.

This section gives an outline of the statutory scheme, not a how to guide, and a reader who needs practical steps should follow [the step-by-step guide to filing an RTI application](/2011/10/01/how-to-file-rti-application-india/) alongside the provisions described here.

### Can a public authority ask why an applicant wants the information?

No. Section 6(2) provides that an applicant shall not be required to give any reason for requesting the information or any other personal details except those that may be necessary for contacting him. Curiosity, purpose and motive are therefore irrelevant to the duty to respond.

Section 6(2) has consequences beyond the counter at which a request is received. If no reason need be given, no reason can be weighed. An officer cannot rank requests by the worthiness of the purpose behind them and cannot demand a showing of public interest as a condition of entertaining an ordinary request. The exemptions may still be invoked where they apply, but that inquiry concerns the information and the interests protected by the relevant provision. It does not concern the character or intentions of the applicant.

Section 6 also deals with the misdirected request. Under Section 6(3), where information is held by another public authority, or the subject matter is more closely connected with the functions of another public authority, the authority to which the application is made shall transfer the application or the appropriate part of it to that other authority and inform the applicant immediately about the transfer. The transfer must be made as soon as practicable and in no case later than five days from receipt of the application. The limit is short by design. A citizen cannot be expected to map the allocation of subjects across government before asking a question, and transfer within five days, with information to the applicant, keeps the request alive rather than returning it as a failed attempt through a wrong door.

The response clocks in Section 7 require precise statement. Under Section 7(1), the Public Information Officer shall, as expeditiously as possible and in any case within thirty days of receipt of the request, either provide the information on payment of such fee as may be prescribed or reject the request for a reason specified in the Act. Thirty days is the ordinary outer limit, running from receipt. Internal movement of a file does not stop the clock. The authority must organise its search and its decision within the period allowed. Where the information sought concerns the life or liberty of a person, Section 7(1) provides a stricter rule, and the information shall be provided within forty eight hours of receipt. The contrast is deliberate. Some information loses its value, or some harm becomes irreversible, if the ordinary period runs. The category is narrow, concerning life or liberty, and within its proper field the forty eight hour rule displaces the ordinary timetable entirely.

Fee provisions qualify the calculation and the cost without weakening that discipline. Under Section 7(3), where a decision is taken to provide information on payment of a further fee representing the cost of providing it, the officer shall send an intimation stating the fee and its calculations, and the period intervening between dispatch of the intimation and payment shall be excluded in calculating the thirty days. The exclusion is tied to that intervening period alone. It creates no general power to pause time for internal deliberation. Under Section 7(5), the fee prescribed shall be reasonable, and no fee shall be charged to a person below the poverty line. Cost may be recovered within the scheme, but cost may not be set or applied so as to defeat access.

Section 7 attaches direct consequences to delay and to silence. Under Section 7(6), where a public authority fails to comply with the time limits, the information shall be provided free of charge, so that an applicant who has waited beyond the statutory period is not charged for the delayed supply and an authority does not benefit from its own default. Under Section 7(2), where the officer fails to give a decision within the period specified, the officer shall be deemed to have refused the request. Deemed refusal ensures that silence leaves the applicant with a remedy, because the failure to decide is treated as a refusal against which the appellate route can be engaged.

Where a rejection is issued, Section 7(8) prescribes its contents. The officer shall communicate the reasons for the rejection, the period within which an appeal against the decision may be preferred, and the particulars of the appellate authority. Reasons connect the refusal to a provision of the Act and allow the applicant and any appellate forum to test whether that provision applies. The appeal period tells the applicant how long is available for a challenge, and the particulars tell the applicant where the challenge must go. A rejection without reasons fails that discipline before its substantive correctness is reached. Together, Sections 5 to 7 close the paths by which a formal right can be defeated without a formal denial, whether through absence of an officer, difficulty of language or writing, misdirection, delay, cost, or an unreasoned order.

## The RTI decision tree

Every application travels the same sequence of questions, and the Act answers each one in a different provision. A reader who works through the steps in order can predict the likely fate of an application before filing it, including the step at which a refusal would have to be justified and the provision it would have to be justified under.

| Step | The question at this step | Governing provision | What follows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is the body a public authority? | Section 2(h) | If it is, the Act applies to it in full; if it is not, the next step decides whether its information is reachable anyway |
| 2 | Can a public authority access the material from that body under another law? | Section 2(f) | If it can, the material counts as information and may be sought from the public authority that holds the power of access |
| 3 | Does the material exist as a record? | Sections 2(f) and 2(i) | Material held in any form, including electronic form, may be sought; the authority cannot be made to create information it does not hold |
| 4 | Is the organisation outside the Act altogether? | Section 24 and the Second Schedule | If it is listed, the Act does not apply to it, except for information on allegations of corruption and human rights violations |
| 5 | Does an exemption cover the material? | Sections 8(1) and 9 | If no exemption applies, the information must be supplied within the time limits in Section 7 |
| 6 | Was the material supplied by a third party in confidence? | Section 11 | The third party must be given notice and a hearing before disclosure, and the decision must be taken within forty days |
| 7 | Can the exempt part be severed from the rest? | Section 10 | The part that can reasonably be severed must be supplied, with reasons recorded for the part withheld |
| 8 | Does the public interest in disclosure outweigh the harm? | Section 8(2) | Even exempt information may be allowed where the balance favours disclosure, notwithstanding any other law |

## Disclosure without asking: Section 4 and the duty to publish in advance

The familiar image of the Right to Information Act 2005 is an applicant filing a request, paying a fee, and waiting for a reply. That image describes the fallback and not the design. Section 4 places the first obligation on the public authority, before any request has been made and before any Public Information Officer has been asked to decide whether a record may be seen. The authority is required to organise its records, to publish a defined body of information about itself, to publish the facts behind important policies, and to give reasons for decisions to the persons affected by them. Disclosure under Section 4 is disclosure without asking, and its premise is that a large part of what citizens need from the State ought never to require an application at all. If Section 4 worked as drafted, the application procedure would serve the specific, the unusual, and the contested, rather than operating as the routine channel through which basic facts about public administration have to be extracted one request at a time.

The starting point is Section 4(1)(a), which deals with record management, computerisation and networking. A duty to disclose presupposes an ability to find what is to be disclosed, and the provision makes that presupposition part of the statute. Records that are scattered and uncatalogued, retrievable only through the memory of a particular clerk, cannot support either proactive publication or a timely answer to a request. The citizen's entitlement is defeated as surely by a record that cannot be located as by a record that is formally refused. Computerisation matters because a record held in a form that can be searched, copied and transmitted can be published widely at low cost and supplied without the delays of manual retrieval, whereas a record trapped in a single paper file in a single office remains in practical terms the property of that office.

Section 4(1)(b) specifies what must be published. It lists seventeen categories of information, and every public authority was required to publish them within one hundred and twenty days of the enactment of the Act. The deadline locates the duty at the commencement of the regime rather than at an indefinite point in the administrative future. The categories were not a suggestion for gradual improvement; they were a founding disclosure, intended to be in place as the rest of the Act came into force, so that a citizen approaching an authority for the first time would already find a published account of what that authority is, what it does, how it decides, what it spends, and whom to approach for information. Read together, the categories form a portrait of an institution drawn from the outside, organised around the questions a citizen would reasonably ask before any specific grievance or transaction arises.

The first categories answer the question of what the authority is and who runs it. The particulars of the organisation, its functions and its duties belong here, together with the powers and duties of its officers and employees, a directory of those officers and employees, and the monthly remuneration received by each of them. A citizen cannot use a right of information intelligently without knowing which body holds the information and which officers carry responsibility for it. The directory converts an anonymous institution into identifiable persons holding identifiable posts, and the publication of remuneration attaches a material fact to each post, namely what the public pays for it. The powers and duties of officers carry the portrait from names to competence in the legal sense: what an officer is authorised to do, what that officer is required to do, and therefore what a citizen is entitled to expect and what may properly be demanded when the expectation is not met.

The next categories answer the question of how decisions are made and under what norms. The procedure followed in the decision making process, including the channels of supervision and accountability, is placed in the public domain, together with the norms set by the authority for the discharge of its functions, the rules, regulations, instructions, manuals and records used by its employees for discharging those functions, and a statement of the categories of documents held by the authority or under its control. This group performs distinct work. The first group tells the citizen who decides; this group tells the citizen by what standards the decision ought to have been made. Without published norms, a citizen who receives an adverse decision can complain about the outcome but has limited means of showing that the outcome departed from the authority's own rules. With published norms, manuals and procedures, the comparison between the rule and the act becomes possible, and an application can be framed with precision because the applicant knows what records the process ought to have generated. The statement of categories of documents held completes the arrangement by disclosing the map of the record itself, so that the citizen is not required to guess at the existence of files whose names remain known only inside the office.

A further group concerns money, and here proactive disclosure carries the heaviest consequences for the detection of misuse. The budget allocated to the authority belongs in the published material, together with the details of subsidy programmes, the amounts allocated to them, the manner of their execution, and the details of the beneficiaries of such programmes. Publication of the budget states the intended flow at its source. Publication of subsidy particulars and beneficiaries states the flow at its destination. Placed side by side, the two disclosures allow a comparison that no single application could easily achieve, because a discrepancy between allocation and receipt becomes visible to anyone who reads both, including residents of the locality concerned who possess direct knowledge of whether a named beneficiary exists and whether a recorded work was carried out. The same logic governs the particulars of recipients of concessions, permits and authorisations granted by the authority, which are also to be published. A concession or a permit confers something of value on one person and, where the thing conferred is limited in supply, withholds it from others. Publication makes the identity of the recipient a public fact, so that patterns become examinable: whether the same names recur, whether persons connected to decision makers appear, and whether the stated criteria for grant match the profile of those who received the grant.

The final group returns to the machinery of the Act itself. The particulars of the Public Information Officers, their names and designations, are among the seventeen categories, together with the facilities available to citizens for obtaining information and the arrangements for consultation with the public in relation to policy. The item may appear small, but it guards the entrance to the entire statute, since a right to request information is of limited use to a citizen who cannot discover to whom the request should be addressed. By placing the particulars of its own information officers within the body of information that must be published without being asked, Section 4(1)(b) ensures that the route into the application procedure is itself publicly known.

Two further duties in Section 4(1) move beyond the standing portrait of the institution. Section 4(1)(c) requires a public authority to publish all relevant facts while formulating important policies or announcing decisions which affect the public. The timing is the substance of the duty. Facts published after a policy has been settled may inform debate about a decision already taken, but facts published while the policy is being formulated allow the public to test the factual premises on which the proposal rests and to bring contrary facts to notice at the stage where they can still alter the outcome. The provision is directed to relevant facts, and it refuses the model in which a government announces a finished policy supported only by a summary composed for the purpose of defending it. Section 4(1)(d) performs a related but more personal function by requiring a public authority to provide reasons for its administrative or quasi-judicial decisions to affected persons. The audience here is not the public at large but the individual whose interests have been determined. A reasoned decision can be understood, accepted, or challenged on identifiable grounds; an unreasoned decision leaves the affected person to guess at the basis of the exercise of power and leaves any challenge unfocused.

Section 4(2) states the principle that gives the scheme its direction. Every public authority is to endeavour constantly to provide as much information suo motu to the public as possible, through various means of communication, so that the public have minimum resort to the use of the Act to obtain information. The phrase minimum resort deserves close attention. The Act does not treat the volume of applications as a measure of its success. It treats applications as the mechanism to be used when proactive disclosure has not already supplied what is needed, and it directs authorities to shrink the need for that mechanism by publishing more and publishing regularly. The endeavour is described as constant, which excludes the notion of a founding publication under Section 4(1)(b) followed by indefinite neglect. Information about an authority changes as officers are transferred, budgets are revised, and programmes are redesigned, and a disclosure regime that is not maintained decays into a historical document that misleads as often as it informs.

Dissemination is taken up expressly in Sections 4(3) and 4(4). Information is to be disseminated widely and in a form and manner easily accessible to the public, taking into consideration cost effectiveness, local language, and the most effective method of communication in the area, with access contemplated through notice boards, newspapers, public announcements, media broadcasts and the internet. These provisions reject a formalistic reading of publication under which an authority discharges its duty by placing a document where the intended audience will never encounter it. Accessibility is a condition of the duty rather than a courtesy added to it. Language is part of accessibility, because information published in a language the local population does not read is, for that population, unpublished. Cost effectiveness is part of the design from the other side, because a method of dissemination so expensive that it cannot be sustained will not be sustained, and the duty aims at a practice that continues rather than a gesture performed once and abandoned.

Measured against that design, the verdict should be stated plainly: proactive disclosure is the least implemented limb of the Act. The annual reports of the Information Commissions, prepared under Section 25 and laid before Parliament or the State Legislature, return repeatedly to the state of implementation, and assessments of the working of the Act return repeatedly to the same failure. Publication under Section 4(1)(b) is incomplete, is not kept updated, or is absent in respect of categories that the provision specifies in detail, and records are not organised in the manner that Section 4(1)(a) presupposes. The pattern is not one of a duty tried and found impracticable in its particulars. It is a pattern of a duty that sits at the front of the Act, is expressed with unusual specificity and carries a founding deadline, and is nevertheless treated inside many authorities as an ancillary exercise subordinate to the handling of individual applications.

The consequence is structural rather than incidental, because weak compliance with Section 4 manufactures application volume. Every fact that ought to have been published in advance and was not published becomes a fact that a citizen must request individually, and every category left unpublished multiplies that effect across every person who needs the same fact. A budget figure withheld from proactive publication does not generate one application; it generates as many applications as there are citizens, organisations and journalists who require that figure, each processed separately and each capable of generating a further appeal if the answer is delayed or incomplete. The resulting volume is the arithmetic consequence of a disclosure duty discharged at a fraction of its intended scope, with the shortfall transferred to a procedure designed for the exceptional case. That volume is then cited in argument as a burden imposed by the Act itself. The sequence deserves to be named for what it is: an authority that does not publish under Section 4 creates the caseload, and the caseload is then used against the statute that imposed the duty to publish. Compliance with Section 4 remains the direct answer to the burden argument, because a fact published once, in an accessible form, in the local language and kept current, answers many potential applicants at a cost lower than the cost of processing many individual requests, and it does so without fees, without waiting periods and without appeals.

## Half a record and somebody else's information: Sections 9, 10 and 11

Sections 9, 10 and 11 govern three situations in which a request cannot be answered by a simple grant or a simple refusal. Section 9 is the narrowest of the three. A Public Information Officer may reject a request where disclosure would infringe copyright subsisting in a person other than the State. The ground is specific in two respects. It is concerned with infringement, not with the mere presence of copyright material in a record, so the question is whether the act of disclosure would itself infringe the right. It is also confined to copyright subsisting in a person other than the State, so material in which the State holds the copyright does not fall within the ground. Placed among the provisions of Chapter II, after the machinery for making and answering requests, Section 9 operates as a limited protection for private rights in material that happens to sit inside a public record. It does not convert every document produced by an outside author into an exempt document, and it does not displace the general scheme under which information held by a public authority is accessible unless a ground for refusal is made out. Its modesty is its meaning: copyright is recognised, the State's own copyright is excluded from the protection, and no wider proprietary barrier to disclosure is created.

Section 10 addresses the far commoner situation in which a record is partly exempt and partly open. Where a request is rejected on the ground that part of a record is exempt, access may nevertheless be given to the part of the record which does not contain exempt information and which can reasonably be severed from the part that does. The provision is a structural refusal of the blanket denial. Without severability, a single exempt passage could be used to withhold an entire file, and the exemption attaching to a paragraph would spread by association to accounts, dates, decisions and correspondence that no exemption covers. Section 10 reverses that spread. The officer is required to separate the protected element from the remainder and to release the remainder, subject to the practical condition that severance must be reasonable, since a document reduced to disconnected fragments may convey nothing or may convey a false impression. The procedure reinforces the substance. The Public Information Officer must give the applicant notice of the decision, with the reasons for it, the particulars of any fee payable, and the rights of appeal, including the period within which an appeal may be preferred and the particulars of the appellate authority. Partial disclosure is therefore not an informal favour accompanied by silent deletions. It is a formal decision, reasoned and appealable, in which the applicant is told that material has been withheld, why it has been withheld, what the released material costs, and how the withholding can be challenged. That notice is what makes severability examinable, because an applicant who knows the stated ground can test whether the ground covers the passage removed and whether more of the record could reasonably have been released.

Section 11 governs information that belongs to somebody else. A third party, under Section 2(n), means a person other than the citizen making a request for information, and the term includes a public authority. Where a Public Information Officer intends to disclose information that relates to or was supplied by a third party and has been treated as confidential by that third party, the officer must give the third party written notice within five days from the receipt of the request. The notice must state that the officer intends to disclose the information and invite the third party to make a submission on the proposed disclosure. The third party is entitled to be heard and may make a submission within ten days from the receipt of the notice, and the officer must take that submission into account. The officer must then make a decision on whether or not to disclose the information within forty days from the receipt of the original request, and must give notice of that decision in writing to the third party. Where the third party objects, disclosure may still be ordered if the public interest in disclosure outweighs in importance any possible harm or injury to the interests of the third party. The exception to that override is trade or commercial secrets protected by law, where disclosure cannot be ordered on that basis. The provision therefore refuses both extremes: a third party has no veto arising merely from objection or from a claim of confidentiality, and the officer has no unlimited power to override objection where the material is a trade or commercial secret protected by law.

The third party route is the point at which the Act's preference for disclosure is tested hardest in practice, and the reason lies in the character of the material that reaches it. Information supplied in confidence often concerns contracts, bids, inspections and dealings between an authority and outside persons, precisely the areas in which public scrutiny has the strongest claim and in which the supplier has the strongest incentive to resist. Two legitimate pressures meet in a single file. The citizen's claim rests on the principle that information held by a public authority is held on public business, and Section 11 honours that claim by denying the third party a veto, by imposing short periods for objection, and by allowing disclosure where the public interest outweighs possible harm. The third party's claim rests on fairness and on the candour that authorities depend upon when persons supply information for regulatory and commercial purposes, and Section 11 honours that claim through notice and a hearing before disclosure occurs. What the procedure reveals, case by case, is whether disclosure remains the default when an identified and organised interest objects to it. A refusal that follows objection without a genuine weighing of the public interest converts notice into a veto that the section withheld. A disclosure ordered without notice converts openness into a breach of the fairness that the section guaranteed. Section 11 holds both failures in view, and its discipline of five days, ten days and forty days, public interest weighing, and a single defined carve out for trade and commercial secrets protected by law is the mechanism by which the Act keeps its preference for disclosure alive at the point of greatest resistance.

## The exemptions: what Section 8 withholds and what it cannot

Section 8 stands at the point where the general right conferred by the Act meets the interests that Parliament recognised as capable of defeating a particular request. Its unit is information, not the public authority as a whole. A request is answered by examining the material sought, identifying the interest said to be injured by release, and testing the request against the language of a specific clause. The exemptions do not create a general discretion to refuse material that is embarrassing, inconvenient or politically sensitive, because none of those words appears in Section 8(1). Clause by clause, the section asks what release would damage, whose process it would disturb, what relationship it would betray, or what personal material it would expose. The clause selected determines the test to be applied, the tailpiece that may follow it and the scope for disclosure in the public interest.

Clause (a) belongs to the security cluster and contains the broadest language in Section 8(1). It covers information whose disclosure would prejudicially affect the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security, strategic, scientific or economic interests of the State, relation with a foreign State, or information likely to lead to incitement of an offence. The clause gathers distinct concerns that should not be collapsed into one another. Sovereignty and integrity concern the constitutional existence and territorial unity of the State. Security and strategic interests concern protection against threats and the conduct of defence and statecraft. Incitement stands on a different footing again, because the clause looks to a likely consequence in the form of an offence. The breadth of clause (a) makes precision especially important. Its language requires an authority to name the protected interest and to connect disclosure to prejudice against that interest, instead of treating national importance as a self-evident answer.

Clause (f) protects information received in confidence from a foreign government. Its focus is the origin of the information and the terms on which it reached India, rather than the Indian State interest that might be damaged by release, and that difference separates it from the foreign relations limb of clause (a). Clause (g) completes the cluster with two linked protections. It covers information whose disclosure would endanger the life or physical safety of any person, and information whose disclosure would identify the source of information or assistance given in confidence for law enforcement or security purposes. The first limb is direct and personal, because the harm is danger to life or physical safety. The second protects the channel by which enforcement and security agencies receive help, because a source exposed after giving information or assistance in confidence may face retaliation and other potential sources may be deterred. Read together, the three clauses protect different things: clause (a) protects interests of the State, clause (f) protects a confidence received from another government, and clause (g) protects persons and confidential sources. A refusal has to be intelligible within the identification made by the clause actually relied upon.

The process cluster protects the integrity of proceedings and deliberations while they retain their character as unfinished or institutionally privileged business. Clause (b) covers information expressly forbidden to be published by a court or tribunal, and information whose disclosure may constitute contempt of court. The first limb gives effect to an express prohibition made elsewhere. The second addresses the legal consequence of disclosure itself where publication may amount to contempt. Clause (b) does not authorise withholding merely because litigation is pending, because its text is tied to express prohibition and to contempt. Clause (c) protects information whose disclosure would cause a breach of privilege of Parliament or a State Legislature. The clause does not exempt every document connected with Parliament or a State Legislature, because the test is breach of privilege. 

Clause (h) protects information that would impede the process of investigation, apprehension or prosecution of offenders. Its operative word is impede, and that word gives the clause a functional test. The question is not whether material forms part of an investigation file, nor whether disclosure might be unwelcome to an investigating agency, but whether release would impede a process directed towards investigation, apprehension or prosecution. The three stages are distinct, and material may bear differently on each. Its protection follows the process and the impediment, rather than attaching permanently to an agency or to every record generated during an inquiry, because Parliament selected a consequence and tied it to specified stages in the enforcement of the criminal law.

Clause (i) deals with cabinet papers, including records of deliberations of the Council of Ministers, Secretaries and other officers. The clause contains, however, one of the most instructive tailpieces in Section 8. The decisions of the Council of Ministers, the reasons for those decisions and the material on which the decisions were based are to be made public after the decision has been taken and the matter is complete or over. The exemption is thus directed to deliberation in progress, while the tailpiece directs attention to the completed decision and the reasoning and material behind it. The same clause adds that matters falling within the other exemptions in Section 8(1) remain exempt, so material that qualifies independently under another clause does not lose that protection merely because it also formed part of the basis for a cabinet decision.

The confidence cluster turns from State interests and institutional processes to relationships of trust in commercial and fiduciary settings. Clause (d) covers information including commercial confidence, trade secrets or intellectual property, where disclosure would harm the competitive position of a third party. The clause requires both a qualifying kind of information and a specified harm. Commercial confidence is not protected in isolation from consequence, because protection is tied to harm to the competitive position of a third party. The inquiry remains specific: what confidence, secret or property is involved, which third party holds the competitive position at risk, and how disclosure would harm that position. Clause (d) then carries its own public interest tail, because disclosure may still be made where the competent authority is satisfied that larger public interest warrants disclosure. The clause describes a protected category, a harm threshold and a route through which larger public interest can warrant release, and an examination that stops after identifying commercial confidence and possible competitive harm has taken only part of the clause into account.

Clause (e) covers information available to a person in a fiduciary relationship, unless larger public interest warrants disclosure. Its focus is neither the commercial character of the material nor harm to a competitive position, but the relationship under which the information is held. A fiduciary relationship imports trust and confidence of a recognised kind, in which one person holds information for or on behalf of another in circumstances that constrain its use. The clause asks whether the information is available to the holder in that relationship. It does not declare that every record held by a powerful institution about a person is fiduciary material, because institutional possession and fiduciary custody are different things. Where the relationship is genuinely fiduciary, the duty attached to it supplies the reason for withholding. Where it lacks that character, clause (e) has no work to do, even if the information is sensitive for another reason that must then be tested under the correct clause. The two tails differ in starting point: clause (d) starts from competitive harm, while clause (e) starts from fiduciary relationship itself. 

The remaining clauses do not all carry internal tails of that kind, and the variation is part of the design. Clauses (d) and (e) show that Parliament knew how to build a larger public interest qualification into an individual exemption. Clause (i) uses a different device, a completion based tailpiece for cabinet decisions. Clause (j), in its former form, used relational tests, a privacy test, a larger public interest justification and a proviso concerning Parliament and State Legislatures. 

Clause (j) requires separate treatment because its text has been replaced and because the replacement changed the structure of the inquiry. In its former form, in force until 12 November 2025, clause (j) exempted information which related to personal information, the disclosure of which had no relationship to any public activity or interest, or which would cause unwarranted invasion of the privacy of the individual. The former clause then allowed disclosure where the Central Public Information Officer, the State Public Information Officer or the appellate authority was satisfied that the larger public interest justified disclosure. A proviso added that information which could not be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature could not be denied to any person. Its disclosure had to lack relationship to any public activity or interest, or disclosure had to amount to an unwarranted invasion of privacy. The former clause forced decision makers to separate questions often compressed into a single assertion of privacy: whether the material related to personal information, whether disclosure had a relationship to public activity or public interest, whether disclosure would cause an invasion of privacy and whether that invasion was unwarranted, and whether a larger public interest justified disclosure despite the personal character of the material. That structure made clause (j) a balancing provision built around personal information, rather than a declaration that personal information as a class stood outside the Act.

The substituted clause is radically shorter. In force from 13 November 2025, clause (j) reads, in full, "information which relates to personal information". The substitution was carried out by Section 44(3) of [the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023](/2019/06/01/digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023/), and the substitution was brought into force on 13 November 2025 by notification G.S.R. 843(E). The replacement removed the public activity test, the unwarranted invasion test, the larger public interest justification inside the clause and the Parliament proviso. What remains within clause (j) is the relational threshold alone. The clause contains no internal route by which relationship to public activity converts personal material into disclosable material, no internal inquiry into whether an invasion of privacy is warranted or unwarranted, and no internal statement that a larger public interest justifies release. The substituted clause broadens the face of the exemption because the qualifying words that confined the former clause are gone, and it leaves the public interest override in Section 8(2) as the provision that remains available outside the clause. Section 8(2) was not amended by the substitution. Petitions challenging the substituted clause are pending before the Supreme Court, which has issued notice, declined to stay the substitution and referred the questions to a larger bench. Case law built on the former clause must therefore be read as decided under a text that no longer exists. A later section of the article takes up those cases in detail.

### Can information that falls within an exemption still be disclosed in the public interest?

Section 8(2) provides that, notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act 1923 or any exemption in Section 8(1), a public authority may allow access to information if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests that the exemption exists to safeguard.

Section 8(2) is a separate override, and its placement gives it a strength distinct from the internal tails in clauses (d) and (e) and from the internal justification that the former clause (j) contained. It opens with a non obstante sweep: notwithstanding anything in the Official Secrets Act 1923, and notwithstanding any of the exemptions allowed under Section 8(1), a public authority may allow access where the stipulated balance is met. It also reaches beyond the Act to the Official Secrets Act, a point reinforced by Section 22, under which the provisions of the Act have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in the Official Secrets Act 1923 or any other law for the time being in force. It is an operative provision that can permit access despite an exemption, and its inquiry begins after, or alongside, the identification of the protected interest that the exemption serves.

The test is comparative. The public interest in disclosure must outweigh the harm to the protected interests. That formulation requires both sides to be identified with sufficient clarity to make weighing possible. On the other stands the harm against which the relevant clause guards, whether that harm concerns security, legislative privilege, an investigation, a commercial position, a fiduciary relationship or personal information. A public interest assertion without content cannot outweigh a specified harm, and a harm assertion without content cannot defeat a specified public interest. The discretion is structured by the outweighing test, because permission arises where public interest in disclosure outweighs harm to the protected interests, and not as an unreasoned favour. Section 8(2) supplies the common measure for clauses that contain no internal public interest tail, and its role is wider than a repetition of the clause specific tails, because it uses an outweighing formula rather than the larger public interest formula found in clauses (d) and (e). 

The survival of Section 8(2) through the substitution of clause (j) gives the subsection a changed importance. Under the former clause (j), a larger public interest justification operated inside the personal information exemption itself, with satisfaction assigned to the information officer or appellate authority. The substituted clause removed that internal justification. Section 8(2), which was not amended, remains the public interest provision under which personal information can still be considered for release despite clause (j). The route is different in source and formulation, because the former internal test asked whether larger public interest justified disclosure, while Section 8(2) asks whether public interest in disclosure outweighs harm to the protected interests. Analysis of personal information that stops at clause (j) is therefore incomplete. The subsection allows secrecy to be displaced where the public case for release is stronger, and it allows secrecy to be retained where the harm side of the balance remains heavier. The distinction between an exemption and an override also affects the order of reasoning, because a decision that records only the exemption and omits the override leaves the statutory analysis visibly incomplete on the face of the order itself for any later reviewing reader.

### Do the exemptions in Section 8 ever expire?

Section 8(3) provides that, subject to clauses (a), (c) and (i), information relating to any occurrence, event or matter which took place or happened twenty years before the date of the request shall be provided to the person who makes that request.

The twenty year rule gives most exemptions a temporal limit. Information relating to an occurrence, event or matter that took place or happened twenty years before the date of the request is to be provided, subject to the three clauses named in the subsection. The rule works by reference to the age of the occurrence, event or matter, not by reference to the sensitivity that the material may once have possessed. The exceptions are clauses (a), (c) and (i). Security and related State interests under clause (a), breach of privilege under clause (c) and cabinet papers under clause (i) are not displaced by the twenty year rule, and they stand as the three permanent exceptions to its expiry mechanism. Where a question arises about the date on which an occurrence, event or matter took place or happened, the date is decided by the Central Government, whose decision is final subject to the usual appeals. 

## Outside the Act altogether: Section 24 and the Second Schedule

Section 24 operates differently from Section 8, and the difference is fundamental. Section 8 exempts information after a request has engaged the machinery of the Act. Section 24 removes specified organisations from the Act altogether. Section 24(1) provides that nothing in the Act applies to the intelligence and security organisations specified in the Second Schedule, or to information furnished by such an organisation to the Central Government. The exclusion is organisational and derivative at once. It covers the listed organisation itself, and it also covers information furnished by that organisation to the Central Government, so material does not enter the Act merely because it has moved from the excluded organisation into the hands of the government that received it. The mechanism is total in the sense that the Act, rather than a single clause within it, is disapplied. There is no inquiry under the Act into the balance for each record of a listed organisation, because the statutory starting point is that nothing in the Act applies. 

The Second Schedule gives Section 24 its reach. It contains twenty six organisations as amended, and the Central Government may amend the Schedule by notification under Section 24(2). Inclusion is therefore achieved through the Schedule rather than through a fresh amendment to the parent Act for each organisation. The principal entries form a map of the intelligence, security, enforcement and protective apparatus of the Union. The Intelligence Bureau stands among the intelligence entries, joined by the Research and Analysis Wing including its technical wing the Aviation Research Centre and by the National Technical Research Organisation and the National Security Council Secretariat. Economic intelligence and enforcement are represented by the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence, the Central Economic Intelligence Bureau, the Directorate of Enforcement, the Narcotics Control Bureau, the Directorate General of Income-tax (Investigation) and the Financial Intelligence Unit. The armed and protective side includes the Special Frontier Force, the Border Security Force, the Central Reserve Police Force, the Indo-Tibetan Border Police, the Central Industrial Security Force, the National Security Guards, the Assam Rifles and the Sashastra Seema Bal, together with the Special Protection Group, the Defence Research and Development Organisation and the Border Road Development Board. It gathers intelligence collection, border and internal security forces, economic enforcement, narcotics control, defence research and protective functions within a single statutory list, and Section 24 gives that list a common consequence.

The Schedule has changed through notification, and two changes explain its shape. State special branch entries that appeared in the original Schedule were omitted in 2008. Three organisations were then added together by notification G.S.R. 442(E) dated 9 June 2011: the Central Bureau of Investigation became entry 23, the National Investigation Agency became entry 24 and the National Intelligence Grid became entry 25. The Strategic Forces Command was added in 2016 as entry 26. Their addition illustrates the practical force of Section 24(2): the boundary of total exclusion can be moved by executive notification amending the Schedule, and organisations placed on the far side of that boundary cease to be reachable under the Act except through the carve outs that Section 24 itself preserves.

Total exclusion is nevertheless qualified by two carve outs written into Section 24(1). Information pertaining to allegations of corruption and information pertaining to allegations of human rights violations are not excluded. An applicant cannot use the carve outs to obtain the general records of a listed organisation, because the exclusion continues to govern material outside the two subjects. Material pertaining to allegations of corruption, however, is not shut out by Section 24, and material pertaining to allegations of human rights violations is not shut out either. Human rights information carries two additional conditions. It is to be provided only after the approval of the Central Information Commission, and it is to be provided within forty five days. The approval condition places an external statutory gate before release, held by the Commission rather than by the excluded organisation alone, and the forty five day condition fixes the period within which the information is to be given. Corruption information is not subjected in the proviso to the same prior approval condition, and the contrast is textual: allegations of corruption enter through the carve out directly, while allegations of human rights violations enter subject to Commission approval and the forty five day rule. The carve outs do not convert a listed organisation into an ordinary public authority. 

Section 24(4) creates the State analogue. A State Government may likewise notify intelligence and security organisations established by that State Government for exclusion in relation to the State, with the same corruption and human rights carve outs. Human rights information at the State level is to be given only after the approval of the State Information Commission and within forty five days. At the State level, the organisations are notified by the State Government in relation to the State and the approving body is the State Information Commission. In both settings, exclusion is created by notification, corruption and human rights allegations are preserved, and human rights information is controlled by prior Commission approval and a forty five day period.

Jammu and Kashmir supplies the Act's one great territorial reversal. As enacted, Section 1(2) provided that the Act extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, and the State enacted its own Jammu and Kashmir Right to Information Act 2009, under which applications were tied to the State's own permanent residents. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 took effect on 31 October 2019, the central Act was extended to the Union territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, the Jammu and Kashmir Right to Information Act 2009 was repealed, the Jammu and Kashmir State Information Commission ceased to exist and its pending appeals and complaints stand transferred to the Central Information Commission. Since that reorganisation, any citizen of India may seek information from public authorities in the two Union territories under the central Act. A territory first placed outside the Act, then governed by a separate State statute with a narrower applicant base, was brought within the central scheme, with the State Commission abolished and its unfinished appellate and complaint work carried to the Central Commission.

## The commissions and the ladder of enforcement

A right to obtain information would remain incomplete if the only remedy for refusal lay with the authority that had refused, and at the head of the structure the Act builds outside that authority stands [the Central Information Commission](/2011/10/15/central-information-commission-explained/), the Act's adjudicating body for central public authorities. A State Information Commission performs the corresponding role for authorities under a State Government. Enforcement does not proceed through a single dramatic sanction. It proceeds through a ladder in which appointment, tenure protection, complaint, appeal, burden of proof, binding direction, compensation and penalty each perform a distinct function.

Section 12 constitutes the Central Information Commission with the Chief Information Commissioner and not more than ten Information Commissioners, and Section 15 sets up the State Commissions on the same pattern. The ceiling matters in both directions. A collegiate body can distribute a heavy appellate load among its members and bring different experience to different questions, while a maximum fixed in the statute gives a measurable standard against which under appointment can be judged.

Appointment is not left to the unassisted choice of the executive. Central Commissioners are appointed by the President, and State Commissioners by the Governor, on the recommendation of a committee. For the Central Commission the committee consists of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. For a State Commission it consists of the Chief Minister, the Leader of the Opposition and a Cabinet Minister nominated by the Chief Minister. The presence of the Leader of the Opposition is the centre of the arrangement. A body that will direct disclosure against government departments, require steps to secure compliance and impose penalties on officers is not selected solely on the nomination of the government whose refusals it will judge. The executive retains a voice through the Prime Minister or Chief Minister and through the nominated Minister, yet that voice sits alongside an opposition voice at the point of entry.

Section 12(5) defines the field from which appointees must come, and Section 15 follows the same pattern for the States. Appointees must be persons of eminence in public life with wide knowledge and experience of law, science and technology, social service, management, journalism, mass media or administration and governance. The breadth reflects the work. One appeal may turn on legal privilege, another on the working of a public programme, another on the form in which records are held and another on questions of public communication. Eminence alone does not satisfy the description without wide knowledge and experience, and knowledge alone does not satisfy it without eminence in public life.

Section 12(6) adds disqualifications that remove channels of outside answerability. A Commissioner must not be a Member of Parliament or a member of a State Legislature, must not hold any other office of profit, must not be connected with any political party, and must not carry on any business or pursue any profession. A legislator owes duties elsewhere, a holder of an office of profit holds a position at another's pleasure, a person connected with a political party carries an allegiance that would colour appeals involving that party, and a person in business or a profession retains clients and interests that might be affected by an order for disclosure. The bar on business and profession also protects the time of the office, because appellate work under this Act is voluminous and a divided attention would import delay as well as conflict.

Removal protection completes the design, and Sections 14 and 17 are best read as a designed independence guarantee rather than as a statement of service conditions. Under Section 14 a Central Commissioner can be removed by the President only on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity after the Supreme Court, on a reference, has on inquiry reported that the Commissioner ought on either ground to be removed, and Section 17 mirrors that protection for State Commissioners. Misbehaviour is not established by displeasure at an order directing disclosure against a powerful department. It must be proved through an inquiry by the highest court before removal on that ground becomes available, and incapacity is subjected to the same discipline so that it cannot be used as a convenient label for an unpopular adjudicator.

Section 14(3) permits direct removal in specified situations, and those situations mark the boundary of the guarantee rather than contradicting it. The President may remove a Commissioner who is adjudged an insolvent, who is convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, who engages during the term in paid employment outside the duties of the office, who is unfit to continue by reason of infirmity of mind or body, or who has acquired a financial or other interest likely to affect prejudicially the functions of the office. Insolvency, conviction, outside employment and a prejudicial interest are concrete matters capable of being shown, not assessments of whether decisions were welcome.

Section 18 gives the Commissions a complaint jurisdiction wider than the appeals that follow in Section 19. The Commission has a duty to receive and inquire into complaints in the situations the section describes, including inability to submit a request, refusal of access, failure to respond in time, an unreasonable fee and incomplete or misleading information. The breadth addresses the ways access fails in practice. A citizen may be stopped before an application is properly received, may be met with silence, may be charged a fee that defeats the request or may receive a reply that is formally supplied but incomplete or misleading in substance. A system that recognised only a formal refusal would miss most of those failures, so the complaint route can expose delay, obstruction and defective supply even where the paperwork suggests that an answer was sent, while the appeal route challenges a decision on the information itself.

For that inquiry the Commission has the powers of a civil court in the matters specified, including summoning and enforcing attendance, requiring discovery and inspection of documents, receiving evidence on affidavit and requisitioning public records. Each power answers a practical obstacle. Summoning secures the officer who can explain the search for a record, discovery and inspection secure the documents by which an exemption claim or a claim of diligent search can be tested, evidence on affidavit places a position on record in a form that can be weighed, and requisition prevents an authority from defeating the inquiry by withholding the file in dispute. Section 18(4) carries the logic further: the Commission may examine any record to which the Act applies even if another law says otherwise. Examination by the Commission is not disclosure to the applicant, and that distinction preserves the exemptions. The adjudicator can inspect the record to test whether an exemption applies or whether a part can be severed, without the inspection itself becoming the disclosure under challenge, and without leaving exemption claims to be self certifying on the authority's own description of a document it refuses to show.

Section 19 organises appeals as a ladder with defined intervals. Under Section 19(1) a first appeal lies within thirty days to an officer senior in rank to the Public Information Officer in the public authority, with condonation available for sufficient cause, and a third party may appeal under Section 19(2). The first rung is internal but not identical, because the senior officer must reconsider a subordinate's refusal, delay or supply, and that reconsideration can correct a mistaken search, a misunderstanding of the request or an overbroad reading of an exemption without the dispute leaving the institution. Section 19(6) disciplines that rung: a first appeal is to be disposed of within thirty days, extendable to forty five days with recorded reasons. A deadline paired with recorded reasons for extension resists quiet drift, sets an expectation of prompt reconsideration and creates a record the Commission can examine if the internal remedy proves ineffective.

Under Section 19(3) a second appeal lies within ninety days to the Central Information Commission or the State Information Commission, with condonation available for sufficient cause. The longer period reflects the different character of this rung, since the applicant must consider the first appellate outcome and prepare a challenge before an independent body whose decision will bind. Section 19(5) allocates the burden at that hearing. In any appeal proceedings, the onus to prove that a denial of a request was justified lies on the Public Information Officer who denied it. The allocation follows custody of the evidence. The applicant does not hold the record, does not control the filing system and cannot ordinarily prove a negative about material in the authority's hands. The denying officer must therefore justify the denial through the exemption invoked, the search undertaken and the reasoning by which disclosure was said to cause the harm the exemption guards against, so that an assertion of confidentiality or sensitivity is the beginning of the officer's case rather than its conclusion.

Section 19(7) provides that the decision of the Commission shall be binding, and Section 19(8) spells out what a binding decision can require. The Commission may require the public authority to take steps necessary to secure compliance, including providing access to information, appointing a Public Information Officer, publishing information, changing practices relating to records, enhancing training for officials and providing annual reports. Each example addresses a recurring cause of refusal rather than only the single instance before it, so a direction can repair the record practice, the missing appointment or the defective understanding that produced the dispute. Under the same subsection the Commission may require the public authority to compensate the complainant for loss or detriment suffered, may impose any penalty provided under the Act and may reject the application. Compensation is distinct from penalty: compensation looks to the complainant's loss caused by delay or wrongful denial, while penalty looks to the officer's default, and a disclosure direction issued long after refusal does not by itself erase the detriment suffered in the interval.

Section 20 states the penalty exactly. Where the Commission, at the time of deciding a complaint or appeal, is of the opinion that the Public Information Officer has, without reasonable cause, refused to receive an application, failed to furnish information within the specified time, mala fidely denied the request, knowingly given incorrect, incomplete or misleading information, destroyed information that was requested or obstructed furnishing it, it shall impose a penalty of two hundred and fifty rupees for each day till the application is received or the information is furnished, subject to a total ceiling of twenty five thousand rupees. The daily figure accumulates with the duration of the default, and the ceiling fixes its outer limit, producing a sanction that can be calculated from the period of delay. The officer must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and the burden of proving that the officer acted reasonably and diligently lies on the Public Information Officer, mirroring the burden in Section 19(5) because the evidence of search, movement of the request and reasons for inability sits with the officer. Under Section 20(2) the Commission shall also recommend disciplinary action against the officer under the applicable service rules in the cases the provision covers, carrying the finding beyond a monetary penalty into the service record and into the hands of the authority that controls discipline.

Section 21 and Section 23 frame the scheme from opposite sides. Section 21 provides that no suit, prosecution or other legal proceeding lies against any person for anything done or intended to be done in good faith under the Act or any rule made under it, so that an officer or Commissioner acting honestly under the Act is not deterred by fear of personal proceedings, while mala fide denial and knowing falsehood remain defaults under Section 20 rather than protected acts. Section 23 provides that no court shall entertain any suit, application or other proceeding in respect of any order under the Act, and that no such order shall be called in question otherwise than by way of an appeal under the Act. The bar channels challenges into the ladder the Act creates. The settled position is that Commission orders are in practice challenged by writ petition under Articles 226 and 32, which the bar does not exclude, so constitutional judicial review remains available while ordinary proceedings bypassing the statutory appeal are shut out. Section 25 makes the adjudicators themselves report. The Commissions prepare annual reports on the implementation of the Act, laid before Parliament or the State Legislature, containing the number of requests made, decisions, appeals and disciplinary action.


## The narrowing: three mechanisms, only one of them an amendment

The first mechanism was legislation, and [the RTI Amendment Act 2019](/2011/11/01/rti-amendment-act-2019-explained/) changed who decides how long Information Commissioners serve and what they are paid. The Right to Information (Amendment) Act, 2019 is Act 24 of 2019. It received Presidential assent on 1 August 2019 and came into force on 24 October 2019, and it amended Sections 13, 16 and 27 of the principal Act. Its method is as significant as its content. It did not rewrite the appellate powers in Section 19, the penalties in Section 20 or the exemptions in Section 8. It altered the conditions under which the persons exercising those powers hold office, leaving the ladder of enforcement standing in form while changing the security of the adjudicators who operate its highest rung.

As enacted in 2005, Section 13 fixed the term of the Chief Information Commissioner and Information Commissioners at five years or until the age of sixty five, whichever was earlier, with no reappointment, and Section 16 made the corresponding provision for the States. Salaries were also fixed in the Act by equivalence: the Chief Information Commissioner was equated with the Chief Election Commissioner, an Information Commissioner with an Election Commissioner, the State Chief Information Commissioner with an Election Commissioner and a State Information Commissioner with the Chief Secretary to the State Government, with statutory provisions for the deduction of pension from salary in the cases they covered. The amendment replaced that statutory settlement. The term became a term as may be prescribed by the Central Government, salaries, allowances and other terms and conditions of service became such as may be prescribed by the Central Government, the statutory pension deduction provisions were removed, and rule making clauses were inserted into Section 27 to support the new prescribing power. What Parliament had itself fixed in the parent Act, for the Centre and for the States alike, became a matter for rules made by the Central Government.

The Right to Information (Amendment) Rules, 2019, notified on 24 October 2019, exercised that power. They prescribe a term of three years for the Chief Information Commissioner, the Information Commissioners, the State Chief Information Commissioner and the State Information Commissioners. They prescribe pay of Rs 2,50,000 fixed per month for the Chief Information Commissioner and pay of Rs 2,25,000 fixed per month for Information Commissioners, the State Chief Information Commissioner and State Information Commissioners, with dearness allowance and other facilities on the footing of officers holding posts carrying the same pay, and with pension set off against that pay. The change in the term from five years to three is therefore the product of the rules, not of a figure written into the amending Act, and the same is true of the pay figures. That sequencing illustrates the critics' structural objection in concrete form: a future change to tenure or pay no longer requires Parliament to amend the statute, because the amending Act has already moved the decision into subordinate legislation.

The Government stated its case in terms of anomaly and ordinary practice. Its argument was that the functions of the Election Commission and the Information Commissions are different, that the earlier statutory equation of their service conditions was anomalous, and that service conditions are ordinarily prescribed by rules rather than enacted in the parent statute. Stated at its strongest, that case treats equivalence as a drafting choice rather than a constitutional necessity, distinguishes the conduct of elections from the adjudication of information appeals and asks why one statutory body should carry pay and tenure borrowed from another body with a different function merely because the 2005 Act had linked them. Former Information Commissioners and transparency campaigners stated the opposing case in terms of independence. Their argument was that tenure and salary held at executive pleasure would compromise the independence of the bodies that hear appeals against the executive itself. Stated at its strongest, that case answers the anomaly argument by identifying the reason equivalence mattered: pay and tenure that the executive cannot alter by rule give an adjudicator security when ordering that same executive to disclose, and a three year term prescribed by the Central Government, for State Commissioners as well as Central Commissioners, places the conditions of service of the appellate body in the hands of one of the parties that appears before it in substance, namely the government whose departments are the principal respondents.

### What changed about Information Commissioners' tenure and pay in 2019?

Before 2019 the Act fixed five years or age sixty five and salaries by equivalence with the Election Commission. The amendment replaced those statutory terms with terms prescribed by the Central Government. Rules made on 24 October 2019 prescribed three years, Rs 2,50,000 monthly for the Chief and Rs 2,25,000 for other Commissioners.

The dispute interacts with the removal protection in the previous section. Sections 14 and 17 were not amended, so removal for proved misbehaviour or incapacity still requires a Supreme Court inquiry. Security, however, has more than one dimension: protection during a term does not settle the length of the term or the salary payable during it, and rule based conditions remove the statutory anchor that equivalence supplied. No change to Section 19 was needed for an effect to be felt, because appeals are decided by persons holding office on those conditions.

The second mechanism also operated through legislation, but through a different statute and on an exemption rather than on the Commissions. Section 8(1)(j), until 12 November 2025, exempted personal information whose disclosure had no relationship to any public activity or interest, or which would cause unwarranted invasion of the privacy of the individual, unless the Public Information Officer or the appellate authority was satisfied that the larger public interest justified disclosure, and its proviso added that information which cannot be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature shall not be denied to any person. Section 44(3) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, Act 22 of 2023, substituted that clause, and the substitution was brought into force on 13 November 2025 by notification G.S.R. 843(E). The substituted clause reads, in full, information which relates to personal information. The substitution removed the public activity test, the unwarranted invasion test, the larger public interest justification inside the clause and the Parliament proviso. A request involving personal information therefore no longer passes through the internal balancing that the former clause required, because the tests by which a public element in personal information could justify disclosure are absent from the clause itself. The route that survives is Section 8(2), which was not amended and which permits a public authority to allow access where the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests, notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act, 1923 or any exemption in Section 8(1). Petitions challenging the substituted clause are pending before the Supreme Court, which has issued notice, declined to stay the substitution and referred the questions to a larger bench, so the substituted wording operates while its validity remains under challenge.

The third mechanism required no legislation at all, because a right of appeal is narrowed in practice when the body that must hear the appeal is left incomplete or overwhelmed. Satark Nagrik Sangathan, in its Report Card on the Performance of Information Commissions for 2025 to 2026, recorded nearly four lakh appeals and complaints pending across Information Commissions in India, with waiting times exceeding a year in many commissions, and the Central Information Commission was brought to its full strength of the Chief and ten Commissioners in December 2025 after years of vacancies. Those figures connect directly to the composition provisions in Sections 12 and 15: sanctioned strength is hearing capacity, and each unfilled post leaves its share of second appeals and complaints to be absorbed by fewer members or to wait longer for a hearing. In Anjali Bhardwaj v Union of India in 2019 the Supreme Court held that the proper constitution and functioning of Information Commissions is integral to the Act, and directed that vacancies in the Central and State Commissions be filled in time and that selection committee proceedings be made transparent. The directions identify the remedy the statute presupposes, namely timely appointment through a transparent committee process, yet their existence also records the gap between the institution as designed and the institution as staffed. Delay at this rung is not a refusal and attracts no penalty under Section 20, but for an applicant whose information concerns a benefit, a proceeding or a time bound decision, a hearing after a long wait can deliver the record after much of its use has passed. The same pattern of narrowing without a change in the text appears in the position on political parties: a determination by the Central Information Commission that six national parties are public authorities subsists, never implemented by the parties and never overturned by legislation, a position whose full story belongs to the next section of this article.

## The courts and the contested boundary of the public authority

Section 2(h) decides the reach of the Act before any question of exemption arises, because a body outside the definition owes no duty to designate Public Information Officers, to publish under Section 4, to answer within thirty days under Section 7(1) or to face an appeal under Section 19. The opening limbs cover an authority or body or institution of self government established or constituted by or under the Constitution, by a law made by Parliament or by a State Legislature, or by notification or order of the appropriate Government. The contest has concentrated on the inclusive limbs, which add any body owned, controlled or substantially financed, and any non Government organisation substantially financed, directly or indirectly by funds provided by the appropriate Government. Those words invite two opposite errors: treating every regulated body as covered, and treating government money as irrelevant unless the Government holds a majority stake. Thalappalam answers the first error and D. A. V. College Trust answers the second, and the two judgments have to be read together.

In Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank Ltd v State of Kerala, decided on 7 October 2013 by K. S. Radhakrishnan and A. K. Sikri JJ and reported at (2013) 16 SCC 82, cooperative societies registered under the Kerala Co-operative Societies Act were held not to be public authorities unless shown to be owned, controlled or substantially financed by the appropriate Government. Registration does not amount to establishment by the statute, and supervision of elections, audit or affairs does not convert a member owned society into a body controlled by Government. Mere registration, regulation or supervision is not enough; control must reach management, not merely compliance. The burden of showing that a body is a public authority lies on the applicant or citizen who seeks it, a matter of practical weight because the citizen stands outside the body, without its funding deeds or accounts, and must assemble enough material on ownership, control or substantial financing before the disclosure machinery can be set in motion. Evidence of government land, recurring grants, nominees exercising real managerial power or financing without which the body could not function can discharge that burden; a registration certificate cannot.

D. A. V. College Trust and Management Society v Director of Public Instructions, reported at (2019) 9 SCC 185, approaches the limbs from the opposite side. A non Government organisation substantially financed, directly or indirectly, by the appropriate Government is a public authority, and aided educational institutions receiving substantial government aid for salaries answered that description. Substantial does not require a majority share; it means financing material to the functioning of the body. Salary aid is the clearest illustration, because salaries pay the staff through whom an institution teaches and keeps records, and such aid is not a peripheral grant that can be surrendered without consequence. Financing routed through a directorate or grants system does not lose its character as funds provided by the appropriate Government because an intermediary handled payment.

Read together, the decisions produce a coherent if demanding test. Assertions about registration, recognition or regulation will not discharge the burden; evidence of ownership, of control reaching management, or of financing material to functioning will, and the financing limb requires neither a majority contribution nor government control. An aided college can be covered although its management remains private, and a cooperative society can remain outside although closely regulated, because the limbs are distinct and each must be proved. The boundary is evidential, which explains why the political parties litigation became the most consequential coverage dispute: there the evidence of indirect support was assembled in detail, a full bench of the Central Information Commission accepted it, and the bodies concerned declined to act on the determination.

### Are political parties public authorities under the RTI Act?

The Central Information Commission held on 3 June 2013 that six national parties, the Indian National Congress, the Bharatiya Janata Party, the Bahujan Samaj Party, the Communist Party of India, the Communist Party of India (Marxist) and the Nationalist Congress Party, are public authorities, and that determination stands unimplemented.

By an order dated 3 June 2013, a full bench of the Central Information Commission held that those six parties are public authorities under Section 2(h), relying on substantial indirect financing by the Central Government: allotment of land at concessional rates, income tax exemptions, free airtime on state broadcasters and similar benefits. Each benefit is material support. A land concession transfers public value year after year; an exemption leaves with the party money an unexempt organisation would surrender to the exchequer; free airtime substitutes for expenditure on a central instrument of political communication. None depends on a grant appearing in a budget line under the recipient name, which is why the Commission framed the benefits as indirect financing. It directed the parties to appoint Public Information Officers and to comply with Section 4, the steps without which coverage remains an abstraction, because a citizen otherwise has no officer to address and no respondent against whom delay can be measured.

None of the six parties complied, and none challenged the order in court. A body that believes a Commission has exceeded its jurisdiction has an ordinary remedy, because Commission orders are in practice challenged by writ petition under Articles 226 and 32, and the bar in Section 23 does not exclude that route; the settled position is that writ jurisdiction remains available. The parties took no such step, so the determination was never tested, stayed or set aside. The Right to Information (Amendment) Bill, 2013, introduced to exclude political parties retrospectively from 3 June 2013, was not passed and lapsed, so legislation did not reverse the determination either. By a further order in March 2015 the Commission recorded that the parties were in non compliance but that it could not compel them, because the Act penalty machinery fastens on designated Public Information Officers and the parties had appointed none. Section 20(1) imposes two hundred and fifty rupees for each day of specified default, subject to a ceiling of twenty five thousand rupees, on the Public Information Officer, and Section 20(2) provides for recommendation of disciplinary action against that officer; a body that refuses designation leaves no officer on whom a penalty proceeding can fasten. The question has since been pursued before the Supreme Court by the original complainants, including the Association for Democratic Reforms. The correct description is an implementation failure, not a legal reversal: a subsisting determination stands unimplemented and has never been overturned by legislation or by a court.

The office of the Chief Justice of India raised a different question, concerning the effect of coverage on an institution whose independence is a constitutional value. In CPIO, Supreme Court of India v Subhash Chandra Agarwal, a Constitution Bench judgment of 13 November 2019, the Court held that the office of the Chief Justice of India is a public authority under Section 2(h). Transparency and judicial independence are both constitutional values, to be balanced; coverage means the office stands inside the Act and disclosure is assessed through the exemptions and the public interest, as Section 8(2) and Section 22 contemplate. Exclusion altogether would have removed routine administrative information for which no exemption could plausibly be claimed, along with genuinely sensitive material.

Two frequently quoted authorities were decided under a text that no longer exists in its original form. Girish Ramchandra Deshpande v Central Information Commissioner and Canara Bank v C. S. Shyam were both decided under the former Section 8(1)(j), in force until 12 November 2025, and must be expressly identified as authorities on that former clause, the text that has since been substituted. The former clause exempted personal information whose disclosure had no relationship to any public activity or interest, or which would cause unwarranted invasion of privacy, unless the larger public interest justified disclosure, and its proviso added that information which could not be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature could not be denied to any person. In Deshpande, in 2012, a public servant service record, disciplinary proceedings, assets and gifts were personal information, disclosable only where a larger public interest was shown, and income tax returns of an individual were not required to be disclosed. In Canara Bank v C. S. Shyam, in 2017, an employee postings, transfers and relieving in a public sector bank were, under the former clause, personal information. Since 13 November 2025 the clause reads, in full, information which relates to personal information, substituted by Section 44(3) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, removing the public activity test, the unwarranted invasion test, the larger public interest justification inside the clause and the Parliament proviso; the override that remains is Section 8(2), which was not amended. Deshpande and Shyam therefore construe deleted language, and their authority is as an account of the former regime and a measure of the narrowing. Petitions challenging the substituted clause are pending before the Supreme Court, which has issued notice, declined to stay the substitution and referred the questions to a larger bench.

Central Board of Secondary Education v Aditya Bandopadhyay, in 2011, settles two points misapplied in both directions. An evaluated answer book is information under Section 2(f), and the examining body does not hold it in a fiduciary relationship. The Act, however, does not oblige an authority to collect, collate or create information not held as a record, nor to furnish opinions or explanations. A candidate can obtain the book because it exists; a candidate cannot compel an explanation of a mark dressed up as information. The incidents of the right under Section 2(j), inspection, notes, extracts, certified copies, certified samples and electronic form, each presuppose material that exists, subject to the separate duty in Section 4(1)(d) to provide reasons for administrative or quasi judicial decisions to affected persons.

Reserve Bank of India v Jayantilal N. Mistry, in 2015, performs the opposite corrective. The Reserve Bank does not hold inspection and audit information about banks in a fiduciary relationship within Section 8(1)(e), and disclosure was upheld. A regulator obtains such material by statutory power for public purposes of supervision, and a bank submits because the law requires submission, not because it reposes a client trust. Mistry reads down the fiduciary exemption by confining it to relationships fiduciary in character, not merely confidential in fact, a reading consistent with Aditya Bandopadhyay: the nature of the relationship is decided by its legal character, not by the preference of the authority holding the record.

## The honest assessment

Scale comes first, with attribution intact. Published estimates, in the Satark Nagrik Sangathan report cards and in estimates cited by the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative, put applications between four and six million a year across the Centre and the States; there is no single consolidated official count. A statute used at that scale by citizens who need give no reason under Section 6(2), with answers ordinarily due within thirty days, or forty eight hours where life or liberty is concerned, is not decorative. The appellate end, however, carries a heavy load. Satark Nagrik Sangathan, in its Report Card on the Performance of Information Commissions for 2025 to 2026, records nearly four lakh appeals and complaints pending across Information Commissions, with waiting times exceeding a year in many commissions, and the Central Information Commission reached its full strength of the Chief and ten Commissioners only in December 2025 after years of vacancies. In Anjali Bhardwaj v Union of India, in 2019, the Supreme Court held that the proper constitution and functioning of Information Commissions is integral to the Act, and directed that vacancies be filled in time and selection committee proceedings be made transparent. A second appeal that waits more than a year loses value even when vindicated, because information is often useful only while the underlying decision can still be questioned.

Two opposite errors dominate discussion, and each fails provision by provision. The first, unqualified success, collapses against Section 4, whose proactive disclosure duty is the centre of the scheme and the weakest part of the record; against Section 19, on pendency; against Sections 12 and 15, on vacancies; against Section 2(h), on six parties outside practical coverage since 2013 under a subsisting determination; and against the substituted Section 8(1)(j), whose deletions can only be described as a contraction. The second error, that the Act is effectively dead, fails in reverse. Against Sections 6 and 7, requests are made in millions, failure to decide is a deemed refusal, late information is free under Section 7(6), and rejection must state reasons, the appeal period and the appellate authority under Section 7(8). Against Sections 19(5) and 19(7), the onus of justifying denial lies on the Public Information Officer and Commission decisions are binding. Against Section 20, a penalty of two hundred and fifty rupees a day up to twenty five thousand rupees, with disciplinary recommendation, remains a personal consequence for refusal without reasonable cause, delay, mala fide denial, knowingly incorrect or misleading information, destruction or obstruction. Against Section 18, Commissions hold civil court powers and may examine any record to which the Act applies even if another law says otherwise. Against Sections 10 and 11, severance requires disclosure of the reasonably severable non exempt part, and third party objection does not end the inquiry where public interest outweighs harm, save for trade or commercial secrets protected by law. A dead statute does not generate four lakh pending appeals; it generates silence.

The verdict the evidence supports is precise. The core machinery of request, appeal and penalty functions at scale: first appeals are decided by an officer senior in rank ordinarily within thirty days, second appeals reach Commissions whose decisions bind, and penalties are a real incident of default. The weakest limb is Section 4, designed to make most applications unnecessary. Section 4(1)(a) requires record management, computerisation and networking; Section 4(1)(b) requires publication within one hundred and twenty days of seventeen categories, including powers and duties of officers, decision making procedure, norms, rules and manuals, a directory of officers, remuneration, budgets, subsidy programmes and beneficiaries, recipients of concessions, permits and authorisations, and particulars of Public Information Officers; Section 4(1)(c) requires publication of relevant facts while formulating important policies; Section 4(1)(d) requires reasons for administrative or quasi judicial decisions to affected persons; and Section 4(2) requires every authority to endeavour constantly to provide as much information suo motu as possible so that the public has minimum resort to the Act. That limb was never seriously implemented on the scale the text demands, and the consequence appears in application volume, because many requests seek exactly the directory, budget, beneficiary and norms material Section 4 required to be published without any request. The substituted clause (j) is a genuine narrowing whose practical effect is being worked out in litigation and Commission practice, with Section 8(2) as the surviving override and the constitutional challenge pending before a larger bench.

Annual reporting supplies a further check on both errors, although an imperfect one. Section 25 requires the Commissions to prepare annual reports on the implementation of the Act, formally laid before Parliament or the State Legislature, containing the number of requests made, decisions, appeals and disciplinary action. A system that reports requests, decisions and appeals in those terms creates a public record against which claims of success or collapse can be tested year by year, authority by authority in the returns that feed the report. The same reporting duty exposes the gaps this assessment has identified, because pendency, vacancies and defaults become visible as figures rather than anecdotes, and a legislature receiving the report receives also the material from which enforcement of Section 4 and timely appointment to the Commissions could be demanded, and that record accumulates whether administrations welcome its conclusions or not.

The administrators burden argument deserves its strongest form. In Institute of Chartered Accountants of India v Shaunak H. Satya, in 2011, the Supreme Court cautioned that indiscriminate and impractical demands under the Act can affect national development and integration and the efficient functioning of governments. Administrators develop the point concretely: each application must be located, read against the exemptions, severed where partly exempt, answered within a statutory clock and defended on appeal with the onus on the officer, by the same staff who run the programme concerned, and repetitive or very wide requests consume time that would otherwise serve people who never file. A right requiring no reasons, for a fee that must be reasonable and is waived below the poverty line, is unusually open to tactical use. The burden is real: files are often incomplete and unindexed, and penalties bear on individual officers for failures originating in the record system of the whole organisation.

Fairness to that argument does not require accepting its usual conclusion. The burden is real, and it is substantially manufactured by the Section 4 failure. An authority that had computerised records under Section 4(1)(a) and published norms, budgets, directories, beneficiaries and concession recipients under Section 4(1)(b) would receive fewer requests and would answer those received from material already assembled for publication, locating records through systems built for access rather than through the memory of individual clerks. Publication is performed once for all readers; request by request production repeats the same labour for every applicant, and an authority that declines the first allocation and pleads the weight of the second describes a consequence of its own default. Burden does not by itself justify narrowing the right: Parliament struck the balance in Section 6(2), provided proportionate instruments in severance, third party procedure, the copyright ground in Section 9 and the exemptions themselves, and confined Section 20 to default without reasonable cause, after a hearing, with the officer bearing the burden of proving reasonable and diligent action. The proper response is enforcement of Section 4, timely filling of vacancies and investment in records, not deletion of the tests that make personal information disclosure a reasoned decision.

## Verdict

Section 22 is the measure of the Act. It declares that the provisions of the Act shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent contained in the Official Secrets Act, 1923 and any other law for the time being in force or in any instrument having effect by virtue of any law other than the Act. Measured by that declaration, the achievements are of substance: a citizen right exercisable without reasons, a thirty day duty with free supply as the sanction for breach, appeals in which the denying officer carries the onus, binding Commission decisions, a daily penalty with a stated ceiling, and an override subordinating older secrecy law to the scheme enacted in 2005. Measured by the same declaration, the failures are failures of fidelity rather than drafting: a proactive disclosure chapter left largely unperformed, a coverage determination against six national parties left unimplemented since 2013 without legislative reversal or judicial setting aside, appellate vacancies left open until a court directed timely filling, and a personal information clause substituted in a form deleting the balancing tests applied to the most common exemption in daily practice.

That double measurement explains why the Act resists both celebration and obituary. Its text subordinates inconsistent law; its administration too often subordinates the text to convenience, to the convenience of an authority that pleads burden while leaving Section 4 unperformed, to the convenience of a party that accepts benefits treated as indirect financing while refusing the designations that would make those benefits answerable, and to the convenience of a narrowing carried through another statute rather than defended as a direct revision of the balance struck in 2005. Yet the same record shows citizens, Commissions and courts working the enacted machinery as written, in answer books and bank inspections, in aided colleges and cooperative societies, in the office of the Chief Justice of India itself, and in millions of unreported applications that never reach a law report because the information was simply supplied. Readers who want the detail behind this summary will find it in the cluster specialist treatments of the definition of public authority, of the exemptions and the substituted clause (j), of the Commissions, appeals and penalties, and of proactive disclosure under Section 4.

The Act that emerges is therefore neither a triumph nor a dead letter. It is a working disclosure statute with one limb, the limb designed to prevent requests, left weak by the authorities charged with performing it, and with one exemption, the exemption invoked most often against individual applicants, narrowed by substitution while its validity awaits a larger bench. To hold both halves of that sentence together is to read the Act as Parliament wrote it and as the record shows it operating. For readers preparing for examinations, the discipline that serves best is to read each proposition back to the section that bears it and to test it against the decided case that construed that section, a habit strengthened by working through training decks and classroom presentations on the Act with the [browser PowerPoint viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Right to Information Act 2005 actually give a citizen?

Section 3 confers the right to information on all citizens, and Section 2(j) explains its content in practical terms. A citizen may inspect work, documents and records, take notes and extracts, obtain certified copies of documents and records, take certified samples of material, and obtain information in electronic form where it is stored in that form. Section 2(f) defines information broadly as any material in any form, including records, documents, memos, e-mails, opinions, advices, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models and data material held electronically. Section 6(2) adds that an applicant shall not be required to give any reason for requesting information, or personal details beyond those necessary for contact.

### Q: Which bodies count as public authorities under the RTI Act?

Section 2(h) defines a public authority as any authority, body or institution of self-government established or constituted by or under the Constitution, by a law made by Parliament, by a law made by a State Legislature, or by notification issued or order made by the appropriate Government. The definition also includes a body owned, controlled or substantially financed by the appropriate Government, and a non-Government organisation substantially financed, directly or indirectly, by funds provided by the appropriate Government. The emphasis falls on creation, ownership, control or substantial financing rather than on the name a body uses or the sector in which it works, so each claim to public authority status is tested against those statutory limbs.

### Q: What information is exempt from disclosure under the RTI Act?

Section 8(1) lists the exemptions, covering information affecting the sovereignty and integrity of India, security, strategic, scientific or economic interests, relations with a foreign State, or likely to incite an offence; information forbidden by a court or whose disclosure may constitute contempt; information causing breach of privilege of Parliament or a State Legislature; commercial confidence, trade secrets or intellectual property harming a third party competitively; information held in a fiduciary relationship; information received in confidence from a foreign government; information endangering life or physical safety or identifying a confidential source; information impeding investigation or prosecution; cabinet papers subject to the post-decision disclosure rule; and personal information under clause (j) as substituted with effect from 13 November 2025.

### Q: Does the RTI Act override the Official Secrets Act?

Section 22 provides that the Act shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent contained in the Official Secrets Act, 1923, in any other law for the time being in force, or in any instrument having effect by virtue of a law other than the Act itself. Section 8(2) reinforces the position by providing that, notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act or any exemption in Section 8(1), a public authority may allow access to information if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests. The override therefore does not abolish secrecy as a category; it subordinates inconsistent secrecy provisions to the Act and preserves a structured public interest test for disclosure.

### Q: What must a public authority publish without being asked under the RTI Act?

Section 4(1)(b) required publication of seventeen categories within one hundred and twenty days of enactment, including the powers and duties of officers, decision-making procedure, norms for discharging functions, rules and manuals, a directory of officers, remuneration, budgets, subsidy programmes and their beneficiaries, particulars of recipients of concessions, permits and authorisations, and the particulars of Public Information Officers. Section 4(1)(c) requires publication of relevant facts while formulating important policies or announcing decisions affecting the public, and Section 4(1)(d) requires reasons for administrative or quasi-judicial decisions to be given to affected persons. Section 4(2) requires every authority to endeavour to provide information suo motu so that resort to individual applications is reduced.

### Q: Are intelligence and security agencies covered by the RTI Act?

Section 24(1) provides that nothing in the Act applies to the intelligence and security organisations specified in the Second Schedule, or to information furnished by such organisations to the Central Government. The Schedule lists twenty-six organisations, including the Intelligence Bureau, the Research and Analysis Wing, the Directorate of Enforcement, the Border Security Force, the Central Reserve Police Force and the National Security Guards. Two carve-outs qualify the exclusion: information pertaining to allegations of corruption and human rights violations shall not be excluded, and information on human rights violations is to be given only after approval of the Central Information Commission and within forty-five days, preserving a limited route to accountability within the carve-out.

### Q: What penalty can be imposed on a Public Information Officer?

Section 20(1) empowers an Information Commission, when deciding a complaint or appeal, to impose a penalty of two hundred and fifty rupees for each day until an application is received or information is furnished, subject to a ceiling of twenty-five thousand rupees, where the officer has without reasonable cause refused to receive an application, failed to furnish information within the specified time, mala fidely denied a request, knowingly given incorrect, incomplete or misleading information, destroyed information that was requested, or obstructed furnishing in any manner. The officer must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and the burden of proving reasonable and diligent action lies on the officer; Section 20(2) also requires a recommendation of disciplinary action under the applicable service rules.

### Q: Can a foreign citizen file an RTI application?

Section 3 confers the right to information on all citizens, and the entitlement created by the Act is therefore framed by citizenship rather than by residence, age, occupation or any stated need for the information. A person who is not a citizen of India does not hold the statutory right in his or her own name and cannot file an application by right under the Act. The practical consequence recognised in the scheme is that information must be sought through an applicant who is a citizen, because the application, the fee machinery, the appeal rights under Section 19 and the complaint jurisdiction under Section 18 all operate as incidents of the right conferred by Section 3 on citizens alone.

### Q: Can a company or a firm file an RTI application?

Section 3 confers the right on all citizens, and companies and firms are not citizens, so a company or firm cannot itself be an applicant under the Act. The settled administrative position recorded in the scheme is that an application must be made by a citizen in an individual capacity, even where the information is sought for use in business, professional or organisational work. That distinction matters because the signature, the fee, the contact particulars and the subsequent appeal all attach to the individual citizen who applies, rather than to the juristic person on whose behalf the information may ultimately be used, and an application presented purely in a corporate name lacks that statutory footing.

### Q: Does the RTI Act extend to Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh?

As enacted, Section 1(2) extended the Act to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, which enacted the Jammu and Kashmir Right to Information Act, 2009 for its own administration. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 took effect on 31 October 2019, the central Act was extended to the Union territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, the 2009 State Act was repealed, and the Jammu and Kashmir State Information Commission ceased to exist. Pending appeals and complaints before that Commission stand transferred to the Central Information Commission, and any citizen of India may seek information from public authorities in the two Union territories, whereas applications under the repealed State Act were tied to permanent residents of the State.

### Q: Can information about a private body be obtained under the RTI Act?

Section 2(f) includes within information any information relating to a private body which can be accessed by a public authority under any other law for the time being in force. The route is therefore indirect: the application is made to the public authority, and the question is whether that authority can lawfully access the material from the private body under another law, not whether the private body is itself a public authority. Section 11 also protects a third party, which includes a private person or body other than the applicant, by requiring notice within five days where confidential third party information is intended to be disclosed, with an opportunity to make submissions before a decision within forty days.

### Q: Is the office of the Chief Justice of India a public authority under the RTI Act?

In CPIO, Supreme Court of India v Subhash Chandra Agarwal, a Constitution Bench held by judgment dated 13 November 2019 that the office of the Chief Justice of India is a public authority under Section 2(h). The Court treated transparency and judicial independence as constitutional values to be balanced, rather than as values in which one automatically displaces the other, and held that disclosure is assessed through the exemptions in the Act and through the public interest. Inclusion within Section 2(h) therefore subjects the office to the machinery of designation, application, appeal and exemption under the Act, while questions about particular records continue to turn on their content and on any exemption properly established.

### Q: Can an RTI application ask a public authority for explanations or opinions?

In Central Board of Secondary Education v Aditya Bandopadhyay, the Supreme Court held that the Act does not oblige a public authority to collect, collate or create information that is not held as a record, nor to furnish opinions or explanations to an applicant. Section 2(f) does include opinions and advices among the forms that information may take where they exist as material held by the authority, and an evaluated answer book was treated in that case as information, but the distinction is between material already held and reasoning that an applicant wishes the authority to generate. A request framed as a demand for justification, inference or fresh opinion therefore reaches beyond the statutory duty of supply.

### Q: Does the RTI Act protect an official who discloses information in good faith?

Section 21 provides that no suit, prosecution or other legal proceeding shall lie against any person for anything which is in good faith done or intended to be done under the Act or under any rule made under it. The protection is attached to action taken in good faith within the statute, covering the officer who supplies information, the officer who assists a Public Information Officer, and others whose functions arise under the Act or its rules. It is a shield against proceedings founded on that protected action, not a general immunity for conduct outside the Act, and its wording links the safeguard directly to the bona fide discharge of a duty or exercise of a power conferred by the legislation.

### Q: Are cooperative societies public authorities under the RTI Act?

In Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank Ltd v State of Kerala, decided on 7 October 2013, the Supreme Court held that cooperative societies registered under the Kerala Co-operative Societies Act are not public authorities unless shown to be owned, controlled or substantially financed by the appropriate Government. Mere registration, regulation or supervision under a cooperative law does not make a society a public authority, because those incidents show oversight rather than governmental ownership, control or financing of the kind described in Section 2(h). The burden of showing that a body is a public authority lies on the applicant or citizen who seeks information from it, so evidence on ownership, control or substantial financing must be produced rather than presumed from registration alone.

### Q: Are non-governmental organisations covered by the RTI Act?

Section 2(h) includes a non-Government organisation substantially financed, directly or indirectly, by funds provided by the appropriate Government within the definition of a public authority. In D. A. V. College Trust and Management Society v Director of Public Instructions, the Supreme Court held that an organisation substantially financed in that manner is a public authority, and that aided educational institutions receiving substantial government aid for salaries answered that description. Substantial financing does not require a majority share of the funding; it means financing that is material to the functioning of the body. An organisation without such financing is not brought within the definition merely by performing useful public work or by being registered, regulated or recognised under another law.

### Q: Does the RTI Act apply to the armed forces?

The Army, the Navy and the Air Force are not listed in the Second Schedule to the Act, and they therefore remain public authorities subject to the Act, with disclosure of particular records governed by the exemptions in Section 8, including the exemption for information affecting the security, strategic or scientific interests of the State. The position differs for organisations that are listed in the Second Schedule under Section 24, which include paramilitary and security bodies such as the Border Security Force, the Central Reserve Police Force, the Indo-Tibetan Border Police, the Central Industrial Security Force, the National Security Guards, the Assam Rifles and the Sashastra Seema Bal, together with intelligence organisations, subject always to the corruption and human rights carve-outs in Section 24.

### Q: Does the RTI Act cover information held only in electronic form?

Section 2(f) expressly includes data material held in any electronic form within the definition of information, and Section 2(i) includes within a record any material produced by a computer or any other device, alongside documents, manuscripts, files, microfilm and facsimile copies of documents. Section 2(j) in turn includes obtaining information in electronic form among the incidents of the right to information, alongside inspection of records, taking notes and extracts, obtaining certified copies and taking certified samples of material. Section 6(1) also permits a request to be made through electronic means, and Section 4(1)(a) addresses record management, computerisation and networking, so the holding, management and supply of information in electronic form fall squarely within the scheme of the Act.

### Q: What happens when only part of a record is exempt under the RTI Act?

Section 10 provides for severability where a request is rejected on the ground that it seeks information that is exempt from disclosure. Access may be given to the part of the record that does not contain exempt information and that can reasonably be severed from any part containing exempt information, rather than the whole record being withheld because one portion attracts an exemption. The Public Information Officer must give notice of the decision, including the reasons for it, the fee particulars where a fee is payable for the severed part, and the particulars relating to the right to appeal, so that the applicant receives the disclosable material and retains a remedy against any severance or withholding that is disputed.

### Q: Can an Indian citizen living abroad file an RTI application?

Section 3 confers the right to information on all citizens, and the entitlement attaches to citizenship rather than to residence within India. An Indian citizen living abroad therefore holds the same statutory right as a citizen living in India, because the Act imposes no residence condition in Section 3 and requires under Section 6(2) only contact particulars that may be necessary for communicating with the applicant, without any requirement to give a reason for the request. The application is made to the Public Information Officer of the relevant public authority in writing or through electronic means under Section 6(1), in English, Hindi or the official language of the area, accompanied by the prescribed fee, and the ordinary time limits and appeal structure apply.
