Steven Spielberg's West Side Story, released in December 2021 from a screenplay by Tony Kushner, is the third major screen version of the Romeo and Juliet story that Jerome Robbins, Leonard Bernstein, Stephen Sondheim and Arthur Laurents built in 1957, and it is best understood not as a remake of the 1961 film but as a restoration of the stage musical's whole argument. The verdict of this article is direct: as a reading of the material, the 2021 film surpasses the 1961 film, because it repairs the two faults that six decades of criticism had documented in the earlier version, the Sharks' thinness and the feud's weightlessness, while keeping the Bernstein and Sondheim score at the centre. It does not surpass the 1961 film as choreography, because no film could, and its Tony is its weakest element. Its commercial failure, $76 million against a $100 million production budget, is a fact about the theatrical market of its release winter, not a verdict on the film. What Spielberg and Kushner made is the version in which the musical's Romeo and Juliet machinery finally runs on both sides of the street.

![Rachel Zegler as Maria and Ansel Elgort as Tony beneath the fire escape in Spielberg's West Side Story - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The story needs no summary beyond its Shakespearean skeleton. Two gangs, the white Jets and the Puerto Rican Sharks, fight over a few blocks of Manhattan's Upper West Side in 1957. Tony, a former Jet trying to leave the life, falls for Maria, the Shark leader Bernardo's sister, at a dance. A rumble leaves Bernardo and the Jets' leader Riff dead, Tony kills Bernardo, and the approved suitor Chino kills Tony, leaving Maria alive to pronounce judgment over his body. Laurents' book had translated the play's devices one by one: the houses became gangs, the balcony a fire escape, Friar Laurence the drugstore owner Doc, Tybalt's function Bernardo's, Paris's function Chino's, and the Prince's authority shrank into two policemen, Lieutenant Schrank and Sergeant Krupke. The 2021 film keeps every one of those substitutions and then goes behind them, asking what the substitutions were for and whether the ground under them can be made solid. Its answer, delivered through Kushner's screenplay, Justin Peck's choreography and Janusz Kamiński's photography, is that the musical was always an argument about a city deciding whose children counted, and that earlier versions had staged that argument with one hand tied.

This article follows the film's own order of battle. It sets out the record first, in the InsightCrunch Spielberg profile, because the remake's meaning is inseparable from its facts: who wrote it, who was cast, what was spent, what was earned and what was refused a licence to be shown. It then reads the screenplay's changes as a single design, tests the Spanish policy as the design's conscience, weighs the casting against the 1961 record it was built to answer, and follows the score and the songs through their restorations and reassignments. The critics are heard in disagreement and adjudicated, the Puerto Rican reckoning is given its full weight, and the box office is separated from the verdict. The standard throughout is the one the series applies to every adaptation of the play: a version earns its place by what it understands about the feud, the lovers and the city that contains them, and by whether its understanding survives comparison with its predecessors. By that standard the 2021 film is not a footnote to a classic. It is the strongest claimant the musical has yet produced on screen, and the argument of the pages that follow is that its makers knew exactly what they were claiming.

## Why Did Spielberg Remake West Side Story at All?

The project began in March 2014, when Spielberg first seriously pursued a new film of the musical and 20th Century Fox acquired the rights. Kushner, who had written Munich and Lincoln for him, began the screenplay in 2017 and stated its method at once: the musical numbers would stay intact, and the story would be drawn closer to the original stage musical than to the 1961 film. That distinction governs everything that followed. The 1961 film, directed by Robert Wise with Jerome Robbins as co-director until the producers removed him mid-production, had its own screenplay by Ernest Lehman, its own reordering of the numbers and its own compromises, and it won ten Academy Awards. A remake of that film would have been an exercise in imitation. A fresh adaptation of the stage work was a different proposition: it allowed Kushner to write new dialogue around the untouched songs, restore numbers to the positions the stage had given them, and rebuild the spoken scenes that Lehman's screenplay had streamlined.

### Did Spielberg remake the 1961 film or readapt the stage musical?

He readapted the stage musical, and the evidence is structural: Kushner's screenplay returns to Laurents' book, restores "Cool" to its pre-rumble position, gives "Somewhere" to a single grieving voice as the stage conception had done, expands the spoken scenes the 1961 film compressed, and treats Wise's film as a honoured predecessor to argue with rather than a template to trace.

The production also had a history before it had a cast. Kevin McCollum, the Broadway producer, approached Spielberg in 2014 and introduced him to the rights holders, and the screen rights were then assembled from the Robbins, Bernstein, Sondheim and Laurents estates, a negotiation that kept the remake an authorized act of stewardship rather than a raid. Kushner, who had written Lincoln for Spielberg, began the screenplay in 2017, and his declared method was set in one sentence: "We didn't want to push the other film aside. We wanted to be in meaningful dialogue with it." The dialogue was literal as well as artistic. Sondheim was consulted on the lyric questions that the new book raised, and the estates' approval meant that the remake could return to the stage text, to material Lehman's screenplay had trimmed, without unpicking the 1961 film's copyright. The result was a production with two inheritances, the stage work and the screen classic, and a screenwriter instructed to argue with the second on the authority of the first.

The film is also a late work in a particular sense: it is the musical its director had been rehearsing, in pieces, for his whole career. Edgar Wright, praising it, remarked that anyone who had watched the set pieces in 1941 and in Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom had an inkling that Spielberg had a great musical in him, and the observation is exact. The Temple of Doom opens with a full production number staged in a Shanghai nightclub; the craft of moving a camera through choreography was present in his work from the start, waiting for a property whose dancing was the story rather than an interlude in it. Spielberg had spoken for twenty years of wanting to direct an old-fashioned musical, and the want is legible in the finished film's confidence: no apology is made for the form, no number is ironized, and the songs are staged as the narrative's load-bearing walls rather than as interruptions of it. That confidence is part of what the hostile reviews were really disputing. Brody's objection to Kushner's additions lands differently when the director's side of the film is understood as pure classicism: the remake modernizes the book and conserves everything else, and a critic who believes the book was the problem all along will read the whole enterprise as a category error.

The assembly took most of a decade, and the calendar matters to the film's final character. Open casting calls went out in January 2018, in New York that April and in Orlando that May, and the search ran as a public promise before it was a company: the production was announcing, in the trade papers' plain sight, that the Sharks would be found in the community the story concerns. Justin Peck was hired to choreograph in September 2018, the same month Elgort was cast as Tony, and the principals fell into place across the following winter, Zegler chosen in January 2019 from the tens of thousands who had answered the call, with DeBose, Alvarez and Rivera cast alongside her. Principal photography ran from July to September 2019 and wrapped in seventy-nine days, inside the last summer of the old world. The film was then finished across a plague, its final music sessions recorded under distancing, its release announced, withdrawn and announced again, until Disney settled on 10 December 2021, sixty years almost to the week after the 1961 film's release, whether by design or by the calendar's own sense of rhyme. A production that set out to argue that the story belongs to every era found itself, by accident, embodying the argument: delayed into a different world from the one that shot it, and received by that world as a dispatch from before the flood.

The premiere's geography closed the circle the film draws. The first audience saw West Side Story at Jazz at Lincoln Center, inside the performing-arts complex whose construction is the catastrophe at the story's centre, the building raised on the rubble of San Juan Hill. The cast had screened the film a fortnight earlier in the David H. Koch Theater, in the same complex, with Sondheim among them, and the lyricist's death three days before the public premiere turned the evening into a double memorial, for the author of the songs and for the neighbourhood under the audience's feet. No publicist could have staged the resonance and none needed to: the remake of a musical about a district destroyed for culture was premiered by the culture that destroyed it, in a hall above the cleared streets, with the film's own argument about memory and erasure running under the applause like a ground bass. Spielberg spoke at the IMAX fan screening a week later about the story's divisions, and the setting had already said it for him.

Spielberg's attachment to the material was biographical before it was professional. The original Broadway cast album was, by his account to Vanity Fair in 2020, the first piece of popular music his family allowed into the home, and he fell in love with it as a boy of ten. He had spoken of directing an old-fashioned musical for twenty years before the rights came free. The finished film is dedicated to his father, Arnold Spielberg, who died in 2020 at the age of 103 while the film was in post-production. The production was shot in the summer of 2019, in Harlem and Brooklyn, in Paterson and Newark, New Jersey, across seventy-nine days, with the sets built at Steiner Studios. Its release was then overtaken by two deaths and a pandemic. Scheduled for 18 December 2020, it was delayed a full year by the closure of cinemas. Stephen Sondheim attended a screening with the cast at Lincoln Center on 17 November 2021, one of his last public appearances, and died nine days later, on 26 November. The film premiered at Jazz at Lincoln Center on 29 November 2021, three days after the lyricist's death, so that the third major version of the musical arrived as an act of mourning as well as of renewal.

Three entries in the profile carry the film's whole meaning. The first is the screenplay credit: Kushner is the only one of the three versions' writers who was a major dramatist in his own right before he touched the material, and the film's spoken scenes are where its argument lives. The profile is worth reading as an argument in its own right, because its least cinematic rows do the heaviest work. The two money rows state the film's tragedy in figures: a $100 million budget, the price of doing the thing properly, with a full score, a built neighbourhood and a company of unknowns held together for years, against $76 million in worldwide ticket sales, the market's answer in the worst month of a plague. The language row states its conscience in a single clause: substantial Spanish, deliberately unsubtitled, a policy no earlier screen version of the story had attempted and the one by which the film will be studied longest. The dates carry its accidents: a premiere held three days after its lyricist's death, a release postponed a full year, an opening timed, in the end, to the sixtieth anniversary of the film it argues with. And the last row carries its heart. The most expensive remake of its decade is dedicated, simply, to the director's father, the man whose record shelf admitted one Broadway cast album into the house in 1957. The profile's columns hold a studio's ledger and a son's memorial in the same table, which is the correct shape for this film's record, since it was always both.

## What Did Tony Kushner Change in the Screenplay?

Kushner told the press at the outset that the songs would not be touched, and they were not: every Bernstein and Sondheim number survives, in Sondheim's lyrics, with the estates' blessing and Sondheim's own consultation on the small lyric questions that arose. What Kushner rewrote was everything between the songs. Spielberg told a Directors Guild audience after a screening that some ninety-five percent of the film's spoken dialogue was new, and the newness is not cosmetic. Laurents, writing in 1957, had built his book at speed around the numbers, and Lehman's 1961 screenplay had trimmed it further. Kushner went back to the situations and asked who these people were before the singing started. The result is a screenplay that treats the musical the way a dramaturg treats a classic play: the text of the score is sacred, the connective tissue is open for rebuilding.

The largest change is the ground itself. The play's feud is an "ancient grudge" (Prologue) whose origin nobody in Verona can remember, and the musical had kept that groundlessness: the gangs fought because fighting was their element. Kushner gives the grudge a date and a demolition order. His film opens over the rubble of San Juan Hill, the largely Puerto Rican and Black neighbourhood then being cleared to build Lincoln Center, and Schrank tells the boys to their faces that their turf is being demolished from under them. Seven thousand residents, most of them Puerto Rican, were displaced by that clearance in life, and Kushner, who lives in a tower built on part of the site, has said the erasure was the context he and Spielberg wanted from the first. The effect on the feud is Shakespearean. Prince Escalus threatens the houses with death for disturbing the streets (1.1), and the threat fails because the feud is older than his authority. Kushner's gangs fight over streets that the city has already condemned, so their hatred has the same structure: a quarrel whose object is vanishing, prosecuted with a fury that the vanishing only sharpens. The analysis of [why the Capulets and Montagues hate each other](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) in the play applies here with a municipal twist: the grudge feeds on itself long after its ground is gone, and in the film the ground is being trucked away as rubble in the open.

The opening sequence states the whole thesis before a word of the book is spoken. Where the 1961 film began with an aerial overture, the camera descending over Manhattan to fingers snapping in a playground, Spielberg begins at ground level among the ruins: a slow pass over a mountain of rubble, a wrecking ball swinging through a tenement wall, and then the Jets, spilling into the cleared blocks to deface an enormous mural of the Puerto Rican flag, which is how the first brawl of the film begins, a fight over a painting of home on a condemned wall. The Sharks answer the defacement by singing "La Borinqueña" under the mural, so that the film's first music is not Bernstein at all but the anthem of the island the Sharks' families left. In the space of five minutes the remake has announced its whole programme: the feud will be staged on ground that is disappearing, the Puerto Rican side of the street will announce itself in its own language and its own song before the plot grants it anything, and the Jets' first act of war will be against an image, a claim about whose memory gets to mark the walls. The play's Chorus promises two hours' traffic and a pair of star-crossed lovers. Kushner's wordless prologue promises a clearance, a mural and a countdown, and the film keeps all three promises.

### Why does the demolition matter to the feud's meaning?

Because it converts turf from a possession into a countdown. The Jets defend streets the city has condemned; the Sharks claim room in a district with years to live. Every threat is spoken over vanishing ground, so the rumble reads less as a contest for territory than as two crews racing to lose the same thing first.

Tony receives the screenplay's most consequential repair. In 1957 and 1961 he is a founder of the Jets who has simply drifted away from the gang, and his wish for a different life in "Something's Coming" floats free of any cause. Kushner gives him a past with teeth: Tony has served a year in prison for beating a rival gang member, an Egyptian King, nearly to death, and is on parole, working in the drugstore and living in its basement, determined never to be that person again. The change aligns Tony with his Shakespearean original more closely than the musical ever had. Romeo is introduced in the play as a young man already prone to extremes of feeling, and the catastrophe requires him to be capable of killing Tybalt in a transport of grief. A Tony who has never hurt anyone makes the killing of Bernardo an anomaly. A Tony on parole, who knows exactly what his own violence costs and has sworn off it, makes the killing a relapse, and relapse is tragedy in its modern form: the hero destroyed not by a flaw he never saw but by the return of the thing he had beaten.

Valentina is the screenplay's boldest invention, and she is an invention by expansion rather than addition. Doc, the drugstore owner, was the musical's Friar figure, an adviser whose counsel arrives too late and whose shop shelters the catastrophe's worst hour. Kushner kills Doc before the story opens and gives the shop to his widow: Valentina, a Puerto Rican woman who had married an Anglo man, employs Tony, shelters the Jets, and speaks to both gangs with the authority of someone who has lived the lovers' experiment and survived it. Where the Friar stakes everything on the hope that the marriage may "turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3), Valentina carries that hope as biography rather than plan. She is also given the film's most discussed musical reassignment, "Somewhere," which the stage had placed inside a dream ballet and the 1961 film had given to the lovers: Valentina sings it alone, after the rumble, as the widow of one world addressing the ruins of two. And it is Valentina, not a policeman, who finally shames the Jets out of her shop, and Valentina who, deceived by Anita's lie, carries the fatal message to Tony with her own mouth, inheriting the Friar's terrible function of delivering the words that kill.

Kushner also splits the Prince's office between two policemen, and the split is one of his sharpest instruments. In the play, Escalus is the law entire: he threatens, he banishes, he judges. Laurents divided him into Schrank the detective and Krupke the beat officer, and Kushner hardens the division into a moral one. Schrank is given the film's ugliest authority. He tells the Jets that their neighbourhood is being pulled down because respectable people have decided that people like them are the blight, he sneers at the Sharks in a Spanish he wields as an insult, and his interest in preventing the rumble is indistinguishable from his interest in watching the gangs thin each other out. Krupke, by contrast, is written as a policeman who knows the boys' names, their mothers and their records, who warns them off the fight because he can see its end, and who in the final street handles arrests like a man clearing up after a disaster he failed to prevent. The play's Prince arrives too late because the feud is older than his law. Kushner's law arrives on time and is useless anyway, because half of it hates the children it polices. That is the screenplay's bleakest addition, and it is an addition about America rather than about Verona: the catastrophe is no longer merely private hatred outrunning public order, but public order arriving already corrupted by the same hatreds.

Around these three large changes, Kushner thickens the Sharks' side of the street until it has the density the Jets always had. Bernardo is an amateur boxer with a trade and a temper, not only a brother with a knife. Anita sews piecework and is saving toward a dress shop of her own, so that her "America," the argument between staying and belonging, is sung by a woman with an actual stake in the question. Chino is given work, prospects and shyness, a real alternative future for Maria rather than a placeholder suitor, which makes his final act a corruption of something visible. The Sharks open the film's spoken world with "La Borinqueña," the Puerto Rican anthem, sung under a mural of the island's flag that the Jets are caught defacing. Maria works the night cleaning shift at the Gimbels department store, and her "I Feel Pretty" is sung there, among the counters and mannequins, to her fellow cleaners. Even the mock wedding changes address: where Laurents and the 1961 film played "One Hand, One Heart" among the dress forms of a bridal shop, Kushner sends Tony and Maria to the Cloisters, the museum of medieval art above the Hudson, where they exchange their vows in a stone chapel room, before God, as Maria insists, with the city's whole Catholic past looking down. The scene is the screenplay in miniature: the same song, the same vow, a setting that turns a shop game into a sacrament.

## Why Is the Spanish in West Side Story Not Subtitled?

A large share of the Sharks' dialogue in the film is in Spanish, much of it in full scenes, and none of it carries subtitles. The policy was announced by the filmmakers before release and defended by all three of its principals in the same terms. Kushner said at the film's international press conference: "It was out of respect that we didn't subtitle any of the Spanish. That language had to exist in equal proportions alongside the English with no help." Spielberg gave the reasoning its sharpest form: "If I subtitled the Spanish I'd simply be doubling down on the English and giving English the power over the Spanish." Moreno, as executive producer, added the practitioner's point, that the acting carries the sense and that an audience denied the crutch pays closer attention. Behind the three statements sits one principle: in a story about two groups competing for standing in one city, the film refused to rank their languages. English in the film is never subtitled either, though half its audience for the songs is hearing Sondheim's verbal density for the first time. Parity, not translation, is the policy.

The Spanish is written as characterization, not decoration. Kushner, who does not speak the language, drafted the Spanish passages with machine translation and then rebuilt them with the actor and choreographer Julio Monge until the registers were right. Bernardo, newly arrived and unsure whether he will stay in America, speaks the most Spanish. Anita, who has decided that America is her home and her shop its proof, presses Bernardo and Maria toward English, sometimes mid-sentence, so that the film's language politics run inside the Sharks' own kitchen. Schrank, the film's voice of civic contempt, taunts the Sharks in a sneering Spanish of his own, and the insult lands precisely because the film has taught its audience to hear Spanish as the language of dignity elsewhere. The policy also has a theatrical warrant the filmmakers did not need to state: the play itself asks its first audiences to follow a quarrel whose origin is never explained, and Shakespeare's English is, for many modern filmgoers, a foreign language carried by performance. Kushner's Spanish simply extends the condition to the other half of the street.

### Does the unsubtitled Spanish shut out viewers who do not speak it?

No, because the film is engineered against exclusion. Its Spanish passages are doubled by action, reaction and context: a threat arrives with a shove, a joke with laughter, a scolding with a slammed door. What a monolingual viewer loses is nuance, an insult's exact wording, and that managed loss mirrors the gangs' own partial understanding of each other.

The policy matters beyond etiquette because it reverses the 1961 film's deepest structural habit. Lehman's screenplay kept the Sharks largely in English, accented and comic where the Jets were plain, and the Puerto Rican characters existed to be understood by the audience on the audience's terms. In Spielberg's film the terms are set by the speakers. When Anita and Bernardo argue about America in two languages at once, the argument's music is the code-switching itself, pride and shame changing tongues mid-thought. Critics who praised the film returned to this passage of it again and again, and the historians of Latino Hollywood, as the section on the authenticity quarrel will show, treated it as the clearest evidence that the remake had understood what the original got wrong.

The policy's hardest test is comedy, and the film passes it in a way that proves the design. Jokes are timed in Spanish throughout the Sharks' scenes, and Spielberg said openly that he wanted part of the audience laughing at moments the rest would only half catch, the way any city audience divides in a shared room. A monolingual viewer is not excluded from the film; the exclusion is rationed, momentary and reciprocal, since the same viewer sails through Sondheim's densest English patter while a Spanish speaker without English works just as hard in the other direction. The two gaps face each other across the auditorium exactly as the two gangs face each other across the street, and the film's wager is that a room which has felt its own partial understanding will read the feud differently from a room that has been handed everything. The wager is the policy's real content. Subtitles would have made the Spanish available; leaving them off makes the audience inhabit the condition the story is about.

The exhibition context gave the policy a scale its makers could not have bought. A studio film released worldwide on thousands of screens, carrying long passages of untranslated Spanish in the second language of its home market, is a different object from an art-house experiment in parity. Distributors in non-Spanish markets subtitled the English and left the Spanish to stand, so that audiences in Berlin and Tokyo experienced the same rationed comprehension as audiences in New York, with one extra layer of distance added. The gamble was that performance would carry what language withheld everywhere, and the reviews from those markets suggest it did: the film's notices abroad dwell on the same scenes as the American ones, the Sharks' kitchen arguments and Anita's confrontations, which are the scenes where the Spanish does its heaviest work. Parity, exported, turned out to be legible even at two removes, which may be the policy's most durable finding: comprehension in a musical has always been partial, carried by bodies, faces and music, and Spielberg's film merely made the partiality official and shared it fairly.

## How Does the 2021 Casting Answer the 1961 Film?

The casting of the 1961 film is the part of its record that time has treated most harshly. Natalie Wood, born in San Francisco to Russian immigrant parents, played Maria, and her singing was dubbed by Marni Nixon. Richard Beymer's Tony was dubbed by Jimmy Bryant. George Chakiris, who won an Academy Award as Bernardo, was a Greek American dancer from Ohio, and the Puerto Rican members of the Sharks were darkened with a shared brown makeup that Rita Moreno has described protesting on the set: she asked why the Puerto Rican characters all had to be the same colour when Puerto Ricans themselves ranged across every shade, and the makeup stayed. Moreno, the production's one Puerto Rican principal, won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress as Anita and spent sixty years as the film's living rebuttal and its living reproach at once. Spielberg's production set out to answer that record at the level of policy. His first decision, taken before Kushner wrote a page, was that every Shark would be played by a performer of Latin American descent, and the company filled the ensemble through open calls in New York, Orlando and Puerto Rico, hearing tens of thousands of hopefuls.

Maria went to the widest of those searches. Rachel Zegler was a seventeen-year-old schoolgirl from New Jersey who answered an open casting call she had seen on social media, having played Maria in a school production; she was chosen from more than thirty thousand applicants, and the film is her debut. She sings the role herself, in a soprano that needs no assistance, and she plays Maria not as an ingenue waiting to be awakened but as the household's realist, the one member of Bernardo's family counting the cost of everything. The contrast with 1961 is not only ethnic. Wood's Maria was a studio construction, assembled from a star's close-ups and another woman's voice. Zegler's is a single instrument, and the film's balcony scene, played on a fire escape against a wall of other people's windows, depends on the audience believing that the girl and the voice are one.

### How does the drugstore scene change in Kushner's version?

Kushner restages it inside a longer night and changes its moral personnel. The Jets' girlfriends protest the assault instead of joining the taunting; Anybodys stands with the women; and Valentina, not Doc, walks in to end it, shaming boys she has fed. Anita's lie is then spoken to Valentina, woman to woman, and turns intimate rather than public.

Ariana DeBose, an Afro-Latina dancer who had been in the original Broadway company of Hamilton, plays Anita as the film's centre of energy, and the record followed her. She won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress, as Moreno had for the same role sixty years earlier, becoming the first Afro-Latina, and the first openly queer woman of colour, to win that award, and joining Brando and De Niro's Vito Corleone and Ledger and Phoenix's Joker as only the third case of two performers winning Academy Awards for the same character, the first such pair of actresses and the first in the same category. She also won the Golden Globe, the BAFTA and the Screen Actors Guild award for the performance. David Alvarez, who had shared a Tony Award as one of the original Billy Elliots and had largely retired from acting for military service, returned to play Bernardo as a boxer whose violence is a profession before it is a temperament. Mike Faist, invited to audition after Spielberg saw him in Dear Evan Hansen, plays Riff as a boy already haunted, thin as wire and twice as likely to snap, and Josh Andrés Rivera gives Chino the stillness of a boy whose whole life was arranged around being acceptable, until acceptability is murdered in front of him.

Two casting choices carry arguments of their own. The first is iris menas, a transmasculine nonbinary performer, as Anybodys. In Laurents' book Anybodys is a tomboy who begs to be treated as one of the Jets and is mocked for it. Kushner has explained the change in his own words: he had heard trans friends call Anybodys the first trans character in the American musical, and he told Spielberg, "I'd like to really treat Anybodys as a trans character, not as a tomboy," to which the director answered, "That's a great idea. Let's do that." The screenplay gives Anybodys a backstory, work at the docks, and a declaration that he is not a girl, and the Jets' cruelty toward him before the rumble, their refusal of his name and his terms, is written as one more measure of what the gang is. The choice had consequences beyond the screen: the film was initially barred across the Gulf states, with the Anybodys character reported as the cause, and Disney declined to make the cuts the censors asked for, though the film did open in the Emirates some weeks later. The second choice is Moreno herself. She served as an executive producer, consulted on the choreography she had once danced, and played Valentina as the production's embodied memory. She turned ninety the day after the American opening, having played Anita at twenty-nine; the film she blesses is the one that recasts her own role with an Afro-Latina and hands her the Friar's part to grow old in.

The production also wove its own ancestry into the crowd. Three members of the 1961 film's Jets, Harvey Evans, who had been Mouthpiece, Bert Michaels, who had been Snowboy, and David Bean, who had been Tiger, appear among the new film's background performers, old Jets standing in the streets of the remake like retired soldiers at a parade. Andréa Burns, who had played Maria herself on tour in the early nineties, appears as Fausta, one of the Puerto Rican cleaning women who sing "I Feel Pretty" around Zegler's Maria at the department store, a former heroine now standing in the heroine's chorus. These are courtesies, but they are also the film's thesis performed as casting: the property is a relay, each generation handing its roles to the next, and the 1961 company is present in 2021 not as competition but as ancestry, exactly the relation Kushner's screenplay declares toward Wise's film. DeBose's Anita makes the same point from the other direction. She has described pressing for the character's Afro-Latina identity to be written into the film rather than played around, and Moreno's support for that reading, the original Anita blessing the new one's difference, is the relay working at the level of the role itself.

The casting has one acknowledged failure, and it sits at the film's centre. Ansel Elgort's Tony divided reviewers from the first screenings and has divided them since, and the division is taken up in the critics' section below. His casting also drew attention for a reason outside the film: a 2020 allegation of sexual assault against him, which he publicly denied, shadowed the release coverage and is part of the record of the film's reception, though the professional argument about his performance stands on what is on the screen, a handsome stillness that the film's defenders call shellshock and its detractors call vacancy.

The adult roles were cast from the American theatre's own ranks, and the choice underwrites the film's claim to be a theatre event as much as a studio one. Corey Stoll plays Schrank with a tired man's relish for other people's despair, giving Kushner's enlarged bigot a voice that sounds like a precinct at closing time, and Brian d'Arcy James plays Krupke as the beat's exhausted conscience, a sergeant whose decency has survived long enough to know it will change nothing. Between them they keep the film's law human-sized, neither the Prince's thunder nor a cartoon of corruption, and their scenes with the Jets have the grain of men who have arrested the same boys before and expect to do it again. Around the principals the ensemble was built young and drilled like a company: dancers from the Broadway and ballet worlds, actors who could be asked to sing their own parts and dance their own fights, with doubles held in reserve only where the law required them for the youngest performers. The effect on screen is of two gangs that move like single organisms, which is the precondition for everything Peck's choreography attempts, and it is the point at which the production's decade of preparation becomes visible as quality rather than as cost.

## Justin Peck's Choreography and the Shadow of Jerome Robbins

No element of the 1961 film is more protected by reverence than its dancing. Robbins choreographed the stage production and co-directed the film, and his movement language, ballet turned into street threat, became the musical's visual signature: the finger snaps, the crouched advances, the rumble as a dance of knives. Justin Peck, the New York City Ballet's resident choreographer, was hired to build a new choreography rather than to reproduce Robbins', and his solution is the film's formal thesis in miniature. Peck keeps Robbins' grammar and writes new sentences in it. The skirt flares of "America" and the first meeting at the gym quote the original steps outright, as homage. Elsewhere the movement is Peck's, designed with the camera rather than in front of it: Spielberg attended the dance rehearsals with a phone in his hand, working out how the lens would travel through formations that Robbins had composed for a proscenium.

The Dance at the Gym shows the method at full stretch. Spielberg and Kamiński open the sequence with a shot that runs from a hallway Steadicam into a cable camera rising over the floor as the two gangs' dancers wheel into their rival formations, three shots joined to read as one unbroken move, with the lighting rigged to shift as the camera turns so that each side of the room blooms as it is faced. Kamiński shot the whole film on Kodak 35mm stock, with lenses adjusted to flare, so that the lovers' scenes carry halos of light and the night streets carry grain, an old medium used to make the past look remembered rather than reconstructed. Where the 1961 film painted New York in saturated studio colour, Spielberg's city is dust, brick and backlight, a neighbourhood visibly being unbuilt, and the choreography inhabits it accordingly. "America," which Wise staged on a rooftop at night as a duel of the sexes, Peck and Spielberg move into daylight and open street, a ten-day shoot across Harlem, Queens and Paterson in which the number stops being a debate in a closed room and becomes a claim on the whole neighbourhood, Shark girls, Shark boys, shopkeepers and children pulled into its wake, DeBose's shoes melting on the summer pavement and being replaced again and again.

The Prologue, the film's first full dance statement, shows the same grammar applied to violence before the story has given the violence a reason. Robbins' original prologue built the feud out of pure movement, snaps, feints and territorial drift across a playground, and Peck rebuilds it across the cleared blocks and rubble of San Juan Hill, the Jets' opening brawl growing out of the mural's defacement in scuffles that keep almost becoming dances and keep falling back into shoving. The choreography of the sequence is deliberately a degree less finished than what follows, boys rather than dancers, energy rather than form, so that the Dance at the Gym, when it comes, reads as the gangs' art at full stretch and the rumble, when it comes, reads as the same art collapsing. Peck's dancers were drawn heavily from the ballet and Broadway worlds, and every principal dances without a double, Elgort's own School of American Ballet training among the production's quiet assets. The continuity of body is the point. In 1961, editing and doubles could make an actor appear to dance; in 2021, the camera's long moving takes make concealment impossible, and the film's physical credibility, the ground of its claim that dance and violence are one vocabulary, rests on the audience's knowledge that the boy falling is the boy who was dancing a minute earlier.

### Why does the camera keep moving during the dances?

Because a fixed camera would turn the numbers into exhibits and the audience into judges of technique. Spielberg's lens travels with the formations instead, crossing the floor with the Jets and being cut off by the Sharks, so allegiance is built before it is tested. When the rumble comes, its cost lands as a shared loss.

The rumble itself is staged in an abandoned salt warehouse, and it is where Peck's approach pays its debt. The fight begins in quotation, the opening passes reusing Robbins' shapes, and then, as the knives come out, the dancing curdles into Peck's own vocabulary of grabs, trips and falls, choreography failing in real time into violence. Peck has said that "Cool," rebuilt as Tony and Riff's struggle over the gun Riff has bought, is the number that defines his approach: two friends fighting for possession of a pistol in movements that keep almost becoming a duet, virtuosity indistinguishable from desperation. It is the film's whole argument about the musical in one scene. Dance in this story is not decoration laid over violence; it is violence at the temperature just below ignition, and the camera's job is to show the exact degree at which the one becomes the other.

The world the dancing moves through was designed with the same double loyalty. Adam Stockhausen's production design built the neighbourhood twice over, in the streets of Harlem and Brooklyn and on constructed sets at Steiner Studios, with an exterior street raised in Paterson, New Jersey, so that the demolished blocks could be dressed, redressed and finally rubbled under control. Paul Tazewell's costumes carry the film's colour argument on the dancers' bodies: the Jets in the cool tones of steel, concrete and the streets, the Sharks in the warm colours and floral patterns drawn from the Puerto Rico they came from, researched from period photographs, some of them Gordon Parks' colour work. The system reads instantly in every crowd scene and costs the audience no attention, which is what a colour code is for. Tazewell's work was nominated for the Academy Award for costume design, the first such nomination for an African American man in that category, a footnote the film's own argument makes legible: the remake kept distributing firsts among the people the original production had used as colour in the background.

Kamiński's photography supplies the film's single most quoted image by accident. On the last day of shooting, after the balcony scene, he proposed that Tony, wandering the night street singing "María," should step into a puddle whose ripples would break the streetlight into rings of light around his feet, a reflection turning into a halo. The shot was improvised in the small hours with what was on the street, and Rita Moreno, visiting the set, worked the clapperboard for the take. The image has since become the film's emblem, printed on posters and quoted in every account of its afterlife, which is fitting for a production whose whole method was to let inherited forms catch new light: a boy, a name, a puddle, and the cheapest trick in photography doing the work of a star filter.

## The Score on Screen: Dudamel, Newman and the Ghost of 1961

Spielberg's first instinct for the music was to ask John Williams, his collaborator across five decades, to take charge of it, and Williams declined the podium while shaping the answer: David Newman would arrange and adapt Bernstein's score, and Gustavo Dudamel would conduct it. The arrangement itself declares the film's relation to its predecessor. Newman kept alterations that Johnny Green had made for the 1961 film, among them the interpolation of the "Cool" fugue motif into the Prologue and the extended trumpet solo in "Mambo," so that the new recording argues with the old one at the level of orchestration while the screenplay argues at the level of the book. The 1957 score, the 1961 performance tradition and the 2021 reading are layered in the pit exactly as they are layered in the script, and a listener who knows the earlier film hears the homage without being shown it.

Dudamel recorded the score with the New York Philharmonic in 2019, with further sessions by the Los Angeles Philharmonic held during the pandemic that postponed the release. The choice of conductor is itself part of the film's casting argument. A Venezuelan maestro, formed by El Sistema's programme of music as social rescue, standing on the podium of New York's own orchestra to record a score about which boys the city counts as its own: the production's politics are present in the credits before a note is discussed. Jeanine Tesori served as vocal coach and Matt Sullivan as music supervisor, and the singing was prepared to a single standard, that the cast would carry the score without rescue. Every actor sings, and the dubbing that defined the 1961 film's heroine has no counterpart anywhere in the cast list.

One production decision deserves its own paragraph because it locates the film's authenticity more precisely than any press statement. All the numbers were pre-recorded and played back on set, as film musicals are made, with three exceptions: "One Hand, One Heart," "Somewhere" and "A Boy like That" with "I Have a Love" were sung live before the cameras, and Elgort was permitted to sing parts of "María" live as well. The exceptions cluster exactly at the film's hinges, the vow in the chapel, the widow's judgment after the rumble and the confrontation in Maria's room where the story's two women fight over a dead man's meaning. Playback gives a musical polish; live singing gives it breath, strain and the risk of a cracked note, and Spielberg's production spends that risk only where the scene is about the voice as an act of faith, a promise, a verdict, an accusation. It is the sound department's version of the screenplay's method. The score is treated as scripture everywhere and as testimony at the moments when testimony is what the drama needs.

The recording itself entered the world ahead of the film, as recordings of this property have done since the cast album entered Spielberg's childhood home. The soundtrack album was released digitally, mixed in Dolby Atmos, on 3 December 2021, a week before the film opened, and in physical form on the release day itself, so that Dudamel's performance of the score could be auditioned, argued over and absorbed before a single frame was public. The sequence reverses the usual order of a film musical's afterlife and fits this property's history exactly: West Side Story has always lived as a recording first and a staging second in the culture's memory, from the 1957 cast album to the 1961 soundtrack that spent a year at the head of the album charts, and the 2021 production, consciously or not, released its argument in the same order, sound before image, the score asking to be judged before the film that carries it.

## Where Do the Songs Sit in the 2021 Film?

A remake of a musical is read most clearly in its running order, because order is argument. The stage production of 1957 had placed "Cool" before the rumble, as Riff's effort to keep his boys disciplined, and "Gee, Officer Krupke" after it, as the Jets' gallows comedy in hiding. The 1961 film swapped the two, moving "Krupke" into the first half as street clowning and giving "Cool" to the Jets after Riff's death, as an anthem of held tempers among the survivors. Article 347's account of [West Side Story on Broadway](/2028/08/25/west-side-story-broadway-1957/) sets out the stage dispositions in full. Kushner and Spielberg's order argues with both predecessors. "Gee, Officer Krupke" stays in the first half, but it is moved indoors, into the police precinct itself, where the detained Jets perform their mockery of the juvenile-justice system under the noses of the men who run it, Schrank's contempt still ringing in their ears. The number stops being a music-hall turn and becomes evidence: the system the boys ridicule is the same one that has already decided what they are.

"Cool" returns to its stage position before the rumble but changes hands and meaning entirely. Laurents gave it to Riff and the Jets; Lehman's film gave it to Ice after the killing. Kushner gives it to Tony and Riff together, on a derelict pier, as Tony tries to take the gun away from his oldest friend and talk him out of the fight, the two of them circling the weapon in Peck's half-duet, half-wrestle. The song's plea for control is thus addressed to the one person whose loss of control will light the fuse, by the one person who has already learned what violence costs. When it fails, as it must, the failure is personal rather than procedural. The restoration of the stage order with new singers is the film's method in a single stroke: the architecture of 1957 recovered, the meaning reassigned to the characters the new screenplay has built.

The other great reassignment is "Somewhere." On stage it belonged to the dream ballet, sung by an offstage voice while dream versions of the lovers danced a world without gangs. The 1961 film brought it down to earth as the lovers' duet over Riff's and Bernardo's fresh graves. Spielberg gives it to Valentina, alone in her shop after the rumble, sung not as a wish the young make for themselves but as a judgment the old pass on the young's chances: the widow of a mixed marriage, who got her somewhere and buried it, singing the musical's one hymn to a place for us over the street where two boys have just died proving there is none. The performance is the film's emotional summit for many of its reviewers, and it completes Valentina's occupation of the Friar's ground. Friar Laurence also stands at the centre of the catastrophe holding a plan that failed; Valentina stands there holding a life that succeeded and could not be handed on, which is the colder comfort.

The order's hinge is the "Tonight Quintet," and the film stages it as the argument in cross-section. Bernstein's great contrapuntal set piece lets five parties sing their expectations of the same evening at once: the Jets arming for the rumble, the Sharks answering, Anita dressing for a night that will not happen, and the lovers suspended inside their own anticipation, each strand certain of what tonight holds and each, except the lovers', correct in the worst way. Spielberg's editing runs the five strands through the neighbourhood the film has built, the precinct's shadow at one end and the condemned streets at the other, so that the audience watches a single hour approach from five directions across one small district. The sequence is the one place where the film's method, its restored order, its split city, its two languages, converges at full pressure, and it ends, as the play's second act ends, with the machinery fully wound: Tony walking toward the salt shed believing he is the peacemaker, which is the belief the next ten minutes exist to destroy.

Even the film's most conservative number carries a production story that reveals the method. "I Feel Pretty" sits, as it always has, directly after the rumble, and Sondheim had disliked the song for decades, embarrassed by its lyric and willing to see it cut, as it was cut from the 2020 Broadway revival. Spielberg, facing a film with no intermission to cushion the gear change from corpses to comedy, questioned it too. Kushner argued for keeping it, on grounds Spielberg later repeated: this is the first point at which the whole audience is ahead of Maria, knowing what she does not, and the audience's protectiveness toward her happiness, sung in ignorance among the Gimbels counters, is what makes the doorbell of bad news unbearable when it comes. The argument won, the song stayed, and the episode is a small parable of the production: reverence where reverence was argued for, revision where revision could be defended, and nothing changed or kept by reflex.

Sondheim's presence in the production is worth a paragraph of its own, because it governed the film's licence. Kushner had known him for years and had argued with him across all of them against the lyricist's settled habit of disparaging his West Side Story lyrics, written when he was twenty-six and learning the trade under Oscar Hammerstein's eye. Sondheim gave the production a free hand on the questions Kushner brought him, and where he suggested a change, a single line of his own about shining shoes that he had long wanted gone, Kushner declined to make it, preferring the 1957 text complete. The exchange captures the remake's constitution precisely: the author still living was consulted, honoured and, on the one occasion he asked for a cut, respectfully refused by a screenwriter protecting the young Sondheim from the old one. Death converted the arrangement into a memorial within the year, but the film the premiere audience saw had been settled while its lyricist could still object, and the absence of any objection is part of its credentials.

## The Play Inside the Musical, Read Again

Because the remake's method is recovery, its character map deserves to be set beside the play's one more time, in the corrected form the series record establishes. The feud's two houses stand as Laurents left them, Jets for Montagues and Sharks for Capulets, but Kushner's additions reattach the musical's figures to their Shakespearean originals at point after point. The Prince, split by Laurents into Schrank and Krupke, is split again by Kushner into contempt and conscience, and the split explains the play's own puzzle, why Escalus's authority fails: in the film it fails because it is divided against itself, Schrank's hatred licensing what Krupke's decency cannot prevent. The Friar, thinned by the musical into Doc's kindly impotence, is rebuilt as Valentina, a figure with the Friar's full portfolio restored: the secret counsel, the sheltering roof, the moral authority over both houses and the fatal errand carried in innocence. The Nurse's line runs to Anita, as it always has, the go-between who sings the heroine toward the match and then, in the drugstore, speaks the betrayal that in the play is the Nurse's counsel to marry Paris and forget the banished husband, here hardened into a lie told out of trauma rather than worldliness. And Paris, in the corrected mapping, is Chino: the approved suitor, decent, provided, and finally the man who kills the Romeo figure at the story's end, while Bernardo carries Tybalt's office, the kinsman whose skill and temper light the fuse and whose death converts the lover into a killer.

The lover's own line is where the recovery runs deepest. Shakespeare's Romeo enters the play in a posture the musical had discarded: already experienced in extremity, in love with Rosaline to the point of sickness, and capable, within a day of his wedding, of killing his wife's cousin in a transport of grief and shame. Laurents and Lehman smoothed that boy into a dreamer, and the smoothing cost the musical its tragic mechanism, since a dreamer's killing of Bernardo can only be an accident of plotting. Kushner's paroled Tony restores the mechanism whole. His violence is behind him and inside him at once, his reform is real and incomplete, and the rumble becomes what Romeo's intervention in the duel is in the play: the peacemaker's arrival at the one appointment where peace costs more than he has. Read against the play, the remake is not a modernization that happens to keep the score. It is a restoration of the source's psychology wearing the musical's clothes, which is why scholars of adaptation can treat it as evidence about Shakespeare as readily as about Bernstein.

The balcony completes the circuit. Shakespeare's lovers meet at a window above a garden, in the one architecture the story has never surrendered, and every era rebuilds it from its own housing stock: a studio Verona balcony for Wood and Beymer, a swimming pool's shimmer for DiCaprio and Danes, and for Zegler and Elgort the fire escape of a tenement, iron stairs against brick, with the whole building's windows lit above them and the street's traffic below. Kushner then sends the same couple to be married in a museum chapel, the Cloisters, trading the Friar's cell for a room of genuine medieval stone freighted up the Hudson a century ago. The two locations state the remake's double allegiance in a single evening's geography: the lovers' present is a fire escape in a condemned district, and their vows are spoken inside the deep past the district is being cleared to make room for, Lincoln Center rising on one side of the story and the Middle Ages on the other.

One more figure belongs in the map, because Kushner's boldest stroke concerns her: the Chorus. Shakespeare opens with a speaker who stands outside the action and tells the audience what the feud is and how it ends. The musical abolished the figure, folding his work into Bernstein's wordless Prologue, and the 1961 film kept the abolition. Spielberg's film finds the Chorus again in an unexpected place. Its opening minutes, the rubble, the wrecking ball, the mural, the anthem, are a prologue spoken by the city itself, and the film returns to that voice whenever the story needs framing: in the Lincoln Center hoardings that overlook the gangs' last streets, in Schrank's announcements of the demolition, in the final procession into the shop. The demolished neighbourhood performs the Chorus's office throughout, telling the audience at every stage what the feud's ground is and what it costs, and the device completes the restoration. Kushner did not only thicken the musical's characters back toward the play's. He rebuilt the play's frame around them, and set the frame in the one material 1957 could not have used, the documented fate of the streets the story names.

## What Did the Critics Say About Spielberg's West Side Story?

The reviews were the warmest any Spielberg film had received in years, and the disagreement they contain is unusually instructive because the dissenters and the champions were looking at the same evidence. Manohla Dargis, in the New York Times, made the film a Critic's Pick and located its achievement in its balance: "a dazzling display of filmmaking craft that also feels raw, unsettled and alive," from collaborators who, rather than embalming a classic with homage or aggressively reinventing it, had found the third way between those failures. Peter Bradshaw in the Guardian gave it five stars and judged its lovers "a more real pair than Richard Beymer and Natalie Wood in the original," praising Spielberg for not hiding the stage origins. Jake Coyle of the Associated Press called it "a feat of reconstruction" and, flatly, a better movie than the 1961 original in almost every respect. Matt Zoller Seitz has since called it the best film musical since All That Jazz. The aggregate record matches the superlatives: ninety-one percent positive on Rotten Tomatoes across nearly four hundred reviews, a Metacritic score of eighty-five, places on the National Board of Review and American Film Institute lists of the year's ten best.

The chorus was broad and crossed the profession's generations. Owen Gleiberman in Variety found the film's brash effervescence infectious, a director's visible joy in making the thing he had waited a lifetime to make. Helen O'Hara in Empire, another five-star verdict, called it faithful to its roots and alive to the world it was released into. Chris Evangelista, writing for a younger readership at SlashFilm, called it a knock-out and one of the best films of Spielberg's career, a verdict echoed by Jason Bailey's observation that its 156 minutes pass in barely a breath. Richard Lawson in Vanity Fair gave the praise its most considered form, writing that Spielberg and Kushner had done justice to what Bernstein, Robbins and Sondheim made, "not subverting its enduring value, but rather, with fire and grace, doing so much to earn it." Quentin Tarantino, no constituency the film was made for, later named it alongside Top Gun: Maverick as a true cinematic spectacle of a kind he had nearly stopped expecting. When directors praise a rival's musical for its spectacle and critics praise it for its conscience, the film has usually found the joint between craft and argument, and the joint here is the one the introduction named: restoration performed as creation.

The dissent came from Richard Brody in The New Yorker, who panned the film on the ground that its creators had made "ill-conceived additions and misguided revisions." Brody's charge deserves to be taken seriously because it identifies the real risk of the Kushner method: every explanation a screenplay adds is a small withdrawal from the score's account of lyric irrationality, and a Tony with a parole officer, a Bernardo with a boxing record and a neighbourhood with a demolition date are all, in one light, refusals to let the musical be a fable. The adjudication this article reaches is that Brody is right about the cost and wrong about the balance. The additions he deplores are concentrated exactly where the 1957 book was thinnest and where the 1961 film had been most evasive, in the lovers' pasts and the Sharks' inner lives, and the numbers, the part of the work where fable lives, were left strictly alone. Explanation governs the dialogue; the songs still happen the way weather happens. A fable that has been given a ground does not stop being a fable. It starts being a tragedy, which is the promotion the play itself underwent when Shakespeare gave the feud a city and the lovers a calendar.

### Is Ansel Elgort's Tony the film's weak point?

Yes, by the weight of the record. Elgort sings creditably and dances better than his detractors allow, but he is out-acted scene by scene by Faist, Alvarez and Zegler, and his stillness reads as absence where Tony's desperation should burn. Ty Burr's phrase for the performance, a pallid Tony, states the consensus with unusual economy.

The Tony question matters to the film's Shakespearean standing because Romeo is the role the whole tradition keeps failing to fill with a merely decorative performer. The play survived Burbage and Betterton and Garrick on the strength of Romeos who could make impulsiveness legible as intelligence. The defence of Elgort, that Tony's numbness is a parolee's damage honestly performed, is available and partly persuasive; it does not explain why the numbness persists unchanged into the balcony scene, where the film most needs ardour.

A second specialist quarrel ran beneath the general reviews, in the dance world, where Robbins' choreography is held as a text in its own right and Peck's replacement of it struck some guardians of that text as the remake's true presumption. The quarrel mirrors the film's literary one with the terms reversed. Where Brody accused the screenplay of adding too much explanation, the choreography's critics accused the dancing of keeping too little of its source, as if fidelity were owed to Robbins' steps in a way it was not owed to Laurents' dialogue. The adjudication runs parallel as well. Peck's method, quotation at the joints and invention in the limbs, treats Robbins exactly as Kushner treats Lehman: as a honoured predecessor whose solutions are cited where they remain unsurpassed and replaced where the new reading needs different movement. A tradition that can only be inherited whole is a museum, and the film's consistent refusal, in book, score and dance alike, was to behave like a museum's tenant. That refusal is what its champions heard as life and its detractors as trespass, and the split in the dance press is Brody's split restated in another art.

The reception has a second act that the opening reviews could not have predicted, because the film's commercial failure briefly rewrote its critical story. In the release season the coverage divided into two conversations that barely touched: the reviewers' film, honoured and argued over, and the trades' film, a flop whose name became shorthand for the theatrical market's collapse. Distance has steadily merged the two into a single judgment, and the later verdicts are more settled than the first ones. Ty Burr, ranking the century's movie musicals for the Washington Post several years on, placed it third and closed the case in one paragraph: "We'll always have the 1961 original, but Steven Spielberg's remake is simply the better-made movie: dramatically richer, better acted, more visually kinetic, less ethnically bogus," its pain grounded, he wrote, in a neighbourhood falling to the wrecking ball. The sentence is worth dwelling on because it adopts, as settled critical language, precisely the claims the film's makers had made for it in advance, the richer drama, the repaired representation, the ground that falls. A remake's final review is written by time, and in this case time has ruled for the defence on almost every count except the hero, which is where the next section's verdict also lands. Spielberg's film repeats the old pattern in modern dress: a production that solves nearly everything except its hero, carried across the line by the ensemble around him. It is fair to record that Zegler, DeBose, Faist and Alvarez do not merely support the film; in its best passages they are the film, and the awards electorate noticed, giving DeBose the Oscar, Zegler the Golden Globe for Best Actress in a musical or comedy, the youngest winner in that category's history at twenty, and the cast a sweep of ensemble honours.

## Does Authenticity Settle the Argument? The Puerto Rican Reckoning

The film's claim to have repaired the original's representation was tested most severely by Puerto Rican and Latino critics, and their disagreement is the one disagreement about the film that cannot be resolved by pointing at the screen. On one side stands testimony like that of Luis Reyes, the film historian and co-author of Hispanics in Hollywood, who attended an early screening and praised the production's realism: its Latin cast in the Puerto Rican roles, the range of skin colours among the Sharks handled as Puerto Ricans actually present them, and dialogue in authentic Puerto Rican and Nuyorican Spanish. Reyes speaks from inside the history the film was trying to correct, and his verdict carries the weight of the archive. Josie Meléndez Hernández wrote in the same direction, that the harmful pieces of the first adaptation had been patched up and the depiction of Puerto Ricans improved, while adding the reservation that it is still not a musical for Latines in the possessive sense.

On the other side stands Arlene Dávila, the anthropologist and scholar of Latino media, writing in El Nuevo Día that Puerto Ricans and Latinos had once again been reduced to "made-up themes and ideas taken from the playbook of what 'Puerto Rican' and 'Latino' are to the white imagination," and that the remedy is not better remakes but original productions written and produced by the community itself. Aurora Flores made the complementary point about process: accents and advisory boards are not authorship, and what is needed is writers, directors and producers. Mandy Velez noted the asterisk at the centre of the casting claim, that Maria is played by an actress of Colombian descent rather than a Puerto Rican. These are not complaints about details that a more careful production could have fixed. They are a structural objection: a film directed by Spielberg, written by Kushner and owned by a studio can redistribute dignity among its characters, but it cannot redistribute the authority to tell the story, and for Dávila that authority is the story.

Between the two positions stands a third body of testimony that complicates both. Susan G. Cole, writing for the Toronto Film Critics Association, held that the opportunity to cast Latino performers as Puerto Ricans was reason enough on its own to remake the musical, and named the screenplay's author as the film's star, a judgment that accepts Dávila's premise, that authorship is where the value sits, while drawing the opposite conclusion about this screenplay. The production had in fact surrounded itself with Latino consultants and an advisory process, and Moreno herself functioned as its most visible internal critic, an executive producer whose set presence was a standing audit of every choice about the Sharks. For Dávila's side, that process is precisely the problem: advice is revocable and authorship is not, and an advisory board can be overruled in a way a writer cannot. For the defence, the process is the mechanism by which the film's specific accuracies, the registers of its Spanish, the skin tones of its Sharks, the weight of its history, got onto the screen, and Reyes's testimony certifies the result. The disagreement is therefore not about whether the film listened. It is about whether listening, however documented its fruits, can substitute for the thing Dávila demands, which is the pen.

The adjudication has to hold both halves. Within the terms the production set itself, performance-side authenticity, the film achieved nearly everything it promised: the casting, the language, the unsubtitled parity, the scholarship of detail, down to the percussion of Nuyorican Spanish that Reyes certified. Judged as an act of authorship, it remains what it was always going to be, a loving reconstruction by two of the American theatre's most decorated outsiders. The film's own ending states the limit with accidental precision. Maria's final speech, Shakespeare's judgment scene reassigned to the survivor, is delivered by a character written in 1957 by Arthur Laurents and re-voiced in 2021 by Tony Kushner. The voice is truer than it was. It is still a voice written for her. The honest verdict is that Spielberg's film closes the representation argument about the 1961 film, which had become indefensible, and opens the authorship argument about every future version, which is where the argument now properly lives.

## The Ending: Maria's Speech and the Carried Body

Every version of this story is finally judged by its last five minutes, because the ending is where the adaptation's politics become unavoidable. Shakespeare closes with the machinery of the state: the Prince descends, the Friar confesses, the fathers compete in grief and promise golden statues, and the judgment "All are punished" falls on a stage full of survivors who must now live inside it. Laurents' great structural invention was to strip that machinery away and give the judgment to the one person with the least standing to deliver it. No prince comes to San Juan Hill. Maria takes the gun, speaks the verdict herself, and the feud ends not by authority but by exhaustion and shame, the gangs joining to carry Tony's body out of the playground as she follows. The 1961 film kept Laurents' design whole, and Spielberg's film keeps it too, and the interest lies in what the new screenplay has arranged around the inherited shape.

Kushner's arrangement darkens every term. Chino, the Paris figure, is not left standing in bewildered innocence, as the play's Paris is left dead at the tomb and the musical's Chino merely led away; he has walked the whole second half toward this shot, warned by his own gang, armed with Riff's gun, and the police arrest him in the street while the body is still warm. Maria's speech, delivered by Zegler standing over Tony with the gun passing from hand to hand as she asks how many bullets remain and whether she is expected to use them on the rest, is played not as hysteria but as an audit: each gang is made to hear the price of the feud stated by its survivor, in the street the city is already demolishing, with Schrank's law reduced to a bystander at its own crime scene. The play's Prince promises that some shall be pardoned and some punished. Spielberg's film inverts the promise. Everyone in the final frame has been punished, and the only pardon on offer is the one the gangs grant each other by lifting the body together.

The carrying itself is the film's closing argument about the feud. In Verona the fathers reconcile over two bodies and convert their hatred into monuments. On Spielberg's street the reconciliation is wordless, physical and temporary: Jets and Sharks stoop together, take Tony's weight between them and carry him into Valentina's shop, the one room in the film where the two peoples have been governed by something other than the grudge, while Maria follows close behind and Valentina watches the procession enter the space her marriage built. Nothing is promised. No statues are vowed, no wedding joins the houses, and Krupke's arrests proceed off to the side like a formality the street has stopped believing in. The film's Shakespeareanism is at its purest here, because the play's ending is also a procession and also a silence, two households carrying their dead past the audience while a prince speaks over them. Kushner removes the prince's voice and lets the carrying say it. The last thing the film shows its audience, before the dedication card to the director's father, is the feud's children performing the feud's only useful act, bearing the weight together, too late for it to do the dead any good and exactly in time to define what the story has been about.

## Why Did West Side Story Fail at the Box Office?

The figures are stark and should be stated exactly. The film cost $100 million to produce. It opened in North America on 10 December 2021 to $10.6 million, first place for the weekend and well below the projections, and it finished with $38.5 million in the United States and Canada and $37.5 million everywhere else, a worldwide total of $76 million. Trade estimates put its break-even point near $300 million once marketing and the costs of the year's delay were counted, which makes it one of the largest financial failures ever attached to a film this admired. A remake that critics placed on ten-best lists and that two national bodies named among the year's finest films was seen in its opening weekend, by the ticket counters' estimate, by about eight hundred thousand people in its home market.

The causes are documented well enough to be separated from the mythology. The release landed in the first surge of the Omicron variant, and the film's natural audience skewed old: more than half of its opening audience was over thirty-five, the demographic then staying home in the largest numbers. It had no star whose name sold tickets to the young, Elgort and Moreno excepted, and Disney's marketing was criticized in the New York Times for failing to sell the one name it did have, the director's. A week after opening, Spider-Man: No Way Home absorbed the market whole. The older explanations offered at the time, that musicals were out of fashion or that the property was exhausted, do not survive the counterexamples of the same season's hits in other genres aimed at the same older audience; the film was a prestige release dropped into the worst fortnight of a plague year and priced, by its budget, as a blockbuster.

The people who did go loved it, which sharpens the puzzle and then dissolves it. Opening audiences gave the film an A on CinemaScore and an eighty-eight percent positive score on PostTrak, with seventy percent saying they would definitely recommend it, and the audience that found it was the audience the film was built from: fifty-seven percent female, fifty-two percent over thirty-five, thirty percent Hispanic or Latino. The failure was a failure of attendance, not of reception, and the industry argued about the attendance in public. Bob Iger, in his final week as Disney's chairman, defended the campaign while conceding that streaming competition, ticket prices and the pandemic may have kept the audience home; Kyle Buchanan in the New York Times answered that the marketing had missed its crucial opportunities, above all the chance to sell the film on its director's name; analysts such as CNBC's Sarah Whitten pointed to the older audience's caution during the Omicron surge and the absence of stars to pull the young. The three accounts are compatible, and together they describe a film released as an event to an audience that had stopped attending events, by a studio that could not decide whether it was selling a classic, a conscience or a director.

The afterlife corrected the imbalance without erasing it. The film arrived on streaming services in March 2022 and was watched, by Samba TV's measurement, in 1.9 million American households within weeks of its Academy Award nominations. Its awards season was a procession: three Golden Globes including Best Motion Picture, Musical or Comedy; five BAFTA nominations and two wins, including the first BAFTA for casting; eleven Critics' Choice nominations; seven Academy Award nominations, among them Best Picture, which made it only the second film, after the 1962 Mutiny on the Bounty, to be nominated for that award as a new adaptation of a source whose earlier film had won it. Spielberg's Best Director nomination made him the first director nominated in that category in six consecutive decades. The film that the market rejected in December was, by March, the version of the musical that the institutions had adopted. Both facts belong in the record, and the second does not cancel the first; it reclassifies it, from a judgment on the film to a judgment on the circumstances, a distinction the history of the play's own flops, from the 1940 Broadway Romeo and Juliet onward, teaches repeatedly.

The economics deserve one cold paragraph. A $100 million production budget is only the visible part of the loss: a year of delay carries interest and holding costs, a global campaign for a December prestige release is bought at blockbuster prices, and an exclusive forty-five-day theatrical window, with IMAX and Dolby placements, commits the studio to event economics whether or not an event materializes. Against that structure, $76 million in worldwide ticket sales, of which the studio sees roughly half, is not a shortfall but a collapse, and the trades' estimate of a $300 million break-even is the measure of how completely the film's commercial design depended on the world of 2019, the world in which it was shot. The film was financed, shot and scheduled as a pre-pandemic object, a Christmas release aimed at four-quadrant nostalgia, and delivered into a market that had reorganized itself around franchises and streaming in the interval. Nothing in that sentence is a judgment on the film's quality, and everything in it is a judgment on the risk that prestige remakes now carry: the more faithfully a production honours a classic's scale, the more completely its finances are exposed to a theatrical market that no longer guarantees a floor under adult drama of any kind.

## The Verdict on Spielberg's West Side Story

Set the three versions in a row and the shape of the tradition clarifies. The 1957 stage production invented the translation: Laurents' substitutions, Bernstein's score, Sondheim's lyrics and Robbins' bodies turned the play into an American argument about whose hatred gets called ancient. The 1961 film, examined at length in the account of [West Side Story on film in 1961](/2028/09/01/west-side-story-film-1961/), fixed that translation in the world's memory and carried its period compromises with it, the dubbed heroine, the darkened Sharks, the numbers rearranged for the screen's convenience. The 2021 film is the first version since Robbins to go back to the sources rather than to the monument: back to Laurents' book before Lehman's trims, back to the stage's own order of numbers, and behind both, back to the play, whose hero is a violent boy trying to stop being one, whose peacemaker is an older cleric with a scheme, and whose ending belongs to the living rather than the dead. Kushner's Valentina, his paroled Tony and his condemned neighbourhood are all, beneath their period dress, recoveries of Shakespearean structures that the musical had simplified away. That is why the remake-as-restoration is the right name for it. The newest version is, in its bones, the oldest one.

Against that, the reservations entered in this article stand. The choreography honours Robbins more than it rivals him, and knows it. Tony is underplayed to the edge of absence. The authorship objection raised by Dávila and Flores is unanswerable from inside the film and will be the first question put to any fourth version. And the commercial failure, whatever its causes, means the film's influence will work through the profession and the classroom rather than through the mass audience the property once commanded. The profession has taken it up on exactly those terms: orchestras have presented the film live to picture, the New York Philharmonic among them, performing Dudamel's own recorded score beneath the film in the hall at the centre of the demolished neighbourhood, so that the work now circulates the way the play circulates, through institutions, revivals and teaching, gathering its audience a room at a time. None of this overturns the verdict. Spielberg's West Side Story is the most complete realisation of the musical yet filmed, the version in which the Sharks' Verona is as densely lived as the Jets', in which the feud is given back its economics and the lovers their pasts, and in which Maria's final judgment over Tony's body, "all are punished" reassigned from prince to survivor, lands with the full weight the play's last scene promises. The cliche said the musical was a period piece, fixed in 1957 like a fly in amber. Spielberg treated it the way the stage has always treated the play itself, as the subject of [the play in twenty-first-century theatre](/2028/06/16/play-in-21st-century-theatre/) demonstrates season after season: a story that survives by being retold at full strength for the moment that receives it. The film is that retelling, and its failure to find its moment says more about the moment than about the film.

The later rankings have been quietly ratifying the verdict ever since. The film has taken its place in the canonical lists beside the version it argued with: tied with the 1961 film at the head of one major magazine's ranking of musical adaptations, placed third among all movie musicals in another, its cinematography voted among the finest of its century by critics' polls. That company is the film's natural shelf. The tradition now holds three complete West Side Stories, a stage original that invented the translation, a first film that fixed it in the world's memory with its period's compromises frozen inside it, and a second film that went back past the monument to the sources and rebuilt the story for a country that had changed around it. A fourth version, whenever it comes, will have to argue with all three, and the argument it will find hardest is not with Wise's classic or Laurents' book but with Kushner's question, the one this article has followed from the rubble of the opening shot to Maria's last speech: whose story is being told, in whose language, on whose ground, and at whose expense.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Spielberg profile, and what does it record about the film?

The InsightCrunch Spielberg profile is the table in this article setting out the film's record in one place: its director, screenwriter, choreographer and cinematographer, its principal cast, its premiere and release dates, its $100 million budget and $76 million worldwide gross, its policy of leaving the Spanish dialogue unsubtitled, its seven Academy Award nominations and its dedication to the director's father. It exists so that the claims made for the film, about its authenticity, its craft and its commercial failure, can be checked against the underlying facts rather than traded as impressions.

### Q: When did Spielberg's West Side Story premiere, and how long did its release take?

The film premiered on 29 November 2021 at Jazz at Lincoln Center in New York, three days after Stephen Sondheim's death, and opened in the United States on 10 December 2021. The release had been scheduled for 18 December 2020 and was postponed for a full year by the pandemic's closure of cinemas, so the finished film waited nearly two years between the end of photography in September 2019 and its arrival before the public, a delay that shaped both its mournful premiere atmosphere and its difficult commercial season.

### Q: Who is Valentina, and why did Rita Moreno play her?

Valentina is Tony Kushner's expansion of the musical's Doc figure: Doc has died before the story opens, and his drugstore is run by his widow, a Puerto Rican woman who employs Tony, shelters the Jets and speaks to both gangs from inside a marriage that crossed the film's divide. Rita Moreno, who had played Anita in the 1961 film and served as an executive producer on this one, took the role, so that the earlier film's Anita became the new film's peacemaker, singing "Somewhere" and inheriting the Friar's function of delivering, in innocence, the message that kills Tony.

### Q: Why is the Spanish dialogue in the film left without subtitles?

The omission was a stated policy of respect and parity, defended publicly by Spielberg, Kushner and Moreno. Kushner said the Spanish had to exist in equal proportions alongside the English with no help, and Spielberg argued that subtitling would double down on English and hand it power over the Spanish. The film is built so that action and reaction carry the sense for viewers who do not follow the words, and the controlled loss of nuance reproduces, in the audience, the partial understanding with which the gangs face one another.

### Q: How was Rachel Zegler cast as Maria?

Zegler was a seventeen-year-old schoolgirl in New Jersey who answered an open casting call circulated on social media, having already played Maria in a school production. She was chosen from more than thirty thousand applicants, and West Side Story is her film debut. She performs all her own singing, a pointed contrast with the 1961 film, in which Natalie Wood's Maria was sung by the uncredited Marni Nixon. Zegler went on to win the Golden Globe for Best Actress in a musical or comedy, the youngest winner in the category's history at twenty.

### Q: What did Ariana DeBose win for playing Anita, and whose record did her win match?

DeBose won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress, along with the Golden Globe, the BAFTA and the Screen Actors Guild award. Her Oscar matched Rita Moreno's win for the same role in the 1961 film, making Anita only the third character in Academy history, after Vito Corleone and the Joker, to win acting Oscars for two different performers, and the first such case involving two actresses in the same category. DeBose was also the first Afro-Latina, and the first openly queer woman of colour, to win the supporting actress award.

### Q: Why does Tony have a prison record in the 2021 film?

Kushner's screenplay gives Tony a year in prison for beating a rival gang member nearly to death, and he is on parole, working at the drugstore and determined to stay reformed. The addition repairs the musical's thinnest joint: in 1957 and 1961 Tony's wish to escape the gang life has no stated cause, and his killing of Bernardo arrives as an anomaly. A Tony who knows his own violence and has sworn off it turns the killing into a relapse, which gives the catastrophe the shape of tragedy rather than accident.

### Q: What is happening to the neighbourhood the gangs are fighting over?

It is being demolished. The film opens over the rubble of San Juan Hill, the largely Black and Puerto Rican district cleared to build Lincoln Center, a clearance that displaced some seven thousand residents, most of them Puerto Rican. Schrank tells the gangs their turf is condemned, and the knowledge hangs over the whole film: the Jets are defending streets they cannot inherit and the Sharks are claiming room in a district with a few years to live. Kushner's addition gives the feud the vanishing ground that Shakespeare's ancient grudge has in Verona.

### Q: How is Anybodys different in the 2021 film?

In Laurents' book Anybodys is a tomboy begging to be treated as one of the Jets. Kushner, at his own proposal and with Spielberg's agreement, treats the character as what trans readers had long identified, a trans boy, played by the transmasculine nonbinary performer iris menas. The screenplay gives Anybodys work, a backstory and an insistence on being recognised on his own terms, and the Jets' refusal of that recognition before the rumble is written as one more measure of the gang. The reading was discussed widely when the film appeared.

### Q: Why was the film barred in several Gulf countries?

Trade reporting at the release attributed the ban across the Gulf Cooperation Council states to the Anybodys character, a transgender role played by a nonbinary actor. Disney declined to make the cuts the censors requested, and the film initially went unreleased across the region, though it did open in the United Arab Emirates some weeks later. Producer Kevin McCollum answered the censors by recalling the Puritans who closed Shakespeare's theatres, arguing that the story would outlast the prohibition, an unusually direct line from the film's Shakespearean source to its modern reception.

### Q: Who choreographed the 2021 film, and how does the dancing treat Jerome Robbins' steps?

Justin Peck, resident choreographer of the New York City Ballet, created new choreography that quotes Robbins at chosen points, the skirt work of "America" and the first meeting at the gym among them, rather than reproducing the 1961 routines. Elsewhere the movement is Peck's own, built with the moving camera: "Cool" becomes Tony and Riff's struggle over a gun, half duet and half wrestling match, and the rumble begins in Robbins' shapes before the dancing breaks down into grabbing, tripping violence. The approach is homage as grammar, not as tracing.

### Q: Why does Valentina sing "Somewhere" in the 2021 film?

On stage the song belonged to the dream ballet, sung by an offstage voice, and the 1961 film gave it to the lovers as a duet. Spielberg assigns it to Valentina alone, after the rumble, in her shop. The reassignment changes the song's tense: instead of the young wishing for a place where they can be together, the widow of a marriage that crossed the divide sings the wish over two fresh graves, as judgment and as elegy. It is the emotional centre of Moreno's performance and the clearest single statement of the film's method, an old number made new by its singer.

### Q: Why did Spielberg and Sondheim consider cutting "I Feel Pretty," and why did the song stay?

Sondheim had been embarrassed by the song's lyric for decades and had allowed it to be cut from the 2020 Broadway revival, while Spielberg doubted whether its gaiety could follow the rumble in a film with no intermission to reset the audience. Kushner argued that the song's placement is the point: it is the first moment the audience knows more than Maria, and the protectiveness aroused by watching her sing in ignorance is what gives the arrival of the news its force. The argument persuaded both men, and the song stayed, staged among the Gimbels counters where Maria works.

### Q: Where is "America" staged in the 2021 film, and how does that differ from the 1961 film?

The 1961 film staged "America" on a rooftop at night, as a duel between Anita and Bernardo and their respective choruses. Spielberg moves the number into daylight and open street, a ten-day shoot across Harlem, Queens and Paterson, in which the argument between staying and belonging spills out of the tenement and pulls in shopkeepers, children and the whole block. The change matches the film's larger method: a debate the earlier film held in a closed room becomes a public claim on the neighbourhood, sung in warm colours against the Jets' steel blues.

### Q: What was the film's production budget, and what did it gross at the box office worldwide?

The production budget was $100 million. The film grossed $38.5 million in the United States and Canada and $37.5 million in other territories, a worldwide total of $76 million, against trade estimates that put its break-even point near $300 million once marketing and delay costs were counted. The gap makes it one of the most expensive commercial failures ever attached to a critically honoured film, a result the record attributes chiefly to the Omicron surge suppressing its older audience, the absence of a ticket-selling star, and the arrival of Spider-Man: No Way Home a week later.

### Q: Why did Stephen Sondheim's death frame the film's premiere?

Sondheim died on 26 November 2021, nine days after attending a Lincoln Center screening of the film with its cast, one of his last public appearances. The premiere followed three days later, on 29 November, so the film arrived as an unintended memorial to its own lyricist. Sondheim had been consulted on the production and had given Kushner a free hand on the small lyric questions that arose, while resisting changes to songs he had written at twenty-six. The film he approved is the one the premiere audience saw, with his name on the songs exactly as written in 1957.

### Q: What did Richard Brody of The New Yorker object to in the film?

Brody panned the film for what he called its "ill-conceived additions and misguided revisions," arguing in substance that Kushner's explanations, the parole, the boxing, the demolition, work against the musical's nature as a lyric fable. The objection identifies a genuine cost: every motive the screenplay supplies is a small withdrawal from the score's irrationality. The counterargument, adopted in this article, is that the additions cluster where the 1957 book was thinnest and the 1961 film most evasive, and that the numbers themselves were left untouched, so explanation governs the dialogue while the songs keep their weather.

### Q: Why do some Puerto Rican critics still object to the remake?

The objection is about authorship rather than accuracy. Arlene Dávila argued in El Nuevo Día that Puerto Ricans had again been reduced to themes from the white imagination's playbook, and Aurora Flores wrote that advisory boards and accents are no substitute for Latino writers, directors and producers telling their own stories. Mandy Velez noted that Maria is played by an actress of Colombian rather than Puerto Rican descent. Against these stand Latino voices such as the film historian Luis Reyes, who praised the casting and the authentic Spanish. The disagreement is genuine and unresolved.

### Q: How did the film perform with awards bodies despite the box office?

It was one of the most honoured films of its year. It won three Golden Globes including Best Motion Picture, Musical or Comedy, two BAFTAs including the first awarded for casting, and two Critics' Choice awards from eleven nominations. At the Academy Awards it received seven nominations, including Best Picture, a feat matched only by the 1962 Mutiny on the Bounty among remakes of Best Picture winners' sources, and Ariana DeBose won Best Supporting Actress. Spielberg's directing nomination made him the first filmmaker nominated in that category across six consecutive decades.

Begin from the profile's three load-bearing entries: the screenplay credit, which marks the film as a fresh adaptation of the stage work rather than a copy of the 1961 film; the unsubtitled Spanish, which shows the retelling redistributing dignity between the story's two peoples; and the dedication, which ties the most technologically advanced version to a father's record shelf in 1957. Set those beside the gross, and the essay's shape appears: each generation's West Side Story is judged first by its moment's politics and only later by its art, and the profile holds both judgments in one frame.
