Election laws occupy a strange position in the civil services examination. The subject is small enough to be bounded: one Part of the Constitution, two principal statutes, a schedule, a handful of amendments and a short line of judgments generates nearly every question the papers have produced on it. Yet it is the subject on which otherwise strong candidates most often write answers that a constitutional lawyer would not recognise, because they prepare it as politics. They learn the controversies of the season, the arguments of the editorial pages and the vocabulary of the television debate, and they reproduce all three in the answer book. The examiner, however, is not testing whether the candidate follows politics. The examiner is testing whether the candidate knows where electoral authority comes from, which institution holds which piece of it, what limits the text places on each holder, and what the courts have said when those limits were disputed. A candidate who can answer those questions can handle any framing the paper invents. A candidate who cannot will be defeated by the first unfamiliar framing, however much political commentary has been absorbed.

This article teaches the subject the way the examination rewards it. It separates three things that preparation material habitually mixes: the constitutional position, which is what the text of the articles provides; the statutory detail, which is what Parliament has enacted under those articles; and the reform debate, which is what commissions, committees and courts have proposed without any of it becoming law. Most lost marks in this field are lost at the joints between those three layers. A proposal by the Law Commission is written up as if it were an amendment. A judgment is cited for a proposition it expressly declined to decide. A provision of the Representation of the People Act, 1950 is attributed to the Act of 1951. The discipline that prevents all three errors is the discipline this article builds: cite the article, not the argument. The namable claim of the article is that election questions in these papers are marked on whether the candidate identifies the constitutional source of the power in issue and the limits on it, and that an answer reasoning outward from the text of the relevant article outscores an answer reciting the merits of a reform, because the examiner is testing whether the candidate understands where authority comes from rather than what the candidate thinks of how it is used.

## How election laws are tested, and where the marks sit

The syllabus location is the first thing to fix, because it decides the level at which the subject must be held. In the Preliminary examination, election law sits inside Indian Polity, and it is tested as fact: the number of an article, the content of a schedule, the year of an amendment, the composition of a body, the identity of the authority that decides a named question. A Prelims question on this field can usually be answered from a single line of the constitutional text, and it is lost by candidates who know the debate but not the line. In the Main examination, the subject sits principally in General Studies Paper II, in the company of the Constitution, governance and the functioning of constitutional bodies, and it is tested as institution and argument: the independence of the Election Commission, the working of the anti-defection provisions, the financing of politics, the representation consequences of the delimitation freeze. The same body of law therefore has to be held twice, once as a set of citable propositions precise enough for an objective paper, and once as a set of institutional questions open enough for an essay-style answer, and preparation that serves only one of the two papers will leak marks in the other.

The syllabus says as much in its own words, and the candidate should notice that it does. The General Studies Paper II syllabus names the appointment to various constitutional posts and the powers, functions and responsibilities of various constitutional bodies, under which the Election Commission is examined, and it carries a line found nowhere else in the paper: the salient features of the Representation of People's Act, naming a statute outright as examinable territory. A syllabus that names an Act is telling the candidate where the questions are manufactured, and it is the only statute the polity syllabus honours in that way, which is itself a one-line answer to the aspirant who asks whether the two Acts need to be read as texts or merely known as names. The Prelims syllabus is less specific and more dangerous, since its undifferentiated Indian Polity heading conceals the fact that this field supplies some of the paper's most mechanical questions, on article numbers, schedule paragraphs, amendment years and deciding authorities, the very items that cannot be improvised from understanding and must simply be known.

The demand types recur, and recognising them is worth more than any additional fact. The first type asks for a provision and its working: what the article or section provides, and how the institution behaves under it. The second asks for a judgment and its consequence: what the court decided, on which ground, and what changed in the law because of it. The third asks for a controversy stated neutrally: a reform proposal, a criticism of an institution, a contested amendment, with the candidate expected to set out the strongest form of each side and conclude on the institutional question rather than on the political one. The fourth asks for a comparison the text itself supplies: between the two Representation of the People Acts, between the two routes by which a legislator can lose a seat, between the power of Parliament and the power of a state legislature to legislate on elections. A candidate who sorts a fresh question into one of these four types within the first minute has already done the hardest part of the answer, because each type has a structure, and structure is what the marking scheme rewards.

The demand words deserve a paragraph of their own, because the same material earns different marks under different instructions and this subject illustrates the difference cleanly. A question that asks the candidate to state or outline a position, on the composition of the Commission or the content of the Tenth Schedule, is paid for completeness and precision of the text. A question that asks the candidate to examine or discuss a working institution, the Commission's independence or the operation of the freeze, is paid for the movement from provision to record: what the text says, what has happened under it, and where the two diverge. A question that asks for critical evaluation, of the anti-defection design or of a funding regime, is paid for the quality of the two cases and the discipline of the conclusion, and it is under this instruction that layer confusion does its worst damage, because a candidate who believes a proposal is already law has nothing left to evaluate. Reading the demand word is therefore not examination folklore but part of the subject itself, and in election law the correlation is unusually reliable: the more evaluative the instruction, the more certainly the question is about an institution's design rather than about the politics the design regulates.

The subject also appears beyond the two general papers, and the candidate meeting it in a law optional, in a political science paper or in a state services examination should know that the anchor set does not change, only the depth of the statutory layer does. The law optional rewards the petition jurisprudence and the case law on Article 324 at fuller length; the judicial services examinations of the states test the two Acts at the level of their sections, including the trial of petitions, which the general papers never reach; the state services papers weight the State Election Commission and the local body provisions that the Union papers treat as a boundary. In each case the preparation that transfers is the architecture, articles, division of statutes, judgment set, and the candidate who built the subject in that shape can deepen the layer a particular paper requires without rebuilding the whole.

One calculation remains before the architecture is taken up, and it is the calculation that justifies the article's existence: the return on time. Election law is among the smallest bounded fields in the polity syllabus. Its generating texts fit in one Part of the Constitution, two moderate statutes and a schedule; its judgment set is six decisions deep; its reform record is a shelf of named reports; and it is examined in some form in nearly every cycle of both papers, because it sits at the junction of the Constitution, the judiciary and the federal structure, three areas the paper is obliged to cover. A subject so bounded and so reliably present rewards a defined investment, the building of the anchor set and the scaffold, out of all proportion to its size, and it punishes the opposite allocation, in which the candidate carries the field as undifferentiated political awareness and revises nothing because nothing was ever organised. The aspirant who has not yet organised this subject is not behind on information; the information is on every shelf. The aspirant is behind on structure, and structure is what the rest of this article supplies.

The method is not peculiar to this subject, and the candidate who has met it elsewhere in this series will recognise it. [The study article on tax laws in the economy papers](/2012/11/15/tax-laws-upsc-economy-guide/) teaches the same separation of constitutional source, statutory machinery and reform argument, and [the article on social justice laws for the Mains](/2017/06/15/social-justice-laws-upsc-mains/) applies the identical discipline to reservation, where the confusion between the constitutional position and the political argument is even more expensive. What changes from subject to subject is the anchor set. What does not change is the rule that an answer is built by placing each proposition the candidate intends to use into its correct layer before a word is written, because a proposition in the wrong layer is not a weaker version of the truth; it is an error, and examiners mark it as one.

## The constitutional spine: Part XV and its neighbours

Part XV of the Constitution, Articles 324 to 329, is the spine of the field, and it repays being learned as an architecture rather than as a list. Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, all elections to Parliament, to the state legislatures, and to the offices of President and Vice-President in the Election Commission. Three features of that vesting carry examination weight. The power is plenary within its field: in Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, the Supreme Court described Article 324 as a reservoir of power on which the Commission may draw where the enacted law is silent, so that the Commission is never powerless in the face of an unprovided situation. The Commission's composition is left open by the text: the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners as the President may fix, appointed by the President subject to any law Parliament makes, which is why the appointment question, taken up later in this article, is a question about a gap the text deliberately left. And the independence of the Chief Election Commissioner is protected in the text itself, by the provision that the Chief Election Commissioner shall not be removed from office except in like manner and on like grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court, a protection the other Commissioners do not hold in the same form, since they may not be removed except on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner.

Article 325 provides that there shall be one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency, and that no person shall be ineligible for inclusion in such a roll on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or any of them, and no person shall claim to be included in a special roll for any constituency on those grounds. The article is the constitutional death of the separate electorate, and its examinable point is the word only: exclusion on other grounds the law provides, such as non-residence or a disqualification the statute imposes, is not touched. Article 326 then grounds elections to the House of the People and to the state assemblies in adult suffrage: every citizen of not less than eighteen years of age, a figure substituted for twenty-one by the Constitution (Sixty-first Amendment) Act, 1988, who is not otherwise disqualified under the Constitution or a law made by the appropriate legislature on the ground of non-residence, unsoundness of mind, crime or corrupt or illegal practice, is entitled to be registered as a voter. The pairing of Articles 325 and 326 is a favourite Prelims construction, because the two articles divide the franchise between them, one fixing the equality of the roll and the other its breadth.

Articles 327 and 328 divide the legislative power over elections, and the division is a standard federalism crossover. Article 327 empowers Parliament to make provision with respect to all matters relating to or in connection with elections to either House of Parliament or to the Houses of state legislatures, including the preparation of electoral rolls and the delimitation of constituencies. Article 328 gives the legislature of a state the corresponding power in relation to elections to its own legislature, but only in so far as provision in that behalf is not made by Parliament. The state power is therefore real but residual, and the two Representation of the People Acts, made under Article 327, occupy most of the field before a state legislature reaches it.

### How does the power under Article 328 differ from the power under Article 327?

Article 327 gives Parliament plenary power to legislate on every matter connected with elections to Parliament and to the state legislatures, including electoral rolls and delimitation. Article 328 gives a state legislature power over elections to its own legislature only where Parliament has not legislated, so the state power is residual and yields wherever the central statutes speak.

The last article of the Part, Article 329, is the one that gives the field its procedural shape. Clause (a) protects the constitutional provisions on delimitation from being questioned in any court. Clause (b) provides that no election to either House of Parliament or to a House of a state legislature shall be called in question except by an election petition presented to such authority and in such manner as may be provided for by or under a law made by the appropriate legislature. The consequence, settled in N. P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer in the earliest years of the Constitution, is that an election is a single integrated process which the courts will not interrupt once it has begun: a grievance about a nomination, a roll or a count must wait for the process to conclude and travel, if at all, by the petition route. Candidates routinely lose this point by writing as though a writ petition lies against any electoral irregularity at any stage; the text says otherwise, and the distinction between what a court may review after a result and what it may not interrupt during the process is one of the field's most reliable mark-earners.

One boundary of the Part must be drawn before the neighbouring provisions are taken up, because the Prelims paper tests it directly. Article 324 does not extend to elections to Panchayats and Municipalities. Articles 243K and 243ZA vest the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, elections to the local bodies in a State Election Commission in each state, constituted by the Governor, and the constitutional design thereby separates the conduct of national and state legislature elections from the conduct of local elections at the level of the text itself. The State Election Commissioner holds a protection modelled on the central one, being removable only in like manner and on like grounds as a judge of a High Court, and the Supreme Court has treated the state commissions as constitutional authorities whose independence in their sphere is not at the disposal of the state government of the day. A candidate who assigns local body elections to the Election Commission of India has failed a distinction the Constitution draws expressly, and the confusion is common enough that the paper returns to it.

Around Part XV sits a ring of neighbouring provisions without which the Part cannot be answered completely. Articles 81 and 170 fix the composition and maximum strength of the House of the People and of the state assemblies. Articles 82 and 170 provide for readjustment of seats and constituencies after each census, and carry the provisos that froze that readjustment, which a later section of this article takes up. Articles 84 and 173 state the qualifications for membership; Articles 101 and 190 the vacation of seats; Articles 102 and 191 the disqualifications; Articles 103 and 192 the authority that decides disqualification questions; Articles 330 and 332 the reservation of seats for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes; and Article 334 the time limit within which that reservation operates. An aspirant who can place each of these without hesitation holds the complete constitutional map of the field, and the sections that follow assume that map and build the statutory and judicial layers on it.

## Who decides: the disqualification architecture

No part of election law is more often mis-stated in answer books than the route by which a legislator loses a seat, because there are two routes, they are governed by different texts, and they end before different authorities. [The complete guide to election law in India](/2017/12/01/election-law-india-complete-guide/) maps that division of authority across the whole field, institution by institution, and this section states the part of the map the examination questions actually patrol. The first route runs through Articles 102 and 191. A person is disqualified for being chosen as, and for being, a member of Parliament or of a state legislature if the person holds an office of profit under the government other than an office Parliament or the state legislature has declared not to disqualify, if the person is of unsound mind and stands so declared by a competent court, if the person is an undischarged insolvent, if the person is not a citizen or has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign state, or if the person is disqualified under any law made by Parliament, a clause under which the disqualifications in the Representation of the People Act, 1951, including disqualification on conviction, take their constitutional footing. The second route is the Tenth Schedule, inserted by the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985, under which a member is disqualified for defecting: for voluntarily giving up membership of the political party to which the member belonged, or for voting or abstaining from voting contrary to a direction of that party without prior permission and without the party condoning the act within the period the Schedule allows.

The examinable precision lies in who decides. Under Article 103, if any question arises whether a member of Parliament has become subject to a disqualification under Article 102(1), the question is referred for the decision of the President, whose decision is final, but before giving a decision the President must obtain the opinion of the Election Commission and must act according to that opinion. Article 192 makes the identical arrangement for state legislatures with the Governor in place of the President. The President and the Governor in these articles are therefore formal decision-makers bound by the opinion of the Commission; the substance of the decision is the Commission's, and an answer that credits the President with a discretion here has misunderstood the design. Under paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule, by contrast, a defection question is decided by the Chairman or the Speaker of the House, whose decision the Schedule declares final. The two routes thus place the same kind of question, whether a member may continue to sit, in two very different hands, one a constitutional commission whose opinion binds the head of state, the other the presiding officer of the very House whose composition is in dispute, and most of the serious reform argument in this field is an argument about that contrast.

Two adjacent bodies of doctrine complete the map of how a seat is gained and lost, and the examination tests all four as a set. The first is qualification, which is a condition of entry rather than a ground of removal: Articles 84 and 173 require citizenship, an oath, a minimum age of twenty-five for the House of the People and the assemblies and thirty for the Council of States and the legislative councils, and such other qualifications as Parliament may by law prescribe, which Parliament has done in the Act of 1951 by requiring that a candidate for a seat reserved for the Scheduled Castes or the Scheduled Tribes belong to the relevant category and that a candidate be an elector for a constituency in the relevant state or in any parliamentary constituency, as the provision specifies. The second is vacation, which is the seat emptying without any question of disqualification arising: by resignation, by absence from the House for sixty days without permission, by the member becoming subject to a disqualification, or by the member's election being declared void, and the double membership rules of Articles 101 and 190 prevent a person from holding seats in two legislatures at once or in both Houses of one. A candidate who separates qualification, disqualification, defection and vacation into four boxes, each with its article and its deciding authority, holds a structure into which every membership question the paper has produced can be filed without remainder.

The office of profit ground, which produces more Article 102 litigation than the rest of the clause together, needs its own precision. The Constitution uses the expression office of profit under the government without defining it, and deliberately so: the articles permit Parliament and the state legislatures to declare by law that specified offices shall not disqualify their holders, and Parliament has exercised that power in the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959, whose schedule of exempted offices has grown by amendment as governments have created boards, councils and corporations and wished to place legislators upon them. The courts have filled the definitional space with tests that look to the substance of the office: whether the government makes the appointment, whether it can remove the holder, whether it pays the remuneration and from what source, what functions the office performs and whether they are performed for the government. The Supreme Court applied the doctrine in 2006 to uphold the disqualification of a member of the Rajya Sabha who had accepted the chairpersonship of a state film development council with the rank and facilities of a minister, holding that the office was one of profit under the government notwithstanding the label of honorary service, since it carried pecuniary gain in the form of allowances and benefits she was entitled to draw. The examinable tension is written into the design: the disqualification exists to keep the legislature independent of executive patronage, while the exemption power lets the very legislatures the clause disciplines decide how far the discipline reaches, and every expansion of the exempted list by a majority is therefore an occasion for the argument to be run again.

### What is the difference between Article 103 and paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule?

Article 103 sends a disqualification question under Article 102(1) to the President, who must obtain the Election Commission's opinion and act according to it, so the Commission decides in substance. Paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule sends a defection question to the Speaker or Chairman, whose decision the Schedule makes final, subject to judicial review on limited grounds.

### Which questions does the Election Commission decide outright, and on which does it only advise?

The Commission decides outright what Article 324 commits to it: the conduct of elections, the preparation of rolls, the registration and recognition of parties and the allotment of symbols. On disqualification under Articles 102 and 191 it only advises, rendering an opinion to the President or the Governor, who must act according to it.

Two refinements complete the architecture, and both are drawn from the case law taken up in the judgment section below. The presiding officer deciding a defection petition acts as a tribunal, not as a political superior, and the decision is open to judicial review on the grounds on which tribunal decisions are reviewed, because the Constitution Bench in Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu struck down paragraph 7 of the Schedule, which had purported to bar the jurisdiction of the courts, while upholding the Schedule itself. And the disqualification on conviction in the statute operates without the shelter that sitting members once had: the provision that protected a sitting member who appealed was struck down in Lily Thomas v Union of India, so a conviction of the kind and sentence the statute specifies disqualifies at once. The candidate who holds the two routes, the two authorities and these two refinements separately holds the disqualification map entire; the candidate who merges them into a single statement about members losing seats will be wrong somewhere in every answer the subject generates.

## The Commission itself: composition, tenure and protection

The institution at the centre of the field has a history the examination expects a candidate to command in outline. The Commission began in 1950 as a single-member body, and Sukumar Sen, its first Chief Election Commissioner, conducted the first general elections on a franchise of a scale no election authority had administered before. The single-member form held for most of the Commission's life. Two additional Election Commissioners were appointed briefly in 1989 and removed within months by a change of rules, and the Commission became durably multi-member in 1993, when the government of the day appointed two Election Commissioners and then placed the multi-member structure on a statutory footing; the Supreme Court, in litigation arising from that change, declined to treat the Chief Election Commissioner as a superior authority over the other Commissioners in the transaction of business, while the text of Article 324(5) continued to give the Chief Election Commissioner alone the protection against removal that a Supreme Court judge enjoys. The working position the candidate should carry is therefore a precise asymmetry: in the Commission's collective functioning the members act together and business is transacted by the Commission as a body, but the shield against removal is unequal, the Chief Election Commissioner's being constitutional and direct, the other Commissioners' being routed through the Chief Election Commissioner's recommendation, and the Registrar of that asymmetry, the statute of 1991 first and the statute of 2023 in its place, is where the conditions of service are found.

The tenure rules are short and frequently asked. A Commissioner holds office for six years or until attaining the age of sixty-five, whichever is earlier, and is not eligible for reappointment to the office. The salary and conditions of service were equated by the statute of 2023 with those of the Cabinet Secretary, a change from the earlier equation with a judge of the Supreme Court, and that alteration itself became part of the argument about the statute, critics reading the change of reference point as a lowering of the office's station within the constitutional order and the government defending it as a rationalisation of service conditions across high offices. The Commission's administrative position carries its own standing criticism: it depends on the government of the day for the personnel who conduct the poll, borrowing the civil services and the police of the Union and the states for election duty, and its expenditure is voted rather than charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, a feature reform reports have repeatedly proposed to change by treating the Commission's budget as charged expenditure on the model of the Comptroller and Auditor General. Each of these points is examinable, and each is an instance of the article's general lesson: the Commission's independence is not one fact but a bundle of separate design choices, appointment, tenure, removal, money and staff, and an answer that treats independence as a single switch, on or off, will always be thinner than the question.

## Composition, reservation and the frozen map

The size and shape of the legislatures are constitutional facts with a statutory history, and the examination tests both. Article 81 caps the House of the People at five hundred and fifty members, five hundred and thirty chosen from the states and twenty from the Union territories, and Article 170 sets the assemblies between sixty and five hundred. Articles 82 and 170 then direct that upon the completion of each census, the allocation of seats to the states and the division of each state into territorial constituencies shall be readjusted by such authority and in such manner as Parliament may by law determine, which Parliament has done through a succession of Delimitation Acts. Articles 330 and 332 reserve seats for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes in the House and the assemblies in proportion, as nearly as may be, to their population, and Article 334 originally limited that reservation, together with the nomination of members of the Anglo-Indian community, to ten years; successive amendments extended the period, and the Constitution (One Hundred and Fourth Amendment) Act, 2019 carried the reservation of seats for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes to eighty years from the commencement of the Constitution while ending the Anglo-Indian nomination.

The freeze is the part of this structure that generates argument, and it must be stated as a sequence of instruments rather than as a grievance. The Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 suspended the readjustment after each census by providing that the allocation of seats and the division into constituencies would remain as they stood on the basis of the census of 1971 until the relevant figures for the first census taken after the year 2000 had been published; the stated object was that states which pursued population control should not lose representation for succeeding at it. The Constitution (Eighty-fourth Amendment) Act, 2001 extended the suspension until the first census taken after the year 2026, but permitted the readjustment and rationalisation of territorial constituencies within each state, without altering the number of seats allotted to the state, on the basis of the census of 1991. The Constitution (Eighty-seventh Amendment) Act, 2003 substituted the census of 2001 for that of 1991 as the basis of that internal exercise. The result is the frozen map: seat shares among the states still rest on the population distribution of 1971, while constituency boundaries inside the states were redrawn on the population distribution of 2001 by the Delimitation Commission constituted under the Delimitation Act, 2002.

The reservation of seats for women sits on top of this structure and cannot be separated from it in an answer. The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, 2023 inserted Articles 330A and 332A reserving, as nearly as may be, one-third of the seats in the House of the People and in the state assemblies for women, including within the seats reserved for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, with a corresponding provision for the assembly of the National Capital Territory. Article 334A, inserted by the same amendment, makes the reservation effective only after an exercise of delimitation undertaken for the purpose after the relevant census figures have been published, and gives it an operation of fifteen years from its commencement, continuable thereafter by law. The structural consequence, which is what an examiner is listening for, is that women's reservation and the end of the seat freeze are locked to the same event, the next delimitation, so that a candidate cannot discuss either intelligently without the other.

The mechanics of the reservation inside the text reward the same close reading. The seats reserved for women are to be allotted by rotation to different constituencies, in the manner Parliament may by law determine, following each exercise of delimitation, so that the reservation is not a permanent carving of a fixed third of the map but a circulating one, which answers the objection, familiar from the rotation of reserved seats for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, that a constituency permanently reserved distorts the representative's accountability to the whole electorate. The fifteen-year period in Article 334A runs from the commencement of the reservation itself, that is from its taking effect after the post-census delimitation, and not from the passing of the amendment, a distinction candidates miss when they compute the sunset from 2023. Two arguments around the amendment belong in the reform column rather than the text column, and a strong answer keeps them there: the demand, pressed by parties across the spectrum during the amendment's passage, for a sub-reservation within the women's quota for women of the backward classes, which the enacted text does not contain; and the criticism of the delimitation condition itself, that a reservation announced in 2023 was made to wait upon a census and a readjustment whose own timetable is contested, so that the amendment's commencement is tied to the most disputed exercise in the field. Both arguments are live, neither alters the text, and the pair of them is a compact demonstration of this article's method, since the candidate who can recite 330A and still place the sub-quota demand correctly outside it has understood the difference between the Constitution and the conversation about it. The detailed working of the delimitation statutes, the composition of the commissions and the finality of their orders belongs to [the dedicated treatment of the Delimitation Acts](/2018/04/01/delimitation-acts-india-explained/), and the strongest answers cite this article's propositions and send the detail there rather than reproducing it.

The working exercise under the freeze deserves its own paragraph, because the order of 2008 is the only delimitation most living voters have experienced and its features are the ones state papers test. Working under the Delimitation Act, 2002, the Commission redrew every parliamentary and assembly constituency boundary in the states it covered on the population figures of 2001, equalising constituencies within each state without touching the number of seats any state held; it re-sited the constituencies reserved for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes to the areas where their populations were highest, which displaced sitting members from seats they had held as reserved seats and reserved others that had been general, a redistribution of incumbency that was politically the most felt consequence of the exercise; and its orders, once published, had the force of law and were laid before the legislatures without power of modification. Two territories stood outside the common exercise for reasons of their own: Assam, whose delimitation was deferred and eventually undertaken separately under the same statute, and Jammu and Kashmir, whose assembly constituencies were delimited under the reorganisation statute of 2019 by a commission of its own. The candidate should hold the general rule and the two exceptions together, because the exceptions are the proof the paper sets for candidates who learned the exercise as a slogan rather than as an event.

## The statute set: two Acts, a delimitation line and an appointment statute

The statutory layer is where the single most useful correction in the whole field lives, because the two Representation of the People Acts divide the subject between them and preparation material constantly assigns the provisions of one to the other. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 is Act 43 of 1950. It deals with the allocation of seats in the House of the People and the state legislatures, the delimitation of constituencies, the officers connected with elections of that kind, the preparation of electoral rolls, and the manner of filling seats in the Council of States. Its working heart is the part on electoral rolls, which provides for the preparation and revision of the roll for every constituency, lays down the conditions of registration, being a citizen, being not less than eighteen years of age on the qualifying date, and being ordinarily resident in the constituency, states the disqualifications for registration, and gives the expression ordinarily resident an extended meaning that protects, among others, members of the armed forces and persons holding a service qualification from losing their home registration by reason of their posting. The Act of 1950, in short, constitutes the electorate.

Three working features of that Act earn their place in an answer book, because the paper has found all three. The first is the qualifying date. Registration turns on age attained by a qualifying date, which for most of the Act's life was a single date, the first day of January of the year of revision, so that a citizen who turned eighteen a day later waited a full cycle for the franchise. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 substituted four qualifying dates in the year, the first days of January, April, July and October, a change whose examinable point is its character: it is an inclusiveness reform effected entirely within the statute, requiring no constitutional amendment, because Article 326 fixes the age and leaves the machinery of registration to law. The second feature is the treatment of citizens abroad. An amendment of 2010 inserted a provision under which a citizen of India absent from the country owing to employment, education or otherwise, and not having acquired the citizenship of another country, may be registered in the constituency of the place of ordinary residence recorded in the passport, a category the Act calls the overseas elector; the registration was conceded without the vote being made castable from abroad, the overseas elector voting in person at the constituency's polling station, and that asymmetry between registration and the act of voting is the point the paper probes. The third feature is the service voter: members of the armed forces and of the forces to which the Army Act applies, members of an armed police force of a state serving outside it, and persons employed by the Government of India in a post outside India, together with their spouses residing with them, may register at their home constituency on a service qualification and vote by postal ballot, a machinery the Commission has progressively moved to electronic transmission of the ballot itself. The three features share a lesson worth stating once: the franchise in the constitutional text is a single sentence, and everything that decides whether an actual citizen casts an actual vote lives in this statute and the rules beneath it.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 is Act 43 of 1951, and the identity of the number with its predecessor is a trap the examination sets deliberately: each statute is Act 43 of its own year, and an answer that writes Act 43 without the year has said nothing precise. The Act of 1951 governs the contest. It states the qualifications and disqualifications for membership of Parliament and of the state legislatures, including the disqualification on conviction in its section 8, the disqualification for corrupt practices, for government contracts and for failure to lodge an account of election expenses; it provides for the notification of elections and the administrative machinery that conducts them; it registers political parties under section 29A; it regulates the conduct of the poll, the counting and the declaration of results; it defines corrupt practices in section 123 and creates the electoral offences; it governs election expenses through section 77 and the account every candidate must lodge; and it channels every challenge to a completed election into the election petition, tried by the High Court under the part of the Act devoted to disputes, from which an appeal lies to the Supreme Court. The division can be carried in one sentence: the Act of 1950 decides who may vote and where, and the Act of 1951 decides who may stand, how the contest is run and how its result may be undone. The full statutory treatment of each Act sits in [the pillar on the Representation of the People Act, 1951](/2017/12/15/representation-of-people-act-1951/) and [the pillar on the Representation of the People Act, 1950](/2018/01/01/representation-of-people-act-1950/), and an examination answer needs the division, the leading provisions and nothing like the whole text.

Within the Act of 1951, the disqualification provisions deserve a second pass at teaching depth, because they distribute one idea across several sections and the paper tests the distribution. Section 8 is the conviction provision, and it is itself three provisions in one: a first list of offences, including offences against the state's security, bribery, rape and other named crimes, where conviction alone disqualifies regardless of sentence; a second list where conviction disqualifies on a sentence of imprisonment of not less than six months; and a residuary clause covering every other offence, where the disqualification requires a sentence of not less than two years. In each case the disqualification runs from the date of conviction and continues for a further six years from release, a tail that candidates habitually drop and examiners habitually notice. Section 8A is a different machine: a person found guilty of a corrupt practice by the High Court in an election petition has the case laid before the President, who decides, on the opinion of the Election Commission, whether the person shall be disqualified and for what period, up to six years, so that the corrupt practice route returns to the Article 103 design of a formal decision on the Commission's binding opinion. Section 10A disqualifies for three years a candidate who fails to lodge an account of election expenses in the time and manner the Act requires, a provision whose modesty of trigger and length of consequence make it a favourite illustration that the expenditure regime punishes the failure to account more reliably than the act of overspending. Section 9A covers subsisting government contracts, section 9 the dismissal from government service for corruption or disloyalty, and section 10 the holding of specified offices in government companies. Held together, the sections teach the architecture the examination is after: disqualification in Indian election law is a statutory list resting on a constitutional enabling clause, administered in its doubtful cases by the President or the Governor acting on the Commission's opinion, and never by the House whose member stands accused.

The petition machinery repays the same treatment in outline. An election petition may be presented by any candidate at the election or by any elector of the constituency, on the grounds the Act enumerates in section 100, which include that the returned candidate was not qualified or was disqualified, that a corrupt practice was committed, that a nomination was improperly rejected, or that the result was materially affected by improper acceptance of a nomination, by a corrupt practice, or by non-compliance with the Constitution, the Act or the rules. The petition must be presented within forty-five days of the declaration of the result, it is tried by the High Court of the state in which the election was held, and the trial is to proceed, so far as practicable, on a day-to-day basis with the statute directing that the court endeavour to conclude it within six months, an aspiration the record of petitions outliving the very term they challenge contradicts in fact. An appeal lies to the Supreme Court. The candidate should also hold the consequence structure precisely: the High Court may declare the election of the returned candidate void, and in a defined set of cases may declare the petitioner or another candidate duly elected, so the petition is not merely destructive of a result but capable of installing the correct one, and where the court names a person guilty of a corrupt practice, the section 8A machinery described above is set in motion. Nothing in this machinery is available before the result, which is the statutory execution of the constitutional bar in Article 329(b), and the two provisions, the article and the part of the Act, should always be cited as a pair.

### What separates a corrupt practice from an electoral offence?

A corrupt practice, defined in section 123 of the 1951 Act, is conduct such as bribery or undue influence, proved in an election petition, that can void the winner's election. An electoral offence is a crime the statute creates, prosecuted in a criminal court and punished with imprisonment or fine. One act can bear both; the forum and consequence differ.

Below the two Acts sits the delimitation line: the Delimitation Act, 1952, the Delimitation Act, 1962, the Delimitation Act, 1972 and the Delimitation Act, 2002, each constituting a Delimitation Commission for the readjustment of its census cycle. For examination purposes the candidate needs the sequence, the fact that each Commission's orders have the force of law and are not to be called in question before any court, and the peculiarity of the last of the line, that the Commission under the Act of 2002 redrew boundaries within states on the census of 2001 while the freeze held the allocation between states at the figures of 1971. Above the Acts sits one more statute of the first importance to the appointment controversy: the Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023, Act 49 of 2023, which repealed and replaced the statute of 1991 and for the first time prescribed by law how Commissioners are chosen, through a Search Committee chaired by the Cabinet Secretary which prepares a panel, and a Selection Committee of the Prime Minister as chairperson, the Leader of the Opposition in the House of the People, and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister, on whose recommendation the President appoints. The statute also carries the conditions of service, a term of six years or until the age of sixty-five, whichever is earlier, and it preserves the asymmetry of removal: the Chief Election Commissioner is removable only in the manner of a Supreme Court judge, and another Commissioner only on the Chief Election Commissioner's recommendation.

The Model Code of Conduct belongs in this section precisely because it is not in it. The Code is a set of norms evolved by the Commission in consultation with the political parties, observed from the announcement of the election schedule until the completion of the process, and it has no statutory text. Parts of its content overlap with law, since bribery, intimidation and appeals to caste or communal feeling are also corrupt practices or offences under the statute, and the Commission enforces the remainder through the plenary power of Article 324, by censuring, by directing and in grave cases by postponing or countermanding. In Subramaniam Balaji v State of Tamil Nadu, the Supreme Court held that promises in an election manifesto are not a corrupt practice under the statute, and in that setting asked the Commission to frame guidelines for manifestos in consultation with the parties, which the Commission then folded into the Code. The examinable proposition is exact: the Code binds because the Commission's constitutional authority stands behind it and because parties have consented to the discipline, not because Parliament enacted it, and the belief that the Model Code is a statute is among the errors the next sections are written to eliminate.

## Parties, symbols and money: the statutory undergrowth

Three parts of the statutory layer sit below the level of the leading provisions and repay teaching attention because the papers reach them regularly. The first is the registration of political parties. Section 29A, inserted into the Act of 1951 in 1989, requires an association seeking registration to declare its allegiance to the Constitution and to the principles of socialism, secularism and democracy and to uphold the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India; registration is the gateway to the privileges of the party system, including the allotment of a reserved symbol and the tax treatment of contributions. The symbols themselves are governed not by the Act but by the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, an instrument the Commission issues under Article 324 and the conduct of elections rules, which divides parties into recognised national and state parties and unrecognised registered parties by criteria of electoral performance prescribed in the Order, allots a reserved symbol to a recognised party and free symbols to others, and decides, when a party splits, which of the rival groups is the party entitled to the name and the symbol. The Supreme Court upheld the Commission's power to decide such disputes by judging the rival claims against the party's constitution and its support in the legislative and organisational wings. The examinable distinction is one of source: the candidate must be able to say that the party in Indian law is a creature of three layers at once, a voluntary association in fact, a registered body under one section of the statute, and a recognised electoral unit under an executive order of the Commission, and that most disputes about parties are disputes about which layer governs the question asked.

The second is election expenditure, where the statute's asymmetry is the analytical point examiners listen for. Section 77 requires every candidate to keep a separate and correct account of expenditure incurred or authorised in connection with the election, and the Commission fixes, by rule, a ceiling on what a candidate may spend, revised from time to time. An Explanation added to section 77 in 1975, reversing the position the Supreme Court had taken in Kanwar Lal Gupta v Amar Nath Chawla, provides that expenditure incurred or authorised by a political party, by its leaders in connection with the campaign, or by other associations and individuals, is not deemed to be expenditure incurred or authorised by the candidate, except where the candidate has authorised it in the manner the Explanation specifies. The consequence is structural: the law caps the candidate's spending while leaving the party's spending uncapped, so the money that dominates a modern campaign flows through the channel the ceiling does not close, and the disclosure regime in the contribution reports is the compensating mechanism the statute substitutes for a cap. In Common Cause v Union of India the Supreme Court pressed the same logic into the parties' own accounts, holding that political parties were bound by the income tax law to maintain and file accounts of their income, so that the contribution record on which the funding jurisprudence rests exists in part because a court required the books to be kept. An answer on electoral reform that does not locate the gap in the Explanation to section 77 is arguing about the wrong provision.

The third is the deposit and the price of frivolity, a small subject that Prelims papers use to test whether the statute has been read at all. A candidate's nomination must be accompanied by a security deposit in the amount the Act prescribes, higher for the House of the People than for the assemblies and reduced for candidates of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes; the deposit is forfeited if the candidate is not elected and does not poll more than one-sixth of the total valid votes cast. The provision's teaching value is its design logic: the franchise to contest is kept nearly open, and the cost of a non-serious candidature is recovered not by barring entry but by pricing failure, a pattern that repeats across the field, where the law characteristically regulates by consequence rather than by permission.

## Four moments that organise the field

Chronology is the enemy of revision in this subject because the provisions arrive from every decade at once, and the cure is to hold four moments and hang everything on them. The first moment is 1950 and 1951, when the machinery was built: the Constitution commenced with Part XV in place, the Act of 1950 constituted the electorate and the Act of 1951 constituted the contest, and the first general elections were held under both within two years of the Republic's founding. Everything in this moment is about creation: a universal franchise adopted at a stroke for a largely illiterate electorate, a single roll, an independent commission, and the petition as the sole route of challenge. The founding exercise itself set the field's habits. Because much of the electorate could not read a ballot paper, each candidate was allotted a separate ballot box bearing the candidate's symbol, into which the voter dropped a blank ballot paper, so that the symbol system which still organises Indian voting began as a solution to illiteracy rather than as a party branding device; and the elections to the House of the People and to the state assemblies were held together, so that the simultaneity which a later reform debate treats as an innovation was in fact the Republic's original condition, abandoned as the assemblies' terms drifted out of alignment over the following two decades. Both facts are the kind the Prelims paper prizes, small, structural and decisive against the plausible alternative. The second moment runs from 1976 to the years 2001 to 2003, and it is the moment of the freeze and its management: the Forty-second Amendment suspended readjustment on the census of 1971, the Eighty-fourth extended the suspension past 2026 while releasing the internal boundaries, and the Eighty-seventh updated the released exercise to the census of 2001. Everything in this moment is about postponement defended as federal fairness, and the Delimitation Act, 2002 is its statutory instrument.

The third moment is 1985 and 2003, the anti-defection settlement and its correction. The Fifty-second Amendment of 1985 inserted the Tenth Schedule to end the practice, notorious since the defections of the late nineteen sixties, of legislators crossing the floor for office; as originally framed it protected a split of one-third of a legislature party as well as a merger of two-thirds, and the split protection became in practice a licence for the very conduct the Schedule existed to punish, defections travelling in groups sized to fit it. The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 deleted the split paragraph altogether, left the merger exception standing, and in the same amendment capped the size of the Council of Ministers and barred a disqualified defector from ministerial office until re-elected or until the term of the House expired. The pair of dates must be held together, because the examination's favourite construction in this area is an answer that describes the Schedule of 1985 as if the correction of 2003 had never happened. The fourth moment runs from 2013 to 2024, and it is the decade in which the courts and then Parliament re-drew the working law: the removal of the convicted member's shelter in 2013, the maturing of the disclosure regime built on the candidate's affidavit, the appointment litigation of 2023 and the statute that answered it, and the electoral bonds judgment of 2024. The bonds episode within the moment has a chronology worth fixing because the paper tests its sequence: the amendments travelled in the Finance Act of 2017, certified as a Money Bill; the scheme was notified in January 2018 and bonds were sold from March of that year; the challenge, brought by the Association for Democratic Reforms among others, produced an interim order in April 2019 under which parties were directed to lodge the particulars of bond receipts with the Commission in sealed cover, so that the money moved under a suspended judgment for five years; and the final judgment of February 2024 struck the scheme down, directed that the issuing bank disclose the particulars of purchases and redemptions from the date of the interim order, and left the Money Bill question, on which a reference to a bench of seven judges was pending, undecided. A candidate who can recite that sequence without notes holds the fourth moment in its examinable form, a story about how a fiscal vehicle, an executive scheme and a disclosure right interacted, which is the pattern of the whole field in miniature. A candidate who holds these four moments can place any provision, amendment or judgment in the field within seconds, which is precisely the skill a paper that mixes its questions across the whole field is designed to reward.

## The reform record: committees, commissions and their fates

The reform debate has a lineage, and the examination credits the candidate who can place a proposal in it, because the lineage is the quickest way of showing whether a proposal is new, revived or perennial. The modern sequence begins outside government: the committee associated with the movement led by Jayaprakash Narayan in the nineteen seventies, chaired by Justice V. M. Tarkunde, proposed changes to the funding and conduct of elections that set the agenda the official bodies later worked through. The first official committee of consequence is the Dinesh Goswami Committee of 1990, a committee of parliamentarians and ministers which recommended, among other measures, that disqualification on defection be decided by the President or the Governor on the advice of the Election Commission rather than by the presiding officer, a recommendation whose descendants appear in every later anti-defection debate including the one the courts joined. The Vohra Committee of 1993 documented, from within the state itself, the nexus among criminals, politicians and officials, and it remains the citation of record for the proposition that the criminalisation of politics is a diagnosed condition and not a polemic. The Indrajit Gupta Committee of 1998 examined state funding of elections and endorsed the principle in a limited form, recommending funding in kind, such as specified materials and facilities, rather than cash subvention, on the reasoning that full state funding awaited a regulatory structure for party finances that did not exist.

The Law Commission's work forms the spine of the modern record. Its One Hundred and Seventieth Report of 1999, on the reform of the electoral laws, ranged across the whole field, including the regulation of political parties and the listing system. Its Two Hundred and Forty-fourth Report of 2014 addressed electoral disqualifications directly and recommended, with safeguards including the requirement that charges have been framed by a court and that the offence carry a specified minimum punishment, that the filing of a charge sheet and the framing of charges for serious offences attract disqualification, precisely the step the Supreme Court declined to take four years later in Public Interest Foundation. Its Two Hundred and Fifty-fifth Report of 2015, the field's most complete official restatement, covered the appointment of Commissioners, for which it recommended a committee including the Chief Justice of India, the regulation of paid news and opinion polls, the finances of parties and candidates, and the strengthening of the Commission itself. The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution, reporting in 2002, added its own electoral chapter to the same effect on several of these points. The fate of the record is itself the examinable finding: across five decades the diagnoses converge to a remarkable degree, on appointment, on defection adjudication, on candidate disclosure and on money, while the enactments are few, specific and usually extracted by litigation rather than volunteered by legislation, the disclosure regime arriving by judgment, the appointment statute arriving after a judgment, and the defection correction of 2003 arriving sixteen years after the Goswami design. An answer that grasps the record as a record, a shelf of named reports with named fates, writes about reform as an institution studying its own problem, which is the level at which General Studies Paper II is pitched.

## The judgment set, and what each case is for

Six decisions carry the examination weight of the field, and each must be learnable as a single proposition with its ground attached. The first is the pair of decisions that built the voter's right to know. In Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms, the Supreme Court held in 2002 that the voter's right to know the antecedents of a candidate flows from the freedom of speech and expression in Article 19(1)(a), since a vote cast in ignorance of the candidate's criminal record, assets and education is not the informed choice the freedom protects, and it directed the Commission to require affidavits on those matters from every candidate. When Parliament answered with a provision that attempted to empty the disclosure of content, the Court in People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India in 2003 struck the sheltering provision down and restored the affidavit regime, which the statute then absorbed through the disclosure sections and the rules under them. The same Article 19(1)(a) root produced, in a later People's Union for Civil Liberties decision of 2013, the direction that the ballot carry a none of the above option, on the reasoning that the freedom to express a choice includes the freedom to reject every candidate while preserving the secrecy of the choice. The line matters to the examiner because it is the clearest example in the field of a right being derived from a general freedom by reasoning about what the freedom is for, and the bonds judgment at the end of the line is unintelligible without it.

### Why does the voter's right to know sit inside Article 19(1)(a)?

The Court reasoned that voting is an act of expression, and expression is not free in any meaningful sense if the voter is denied information essential to the choice. A candidate's antecedents, and later a party's funding, are such information, so the freedom of speech and expression carries a right to receive it against a law that suppresses disclosure.

The disclosure line did not stop at the affidavit, and its second phase is the bridge between the judgment set and the criminalisation debate. In Public Interest Foundation v Union of India, decided in 2018, the Court was asked to go the further step and disqualify candidates against whom charges had been framed for serious offences. It declined, holding that adding a disqualification is a legislative act which the Court could not perform by direction, and it held instead that the disclosure regime should be given teeth: candidates would declare their criminal antecedents in bold letters in the prescribed form, political parties would publish the antecedents of their candidates on their websites and in newspapers and electronic media, and the Commission would give the information wide publicity. When compliance lagged, the Court returned to the matter in 2020 in proceedings arising from the Bihar elections and directed parties to publish, within a short fixed period of selecting a candidate, the reasons for selecting a candidate with a declared criminal record, and to state why a candidate without such a record could not have been chosen, reasons which the Court directed must refer to qualifications and merit and not to winnability alone. The sequence is the examinable unit: the courts built disclosure, refused disqualification, and then policed the publicity of disclosure, and the reform question the sequence leaves open, whether accusation at the charge stage should bar a candidate, sits exactly where the Court left it, with Parliament.

The second decision is Lily Thomas v Union of India, which in 2013 struck down section 8(4) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951. Section 8 disqualifies a person convicted of specified offences, and in the case of other offences on a sentence of imprisonment of not less than two years; sub-section (4) had provided that where the convicted person was already a member, the disqualification would not take effect until three months had passed, and if an appeal was filed within that period, until the appeal was disposed of. The Court held that Parliament, which Articles 102(1)(e) and 191(1)(e) empower to state disqualifications, has no power to state different commencement dates for a person who is a member and a person who is not, since the articles speak of disqualification for being chosen as, and for being, a member in the same words; the sub-section fell, and a sitting member convicted in the specified way stands disqualified from the date of conviction, the only route back being a stay of the conviction itself on appeal. The third decision is Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu, in which a Constitution Bench in 1992 upheld the Tenth Schedule against the challenge that it destroyed the democratic character of the legislatures and the freedom of the member, holding that the Schedule addressed a real evil and that the member's freedom of speech in the House was subject to the disqualification the Constitution itself imposed; but the Bench struck down paragraph 7, the clause barring the jurisdiction of the courts, because a provision that changes the effect of the articles governing the Supreme Court and the High Courts required ratification by half the state legislatures under the proviso to Article 368(2), which had not been obtained. The surviving position is a study in precision: the presiding officer decides, the decision is final in the Schedule's terms, and it remains reviewable as the decision of a tribunal on grounds of mala fides, perversity and violation of the constitutional mandate, though not before the officer has decided, save in the rarest cases. The full treatment of the Schedule's grounds, its deleted and surviving exceptions and the case for and against the whip sits in [the article on the anti-defection law in India](/2018/01/15/anti-defection-law-india-explained/), and the candidate preparing this controversy should read the two together.

The fourth decision, Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Speaker of the Manipur Legislative Assembly, decided in 2020, addresses the weakness that Kihoto left: the Schedule fixed no time within which the presiding officer must decide, and petitions were being left undecided for years while the allegedly defecting member continued to sit and, in the case before the Court, to hold ministerial office. The Court held that a presiding officer should decide a defection petition within a reasonable period, and that, absent exceptional circumstances for which there is good reason, three months from the filing of the petition is the outer limit; it further invited Parliament to consider substituting an independent tribunal, headed by a retired judge of the Supreme Court or a retired Chief Justice of a High Court, for the presiding officer as the adjudicating authority. The proposition to carry into the examination hall is the distinction between the norm and the text: the three-month limit is a judicially stated standard of reasonableness, not a period written into the Schedule, and answers that attribute it to the Schedule's text misstate both the judgment and the Schedule.

The fifth decision is Anoop Baranwal v Union of India, delivered by a Constitution Bench on 2 March 2023, on the appointment of Commissioners. Article 324(2) makes appointments by the President subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament, and for over seven decades Parliament had legislated only on conditions of service, leaving the appointment itself to the executive's unaided choice. The Bench held that until Parliament made a law governing the selection, appointments of the Chief Election Commissioner and the Election Commissioners would be made on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the House of the People and the Chief Justice of India, reasoning that the independence of the Commission was a constitutional value the appointment process had to be designed to protect, and that a selection resting with the executive alone placed the referee of the electoral contest in the gift of one of its players. Parliament responded within the year with the Act of 2023 described earlier in this article, which adopted the committee form but placed a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister where the judgment had placed the Chief Justice, and the statute was challenged in its turn, with the Court declining to stay its operation at the interim stage. The whole sequence, textual gap, judicial stopgap, legislative answer, fresh challenge, is the field's best illustration of how an institutional question moves, and it is examined as exactly that.

### Why did the Anoop Baranwal bench place the Chief Justice of India on the committee?

Parliament had never legislated the selection of Commissioners under Article 324(2), so appointments rested with the executive alone. The bench reasoned a body conducting elections in which the executive is a contestant cannot be chosen by that executive unaided without compromising the independence Article 324 presupposes, and filled the gap with a committee including the Chief Justice, until Parliament legislated.

The sixth decision is the electoral bonds judgment, Association for Democratic Reforms v Union of India, reported at (2024) 5 SCC 1 and delivered unanimously by a Constitution Bench on 15 February 2024, and it is the decision on which precision is most often lost. The scheme it struck down, notified on 2 January 2018 under amendments made by the Finance Act, 2017, had allowed donations to political parties through bearer <redacted> bought from the State Bank of India, exempted such contributions from the contribution report parties must file, relieved parties of the duty to keep records of them, and removed the ceiling that had limited a company's political contributions to a percentage of its average net profits. The judgment struck the scheme and the amendments down on two distinct grounds, and the grounds must never be merged. On the first ground, the Court held that the amendments curtailing disclosure violated Article 19(1)(a), because information about the funding of political parties is essential to the voter's effective choice, and that the measure failed the test of proportionality applied to that freedom: curbing unaccounted money in politics was a legitimate aim, but anonymous donations on this scale were not the least restrictive means of pursuing it, and the informational privacy of the donor, which the Court recognised, did not extend to contributions made as political investment rather than as an expression of political affiliation. On the second ground, the Court held that the amendment to the Companies Act deleting the profit-linked ceiling was manifestly arbitrary and so violated Article 14, because it treated the political contribution of a company, an artificial person whose outlay may be a commercial transaction, as indistinguishable from the contribution of a citizen, and because it removed the safeguard that had kept loss-making and shell entities out of political finance. A replacement scheme with full transparency would answer the first ground and could still fall on the second, which is why describing the judgment as resting on the right to information alone, the single most common error in the preparation material on this case, understates the judgment by half. Two further precisions belong to a first-class answer: the decision was unanimous, with the lead opinion of the Chief Justice and a separate concurrence by Justice Sanjiv Khanna, who agreed in the result by a partly different route on the standard of review; and the Court decided nothing on the challenge to the Finance Act's certification as a Money Bill, that question standing referred to a bench of seven judges, so the judgment is no authority on Article 110 either way. The statutory detail of the scheme belongs to the pillar on the electoral bonds law and the full analysis of the holdings to the article on the electoral bonds judgment; what this article requires is that the candidate carry the two grounds separately, with the article numbers attached.

Supporting these six, two older decisions should be held in reserve because they answer the structural questions the six presuppose. N. P. Ponnuswami, already noticed, establishes that the election is an integrated process and the petition its only challenge. Mohinder Singh Gill establishes the breadth of Article 324 and, equally quoted by examiners, its discipline: the Commission's plenary power is to be exercised in conformity with the enacted law where law exists, and its actions remain open to judicial review once the electoral process they govern has run its course. Between them, the two decisions define the Commission's constitutional personality, a body with a reservoir of power inside a framework of law, and a candidate who can state that sentence has the foundation on which every institutional argument in the field is built.

A third supporting decision marks the reservoir's bank, and it is the one that converts the Gill proposition from a slogan into a rule. In A. C. Jose v Sivan Pillai, the Supreme Court considered the Commission's direction that votes be recorded by electronic voting machines in a Kerala by-election at a time when the Act and the rules made no provision for machines at all. The Court held the direction bad: Article 324's plenary power operates in the interstices of the law, but where the enacted text occupies the ground, prescribing that votes be recorded by ballot in a ballot box, the Commission cannot substitute a different machinery by direction, however efficient the substitute may be. Parliament then amended the Act to authorise voting machines, and the machines entered the law by the front door of legislation. The pair of cases, Gill and Jose, states the complete doctrine the examiner is listening for whenever the Commission's power is in issue: the power is plenary where the law is silent, subordinate where the law has spoken, and reviewable when the electoral process it governs is complete, and an answer that carries only the first limb of the three has carried the part of the doctrine that flatters the Commission and dropped the parts that discipline it. The same three-limb structure, incidentally, is the correct frame for the Model Code's authority, which is at its strongest precisely where the Code enforces what the statute already forbids and at its most contestable where it restrains conduct the enacted law permits, a point the stronger answers on the Code make and the weaker ones never reach.

Read the scaffold by columns as well as by rows. The first column of citations is short in every row, which is the article's whole thesis in miniature: a handful of articles generates the field, and the candidate who cites them exactly has done the work the examiner is scoring. The reform column is deliberately the most perishable, and it is the column to keep under review, because a proposal acknowledged after it has been enacted, or described as enacted while it remains a proposal, converts the strongest row of the scaffold into the answer's largest error. The closing column is the one to internalise rather than to copy: each closing position is an institutional proposition, a statement about where authority sits or how a design behaves, and it can be defended from the citations in its own row without any claim about which party or programme benefits, which is the property that makes it safe in the examination hall.

## A worked approach to a typical demand

The scaffold becomes real when it is run once against a demand of the kind the paper actually sets. Take the demand that recurs in several forms across the years, that the anti-defection law has failed to curb the instability it was enacted to prevent and that its adjudication should be taken from the presiding officer. The untrained answer begins with episodes, a fallen government, a resort, a midnight swearing-in, and argues from the most vivid one. The trained answer begins by locating the question in its row and its layer. The row is anti-defection and legislative stability. The layer of the opening is constitutional text: the Tenth Schedule, inserted in 1985, disqualifies a member for voluntarily giving up party membership and for defying the whip, protects a merger supported by two-thirds, and since 2003 protects no split. The adjudication sentence belongs to the same layer: paragraph 6 commits the question to the presiding officer and calls the decision final. The opening of the answer therefore states the design in four sentences and has already outscored the anecdotal opening, because it has shown the examiner the whole machine before judging any part of it.

The middle of the answer tests the design against its record, which is the layer of judgment and report. Kihoto Hollohan supplies the review position: the officer is a tribunal, the decision is reviewable on limited grounds, paragraph 7 fell for want of ratification. Keisham Meghachandra Singh supplies the delay position: no period in the text, a judicial norm of three months as the outer limit of reasonableness, and a proposal for an independent tribunal headed by a retired judge. The Goswami Committee supplies the reform lineage: the alternative adjudicator, the President or the Governor acting on the Commission's advice, was proposed in 1990, so the demand's suggestion is thirty years old and its non-enactment is itself evidence about where the obstacle sits. The assessment the question invites can then be made precisely: the law changed the form of defection rather than abolishing it, defections migrating to the protected channels of merger and resignation, which is a finding about drafting; and the adjudication defect is not bias in any single decision but the absence of a clock in the text, which is a finding about design. The conclusion follows the structure the article teaches: the cure for a defect of adjudication timing is a period written into the Schedule or a change of adjudicator by amendment, both constitutional acts, and until one of them is performed the norm of Keisham is the discipline the system has. Nothing in that answer required the candidate to approve or condemn any defection that ever occurred, and every sentence in it can be footnoted to a text, a judgment or a named report. That is the worked form of the article's claim: cite the article, not the argument, and the argument assembles itself.

## Writing contested answers without a partisan position

The anxiety that haunts this subject, that a candidate's view on a contested electoral question will be penalised, rests on a misunderstanding of what is being marked. An examiner cannot reward agreement and cannot detect loyalty; what the examiner can reward, and what the marking scheme describes, is the handling of material: both cases stated at strength, the constitutional question separated from the political one, a conclusion reached on the question the material can settle. An answer that endorses a party's position and an answer that denounces it fail in the same way, by substituting alignment for analysis. An answer that refuses to conclude fails in the opposite way, by reading as evasion. The technique this article teaches sits between the two failures: conclude, but conclude on the tractable question, which in this field is nearly always a question about the source of a power, the limits on it, or the design of an institution, and name the remaining disagreement for what it is, a disagreement about values or about predictions, which the candidate records without pretending the Constitution resolves it.

The delimitation controversy is the cleanest training example because the regional charge on it is real. Readjustment after the freeze lifts would transfer seats toward the states whose populations have grown fastest, which are in the main the northern states, and away from states that stabilised their populations early, in the main the southern states, and the southern objection, put at its strongest by the governments and commentators who advance it, is that representation would then punish demographic success and reward its opposite, converting a population policy the Union itself promoted into a loss of voice in the Union's legislature. The counter-case, put at its strongest, is that representation by population is the Constitution's own rule in Article 81, that the freeze was always an exception justified for a stated period and purpose, and that a citizen's vote in a state whose constituencies hold several times the population of another state's carries a fraction of the weight, an inequality among citizens that no federal argument fully answers. A candidate who can state both of those cases in their strongest forms, identify the text that generates the tension, name the devices that have been proposed to soften it, including postponement, enlargement of the House so that readjustment redistributes only new seats, and the protection of every state's existing share as a floor, and then conclude on the institutional question, which device the Constitution permits and which would need amendment, has written the answer the paper was designed to elicit, and has taken no side that any examiner could penalise.

Simultaneous elections train the same muscle on a proposal rather than a judgment. The proposal to hold elections to the House of the People and the state assemblies together has been examined by the Law Commission, which in its Two Hundred and Fifty-fifth Report and in later draft work treated it as requiring constitutional amendment, and by a high-level committee chaired by Ram Nath Kovind, a former President of India, which reported in favour of a phased move to a common cycle; Bills to amend the Constitution and the connected statutes were introduced in Parliament and referred to a joint parliamentary committee, and there the matter stands as a proposal under examination, not as law. The case for it, at its strongest, is administrative and fiscal: the near-continuous cycle of elections keeps governments in campaign posture, imposes repeated enforcement of the Model Code with its pause on policy, and multiplies the cost of conducting polls. The case against, at its strongest, is federal and democratic: a common cycle subordinates the life of a state assembly, which the Constitution ties to its own legislature's confidence, to a national timetable, and it nationalises state contests in which local questions deserve their own verdict. The examinable conclusion is not which case is better. It is that the proposal cannot be delivered by ordinary legislation, because the fixed terms of the assemblies and the provisions for their dissolution are constitutional text, and that its federal cost and administrative saving are the two axes on which any assessment must be organised. That conclusion is a conclusion, reached and defended, and it commits the candidate to nothing except an understanding of where the question lives.

One further discipline belongs in this section, because it is where contested answers most often destroy themselves: the use of figures. Assertions about the number of candidates facing criminal cases, the cost of elections, the volume of party funding or the scale of seizures during a campaign circulate freely in preparation material, frequently without a source, a year or a definition, and they decay as they circulate. The rule the preceding articles of this cluster observe, and the rule to import into the answer book, is that a figure enters an answer only with its source and its period attached, the affidavit-based analyses of candidate declarations published by election watch organisations for a named election, the Commission's own expenditure and seizure statements for a named poll, or it does not enter at all. A qualitative statement that a substantial share of candidates declare criminal cases pending against them is defensible without a number; a precise percentage without a source is not defensible at all, and an examiner who knows the field knows which of the two a serious candidate writes.

## The confusions that cost marks

Six confusions recur in this field with such regularity that an examiner can be forgiven for treating their absence as a mark of preparation in itself. The first, already given its own treatment above, is the merger of the two Representation of the People Acts: the Act of 1950 constitutes the electorate through the rolls, and the Act of 1951 governs the contest, the candidates and the petitions, and any sentence that assigns a roll provision to the later Act or a disqualification provision to the earlier one is wrong on its face. The second is the belief that the Model Code of Conduct is a statute or a set of statutory rules. It is neither; it is a consensus instrument of the parties and the Commission, enforced through Article 324 and through those of its prohibitions that coincide with offences and corrupt practices in the enacted law, and an answer that speaks of a provision of the Code, or of its enactment, has announced its own error. The third concerns the merger exception in the Tenth Schedule. Preparation material routinely calls the exception a loophole, meaning an unintended gap exploited against the draftsmen's purpose. Paragraph 4 is not a gap; it is an express provision protecting a merger of the original political party with another party where not less than two-thirds of the members of the legislature party agree to it, and it was retained in 2003 when the split provision beside it was deliberately deleted. The accurate criticism of paragraph 4 is a criticism of a choice the Constitution made and renewed, that collective defection in merger form is legitimate while individual defection is not, and an examiner rewards the candidate who criticises the choice as a choice.

The fourth confusion is the description of proposals as enacted law, and it is the most damaging because it falsifies the ground under every other proposition in the answer. The disqualification of candidates at the stage of the framing of charges is a proposal, recommended by the Law Commission in its Two Hundred and Forty-fourth Report with safeguards, considered by the Supreme Court in Public Interest Foundation v Union of India, where the Court held that adding a disqualification is for Parliament and confined itself to directing wider publicity of candidates' antecedents, and not enacted; the independent tribunal for defection questions is a proposal made by the Court in Keisham Meghachandra Singh, not an institution that exists; simultaneous elections are a proposal in committee, not a scheme in force; state funding of elections is a proposal partially endorsed by the Indrajit Gupta Committee in 1998 in the form of funding in kind, not a system in operation. Each of these belongs in an answer, but in the reform column of the scaffold, labelled as what it is. The fifth confusion is the single-ground account of the electoral bonds judgment, treated above: two grounds, Article 19(1)(a) for the disclosure provisions and Article 14 for the corporate ceiling, and the Money Bill question expressly undecided. The sixth is the assignment of decisions to the wrong authority: defection is decided by the presiding officer and not by the Election Commission, a disqualification under Article 102 is decided in form by the President on the Commission's binding opinion and not by the House, and the result of an election is undone by the High Court in an election petition and not by the Commission on a complaint. Each of these is a one-line correction, and each is worth more in the hall than a paragraph of general commentary, because the paper is constructed to catch exactly these substitutions.

Two further confusions complete the set. The seventh is the belief that all three members of the Commission stand in the same constitutional position. They do not: the Chief Election Commissioner is removable only in the manner and on the grounds of a Supreme Court judge under the text of Article 324(5), while another Election Commissioner may not be removed except on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner, and the appointment statute of 2023 preserved that asymmetry while changing the selection method and the service equation. An answer that proposes to cure the Commission's dependence by giving all its members the Chief Election Commissioner's protection has at least seen the asymmetry; an answer that assumes the protection is already equal has not seen the institution at all. The eighth is the description of the right to vote as a fundamental right. It is not located in Part III. Article 326 entitles the adult citizen to be registered as a voter subject to the disqualifications it names, the right to cast the vote is exercised under the statute, which is why the Supreme Court in Kuldip Nayar v Union of India described the right to vote as a statutory right, while the expression embodied in the vote has been protected through Article 19(1)(a) in the disclosure and none of the above decisions. The precise position is a three-layer one like everything else in this field, a constitutional entitlement, a statutory exercise and a fundamental freedom in the act of expression, and the candidate who collapses the three layers into a single assertion will be wrong in whichever direction the question is pressed.

## What to memorise, what to reconstruct, and what to do with figures

Revision in this field divides cleanly into three baskets, and the candidate who sorts material into them early will spend the final weeks better than the candidate who revises the whole subject at one depth. The first basket is what must be memorised because it cannot be reconstructed under pressure: the numbers and locations of the governing articles, 324 to 329 and the neighbours around them; the identity of the two Acts and their Act numbers with their years; the paragraphs of the Tenth Schedule that do the work, paragraph 2 for the grounds, paragraph 4 for the merger, paragraph 6 for the decision; the sequence of the freeze amendments, Forty-second, Eighty-fourth, Eighty-seventh, with the census year each one engaged; the names and years of the six governing judgments and the single proposition each carries; and the composition of the Selection Committee under the Act of 2023. These are the Prelims facts and the Mains citations at once, and together they fit on two pages. The second basket is what should be understood rather than memorised: the reasoning by which the right to know was located in Article 19(1)(a), the reason paragraph 7 of the Tenth Schedule fell while the Schedule stood, the federal logic of the freeze, the difference between the proportionality ground and the manifest arbitrariness ground in the bonds judgment. Understanding of this kind cannot be crammed and does not need to be; it needs to have been read slowly once and argued through, after which the candidate can regenerate it in the hall in whatever order the question demands.

The handful of section numbers that do function as identifiers deserves to be named, since it is shorter than candidates expect and every member of it is asked after. In the Act of 1951 they are section 8 for disqualification on conviction and section 8A for the corrupt practice decision, section 10A for the failure to lodge the expense account, section 29A for the registration of parties, section 62 for the right to vote at an election, section 77 for the expenditure account and its Explanation, section 100 for the grounds on which an election may be declared void, and section 123 for the corrupt practices. In the Act of 1950 the corresponding handful is the roll provisions taken as a group, sections 16 to 24 in their working parts, with section 20 carrying the meaning of ordinarily resident. A candidate who can attach the correct idea to each of those numbers owns the statute book of the field for every purpose the general papers have, and the sections beyond the handful belong to the law optional and the judicial services papers, where they are properly tested at depth.

The third basket is what should deliberately not be memorised: the long tail of section numbers in the two Acts beyond the handful that function as identifiers, the expenditure ceilings and deposit figures that are revised by rule and notification and will date any answer that leans on their precision, and the statistical claims of the debate, which belong in answers, if at all, in the sourced and dated form described earlier. There is a discipline question underneath the third basket that the examination quietly tests. A paper on institutions rewards the candidate who knows which of the field's numbers are constitutional and therefore fixed, which are statutory and therefore amendable, and which are executive figures that move by notification; the candidate who recites all three classes with the same confidence has revealed that the distinction was never learned, and the distinction is itself one of the field's organisable ideas. The Commission's reports, the Law Commission's numbered reports and the named committees, the Goswami Committee on electoral reforms, the Vohra Committee on the nexus between crime and politics, the Indrajit Gupta Committee on state funding, are worth holding by name and subject rather than by content in detail, because an examiner credits the candidate who can place a reform proposal in its institutional lineage: who proposed it, when, and whether anything was done with it.

## The anchor file and the practice habit

Two working habits convert this article from reading into preparation. The first is the anchor file: a personal, cumulative record in which each article of the constitutional spine, each statute in the set and each judgment in the six is given an entry of fixed shape, stating the proposition in one sentence, the ground or provision it rests on, and, separately, the directions or proposals associated with it that are not law. The separation inside the entry is the point. A file that mixes holdings with directions, and enacted provisions with recommended ones, reproduces in private the confusion this article exists to cure, while a file that keeps the layers apart becomes, by the end of the subject, a two-column memory of the entire field that can be revised in an evening. Keeping that file in a private workspace such as VaultBook, where the candidate's own case summaries and provision maps sit apart from shared preparation material, suits a document that is meant to accumulate corrections over a full preparation cycle. A specimen entry shows the shape. Under Kihoto Hollohan the proposition line reads: the Tenth Schedule is valid, paragraph 7 is void for want of ratification, and the presiding officer decides as a tribunal subject to review on limited grounds. The ground line reads: Articles 136, 226 and 227 read with the proviso to Article 368(2), the ratification requirement being what killed the ouster clause and nothing else. The not-law line reads: the decision does not fix any period for the officer to decide in, and it does not transfer the decision to the Commission, both of which are later developments belonging to Keisham and to the reform shelf respectively. An entry in that shape can be written in four minutes, revised in one, and trusted in the hall, because everything in it is either a holding with its ground or an express warning about what the case is not.

The second habit is practice against the actual record: working through previous-year questions on this subject and sorting each one into the scaffold's rows before attempting it, which is the exercise for which [topic-wise previous-year question practice on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) exists. A question that cannot be sorted has usually been misread, and a question that sorts into two rows is usually the paper's discriminating question, the one that separates the ranks; both discoveries are worth more than another reading of the notes. The teacher's use of the same material runs parallel: this is a subject on which a classroom divides along the lines of the politics outside it, and the scaffold gives a teacher the means of keeping the division analytical, by requiring every contribution to name its article, its statute or its judgment before it is allowed to become an opinion.

## Closing verdict

The field of election law, as the examination constructs it, is a closed system with open questions. The system is closed because its generating materials are few and fixed: a Part of the Constitution and its neighbouring articles, two statutes that divide the subject between the electorate and the contest, a schedule with a deleted paragraph, a freeze stated in three amendments, a recent appointment statute and six judgments, each with one proposition and one ground. The questions are open because the institutions those materials build, a commission chosen by the executive it referees, a presiding officer judging the majority that sustains the chair, a map of representation frozen at the population of a half-century past, a funding regime whose governing judgment is younger than the candidates reading it, generate genuine institutional argument that no honest preparation should flatten. The series thesis holds in this register as in every other: the Constitution and the statutes are the text, the amendments, the judgments and the reform record are the record, and separating them is what distinguishes an analysis from an opinion. An aspirant who finishes this subject able to say, of any electoral proposition, which layer it belongs to, which article or provision carries it, and what would have to change, and by whose act, for it to be otherwise, is ready for any question the papers can frame on it. This article is examination preparation and general legal information; it is not advice on any live election dispute, petition or nomination, and nothing in it substitutes for the text of the provisions themselves, which the candidate should always read in the bare Act before relying on any summary, including this one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How is election law tested in the civil services examination?

In the Preliminary paper it is tested as fact within Indian Polity: the number of an article, the content of a schedule, the year of an amendment, the authority that decides a named question, each answerable from a single line of constitutional or statutory text. In the Main examination it sits in General Studies Paper II and is tested as institution and argument, through questions on the Election Commission's independence, the anti-defection provisions, political funding and the delimitation freeze. The two papers therefore demand the same material held at two depths, once as citable propositions and once as institutional analysis, and preparation serving only one depth loses marks in the other.

### Q: Which constitutional articles on elections must an aspirant know?

The spine is Part XV, Articles 324 to 329: the Commission's vesting power, the single electoral roll, adult suffrage, the legislative powers of Parliament and of the state legislatures, and the bar on calling an election in question except by petition. Around it the aspirant needs Articles 81 and 170 on composition, Articles 82 and 170 on readjustment after a census, Articles 84 and 173 on qualifications, Articles 102 and 191 on disqualifications, Articles 103 and 192 on the authority that decides them, Articles 330 and 332 on reserved seats, and Article 334 on the period for which reservation operates. With the Tenth Schedule added, that set generates nearly every question in the field.

### Q: Which election judgments matter most for the examination?

Six carry the weight: the Association for Democratic Reforms decision of 2002 and the People's Union for Civil Liberties decision of 2003, which built the voter's right to know a candidate's antecedents on Article 19(1)(a); Lily Thomas in 2013, which struck down the convicted sitting member's protection in section 8(4); Kihoto Hollohan in 1992, which upheld the Tenth Schedule while striking its ouster of judicial review; Keisham Meghachandra Singh in 2020, which required the presiding officer to decide within a reasonable period; Anoop Baranwal in 2023, on the appointment of Commissioners; and the electoral bonds judgment of 2024, with its two distinct grounds. Behind them, Ponnuswami and Mohinder Singh Gill answer the structural questions.

### Q: How do you write about electoral reform in a Mains answer?

Begin from the constitutional position, not from the proposal: state what the relevant article or statute provides, then state the defect the reform addresses, then the proposal itself, attributed to the body that made it, whether a Law Commission report, a committee or the Election Commission. Set out the strongest objection to the proposal beside the strongest case for it, and close on the institutional question the proposal raises, such as who should decide, or which text would have to change. The cardinal discipline is labelling: a proposal must be written as a proposal throughout, because the moment a recommendation is described in the grammar of enacted law, the answer's foundation is false.

### Q: What is the commonest error in election law questions?

The commonest error is layer confusion: treating a reform proposal as enacted law, attributing a provision of the Representation of the People Act, 1950 to the Act of 1951, or citing a judgment for a proposition it declined to decide. Within that family, the single most repeated instance is the account of the electoral bonds judgment as resting on the right to information alone, when its corporate funding holding rests separately on Article 14. A second frequent error is assigning decisions to the wrong authority, sending defection to the Election Commission or an Article 102 disqualification to the House. Each error is cheap to avoid once the field is held as constitutional text, statute and reform record in separate layers.

### Q: How much of the Model Code of Conduct is examinable?

The examinable content is the Code's legal character rather than its text. The candidate needs to know that the Code is not a statute and has no provision of its own, that it was evolved by the Election Commission in consultation with the political parties, that it operates from the announcement of the schedule to the completion of the process, and that it is enforced through the plenary power of Article 324 together with those of its prohibitions that coincide with corrupt practices and offences in the enacted law. The one judgment to attach is Subramaniam Balaji, where the Court held that manifesto promises are not a corrupt practice and asked the Commission to frame manifesto guidelines, which entered the Code.

### Q: Which election topics overlap with federalism?

Three overlaps recur. The first is legislative competence: Article 327 gives Parliament the power over elections to Parliament and to the state legislatures, while Article 328 leaves the state legislature a residual power over its own elections where Parliament has not legislated, so the two Representation of the People Acts are also a lesson in how a central occupation of a field works. The second is delimitation, where the freeze protects states that controlled population against states that did not, a bargain among the units of the Union. The third is simultaneous elections, whose cost is stated almost entirely in federal terms, since a common cycle subordinates the terms of state assemblies to a national timetable.

### Q: How do you keep up with electoral reform developments?

The durable method is to track instruments rather than commentary: amendment Acts as they are passed, judgments as they are delivered, and the reports of the Law Commission and of committees as they are published, entering each into the anchor file under its correct layer with its date and its status. A reform enters an answer at the weight its status carries, so a Bill in committee, a report awaiting action and an enacted amendment must be recorded as three different things. What should not be tracked is the daily argument around a development, which contributes vocabulary but no propositions, and which dates fastest of everything in the subject.

### Q: What is the difference between a Prelims question and a Mains question on election law?

A Prelims question has one correct proposition and asks the candidate to recognise it: which article vests superintendence in the Commission, which amendment deleted the split provision, which authority decides a defection petition. It is answered from the memorised layer of the subject, and partial knowledge earns nothing. A Mains question has a demand rather than a single answer: it asks the candidate to examine, discuss or critically evaluate an institution or a proposal, and it is marked on structure, on the separation of the constitutional position from the statutory detail and the reform debate, and on a conclusion reached on the institutional question. The same provision, paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule for example, is a fact in one paper and an argument in the other.

### Q: Which facts in election law are worth memorising, and which should be reconstructed?

Memorise the identifiers: the governing article numbers, the two Acts with their numbers and years, the working paragraphs of the Tenth Schedule, the sequence of the freeze amendments with the census each engaged, the names and years of the six governing judgments, and the composition of the Selection Committee under the Act of 2023. Reconstruct the reasoning: why the right to know sits in Article 19(1)(a), why paragraph 7 fell while the Schedule stood, why the freeze was defended, and how the two grounds of the bonds judgment differ. Reasoning that has been understood once can be regenerated under pressure; identifiers cannot, so the memorised list should stay short and exact.

### Q: How should an aspirant use previous-year questions while preparing election law?

Sort before solving. Take each previous-year question on this subject and assign it to one of the scaffold's rows, the Commission, criminalisation, funding, defection, delimitation or the franchise, and note which layer it tests, constitutional text, statutory detail or reform debate. A question that resists sorting has usually been misread, and identifying the misreading is the learning. Only after sorting should the question be attempted in writing, against the row's citations, and the attempt compared with the scaffold to find which citation was missed. Used this way the back papers stop being a sample of what was asked and become a map of how the subject is constructed.

### Q: Is it safer to describe a judgment than to name it wrongly in an election law answer?

Yes, and the discipline is worth stating plainly: state the proposition first, in one sentence, and attach the case name only where it is certain. A correct proposition with the case described by its year and its subject earns nearly full credit, because the examiner is testing the proposition. A wrong name attached to a correct proposition converts a sound answer into an unreliable one, and a name attached to a proposition the case never decided is worse than either. Where a judgment is one of the governing six, the name should be learned with its ground as a single unit, since in this field several famous cases share parties, benches and subject matter and are routinely transposed.

### Q: How do you write an answer on simultaneous elections without taking a party position?

Separate the three questions the proposal contains. The first is mechanical and constitutional: the terms of the assemblies and the provisions for dissolution are constitutional text, so a common cycle cannot be created by ordinary legislation, and the amending route is itself part of the answer. The second is administrative: the savings in cost, personnel and campaign governance claimed for a common cycle, which can be stated and weighed. The third is federal and evaluative: whether state contests should be subordinated to a national timetable, on which the candidate records both cases at strength and concludes on the institutional design rather than on which side of the debate deserves to win. The proposal's status, introduced and in committee, must be stated as its status.

### Q: How much statistical detail should an election law answer carry?

Very little, and only in sourced form. Figures on candidates' criminal antecedents, election expenditure, party funding and seizures circulate in preparation material without sources, years or definitions, and decay as they circulate; an examiner who knows the field discounts them on sight. A figure earns its place when it arrives with its source and period attached, the affidavit analyses published for a named election or the Commission's statements for a named poll, and when it does work in the argument rather than decorating it. A qualitative statement that a substantial share of candidates declare pending criminal cases is defensible; an unsourced percentage is not, and the safe habit is to build answers whose conclusions stand if every number is removed.

### Q: How are anti-defection questions usually framed in the Mains paper?

They are framed as institutional design questions wearing political clothes. The recurring demands are whether the law has achieved the stability it promised, whether the presiding officer is the right adjudicator, whether the whip should extend beyond confidence and money votes to every division, and whether the merger exception can be defended beside the deleted split. Each framing expects the same foundation: the grounds in paragraph 2, the deletion of paragraph 3 in 2003, the survival of paragraph 4, Kihoto Hollohan on review, and Keisham Meghachandra Singh on delay. The candidate who builds that foundation first and argues second will find that the partisan surface of the question does not need to be touched at all.

### Q: What depth on the Delimitation Acts does the examination actually reward?

The rewarded depth is the structure, not the schedules. The candidate needs the sequence of the four Acts, the fact that a Commission's orders have the force of law and are protected by Article 329(a) from being questioned in court, the freeze amendments and the census years they engaged, and the peculiar position of the exercise under the Act of 2002, which redrew boundaries within states on the census of 2001 while the allocation between states stayed at 1971. What is not rewarded is the reproduction of constituency-level detail, commission membership lists or state-wise seat arithmetic, which belongs to the reference shelf. In a Mains answer the Acts earn their place as the machinery through which the constitutional bargain on representation is executed.

### Q: How should a candidate handle a question on a reform proposal that has not been enacted?

State its status in the first lines and never let the grammar slip afterward: the proposal is a proposal, and the verbs must stay conditional. Name the proposing body and its report or committee, since an unattributed proposal reads as the candidate's own opinion, and set out the existing law the proposal would change, because the examiner cannot credit an assessment of a change whose starting point is unstated. Then give the strongest case on each side and conclude on feasibility in institutional terms: what text would have to be amended, whose consent the change requires, and which existing authority's position it alters. Disqualification at the framing of charges, the independent defection tribunal and state funding all reward exactly this handling.

### Q: Where else in the examination does election law appear besides General Studies Paper II?

It appears across the examination's surfaces. In the Preliminary paper it is a standing part of Indian Polity, tested as fact. In the essay paper, electoral reform, the health of representative institutions and the financing of politics are recurring themes that reward the same anchor set at essay length. In law optional papers the constitutional provisions and the petition jurisprudence carry direct weight, and in political science the field feeds the units on the party system and the working of the Constitution. In the interview, questions on electoral reform are a standard probe of whether a candidate can hold a contested institutional question neutrally, which is the precise skill this subject is used, at every stage, to test.

### Q: How should a teacher keep a classroom discussion of electoral reform non-partisan?

By imposing the citation discipline before allowing the argument: every contribution must name its article, its statute or its judgment before it is permitted to become an opinion, and contested questions are set in the form the examination uses, as questions about the source of a power, its limits or the design of an institution. The teacher's own position should be the last thing the room learns, and the scaffold supplies the neutral ground, because a class can dispute which closing position a row supports while agreeing on the citations the row contains. The delimitation question is the best exercise in the set, since it requires a regionally charged matter to be stated in terms every region would accept as fair.

### Q: What is the best order in which to revise the election law cluster before the Mains?

Revise in the order the subject is built. Begin with Part XV and its neighbouring articles, since every later layer cites them. Move to the two statutes and fix the division between the electorate and the contest, then to the Tenth Schedule with its two amendments, then to the freeze sequence and the delimitation line, and only then to the judgments, which are unintelligible as a set until the provisions they interpret are in place. Finish with the reform record, proposals labelled by body and status, and with the scaffold, attempting one sorted previous-year question per row. The order matters because each stage supplies the citations the next stage argues about; revising judgments first produces answers that quote conclusions without their texts.
