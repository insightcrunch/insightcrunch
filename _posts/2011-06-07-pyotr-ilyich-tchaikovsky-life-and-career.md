## A thousand letters and a standing agreement never to meet

For fourteen years, a widow in Moscow and a composer she had never spoken to conducted one of the most productive relationships in music entirely on paper. She settled on him an allowance of six thousand roubles a year, a sum large enough to free him from teaching and from every other form of paid drudgery, and she attached a single condition that he accepted at once and honoured for the whole fourteen years: they were never to meet. If their paths crossed in a street or a theatre foyer, they were to pass as strangers. She heard his music constantly, read his confessions weekly, knew his finances, his illnesses and his despair in detail, and never once heard his voice. When readers ask how the Tchaikovsky life and career could produce so much music in so short a working life, the answer begins with this arrangement, because the allowance bought the hours and the distance bought the candour. The patron was Nadezhda von Meck. The composer was Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky. The letters they exchanged number more than a thousand, and they survive as the single richest documentary record of his inner life.

The thesis of this biography is that Tchaikovsky was the first Russian composer to sustain a genuinely professional career in the Western sense, and that he built that career inside institutions that had barely been invented when he needed them. No Russian conservatory existed when he was born. No Russian musician of his generation earned a full living from composition, publishing and conducting contracts across several countries. He did, and the route by which he did it explains both the productivity and the strain. The money that freed him came from a private patron rather than from a court, a church or a university, and it came with a rule of distance that suited his temperament so precisely that it reads less like a restriction than like a diagnosis. The private life he recorded in those letters was then twice edited by other hands, first by his brother and then by a state, so that the life has to be told together with an account of who controlled the telling.

Why would a patron who adored a composer's music refuse ever to be in the same room with him? The question opens in this first section and is not answered quickly, because the answer requires the whole correspondence to be understood: what she wanted from him, what he needed from her, and why the paper relationship could carry weights that a personal acquaintance could not. That answer lands in section thirteen, once the allowance, the letters and the ending have been laid out in sequence. First, the life has to be assembled from the beginning, in a household at the edge of the empire where nothing about the boy suggested a professional musician, because professional musicians, in the Russia of his childhood, effectively did not exist.

The correspondence survived, which is itself a fact of the first importance for everything this article can claim. Both sides of it were kept: his letters to her were preserved by her family, and hers to him were preserved by him and passed into the archive his brother founded at Klin. For decades the letters were printed only in selections, and in editions subject to the cuts examined in sections twenty and thirty-two, so that the full text of the exchange became generally readable only in the later twentieth century. What the bulk establishes, once read whole, is the relationship's ordinary machinery: money discussed without embarrassment, music discussed without flattery, family news, illnesses, books, servants, travel plans and gossip, the complete fabric of a friendship that happened to be conducted at a distance of several hundred miles and a remove of one agreed rule. The rule is the famous part. The fabric is the evidence.

## Votkinsk, the mining engineer's household and a mother's early death

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky was born on 7 May 1840 in Votkinsk, an ironworks settlement in the Vyatka province east of Moscow, where his father, Ilya Petrovich Tchaikovsky, managed the Kamsko-Votkinsk works. The posting was responsible, salaried and remote. The household was large, comfortable by provincial standards and musical in the unselfconscious way of its class: there was a piano, there were songs in the evening, and there was an orchestrion, a mechanical organ whose performances of Mozart and Italian opera excerpts the boy absorbed before he had any formal teaching. His mother, Alexandra Andreyevna, came from a family of French descent, played the piano and sang, and was the emotional centre of the household for all of her children. Between 1844 and 1848 the children were taught by a governess, Fanny Dürbach, whose influence Tchaikovsky acknowledged for the rest of his life; he kept her letters, corresponded with her in old age and credited her with the order and tenderness of his earliest education.

The family left Votkinsk as his father's postings changed, and in 1850 the boy was taken to St Petersburg and prepared for the School of Jurisprudence, the institution that would shape his next nine years. The separation from his mother marked him permanently; his letters across forty years return to it. In June 1854, when he was fourteen, Alexandra Andreyevna died of cholera during an epidemic. David Brown, the documentary biographer whose four-volume study of the life and works remains the fullest account in English, treats that bereavement as the first fixed point of the composer's emotional biography, and the composer's own correspondence supports the weight Brown gives it: the grief was neither brief nor decorative, and references to his mother recur in his writing at moments of crisis for decades afterwards.

Nothing in this childhood pointed at a musical profession, because the profession was unavailable. Russia in the 1840s had opera houses staffed largely by imported Italians and Germans, court orchestras, serf musicians on great estates, church choirs and a brilliant salon culture, but it had no conservatory, no system of graded instruction, no examining body and no career ladder by which a Russian boy of respectable family became a professional composer. Music for a family like the Tchaikovskys was an accomplishment, like drawing or French, not a trade. The boy's evident sensitivity to music, his improvising at the piano and the intensity of his responses to it, were therefore registered by the adults around him as temperament rather than as vocation. The state had already chosen his vocation for him, and it wore a uniform and carried a brief of statutes.

The household around the boy was wider than the parents who dominate the standard telling. His elder brother Nikolai was followed by Pyotr himself, then by a sister, Alexandra, born in 1842, who became the family's emotional centre in adulthood and the keeper of the estate at Kamenka where the composer later did much of his work; then a brother, Ippolit, who made a naval career; and finally, in 1850, the twins Anatoly and Modest, whose roles in this biography are large enough to have their own section. Piano lessons began early, with a local teacher, Maria Palchikova, and the boy's progress was quick enough to be noticed, though in a mining town in the 1840s there was no one qualified to say what such progress meant. Fanny Dürbach, arriving in 1844, gave the household its order and its languages, and the letters the grown composer wrote her nearly half a century later, full of gratitude and exact recollection, are among the most attractive documents of his childhood. What Votkinsk gave him, in sum, was security, music as domestic pleasure, and no path whatsoever from the second to a profession.

## The School of Jurisprudence and what it was for

The Imperial School of Jurisprudence in St Petersburg existed to produce administrators for the Russian state. Founded under the patronage of Prince Pyotr Oldenburgsky, it took boys of good family, many of them intended for the civil service from childhood, and gave them a legal education of genuine rigour combined with the polish expected of men who would staff ministries and courts. Tchaikovsky entered in 1850, after a preparatory period spent largely apart from his family, and graduated in 1859. He was remembered by schoolfellows as sociable, charming, quick and musically gifted, the boy who played for dances and entertained the dormitory, and also as a student of perfectly respectable attainment who gave no sign of the eminence that would follow. One of those schoolfellows, the poet Aleksey Apukhtin, became a lifelong friend and one of the channels through which the composer's private world is documented.

### Was Tchaikovsky trained as a lawyer?

Yes, fully and professionally. He spent nine years at the Imperial School of Jurisprudence in St Petersburg, which trained civil servants for the Russian state, graduated in 1859 and was appointed to the Ministry of Justice, where he worked as a clerk for four years. His legal training was a complete professional qualification, earned and used.

The school matters to the biography for three reasons beyond the career it assigned him. First, it gave him the habits of a document-minded man: he kept papers, dated letters, preserved drafts and understood, as few musicians of the century did, that a written record outlives a reputation. Second, it placed him inside the class that governed Russia, so that when he later negotiated with publishers, ministers and grand dukes he did so as an insider speaking the state's own language rather than as a bohemian petitioning from outside. Third, and most consequential for the end of his life, the school created a cohort. The Jurisprudence graduates of his era formed a tight, honour-bound network scattered through the administration, and one of the disputed accounts of his death, examined in sections twenty-nine and thirty, depends entirely on what that cohort is alleged to have done half a century later. The school is therefore not background colour. It is the first institution of his life, and this biography returns to it twice: when he abandons its career in his early twenties, and when its name is invoked over his coffin.

The school's own musical life deserves a sentence, because it kept the boy's gift exercised while the institution aimed him elsewhere. He sang in the school choir, played for his fellows, and was taken, as the better Petersburg schoolboys were, to the opera, where the Italian company then reigning in the capital made the impression his later letters never stop describing. His teachers remembered diligence rather than brilliance in the law subjects, and his contemporaries remembered charm, mimicry and music. The combination is the man in embryo: professionally adequate at the work the state assigned, privately consumed by the work it had not. It is also worth recording what the school cost the family and bought the state, since the biography's later sections turn on obligations of exactly this kind. The School of Jurisprudence was not a prison from which a genius escaped. It was an honourable machine that did what it promised, and its graduate's resignation four years after leaving it was a considered breach of a fair contract, which is how he himself, in his letters, appears to have understood it.

## A clerk in the Ministry of Justice

On graduating in 1859, Tchaikovsky was appointed to the Ministry of Justice in St Petersburg and began the life of a junior civil servant. The work was clerical: processing files, drafting documents, serving in the ministry's departments in the slow order of the imperial table of ranks. He rose modestly during four years of service, earning promotion to titular counsellor, and by every outward sign he was becoming what the School of Jurisprudence had designed him to become. His salary was small, his prospects ordinary and his diversions those of a young man of his circle: the opera, the Italian company in particular, evenings with friends, amateur music-making. What distinguished him from his fellow clerks was not rebellion or failure. He performed his duties adequately and was liked. It was the steadily growing share of his attention that music claimed outside office hours.

The young clerk sang in a choir, attended the Italian opera with a devotion his letters describe in almost physical terms, and in the autumn of 1861 took the decisive preliminary step: he enrolled in the music theory classes that Nikolai Zaremba taught under the auspices of the Russian Musical Society. This was not yet a career decision. The classes were evening study, compatible with the ministry desk, and Russia was full of cultivated amateurs who studied harmony the way others studied languages. But the surviving evidence of his own words from this period shows a man reordering his life around the study. He wrote to his sister Alexandra about his ambitions with an openness he did not permit himself at the ministry, and he began to understand the choice in front of him as exclusive: the state would not keep a clerk whose real working day began when the office closed.

His father, approached with the possibility of a musical career, responded with a tolerance that surprised even the son, remarking in substance that he would not oppose the change though he doubted music would feed him. That paternal forbearance matters, because the resignation that followed was not a flight from tyranny. It was a calculated abandonment of security by a twenty-two-year-old who knew exactly what security was worth, having held it in his hands for four years.

One episode of the clerk years belongs here because it widened the world the resignation would open onto. In the summer of 1861 Tchaikovsky travelled abroad for the first time, accompanying a family friend, the engineer Pisarev, on a business journey through Germany and Belgium to London and Paris, serving as interpreter and companion. He heard the orchestras, walked the opera houses and bought music in the capitals his profession would later require him to conquer, and his letters home from the journey are those of a tourist of genius: delighted, observant and entirely unaware that he was reconnoitring his own future. Within weeks of his return he had enrolled in Zaremba's classes. The sequence in the documents is plain, the ministry, the journey, the classes, the resignation, and it shows a decision assembling itself over two years rather than a lightning strike. Men who change professions at twenty-two after two years of preparation are not romantics fleeing a desk. They are planners changing desks.

## Leaving a secure career at twenty-one

The leaving happened in two stages, and the distinction between them is the first revelation this biography rests on. In the autumn of 1861, aged twenty-one, Tchaikovsky began serious musical study, attending Zaremba's harmony classes while still a serving clerk; that was the decision. In April 1863, aged twenty-two, he resigned from the Ministry of Justice altogether and enrolled as a full-time student; that was the execution. Biographies that compress the two into a single gesture miss the character of the man. He did not leap. He tested the water at twenty-one, discovered he could swim, arranged his affairs, and then, at twenty-two, walked out of a salaried post with a pensionable future into a profession that, for a Russian, had no established existence at all. He trained as a lawyer, worked as a government clerk, and left that career for an institution that had opened its doors the year before he resigned.

The institution was the St Petersburg Conservatory, founded in 1862, and he was among its first students, entering its classes in composition under Zaremba and later under the conservatory's director himself. The financial consequences were immediate and plainly recorded: he lived on slender means, gave private lessons, and depended in part on family tolerance. His father's circumstances had also narrowed after retirement, so the household could not cushion the experiment. What the young man possessed instead of money was the thing the ministry had accidentally given him: professional habits. He worked to schedule, kept accounts of his progress, and treated composition exercises with the seriousness of state papers.

Roland John Wiley, the scholar whose work on Tchaikovsky's stage works and working documents sets the standard for documentary care in this field, has emphasised how unusual this orderly formation was among Russian composers of the generation. Glinka had been a gentleman amateur with long foreign residences. The men of Balakirev's circle, encountered in section fifteen, were largely self-taught or trained in other professions and wore their amateur status as a badge of national authenticity. Tchaikovsky, uniquely, submitted himself to the full academic discipline of counterpoint, fugue and orchestration that the West took for granted, and he did it late, at an age when Western composers were already published. The lateness explains a feature of his whole career: he was always catching up in public, and he knew it, and the anxiety of the late start never entirely left his letters.

The two-stage leaving also explains the tone of the surviving correspondence about it. There is no scene of paternal thunder, because there was no rebellion to thunder at; there is a young official reporting, stage by stage, a study begun, a teacher's encouragement received, a salary weighed against a calling and found wanting. Zaremba's role should be named plainly: it was his judgment, conveyed to the student through 1862, that the talent was professional in calibre, and teachers' judgments of that kind, from that source, were the closest thing the Russia of the time had to a vocational test. When the conservatory opened in the autumn of 1862 and absorbed the Russian Musical Society's classes, the test acquired an institution to certify it. The resignation of April 1863 followed the certification as naturally as a signature follows a reading of the document. He was twenty-two, a month short of twenty-three, and he had spent his twenty-first year making sure.

## A conservatory two years old

The St Petersburg Conservatory opened in the autumn of 1862 under the direction of Anton Rubinstein, the most famous Russian musician alive, a pianist of European reputation who had concluded that his country would never produce composers of European standing until it produced European training. The conservatory operated under the Russian Musical Society, which Rubinstein had founded in 1859 with the patronage of the Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna, whose salon and subsidy underwrote much of the enterprise. Its first home was modest, its first intake mixed in age and preparation, and its ambitions were frankly imitative: to reproduce in Russia the conservatory system of Leipzig and Vienna, with graded classes in theory, composition, piano, strings and winds, and examinations at the end.

Tchaikovsky studied composition first with Zaremba, a theorist of strict German schooling, and then with Rubinstein himself, whose demands were orchestral and practical: students wrote for real instruments, heard their work, and were judged by results. He also studied flute and organ as secondary subjects, a breadth that later fed his unmatched knowledge of what orchestral players could actually do. His graduation exercise, a cantata on Schiller's ode To Joy, was performed in December 1865 and drew from the critic César Cui a published judgment that the graduate had no compositional talent at all, a verdict the young composer kept and remembered. He left the conservatory at the end of 1865 with a silver medal, solid professional technique and no employment, in a country whose entire apparatus of professional music could be listed in a paragraph.

The speed of what happened next belongs to the institutional history as much as to the man. Anton Rubinstein's younger brother Nikolai had established the Moscow branch of the Russian Musical Society and was preparing to open a Moscow conservatory; he needed a professor of theory and composition, and Anton recommended his graduate. In January 1866 Tchaikovsky moved to Moscow to teach in classes that became, that September, the Moscow Conservatory. He was twenty-five years old, a professor in an institution younger than his own studies, in a city where his name meant nothing. The appointment was the first salary of his musical life and the platform of the next twelve years.

Among his fellow students was Herman Laroche, who became his closest critical friend, the dedicatee of early works and the author of some of the shrewdest early assessments of his music; the friendship, formed over student exercises, lasted the whole life and left a memoir that scholars still use. The student body around them was the first of its kind in Russia: a deliberate mixture of the talented poor on scholarships and the respectable on fees, taught by a faculty Rubinstein had recruited from the German-speaking musical world and from the imperial theatres. Student orchestral exercises were performed at the Russian Musical Society's concerts, so the young composer's work was heard in public while he was still being examined on it, an arrangement that explains the speed of his early production. He wrote orchestral music as a student because the institution put an orchestra in the room. When Nikolai Rubinstein came looking for a theory professor for Moscow, he was hiring out of the first graduating cohort the country had ever produced, and he hired its most decorated member within weeks of the examinations.

## Why Russia had no professional music training before this

The absence the Rubinsteins set out to cure had structural causes, and section seven exists because without them Tchaikovsky's career reads as luck rather than as history. This is the second revelation of the biography: professional music education did not exist in Russia before his generation, so he is a product of a system being built around him rather than an inheritor of one. The imperial theatres trained their own singers and dancers in closed schools and imported their conductors, composers and leading singers from Italy, France and Germany. The Orthodox Church maintained choral training for its own purposes. The aristocracy maintained serf orchestras until emancipation in 1861 abolished the institution that had supplied them. What none of these produced was a Russian professional class of composers, and what none of them offered a talented Russian boy was a route.

The deeper cause was social classification. In the imperial order, a professional musician ranked as a servant or a tradesman; a gentleman might compose, as Glinka did, but he did not work as a musician, and the distinction was enforced by money and by shame. The result by the 1850s was a paradox that Russian critics themselves lamented: a country of intense musical appetite, importing its opera, its teachers and its standards wholesale, and arguing in its journals about a national music it had built no schools to create. The Russian Musical Society and the two conservatories were the answer of one generation to that paradox, and they were contested answers: the nationalist circle around Balakirev regarded the conservatory as a German import that would flatten Russian talent into academic routine, a charge Tchaikovsky spent his career both refuting and, in his anxious moments, half believing.

He therefore stands at a hinge. Behind him, Russian composition is the work of gifted amateurs and imported professionals. Ahead of him, within his own lifetime, the conservatories are producing classes of trained Russian musicians annually, and he is their first exhibit: the first conservatory-trained Russian composer to win a European reputation. The institutions he needed did not exist when he was born, were founded while he was a clerk, employed him within four years of his graduation, and were still arguing about their own legitimacy while he taught in one of them. A career built inside institutions being invented is this biography's subject, and every later phase of his life, the teaching, the patronage, the publishing and the tours, runs on machinery his generation had just assembled.

The founding itself was an act of politics as much as pedagogy, and its mechanics explain the conservatory's contested birth. The Russian Musical Society, founded in 1859, began with public concerts in the capital under Rubinstein's direction, the first regular orchestral series in Russia conducted to a professional standard by a Russian institution; the classes followed in 1861, and the conservatory, chartered in 1862, crowned them. Private music teaching had existed in the capitals for generations, supplied largely by foreign musicians resident in Russia, and the imperial Court Chapel trained singers for the liturgy to a high standard. What had never existed was the combination the conservatory embodied: a single public institution, open by examination rather than by servitude or court appointment, that took a student from first harmony lessons to professional certification and put its diploma behind him in the labour market. Tchaikovsky's generation was the first that could be produced by such a machine, and the machine's newness is written all over his career, in its opportunities, in its insecurities and in the polemics that followed him to the grave.

## Anton Rubinstein and what he taught

Anton Rubinstein's place in this life is double, and the biography keeps the two halves distinct. As founder and director he was the institutional author of Tchaikovsky's training, the man whose decision to build a conservatory in 1862 created the door through which the clerk walked. As teacher he was exacting, impatient with dilettantism and generous with the one currency that mattered, practical opportunity: his students composed for orchestra and heard the results, which in the Russia of 1863 was an education obtainable nowhere else in the country. What he taught, beyond counterpoint and form, was a professional ethic: music as a trade with standards, deadlines and paying audiences, to be practised with the same seriousness a lawyer brought to a brief. The pupil absorbed the ethic whole. Tchaikovsky's adult working life, with its contracts, its schedules, its careful cultivation of publishers and its horror of the missed deadline, is Rubinstein's lesson carried into practice.

The personal relationship cooled as the pupil's fame grew. Rubinstein's later judgments on Tchaikovsky's music were mixed and sometimes wounding in print and in conversation, and the younger man's letters register the hurt of a student who never stopped wanting the master's full approval. The biography does not need to adjudicate the master's taste; what matters is the transmission. When Tchaikovsky in turn taught at Moscow, the discipline he imposed was the discipline he had received, and when the brothers Rubinstein appear at the hinge points of this narrative, Anton founding the school that trained him and Nikolai running the school that employed him, the pattern is the same: the institutions preceded the career and made it conceivable.

One further fact about the teaching deserves its place here because later sections depend on it. Rubinstein trained his composition students on the German classical canon and on contemporary German and French practice, not on Russian folk material, which the conservatory treated as raw resource rather than as method. Tchaikovsky's formation was therefore cosmopolitan at its root. The argument about his Russianness, which belongs to [the companion debate article](/2011/08/07/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-legacy-debate/) and is only touched in this biography where it shaped his circumstances, begins in this classroom: he was accused throughout his career of being too Western by the nationalists and too Russian by some Western critics, and both charges trace back to a training that was deliberately European, delivered in a country whose musical institutions were being built to European plans by Russian hands.

Rubinstein's own example stood behind the ethic as its proof. He was himself the first Russian musician to sustain a solo career of the European type, a pianist who had conquered the continent's stages as a child prodigy and an adult virtuoso, and who returned to Russia determined to build at home the professional world he had found abroad. The determination cost him: the conservatory project was attacked from the nationalist side as Germanisation and from the court side as presumption, and he resigned the directorship in 1867, exhausted by the fighting, returning to it only two decades later. His pupil watched all of this from Moscow and learned the second half of the professional lesson, the half no curriculum lists: that institutions in Russia were built by individuals against resistance, maintained by stubbornness and liable to be unbuilt by the same forces. When Tchaikovsky later lent his name and his labour to the Moscow Conservatory's causes, and when he despaired in his letters of Russian musical politics, he was spending the inheritance of Rubinstein's example in both its currencies, the standard and the warning.

## The move to Moscow and the teaching post

Moscow in January 1866 received a professor of twenty-five who arrived with a silver medal, a cantata that a leading critic had dismissed, and almost no money. Nikolai Rubinstein, director of the Moscow classes and from September 1866 of the Moscow Conservatory, installed him as teacher of harmony and theory, at first lodged him in his own household, and for the next twelve years acted as employer, advocate and, on one famous occasion recorded in section eleven, the most devastating critic the composer ever faced. The conservatory salary was modest, initially a few hundred roubles a year supplemented by private pupils, and the teaching load was heavy: Tchaikovsky taught the theory curriculum to classes of students, many of them older in worldly experience if not in years, and he prepared his materials with the clerk's conscience he brought to everything, at one point producing his own harmony textbook, the Guide to the Practical Study of Harmony, published in 1871, because the available Russian texts did not satisfy him.

The Moscow decade made him professionally literate in ways St Petersburg would not have done. Moscow's musical life was smaller, warmer and less court-bound than the capital's; its merchants patronised concerts, its conservatory concerts gave students and professors a public platform, and Nikolai Rubinstein's Russian Musical Society concerts were the arena in which a young Russian composer's orchestral works were tried. Tchaikovsky's First Symphony, Winter Daydreams, was begun in 1866 under the strain of the new teaching load and finished that year in a state of nervous exhaustion that alarmed his family; it was performed, in part and then in full, under Nikolai's direction in 1868. The pattern of the decade was set: teaching through the academic year, composing at speed in the summers, and a slow accumulation of works, performances and critical attention, favourable and unfavourable in roughly equal measure.

He lived frugally and somewhat chaotically, at first in Nikolai's rooms, later in lodgings of his own, and his letters to his family from these years are the record of a man building a professional identity while remaining, in money and in domestic arrangement, a dependent younger brother. His brothers Anatoly and Modest, twins twelve years his junior, appear in these letters as the family members to whom he wrote most freely, a fact that matters later: the correspondence that documents his private life runs disproportionately through the brothers, and one of those brothers would become his first biographer, with everything that implies for the record examined in sections thirty-one to thirty-three.

His Moscow circle supplied the witnesses on whom this part of the biography rests. Nikolai Kashkin, a fellow professor, left memoirs of the young teacher that scholars treat as among the most reliable in the whole literature: the lodgings, the lessons, the evenings of music and talk, the professor's generosity to students and his chronic shortage of money. Through Kashkin and others we see the Moscow Tchaikovsky whole, a sociable, funny, hardworking young professional, not the invalid of later legend, building a reputation in a city that judged musicians by what their classes and their concerts contained. The First Symphony's strained gestation belongs to this world: composed in the summer of 1866 after a teaching term that had left him sleepless and overwrought, it was written by a man whose family feared the new profession would break his health within a year of his choosing it. The fear recurs in the letters at every deadline for the rest of his life, and the productivity recurs with it, and the biography simply notes that both were permanent.

## Life as a conservatory teacher and critic

The teaching years were not a prelude to the career; they were the career's foundation, and they consumed him. Tchaikovsky taught at the Moscow Conservatory from 1866 into 1878, carrying classes in harmony, theory and composition, examining, and performing the administrative labour of a young institution. His letters complain about the burden with regularity and precision: the teaching ate the mornings, the preparation ate the evenings, and composition was squeezed into vacations at Kamenka, his sister Alexandra's Ukrainian estate, which became his summer workshop. Yet the same letters show what the classroom gave him. A professor who must explain harmony daily for twelve years acquires a command of the mechanics of music that no amount of private study confers, and the fluency of his mature orchestration, whatever else explains it, was laid down in those years of compelled clarity about how chords behave.

To supplement the salary he wrote music criticism, principally in the early 1870s, for Moscow newspapers, reporting on concerts and opera with a sharpness that made enemies and a fairness that made his judgments worth reading. The criticism is a biographical document in its own right: it shows the young professor measuring Moscow's musical provision against European standards, praising Wagner's operas as music while resisting the cult around them, championing Schumann, and insisting, against the nationalist party, that Russian music would be built by trained craftsmen or not at all. He abandoned journalism as composition began to pay, but the habit of public judgment stayed with him, and his later correspondence with von Meck is full of assessments of other men's music delivered with a critic's freedom.

The decade's balance sheet, as he approached his late thirties, was mixed in a way his letters do not disguise. He had a European-grade technique, a professorship, a growing list of performed works and a small reputation beyond Moscow. He also had exhaustion, recurrent nervous strain, a salary that barely supported him, and the sense, recorded repeatedly, that the teaching life was consuming the composing life it was supposed to serve. What he lacked was time, and time in his world could only be bought. The buyer appeared at the end of 1876, in the form this biography has already introduced, and sections twelve to fourteen are given to her.

The classroom also made him a working translator and editor of other men's systems, which belongs to the biography because it documents how completely the new profession absorbed him. He translated François-Auguste Gevaert's treatise on instrumentation into Russian for his students' use, workmanlike labour that put the standard European text on orchestral practice into his pupils' hands and into his own fingers at the same time. Among those pupils, in the following decade, was Sergei Taneyev, admitted to the conservatory as a child and formed in Tchaikovsky's theory classes, who became the composer's most trusted musical confidant, the first performer of several of the piano works and the correspondent whose letters the composer answered with a technical candour he granted no one else. A teacher is known by such products. The Moscow classroom's harvest includes the Russian school's next master of counterpoint, trained by the clerk who had resigned to learn the subject at twenty-two.

## The first symphonies and the early failures

The works of the Moscow decade belong to the companion works article, but their public fortunes are biography, because they determined his circumstances. The First Symphony, completed in 1866 at real cost to his health, was received respectfully and has kept its place. The early overture The Storm and the opera The Voyevoda fared worse; The Voyevoda failed, and Tchaikovsky later destroyed its score, an act of self-judgment characteristic of a man who kept almost everything else. The opera The Oprichnik reached the stage in 1874 and won a prize from the Russian Musical Society while drawing hostile notices; its composer judged it more coldly than its judges did. The pattern of these years is not failure but unevenness under scrutiny, a young professional learning his trade in public because Russia had no other place to learn it.

The emblematic episode came at Christmas 1874, when he played his newly finished First Piano Concerto through to Nikolai Rubinstein, whose advocacy he needed, and received a verdict of total condemnation delivered, by the composer's account in a later letter, without a single softening word. The concerto was worthless, unplayable, vulgar, its themes stolen. Tchaikovsky refused to alter a note of the dedication structure the judgment demanded, dedicated the work instead to the pianist Hans von Bülow, and Bülow premiered it, triumphantly, in Boston in October 1875. The episode matters here for two reasons. It shows the young professor already capable of defying the most powerful musician in his daily world when his judgment was engaged; and it shows his reputation beginning to travel by a route no Russian composer had used before, through foreign virtuosi and foreign premieres. Nikolai, to his credit, later learned the concerto, performed it superbly and became its champion, and the friendship survived. But the hierarchy of the Moscow years had quietly changed: the teacher now had a pupil whose music was being premiered on another continent.

The decade's later works sharpen the pattern. The Second Symphony of 1872, built partly on Ukrainian folk material gathered at Kamenka, was the first of his orchestral works to win an unambiguous public success in Moscow, and its reception taught him the audience existed. The fantasy overture The Tempest followed in 1873, on a programme suggested by the critic Stasov, and in 1875 came the commission that placed him inside the imperial theatre system: the Bolshoi commissioned a full-length ballet, Swan Lake, for a fee of eight hundred roubles, the first time a Russian theatre had commissioned a ballet score from a symphonist of the new school rather than from a house specialist. The works themselves are weighed in the catalogue article. Their biographical meaning is the slope of the curve: by the middle 1870s the professor's name, not merely his classroom, was an asset that theatres competed for, and the resignation of the following years was a resignation from strength, not from failure.

## The von Meck allowance and the rule of distance

Nadezhda Filaretovna von Meck was born in 1831, nine years before Tchaikovsky, into a musical family, married at seventeen to Karl von Meck, an engineer who built railways and made one of the great Russian fortunes of the century, and was left a widow in 1876 with eleven children, vast estates and a household run as a small court. Music was the centre of her private life: she maintained musicians in her employ, followed new work with a connoisseur's attention, and spent money on it with the deliberation of a minister on a portfolio. She had heard Tchaikovsky's music, admired it intensely, and in December 1876 made her first approach, commissioning through an intermediary some small compositions and sending payment well above the fee. His letter of thanks began a correspondence. Within months she had settled the arrangement that this biography opened with: an allowance of six thousand roubles a year, paid in monthly instalments, with no duties attached except the correspondence itself, and with the express condition, proposed by her and embraced by him, that they never meet.

### Who was Nadezhda von Meck?

Nadezhda von Meck, born in 1831 and widowed in 1876, managed a railway fortune, raised eleven children and kept musicians in her household as a serious, informed lover of music. From 1877 she paid Tchaikovsky six thousand roubles a year and corresponded with him for fourteen years on the condition that they never met.

Here the shareable fact of this biography is stated plainly, because everything else in these sections is commentary on it. Tchaikovsky and Nadezhda von Meck exchanged more than a thousand letters across fourteen years and deliberately arranged never to meet or speak, a condition both of them preferred. She was, in the language of the letters, his best friend, and the friendship existed only in writing: in fourteen years of intimacy that included money, confession, artistic counsel and family news, the two people at its centre never exchanged a word aloud. This is the third revelation of the biography: the allowance funded fourteen years of composition, and both parties maintained the distance deliberately rather than by accident. It was not shyness that circumstances imposed. It was architecture, designed by her, gratefully inhabited by him, and load-bearing: remove the distance, as section fourteen shows the ending proved, and the structure could not stand.

The mechanics deserve precision, since later claims about the relationship are built on vagueness. The six thousand roubles a year was roughly three times a senior professor's salary; it freed him, from 1877, from any need to teach for money, and in 1878 he resigned his conservatory post. The payments were regular and businesslike, administered like any other household disbursement. The condition of non-meeting was managed with comic rigour when geography threatened it: she lent him her Moscow house and her estate at Brailov for his holidays, always absenting herself first, and on the rare occasions when both were in the same city or the same Italian town, their movements were coordinated through servants and letters so that the rule held. They came close enough, twice, to see one another at a distance, at Florence and in the country near her estate; on neither occasion was there acknowledgment, speech or any breach of the agreement. The rule, in short, was kept the way contracts are kept, by people who wanted it kept.

Her own musical household shows the seriousness behind the patronage. She maintained string players in residence at her Moscow house and at Brailov, engaged visiting virtuosi for private performances, and ordered her domestic music with the same rigour as her estates; the young Claude Debussy spent the summers of 1880 to 1882 in her service as household pianist and travelling companion, playing trios with her resident musicians and accompanying the family through Russia and Europe, an episode that belongs to French music's biography as much as to hers. She was, in short, a professional-grade amateur of the rarest kind, a listener with standards, money and a horror of the social forms through which patronage was usually transacted. The rule of distance was not an eccentricity pasted onto the arrangement. It was the house rule of her whole musical life, applied to its largest investment.

## What the correspondence was actually for

Now the question opened in section one can be answered. Why would a patron and a composer refuse to be in the same room? Because the correspondence was not a substitute for a relationship they were prevented from having; it was the relationship, chosen in the form that let it carry the most weight. For von Meck, writing solved the problem of patronage itself. She despised the social theatre of the salon, disliked receiving and being received, and had concluded, before she ever wrote to him, that personal acquaintance corrupts the purity of an artistic devotion: to meet the man was to risk the music. Her letters say, in substance and repeatedly, that distance preserved what proximity would diminish, that she could love the composer in his work only so long as the fallible person stayed out of the room. For Tchaikovsky, the arrangement solved the opposite problem. He was, by his own repeated testimony, wretched in sustained society, terrified of obligation performed face to face, and capable of complete candour only on paper. The letters let him confess, complain, analyse his own music and report his despair to a reader who never had to be entertained, charmed or faced across a table the next morning.

The correspondence was therefore for several concrete things at once, and the biography lists them because later mythology prefers a single swooning explanation. It was a critical seminar: she questioned him about his works in progress, received his explanations of the Fourth Symphony's programme, debated repertoire and performers, and served as the first intelligent audience for music the public had not heard. It was a news service: she reported Moscow musical politics, he reported his travels, his reading, his health and the progress of each score in a running log that no other document matches. It was a bank with a confessional attached: the allowance arrived monthly, and the letters were its human counterpart, though he is careful in them, and she is careful in hers, never to let the money be named as the price of the intimacy. And it was, on his side especially, a confessional in the strict sense, a place where the facts of his nature and his unhappiness could be set down for the one reader who was guaranteed, by the founding rule, never to use them in a room.

Roland John Wiley's documentary work on the correspondence underlines what the bulk makes plain: the letters are not the overflow of the arrangement but its substance, running at their height to several exchanges a week, covering everything from the analysis of a symphony to the price of lodgings. When the arrangement ended, it was the letters he begged to continue, not the money he begged to restore. That ordering tells the truth about what the correspondence was for.

The correspondence's finest documentary hour came early, in February 1878, when she asked him what the Fourth Symphony, which he had dedicated to her under the words to my best friend, was about, and he answered with the fullest programme he ever gave for any work: the opening fanfare as fate, the movement's struggle, the consolations and relapses of the whole design, laid out in a letter of several pages. The exchange shows the arrangement working at its designed capacity. A patron intelligent enough to ask the question properly received an answer no public programme note of the century matches, and the answer survives because the relationship was conducted in writing by two people who kept what they wrote. Multiply that exchange by fourteen years of premieres, crises, journeys and scores, and the correspondence's bulk explains itself: it is the workshop diary of the career, kept jointly with the one reader entitled to ask anything except to meet him.

## Why the arrangement eventually ended

In October 1890, after fourteen years, von Meck wrote that the allowance must stop. Her letter pleaded financial ruin: the family estates, she said, were in disorder, her affairs in the hands of others, and she could no longer sustain the payments. She asked him to remember her, used the language of permanent farewell, and added a sentence that read to him, and reads to any reader, as a closing of the account in every sense. He replied at once, warmly, saying in substance that the money was the least of what she had given him and asking that the correspondence, at least, continue. She never answered. A second letter from him, written when he heard she was ill, also met silence. She lived a little more than three years longer, dying in January 1894, some two months after him, and no explanation from her hand survives beyond the letter itself.

The biographical record requires the ending to be stated with both of its faces visible. Her family's finances were genuinely strained in those years: the railway fortune had been mismanaged by sons-in-law, debts were real, and economies were being imposed across her households, so the letter's central claim was not a fiction. At the same time, six thousand roubles a year was not the item on which that fortune stood or fell, she was ill and withdrawing from the world in ways her family described, and she had ended other intense attachments in the same epistolary way, by letter and by silence. David Brown, weighing the evidence in his biography, treats the financial explanation as true but insufficient, and that judgment, true but insufficient, is where this biography also leaves the mechanics: the money excuse was available, real and not the whole story.

What is documented without ambiguity is the effect. Tchaikovsky never reproached her in any surviving letter, continued to speak of her with gratitude, kept her portrait, and described the loss to his brothers as the loss of the central relationship of his life. The fourteen years had made the correspondence load-bearing in his emotional architecture exactly as it was in his finances, and the ending demonstrated the founding rule's final consequence: a relationship conducted entirely in writing can be ended entirely in writing, by one party, without appeal, in a single letter.

The financial face of the ending deserves one paragraph of its own, because it separates the documented wound from the imagined one. By October 1890 Tchaikovsky was not dependent on the allowance as he had been in 1877. He had the imperial pension of three thousand roubles a year granted in 1885, a publisher paying steadily for a catalogue in demand across Europe, and conducting fees from three countries. The loss of six thousand roubles a year required economies, and his letters arrange them with a clerk's calm. What the letters cannot arrange is the silence. He told his brothers that the relationship had been the centre of his existence for fourteen years and that its ending, without explanation he could answer, left him feeling the ground of his life altered. He never spoke against her, then or later. He inquired after her health through intermediaries during her final illness, which overlapped his own last year, and the record closes with the two correspondents dying within some two months of each other, the rule of distance kept, in the end, by death itself.

## The circle around Balakirev

Parallel to the conservatory world that employed him ran the other pole of Russian musical life, the circle around Mily Balakirev in St Petersburg: César Cui, Modest Mussorgsky, Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov and Alexander Borodin, the group a critic christened the Mighty Handful. Their programme was national and anti-academic. Russian music, they held, would be created not by German-trained professors but by Russians working from Russian song, Russian history and Russian instinct, and Balakirev, the only one of them with a commanding technique, acted as mentor, editor and, his beneficiaries often felt, dictator. Tchaikovsky's relations with the circle were the most complicated of his professional life, because he agreed with their ends and rejected their means, and because Balakirev, alone among them, took his talent entirely seriously from the first.

The contact was substantive. Balakirev proposed the subject of Romeo and Juliet, criticised the first version bar by bar in letters of extraordinary frankness, and received the dedication of the revised overture in 1870; the process is preserved in a correspondence that shows the nationalist mentor functioning, in effect, as the conservatory man's second teacher, this time in dramatic instinct. Tchaikovsky also drew from the circle its subjects and its licence: the symphonic poem, the Shakespearean overture, the freedom to build large forms on programmes rather than on textbook plans. In return the circle never quite claimed him. Cui's reviews of his work were frequently hostile, the group's theorist Vladimir Stasov kept him at the edge of the national pantheon, and his professorship marked him in their eyes as the academy's man. His mature position, visible across his criticism and correspondence, was independence purchased at the price of belonging nowhere: he took Balakirev's subjects, Rubinstein's craft and his own judgment, and declined both parties' orthodoxies.

The relationship cooled through the 1870s as Balakirev withdrew into religious crisis and silence, and it never resumed its early intensity, though the two men met again in later years with restored civility when Balakirev re-emerged to conduct. For the biography the circle's importance is that it formed the other half of the professional air Tchaikovsky breathed: the conservatory gave him technique and salary, the kuchka gave him subjects and opposition, and the friction between the two educations is audible, as the [study of Tchaikovsky's musical style](/2011/06/21/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-musical-style-explained/) shows, in everything he wrote.

The relationship's footprint reaches further than the correspondence itself. Balakirev's part in his formation resurfaced a decade and a half later, when the subject of Byron's Manfred, which Balakirev had pressed on Berlioz in an earlier generation, was passed to Tchaikovsky through Stasov in 1882, and the Manfred Symphony of 1885, the largest orchestral work of his middle years, was composed to Balakirev's programme by the conservatory professor the nationalists had never claimed. The episode is the circle's long tail made visible: its subjects outlived its politics, and its best pupil, in the end, was the man it had declined to adopt. His independence had a price, recorded in the coolness of the Petersburg critics through the 1870s, and a dividend, recorded in everything after: he owed no school his method and no party his subjects, and when Europe received him in the 1880s it received a composer whom no Russian faction could plausibly claim to have manufactured.

## The marriage of 1877

In the spring of 1877 Tchaikovsky received a letter from a young woman named Antonina Ivanovna Milyukova, a former student at the Moscow Conservatory, declaring her love for him. She wrote again. She threatened, in the idiom of such letters, that she could not live without him. He was thirty-seven, established enough to feel the weight of his position, and preoccupied at that moment with the composition of Eugene Onegin, Pushkin's story of a man who coolly rejects a young woman's written declaration of love and lives to regret it. In May he proposed marriage, was accepted, and on 6 July 1877, Old Style, the two were married in Moscow. The marriage lasted, as a shared household, a matter of weeks. By the autumn he had fled to his sister's estate and then abroad, the marriage was over in everything but law, and it was never dissolved. Antonina outlived him by twenty-four years and died in 1917, in psychiatric care, still legally his widow.

### Why did Tchaikovsky marry?

Tchaikovsky married in July 1877 for reasons his letters state frankly: he wished to end the gossip surrounding a bachelor professor, he feared refusing a woman who had declared her love in writing, and, absorbed in setting Eugene Onegin, he dreaded playing Pushkin's cold rejecter in his own life. He believed affection could follow respect.

Why would a man marry a woman he barely knew, in a matter of weeks, against the instincts his whole private life contradicted? The question opens here, in the section of the wedding, and it closes in the next section, where the correspondence, his own and his family's, supplies the answer in his words rather than in the narrator's: the marriage was a defensive construction, built against gossip, against blackmail's shadow, against the Onegin parallel and against his own nature, by a man who had reasoned himself into believing that the will could do what only feeling can. The next section gives that reasoning its full documentary weight, because the marriage is the episode of this life most often told as either farce or tragedy, and it was neither. It was a decision, taken for stated reasons, by a man who wrote those reasons down.

Antonina's side of the preliminaries is thinner in the record, and the biography marks the thinness rather than filling it. She had attended the Moscow Conservatory as a piano student, had heard the professor lecture and perform, and had fixed on him, by her own later account, with the complete devotion of a young woman for whom the composer embodied the musical world she had entered and not mastered. Her letters to him, which survive in part, are extravagant, repetitive and, read without cruelty, touching: she offered the one asset she believed she had, a worshipping domesticity, to a man whose letters in reply, courteous and fatally encouraged, she reasonably took for acceptance of the offer's terms. His announcement to his family was uneasy from the first letter; he wrote to Anatoly of the engagement with a defensiveness that suggests he was already arguing with his own judgment. The wedding went forward on the momentum of the proposal, as weddings do, and the couple spent a brief Moscow honeymoon before the household whose collapse the next section records was even established.

## Why the marriage was contracted

The documentary record of the decision survives in his letters to his brothers and to von Meck, and it is unusually complete, because he explained himself at length before, during and after the catastrophe. Four strands run through the explanations, and the biography keeps them separate because biographers have too often chosen one and suppressed the rest. The first is social defence. A bachelor of thirty-seven, a public professor with pupils of both sexes and a private life that colleagues gossiped about, could calculate the protection a wife provided: marriage answered questions, ended a certain kind of speculation and placed him inside the respectable order his class expected. His letters say explicitly that he wished to stop mouths and to secure his reputation against the day when rumour might become accusation.

The second strand is the woman's letters themselves. Antonina's declaration arrived in writing, insistent and repeated, with an ultimatum's pressure behind it, and the correspondence leaves no doubt that he feared the consequences of refusal, for her and for himself, in a city where such a rejection, publicised, could ruin them both. The third strand is the opera he was composing. He was at that spring setting the scene in which Tatyana writes her letter to Onegin and is refused with a lecture. His letters to his brother state the parallel himself: he could not bring himself to do to a real woman what Onegin does to Tatyana. Art had supplied the script his conscience then obeyed. The fourth strand is the most poignant in the documents: he persuaded himself that love of the kind he could offer, respect, gratitude, companionship, calm, might be enough, and that passion of the kind he could not feel for a woman might simply not be necessary. He wrote that he was not in love with her, and proposed anyway, on the theory that the rest could be constructed.

Here the fourth revelation of the biography lands. The marriage lasted a matter of weeks in practice, and the correspondence documents both why it was contracted and why it failed, in the same hand and almost in the same breath. No later interpreter is needed to explain it, and none should be trusted who simplifies it: the record shows a man who married to silence gossip, to escape a fictional parallel, to answer a written ultimatum and to test a theory of constructed affection, and who discovered within weeks that each reason had been an evasion of the one fact he had not written down plainly, that he could not live as a husband at all.

The Onegin strand of the reasoning can be dated with unusual precision, and the precision is worth having because it converts a literary anecdote into a documented mechanism. He began composing Eugene Onegin in May 1877, in the weeks of the engagement, and the first music he wrote for it was Tatyana's letter scene, the heroine's written declaration and its aftermath. His correspondence of those exact weeks moves between the opera and the marriage in a single breath: the fear of behaving as Onegin behaves is not a critic's inference drawn later but the composer's own stated motive, written while the ink of both decisions was wet. A man may be wrong about his own motives, and biographers are paid to doubt them; but when a motive is stated in advance, in writing, by a subject whose letters elsewhere prove ruthlessly honest about himself, the doubt has to argue uphill. The marriage was contracted, in documented part, because a composer could not bear to conduct his own life in the rhythm of the scene he was setting.

## How the marriage collapsed

The collapse was immediate and total. Within days of the wedding his letters to his brothers change register from resolution to despair. The household the couple established in Moscow contained, by his account, a woman whose conversation, musical understanding and habits he found unendurable, and whose physical presence as a wife produced in him a revulsion he described to Anatoly with a candour that is painful to read. Antonina's own later account, given in interviews and statements after his death, presents the marriage from the other side: she had idolised the composer from the conservatory, understood the wedding as her life's fulfilment, and experienced his coldness and flight as bewildering cruelty from a man who had sought her hand weeks before. The biography reports both sides because the brief of this life requires it and because the documents support both: his letters prove his misery, and her testimony proves hers, and the two miseries are not alternatives between which a reader must choose. They are the same event.

By September 1877 he was in a state his family recognised as dangerous. Modest's biography records that in that month Tchaikovsky made an attempt on his own life; the episode is part of the family record, is treated by the modern documentary biographers as consistent with his letters of the period, and is given here without detail, as the subject requires. What is certain from his own hand is the flight that followed: he left Moscow, spent time at Kamenka under his sister's care, was advised by doctors to live apart from his wife permanently, and travelled to Switzerland, settling at Clarens on Lake Geneva to recover and to work. The brothers negotiated the separation. Antonina refused a divorce, as was largely her legal right within the church's jurisdiction, and the marriage subsisted on paper for the remaining sixteen years of his life, a fact she asserted, with the standing of a widow, long after his death.

The professional consequence was paradoxical and should be stated plainly. The catastrophe of 1877, which his letters feared would end him, coincided with the beginning of the allowance, the resignation from teaching and the freest decade of his working life. The Fourth Symphony, the opera he had interrupted, and the works that made his European name all follow the collapse. The biography draws no moral from the sequence. It records that the marriage destroyed a household and liberated a career, and that both halves of that sentence are documented.

Antonina's afterlife completes the section's obligation to report both sides. The separation was administered by Anatoly, who negotiated with her family and arranged a settlement under which she received support; Tchaikovsky paid her an allowance for years afterward, a fact his account books and correspondence record without rancour. She stayed in Moscow, lived in narrowing circumstances, formed in later years another attachment outside marriage, and had children whom poverty placed in foundling care, a history recovered by Poznansky's research and recorded here once, without embellishment, because it is the documented cost of the story on its other side. In old age, interviewed as the composer's widow, she spoke of him with a pride that had outlasted everything else. She ended her life in a Moscow asylum in 1917. The law that would not free either of them freed neither: the marriage's paper existence, from 1877 to 1893 and in her widowhood beyond, is the longest single document of the catastrophe.

## His sexuality and the documentary record

This section states, as the stance charter of this series requires, what the documentary record establishes about Tchaikovsky's private life, at the confidence the evidence supports. Tchaikovsky was homosexual. The fact is documented in his own letters and diaries, written across decades to correspondents he trusted, and it is stated here as biographical fact, not as inference, rumour or scandal. His letters to his brothers Anatoly and Modest, and to his nephew Vladimir Davydov, discuss his attractions, his relationships and his struggles with his nature in terms that leave no serious room for doubt, and the passages in which he does so are exactly the passages that the editors of his correspondence, from his brother onward, cut or softened for publication. The erasure is part of the evidence, and sections thirty-one to thirty-three take it up.

The record supports a precise picture rather than a vague one. His earliest surviving letters on the subject, from the 1870s, show a young man negotiating between his nature and the conventions of his class, fearing exposure, considering marriage as camouflage, which the previous sections have shown him attempting, and gradually arriving at an accommodation he described in later letters with something like rueful stability. He had relationships, some sustained, conducted under the discretion his position required. He wrote about the temptations and the guilt in the same letters that discuss his scores and his digestion, which is to say he treated his nature as a fact of his constitution, managed rather than hidden from himself. The legal context was severe: Russian law criminalised the acts, professional ruin would have followed a public scandal, and the gossip he feared in 1877 was therefore not vanity but an accurate assessment of his exposure.

Alexander Poznansky, the archivist and biographer who worked directly from the uncensored documents in Russian archives and whose 1991 biography restored this material to the centre of the record, established the documentary basis on which all later discussion rests. Poznansky's characterisation of the evidence is the one this biography adopts: the letters are explicit, the diaries are explicit, the testimony of the circle agrees, and the question is not whether the record exists but why it was suppressed for so long, and by whom. The suppression matters to the death dispute of sections twenty-nine and thirty, because the later claim that he died to avert a scandal about his private life presupposes a world in which such a scandal could destroy him, and the claim's plausibility, as rhetoric, is built from the same fear that drove the marriage. The biography insists on the separation: that the fear was real and documented does not make the death claim documented, and the evidence for each must be weighed by its own standard.

Two anchors keep this section from abstraction. The first is his nephew. Vladimir Davydov, his sister Alexandra's son, called Bob in the family, was the person to whom the mature composer's warmest letters were written, and the Sixth Symphony carries his dedication; the correspondence with Bob, read whole, is both a family record and the documentary setting in which the last work's intentions were stated to the person they most concerned. The second anchor is his brother Modest, who shared his brother's nature and understood it from inside, and whose later custody of the record, examined in section thirty-one, was therefore the custody of a man editing his own exposure as well as his brother's. Between these two anchors the letters' testimony is consistent across forty years: the nature was constant, the management of it changed, from the fear of the 1870s to the guarded equilibrium of the final decade, and the written record of that management is the most complete left by any nineteenth-century composer.

## What his letters and diaries contain

The letters survive in their thousands, and they are the spine of every modern biography, which is why their editorial history is a biographical fact and not a bibliographical footnote. He wrote constantly: to his brothers, to his sister, to von Meck, to his publisher Pyotr Jurgenson, to conductors, pupils, patrons and friends, in a prose that is funny, exact, self-lacerating and professionally meticulous by turns. The letters report the composition of the works movement by movement, quote his fees and his debts, describe his reading, his digestion, his terror of conducting and his delight in the countryside, and record, in the letters never intended for publication, the private life discussed in the previous section. The diaries, kept intermittently, are terser and stranger documents: brief daily entries, accounts of walks and weather and work, interleaved with passages of self-examination in which he argues with his own character and resolves, in writing, to amend it.

The difficulty is that for most of the twentieth century readers could not read what he wrote. The first collected editions of the letters, prepared under Modest's supervision and continued by later editors, cut the correspondence in three directions at once: the private life was removed, the sharp judgments on living musicians were softened, and the money and the despair were trimmed toward respectability. The Soviet collected edition of the letters, published across the middle of the century in five volumes and long treated as complete, applied the same scissors with political authority behind them, so that a scholar quoting the standard edition could be quoting a text its author would not have recognised. Where a claim in this biography rests on a letter, the distinction matters: a claim drawn from the uncensored archival text is evidence, and a claim drawn only from a cut edition is evidence of what the editors wished shown, and the two must never be priced the same. That is the custodial rule applied throughout this article, and it is why Modest's biography, examined in section thirty-one, is used here as a subject of the story rather than as a source for its events.

What the uncensored record adds, when it is read whole, is not sensation but proportion. The private life occupies its true, modest share of a correspondence dominated by work; the despair sits alongside jokes, commissions and weather; and the man who emerges is neither the neurasthenic saint of the old biography nor any of the caricatures that replaced him, but a working professional of ferocious productivity managing a complicated constitution in a dangerous legal climate, and writing it all down.

The mechanics of the cutting deserve one paragraph, because later sections rely on readers understanding exactly what a bowdlerised edition does to evidence. The editors worked by excision and softening: a passage about an attachment became a passage about a friendship or vanished; a sum of money lost its figure; a judgment on a living colleague lost its name; despair was trimmed to fatigue. The cuts were not marked. A reader of the standard edition therefore could not know where the text had been altered, which is a worse corruption than open abridgement, since it converts an edited document into a forged one for every practical purpose. Poznansky's published collations, setting edition against manuscript, remain the indispensable demonstration, and they justify the rule this biography has applied throughout: nothing about the private life, the money or the despair is quoted in this article from any edition known to have cut them, where an uncensored text exists to quote instead.

## The travelling decade

From the resignation of his teaching post in 1878 until the middle 1880s, Tchaikovsky's life had no fixed institutional shape, and the biography follows the pattern he built instead. Winters were spent travelling or in temporary lodgings, summers at the places that functioned as his workshops: Kamenka, his sister Alexandra's estate in Ukraine, where the household absorbed him without ceremony; Brailov, von Meck's estate, lent to him in her absence under the founding rule; and later his own rented houses at Simaki and Maidanovo and the house he bought at Klin in his last years. Between these anchors he moved through Italy, France, Switzerland, Austria and Germany in long annual circuits, composing in hotel rooms and borrowed studies with a regularity that makes the map of his travels double as a catalogue of his works: the Italian sojourn that produced the Capriccio Italien, the Florentine weeks behind the Souvenir de Florence, the Swiss and Italian recoveries after 1877, the Paris seasons in which he tested his music against the most sceptical audience in Europe.

The decade's economics underwrote the motion. The von Meck allowance paid in monthly instalments wherever he was; Jurgenson, his publisher from the late 1870s, paid for works and managed his affairs in Moscow with a loyalty that made their correspondence a second archive of the career; and after 1885 an imperial pension, discussed in section twenty-five, added the state's money to the patron's and the publisher's. The travelling was not exile and not tourism. It was the working method of a man who composed best away from every room in which he was expected to be someone, and who had arranged, with his patron's money and his brother's management, to live almost entirely in rooms where he was expected only to write.

The human cost is also in the record. His letters from the road swing between exhilaration at freedom and a homesickness so persistent it became a composition subject in itself; he missed his family, his language and Russian seasons with a physical ache, and then, returned, missed the road. The duality is biographical bedrock, not colour: the professional Russian career he was inventing required Europe, its orchestras, publishers and audiences, and the man who conducted that career was happiest, by his own repeated account, in a Ukrainian village arguing with his sister's children. The works of these years belong to the [catalogue of Tchaikovsky's greatest compositions](/2011/07/07/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-greatest-compositions/); their making, on trains and in borrowed rooms across a continent, belongs here.

His working routine on the road and in his settled summers is documented to the hour, and it explains the output better than any theory of inspiration. The letters describe a regimen he kept for decades: an early walk, work through the morning, correspondence and reading in the afternoon, a long afternoon walk in which the morning's music was turned over, and an evening of reading, cards or family talk. At Kamenka he composed in a separate lodge in the park; at Maidanovo and later at Klin, in a house arranged around the same timetable; in hotels abroad, the timetable travelled with him and the chambermaids of Europe learned it. The regimen's rigidity was the point. A man whose nerves punished irregularity built a machine for regularity, and the machine's product, between 1878 and 1893, was the greater part of the catalogue on which his reputation rests. The travelling decade was not a rest between institutions. It was an institution of one, run to a timetable, and it out-produced every conservatory in Europe.

## Conducting, and the fear of it

Tchaikovsky conducted his own music in public for most of his career, and for most of that career he did it in a state of fear that his letters describe without embarrassment. The fear had a peculiar documented form in his early conducting years: he was haunted, while on the podium, by the conviction that his head might fall from his shoulders, and his early appearances show him holding his chin with his left hand as he beat time with his right, a precaution Anatoly's recollections and his own letters both preserve. Conducting for him was never the natural extension of authorship that it became for the virtuoso composers of the next generation. It was an ordeal undertaken because the profession he was inventing increasingly required the composer to appear in person before foreign orchestras and foreign publics, and because no one else could be trusted to introduce a new Russian score in Leipzig or New York.

His debut as a conductor of his own work in his student years had been unremarkable, and through the Moscow decade Nikolai Rubinstein conducted his premieres while the composer listened. The change came in the later 1880s, when engagements to conduct his own music abroad began to arrive in numbers: the revival of The Cherevichki, which he conducted in Moscow in 1887, served as his re-apprenticeship, and the European tours that followed, beginning with Leipzig in January 1888, established him as a competent, sometimes inspired conductor of his own scores, welcomed by orchestras who found his beat clear and his manner modest. The fear diminished with practice but never vanished; his diaries of the tours record the dread before each concert and the exhausted relief after it with a clerk's fidelity.

For the biography the conducting matters as professional evidence. A composer who conducts his own premieres in Leipzig, Hamburg, Prague, Paris, London and New York is exercising a new kind of career, one in which the work travels with the man and the man is contracted, paid and reviewed as a performer of his own music. Section twenty-four takes up the American culmination of that career; here it is enough to mark that the trembling amateur of the 1860s, holding his chin against an imaginary catastrophe, had by the 1890s conducted on three continents, and that the transformation was willed, documented and never enjoyed.

The tours of 1888 and 1889 supply the itinerary of that transformation, and the biography records it because the engagements, not the applause, are the evidence. In the first months of 1888 he conducted in Leipzig, Hamburg, Berlin and Prague, then Paris and London; in 1889 he returned to Hamburg for the Fifth Symphony and travelled on through Western Europe in a circuit of orchestral societies, opera houses and publishers' offices. His travel diaries, kept with a clerk's fidelity, note the rehearsals' quality, the orchestras' discipline compared with Russian standards, the fees, the dinners and the homesickness in a single unvarying hand, and they show a professional assessing markets as much as a musician collecting triumphs. He conducted other men's music rarely and his own almost always: the tours were the sales journeys of a one-man firm, and he knew it, and the knowledge sits in the diaries beside the dread and the gratitude without any embarrassment. That is what a Western career looked like when a Russian first built one.

## The European reputation

By the late 1880s the travelling method had produced its intended result: Tchaikovsky was, in the practical language of contracts and engagements, a European composer. His works were published by firms in Moscow, Leipzig and Paris; his scores were in the repertories of orchestras that had never employed a Russian; and the tours he undertook from 1887 onward were those of a visiting celebrity rather than of an unknown provincial. Leipzig's Gewandhaus received him in January 1888 with an warmth his diary struggled to believe; Hamburg, Prague, Paris and London followed; Hans von Bülow, who had premiered the First Piano Concerto in Boston in 1875, championed the symphonies in Germany; the Fifth Symphony was introduced under his own baton in Hamburg in 1889 in the presence of a young Gustav Mahler, who later conducted it as a matter of course. In June 1893 Cambridge conferred on him an honorary doctorate in company with Saint-Saëns, Bruch, Boito and Grieg, a list that reads as the official European roll of the profession he had entered from outside it.

Richard Taruskin, the historian whose work on Russian music's Western reception frames much modern discussion, reads reputations like this one as negotiations between two systems of value: the Western institutions that confer standing and the national culture that claims the laureate. Tchaikovsky's European career is the cleanest nineteenth-century case of that negotiation conducted by the composer in person. He did not wait to be discovered, translated and explained by others; he travelled, conducted, dined, corresponded with publishers in three languages and adjusted his professional self to each capital's requirements, while his letters home recorded the cost of the performance with a candour the public record never saw. The reputation was therefore not an accident of the music's quality alone. It was built, tour by tour and contract by contract, by the most professionally literate Russian musician of his century, who happened also to be its most gifted composer. What that reputation then did to the composers who came after him is traced in the study of [Tchaikovsky's influence on music](/2011/07/21/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-influence-on-music/).

The human register of the triumph is worth its sentence. The diaries and letters of the tours are full of a middle-aged man's astonished gratitude, his terror of the podium, his irritation with bores and banquets, and his recurring disbelief that the clerk who resigned in 1863 was being fêted in the Gewandhaus. The biography notes the disbelief because he noted it first, and because it confirms the thesis of this article: the career was unprecedented for a Russian, and its builder knew it.

Prague supplied the tours' most complete vindication and earns its place here. In November 1888 he conducted there the first production of Eugene Onegin outside Russia, before an audience that received the opera, and its composer, with an enthusiasm the Russian capitals had never granted either; his diary's account of that evening is the record of a man discovering that a work written for home could be at home abroad. The Bohemian success mattered beyond its warmth. Onegin was the work his Russian critics had treated as a charming domestic lyric; Prague treated it as a masterpiece, and the discrepancy educated him about the geography of judgment for the rest of his career. He returned to Prague as to a second home, dedicated work there, and spoke of the Czech musicians with a gratitude his letters reserve for very few institutions. Europe's verdict, delivered piecemeal in Leipzig drawing rooms and Prague stalls, had by 1890 assembled itself into a reputation no ministry could have conferred and no Russian faction could withdraw.

## The American visit and Carnegie Hall

In the spring of 1891 Tchaikovsky crossed the Atlantic to conduct at the opening of the new Music Hall in New York, the hall Andrew Carnegie had built and that soon took Carnegie's name. He arrived in April, was received as the festival's principal foreign guest, and conducted his own works across the opening festival's concerts, including the Festival Coronation March at the first concert on 5 May 1891, music written a decade earlier for a tsar's coronation now serving to open a merchant's hall in Manhattan. He also conducted in Baltimore and Philadelphia, was fêted, interviewed and exhausted, kept a diary of the whole expedition, and sailed home within the month having concluded, as the diary records, that America overwhelmed him with its energy, its noise and its kindness in about equal measure.

The visit is the fifth revelation of this biography and the proof of its thesis in a single episode: he had become the first Russian composer with a genuine Western career, conducting in New York at the opening of Carnegie Hall. No Russian composer before him had been engaged on another continent as the representative attraction of a festival; the invitation measured the distance travelled since 1862 more exactly than any review. His diary of the trip, published and therefore quotable in substance, is also the most purely entertaining document of his travels: the skyscrapers, the elevated railways, the terrifying hospitality, the longing for Russian quiet, and beneath it all the professional's recognition that this audience, this hall and this fee structure belonged to the future of his profession.

He never returned to America, and the works that followed the visit, the Nutcracker, Iolanta and the Sixth Symphony, were all made in Russia in the final two years of his life. But the American month stands in the record as the career's furthest reach and its clearest external validation, the point at which the clerk's resignation of 1863 can be measured against its result: the boy from Votkinsk, trained in a conservatory founded the year he joined it, opened the most famous concert hall in the New World.

The diary of the American weeks also preserves the trip's excursion register, which the biography notes because it humanises the professional milestone. He travelled to Niagara Falls and recorded the spectacle with a tourist's dutiful awe and a tired man's brevity; he visited Washington; he endured the American banquet circuit with its speeches in a language he followed imperfectly; and he wrote home about American orchestras with the respect of a man who had rehearsed them. The festival itself was conducted under the organisation of Walter Damrosch, whose New York institutions had engaged him, and the concerts interleaved his works with the standard repertory in the manner of a European festival transplanted. He had crossed the Atlantic only weeks after the death of his sister Alexandra in March of that year, the family's worst loss since 1854, and he sailed for home at the end of May with the triumph and the grief entered in the same hand across the same season. The juxtaposition is the biography's own in miniature: the career's furthest peak, reached by a man travelling in mourning.

## Money, commissions and imperial patronage

The career rested on three income streams, and the biography states them together because their combination was the professional innovation. The first was patronage: the von Meck allowance of six thousand roubles a year from 1877 to 1890, already described. The second was the market: Jurgenson's publishing contracts, performance fees, conducting engagements and commissions, including the 1812 Overture, written in 1880 for the consecration festivities of the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow, and a steady traffic in occasional works whose fees he negotiated with a clerk's eye for the terms. The third was the state: in 1885 Alexander III granted him a pension of three thousand roubles a year for life, an honour that placed the former ministry clerk on the imperial payroll as an ornament of the reign, and the court connection brought commissions and performances at the Imperial Theatres, where the ballets and The Queen of Spades were produced under the director Ivan Vsevolozhsky's enlightened management.

The phase structure of the working life can now be read in one view.

| Phase | Approximate span | Position held | How he was paid | What ended the phase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ministry clerk | 1859 to 1863 | Junior official, Ministry of Justice | State salary | His resignation to study full time |
| Conservatory student | 1862 to 1865 | Student, St Petersburg Conservatory | Family support and small earnings | Graduation and the Moscow appointment |
| Moscow professor | 1866 to 1878 | Professor of theory, Moscow Conservatory | Conservatory salary, pupils and criticism | The von Meck allowance made teaching unnecessary |
| Independent composer | 1877 to 1890 | No post; travelling composer | The von Meck allowance, publishing and commissions | The withdrawal of the allowance in 1890 |
| International conductor-composer | 1887 to 1893 | Guest conductor and house composer to the Imperial Theatres | Conducting fees, publishing, the imperial pension | His death in 1893 |

The overlaps in the table are deliberate and biographical: the allowance overlapped the last teaching year, the conducting career overlapped the independent years, and no phase ended with the clean finality of a resignation letter except the first. What the table shows is the shape this biography has argued throughout, a career assembled from the new institutions of Russian music, conservatory, private patronage, commercial publishing, imperial favour and the international concert circuit, each arriving in his life almost as it was invented.

The publishing relationship behind the second stream deserves its own sentence, because it was the closest thing the career had to a business manager. Pyotr Jurgenson's Moscow firm became his principal publisher in the middle 1870s and remained so for life, buying works outright and later paying for them on terms generous enough to function, in the lean months, as credit; their correspondence, businesslike and increasingly affectionate across twenty years, records fees, print runs, foreign rights and the composer's perpetual surprise at what the market would bear. Jurgenson also built the foreign network, placing the scores with German and French houses, so that a work premiered in Moscow was in Leipzig shops within the season. When this biography calls the career professional, the Jurgenson correspondence is a large part of what the word means: contracts, deadlines, advances and a publisher who treated a composer's catalogue as an asset to be managed, not a favour to be granted.

## The brothers, and Modest as collaborator

Tchaikovsky's family remained the fixed point of his emotional geography. His sister Alexandra, married into the Davydov family at Kamenka, was his summer refuge and one of his most trusted correspondents until her death in 1891, a loss his letters of that year record as devastating. Of the twins, Anatoly pursued the legal career the composer had abandoned, rising in the administration, and served the family as its man of business and its confidant in every crisis from the marriage onward. Modest, the younger twin, chose literature, wrote plays, and became his brother's collaborator in the strict professional sense: he drafted the libretti of The Queen of Spades and Iolanta, worked at his brother's elbow through their composition, travelled with him, managed his households in the Klin years and stood, at the end, at the centre of the final weeks in St Petersburg.

The collaboration was genuine and its fruits are in the repertory. Modest's libretti, whatever their literary standing, were built to his brother's exact theatrical instincts, and the speed of The Queen of Spades, composed in forty-four days in Florence in 1890, was possible because the collaborator was in the next room. But the biography must mark, at the point where Modest enters the career as a working partner, the second role that the record assigns him. He was also the family's keeper of the flame: after 1893 he founded the house-museum at Klin, assembled the archive, edited the letters with the cuts described in section twenty, and wrote the first biography, the three-volume Life published between 1900 and 1903. The collaborator and the custodian are the same man, and the second role has shaped the first one's reputation ever since. Sections thirty-one to thirty-three examine what that custody did to the record; here the point is simpler and should be made with the fairness Modest is owed in his own sections of this life. Without Modest there would be no Klin archive, no preserved house, no gathered correspondence, and far less for the restorers of section thirty-three to restore. The custody preserved what it also censored, and both verbs are true.

Anatoly's role in the crises should be stated with the same plainness, since the biography has used his services twice without describing them. It was Anatoly who, in the autumn of 1877, travelled to his brother, managed the confrontation with the marriage's reality, dealt with Antonina and her family, and organised the separation's finances; it was Anatoly who handled the legal and practical machinery of the Klin household in the last years and who stood with Modest through the final St Petersburg days. The twins between them performed for the composer the services his own temperament could not perform for itself in the world of documents and confrontations, and their brother's letters are salted with gratitude for exactly that division of labour. The family, in this biography's accounting, was the composer's first institution, older than the school and longer-lived than the conservatory, and it was still on duty at the end.

## The last works and the final season

The last two years compress the whole career into a short span. The Nutcracker and the one-act opera Iolanta were composed as a double commission for the Imperial Theatres and premiered together in December 1892, to a divided reception that his letters met with exhaustion rather than surprise. Through the spring and summer of 1893 he composed the Sixth Symphony at Klin and on his travels, writing to his nephew Vladimir Davydov, its dedicatee, with a pride he rarely permitted himself about any score: he considered it, he said, the best thing he had composed. He conducted its premiere in St Petersburg on 28 October 1893, New Style, before an audience whose response was respectful and puzzled rather than warm, a reception he registered with disappointment on the night and with plans, recorded in conversation with his brother, to revise nothing but to let the work find its listeners.

The final season also carried the career's honours in a cluster: the Cambridge doctorate in June, election to corresponding membership of the French Academy, and a schedule of conducting engagements reaching into the next year, including a return to the West. Simon Morrison, whose work on Russian stage music and its production history illuminates the world of the Imperial Theatres in which these last works were made, situates the final season where it belongs, inside a working theatrical system at its most professional, not inside a legend. The composer of October 1893 was fifty-three, famous on three continents, negotiating contracts and planning revisions, with the Sixth Symphony's reception to digest and a new season to conduct. That is the documented state of the life nine days before its end, and it is the necessary prologue to the sections that follow, because the manner of the ending has been argued over ever since, and the argument begins from what those nine days actually contain in the record.

The symphony's own making belongs to the works article, but its biographical facts close this section. He composed it between February and August 1893, sketched in Klin and completed on his travels, and wrote about it to Davydov with an openness about its secret programme, a programme of life, faith and death as he described its outline to his brother, that he refused to publish. The premiere's cool reception puzzled rather than wounded him; he attributed it to the finale's strangeness and expected time to do the rest. Time did it quickly and in his absence: at the memorial performance in St Petersburg on 18 November 1893, under Eduard Nápravník, twelve days after the composer's death, the Sixth Symphony was received with the comprehension and grief that installed it at the centre of the repertory, where it has remained. The sequence, puzzlement, death, apotheosis inside a month, is the seedbed in which the legends of the next sections grew, and the biography marks it here so that the growth can be measured against the documented soil.

## The final weeks and the recorded cause of death

In the last days of October 1893 Tchaikovsky was in St Petersburg, staying with Modest, in the afterglow and the letdown of the Sixth Symphony's premiere on 28 October. Within days he was ill. The physicians who attended him, the brothers Bertenson, both established doctors with court connections, diagnosed cholera, which was present in the city in those years as it had been, in waves, since 1892. He died on 6 November 1893, New Style, nine days after conducting the premiere. The death was announced as cholera, the funeral was held with full honours in the Kazan Cathedral, the costs borne by the imperial purse, and he was buried in the Tikhvin Cemetery of the Alexander Nevsky Lavra among the honoured Russian dead. Modest and Anatoly managed the last days and the obsequies, and the record of the illness that the doctors and the family left is the record section twenty-nine will show being challenged nearly ninety years later.

### How did Tchaikovsky die?

The recorded cause of Tchaikovsky's death in November 1893 was cholera, diagnosed by the physicians who attended him in St Petersburg during an outbreak of the disease in the city. That diagnosis is what the contemporary documents state. A later published claim disputes the recorded cause; the following sections examine that dispute as a question of evidence.

Why do accounts of his death disagree, when the contemporary documents appear to speak with one voice? The question opens here, at the grave, and it closes in section thirty, after the later claim has been stated at its strongest, its origin named, its critics heard and the surviving evidence weighed. The biography asks the reader to hold the question open across exactly those two sections, because the dispute is the clearest case in this life of the problem that runs through sections nineteen to twenty and returns in thirty-one to thirty-three: what happens to a fact when the record of a man's private life has been edited by the people who loved him and the state that claimed him. The disagreement about the death, it will be argued, is not really about 1893. It is about the century of custody that followed.

The obsequies themselves entered the record as the state's final act of the career, and they are worth a paragraph before the dispute begins, because the dispute feeds on their scale. The funeral service in the Kazan Cathedral drew crowds that the police estimates of the day numbered in the thousands; the imperial family bore the costs; the coffin was carried by colleagues and students to the Alexander Nevsky Lavra, where the Tikhvin Cemetery was becoming the national pantheon of Russian art. A death announced as cholera in a city where cholera was claiming ordinary citizens weekly was given, in this one case, a prince's funeral. That contrast, between the commonplace diagnosis and the uncommon honours, is itself documented and innocent: the honours measured the fame, not the diagnosis. But it handed later suspicion its favourite exhibit, the sense that something about the week did not add up, and sections twenty-nine and thirty exist because that sense, detached from the record, hardened into a story.

## Why the account of the death is disputed

The recorded cause stood essentially unchallenged in print for more than eighty years. The challenge, when it came, came from outside Russia. Alexandra Orlova, a Soviet musicologist who emigrated to the United States in 1979, published from that year onward an account she attributed to an oral tradition preserved among alumni of the School of Jurisprudence. In her telling, a complaint touching the composer's private life was about to reach the Tsar through a highly placed intermediary; a group of his former schoolfellows, acting as a self-constituted court of honour to protect the school's name, confronted him in the last days of October 1893 and exacted a promise that he would end his own life; and the cholera announcement, in this account, was the cover story the same network then arranged. The claim, it must be said with the distinctions this series requires, was alleged in print by Orlova on the basis of testimony at several removes. It was never charged in any proceeding, never tested against documents in its proponents' hands, and never proved. It is a claim about the death, and its history is a history of the claim, not of the event.

The claim's strengths should be given their full form, because the stance charter requires it and because they explain its durability. The story answers to real features of the world it describes: the Jurisprudence cohort was genuinely close and honour-conscious; the legal peril surrounding the composer's private life was genuine, as section nineteen established; Modest's management of the record creates real gaps in which suspicion can live; and the official commemoration was so smooth and so state-managed that it invites the thought of management behind it. The claim also arrived at a moment, the late 1970s and 1980s, when the restoration of the private record was beginning and when every Soviet-era silence looked like a hiding place. A legend that explains a silence will always find readers while the silence lasts.

Against the claim stand two principal scholarly positions, and the biography names them as the brief of this life directs, because David Brown and Alexander Poznansky oppose it from different ground and the difference matters. Poznansky, working from the archives, published the documentary case against the claim from 1988 onward: the alleged chain of testimony cannot be verified at any link, the named participants' movements and the chronology of the last days contradict the story's requirements, no document of the supposed complaint or confrontation exists in archives that preserve trivial paperwork about the composer in abundance, and the legend's own genealogy, traced through its retellings, shows it growing in the telling in the manner of folklore. Brown, in the final volume of his biography, rests the case differently: the contemporary medical record, the attending physicians' diagnosis made during a documented cholera outbreak, the family's correspondence of the last days, which shows anxiety about illness and none of the behaviour the claim requires, together constitute evidence of the ordinary kind, and against evidence of the ordinary kind an undated oral tradition at third hand cannot stand. Where Poznansky demolishes the legend's provenance, Brown defends the documents' sufficiency. Both conclude that the recorded cause is the cause the evidence supports.

The claim's afterlife in print is part of its history and part of the evidence problem, because repetition is not corroboration. Through the 1980s and 1990s the court-of-honour account travelled from émigré journals into Western reference works, newspaper features and popular biography; Anthony Holden's 1995 life of the composer, the most widely read English biography of its decade, treated the claim with a sympathy that carried it to a general readership the scholarly debate never reached. Each retelling tended to shed the claim's provenance, the third-hand oral chain, and to keep its narrative satisfactions, the schoolboy honour, the conspiracy of gentlemen, the silenced scandal. The biography notes the mechanism without malice toward its operators: a story this well shaped will outrun its evidence in any market, and the market for it had been prepared, as section twenty argued, by a century in which the official record of this life was known to have been edited. Suspicion had been taught by the editors. It was then spent, by the legend's readers, on the doctors.

## What the evidence actually supports

Section thirty closes the question opened at the grave, and it closes it structurally, as this series requires for genuinely open matters: by stating what the evidence supports, what it does not, and what would have to be true for each side to be right. The evidence supports the following without strain. Tchaikovsky died in St Petersburg on 6 November 1893. The physicians attending him recorded cholera, during a period when cholera was active in the city. His family, who were present and who left extensive correspondence about those days, recorded an illness and a death, and nothing in their surviving papers, including papers never meant for print, behaves as the later claim would lead one to expect. No document supporting the court-of-honour claim has been produced from any archive, Russian or Western, in the decades since the claim was published, although the archives bearing on his life have been worked intensively by scholars of opposed views. The claim rests on oral transmission alone, published eighty-six years after the event by a scholar reporting what she had been told by a man reporting what he had been told.

For the recorded account to be wrong, the following would all have to be true at once: that a complaint existed which no archive holds; that a confrontation occurred which no participant, servant, colleague or family member ever recorded; that two court physicians falsified a diagnosis during an epidemic in which the diagnosis was commonplace; and that a family which preserved the composer's most compromising letters preserved nothing of the crisis that killed him. For the claim to be right, only its own chain of whispers has to be trusted. The biography therefore reports the death as the record reports it: Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky died of cholera, as diagnosed by his doctors, on 6 November 1893. The dispute is reported in full because the dispute is itself a fact of the life after death, and because its real subject is not the cause of death but the custody of the private record: a century that deleted a man's letters taught its readers to suspect every official sentence, and the suspicion, once taught, attached itself to the most official sentence of all. Sections thirty-one to thirty-three follow that custody to its end.

One further consideration belongs to the weighing, because it is the strongest thing that can honestly be said for continued doubt, and the charter requires the strongest form. The custodians of this life did suppress documents on a large scale, as sections twenty and thirty-two demonstrate, and some of what they suppressed concerned precisely the private territory the legend invokes. A reader is therefore not irrational for asking whether the death record, too, was managed. The answer the evidence gives is specific rather than general: the suppression that can be proved was editorial, performed on paper, over decades, by named men whose scissors left traces that later scholars could follow and reverse; the suppression the legend requires was theatrical, performed in days, by a conspiracy of gentlemen, physicians and family that left no trace in the most paperwork-saturated biography of the Russian nineteenth century. The first kind of custody is documented. The second is inferred from the first, and the inference, however understandable, is not evidence. What would change the verdict is equally specific: a contemporary document, a participant's paper, a file in the school's or the ministry's archives. Scholars who detest each other's conclusions have searched those archives for thirty years. The document has not appeared.

## Modest's biography and the first portrait

Between 1900 and 1903 Modest Tchaikovsky published in Moscow the three volumes of The Life of Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky, the first full biography and the foundation on which the next eighty years of writing about his brother rested. It is a considerable achievement of assembly: Modest had the letters, the diaries, the family papers and his own presence at most of the life's turning points after 1877, and he built from them a portrait of industry, tenderness and suffering that made the composer legible to the world. It is also, and this biography treats it throughout as a subject rather than a source, an act of custody. Modest knew the whole record, including the private life documented in section nineteen, and he decided what of it the world would be given. The letters quoted in his volumes are selected and in places adjusted; the marriage is rendered with a discretion that protects his brother by diminishing his wife; the final days are given in the family's authorised form; and the private life is not denied, which would have required lying, but dissolved, which required only silence.

The distinction matters because Modest was not a villain of this record but its first editor, acting from motives the biography can state fairly: family honour in a society whose laws made the truth dangerous to the living as well as to the dead, a brother's love, and a genuine belief that the music, not the man, was the legacy. His Life is therefore evidence of the highest value for one class of questions, what the family knew, kept and chose to show, and evidence of no independent value for another class, the events it renders discreet. Where this article has used the events of the life, it has used them from the documents Modest preserved and the modern scholarship that read those documents whole, and where Modest's version is the only version, as with parts of the 1877 crisis, the article has said so at the point of use. That discipline, applied consistently, is the only respectful way to use a loving forgery of emphasis: neither to swallow it nor to sneer at it, but to read it as the first chapter of the afterlife it created. Readers setting that first portrait beside the restored record for a class can display the paired texts in the browser-based presentation viewer at https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html, which opens presentation files directly in the browser.

The Life's travels carried the first portrait abroad before any rival existed. A German translation followed within a few years, and an English version, abridged and edited by Rosa Newmarch, appeared in 1906, fixing for the English-speaking world the same proportions Modest had fixed for Russia: the industrious, suffering, tender composer, the catastrophic marriage rendered as a mystery of temperament, the private life dissolved into reticence. Western readers who believed they were reading the life were reading the family's edition of it, and the distinction went unnoticed for two generations because the alternative sources, the letters in their uncensored form, were locked in the same custody in Klin. Modest had also, in 1894, founded the museum in the Klin house, gathering the manuscripts, the library, the furniture and the papers into the archive that bears his brother's name. The museum and the biography are one project in two media, a house and a book, each arranging the same relics in the same order, each omitting the same room. Later sections walk through the house. This one simply records who built it, and why what it preserved made its own arrangement eventually correctable.

## Soviet biography and the erasure

Modest's discretion became, in the Soviet period, a system. The Soviet state adopted Tchaikovsky as its supreme musical classic, the acceptable face of the Russian nineteenth century: his works anchored the conservatory curricula, the centenary of his birth in 1940 was celebrated as a state festival, his house at Klin became a shrine museum, and his music was exported as proof of Russian genius. The adoption had a price, and the price was the man. The Soviet collected edition of his letters, published in five volumes across the middle of the century and treated for decades as the scholarly standard, systematically removed or rewrote the passages documenting his private life, along with much of his religious doubt, his despair and his contempt for officialdom. Soviet biographies followed the edition: the composer they presented was a patriot, a professional and a sufferer of unspecified sorrows, whose marriage failed for reasons vaguely assigned to his wife's inadequacy and whose nature required no discussion because, in the authorised text, it did not exist.

### Why was Tchaikovsky's biography rewritten in Russia?

The rewriting began with his brother Modest's biography, which omitted the private life from family motives, and was systematised in the Soviet period, when the state installed Tchaikovsky as an emblem while its letter editions cut the passages documenting his sexuality and despair. An emblem cannot carry a criminalised private life, so the record was edited to fit the monument.

Here the sixth and final revelation of the biography lands, and it is the claim the whole article has been earning. Soviet-era biography removed his sexuality from the record while simultaneously installing him as a national emblem, which means the standard twentieth-century account of his life was politically produced. The two operations were one operation: the louder the state proclaimed the composer, the more completely the man had to be deleted, because the emblem and the documented human being could not occupy the same pedestal under Soviet law and Soviet doctrine, which continued to criminalise the private life the letters record. The result was a biography with a hole in its centre exactly the shape of its subject, and generations of readers, Russian and Western alike, who sensed the hole and filled it with whatever their own age preferred: neurasthenia, patriotism, doomed romance, and eventually, when the silence itself became suspicious, the court-of-honour legend examined in sections twenty-nine and thirty. The erasure did not merely hide the life. It manufactured the market for myths about it.

The fairness this biography owes the record applies here too. The Soviet scholarship that performed the erasure also performed preservation on a scale no one else attempted: the Klin archive was maintained, the complete works were edited and published, the documentary chronicle of the life was assembled year by year, and the raw material from which the restoration of section thirty-three was built survived because Soviet custodians kept it, catalogued, in the house his brother founded. The state deleted the man from the printed record and preserved him in the archival one. Both facts are documented, and the second is the reason the first could eventually be undone.

The era's scholarship was not monolithic, and fairness requires the distinction. Boris Asafyev, the most influential Soviet writer on Tchaikovsky, built serious analytical work on the music inside the permitted frame, and the documentary chronicles assembled by Soviet archivists, the day-by-day records of the life compiled from newspapers, letters and theatre archives, are tools the restorers themselves relied on. The erasure was a policy about one stratum of the life, executed through the letter editions and the biographical formulae, not a general falsification of the record; the same system that forbade printing the private letters catalogued them impeccably in the archive. That doubleness is the Soviet chapter's true shape, and it is why the restoration, when the political weather changed, could proceed from the state's own shelves rather than from rediscovery. Nothing had been lost. A great deal had merely been unprintable, and the difference between lost and unprintable is the difference between a tragedy and an irony, which is to say, between Modest's century and ours.

## When the record was restored

The restoration was the work of the last decades of the twentieth century and of named scholars, several of them this biography's authorities. Its precondition was access: from the 1970s onward, Western scholars were admitted, under conditions, to the Soviet archives, and David Brown built his four-volume life and works, published between 1978 and 1992, substantially on documents that the standard editions had cut, noting the cuts as he went. Its turning point was the archival work of Alexander Poznansky, a Soviet-trained scholar who worked in the Klin and Moscow archives, emigrated, and from the late 1980s published the documents whole: his biography of 1991 placed the private record at the centre of the life, where the letters had always put it, and his documentary studies dismantled, piece by piece, the legends that had grown in the silence, including the death legend examined in sections twenty-nine and thirty. Roland John Wiley's studies of the stage works and his later biography continued the same documentary discipline, and Russian scholarship of the post-Soviet years began printing in Russia what Russian archives had held all along.

The restoration changed the biography's centre of gravity without changing its facts. No document exhumed in those decades altered the chronology of the career, the marriage, the allowance or the death as the contemporary record states them. What changed was the legibility of the life: the private passages, read in place, explain the marriage without melodrama, explain the von Meck rule without romance, explain the fear of gossip without prurience, and leave the death where the doctors left it. A reader who wants the Tchaikovsky of the uncensored record does not get a different man. The reader gets the same man with the centre filled in, which is why the restoration, rather than any legend, is the real event of his posthumous life.

One caution closes the subject, because custody did not end with the restorers. Every age edits this life toward its own needs, including the age that restored it, and the test this biography has applied throughout, claim by claim, is not whether a source is sympathetic, Soviet, fraternal or modern, but whether the document survives, what exactly it says, and what its keeper stood to gain. Applied to Modest, to the Soviet editions, to Orlova and to the restorers alike, that single test is the difference between a biography and a custody battle continued by other means.

The restoration's Russian chapter completes the story and returns the life to its own country. Through the 1990s, Russian journals and publishers began printing what the archives held: diaries and correspondence in fuller texts, documentary studies by the Klin museum's own scholars, and a collected-works scholarship that treated the cuts of the old editions as data rather than as law. The house at Klin, the archive Modest founded to guard a portrait, became the instrument of the portrait's correction, its reading rooms open to scholars of every camp in the death dispute and the private-life literature alike. There is a fittingness in the mechanism that the biography simply records: the custody preserved the evidence, the evidence outlasted the custody's reasons, and the man's own institution, the archive, performed the restoration that no outsider could have compelled. The life that began in a house arranged by a governess ends, as a documentary matter, in a house arranged by his brother, with its doors open.

## Tchaikovsky Life and Career: a career built inside institutions being invented

The life can now be summarised as the thesis of section one stated it. A boy from an ironworks town was trained by the state for the state, resigned at twenty-two from a ministry desk, and walked into a conservatory founded the year he joined it. He taught for twelve years in a second conservatory younger than his own training, and was freed to compose by a patron whose condition of total distance suited his nature so exactly that the arrangement produced more than a thousand letters and fourteen years of work. He married, disastrously and documentably, for reasons he wrote down. He built, tour by tour, the first genuinely Western professional career of any Russian composer, conducted at the opening of Carnegie Hall, drew a salary in turn from a school, a widow, a publisher, an emperor and the box offices of three continents, and died at fifty-three with the state's honours at his funeral. Then his brother edited him, a state canonised and censored him, and scholars of a later generation gave him back.

The institutions frame every phase. The School of Jurisprudence made the clerk and, in legend at least, pursued the composer past the grave. The conservatories made the craftsman and employed him. The publishing house, the patron's allowance, the imperial pension and the international circuit made the professional. And the archive, the last institution of the life, made the afterlife, preserving in its folders everything the printed record was forbidden to say. To study the Tchaikovsky life and career is therefore to study two things at once: a man of documented genius and documented difficulty, and the century of institutions that successively employed, freed, honoured, censored and restored him. Readers who want the machinery of the music itself should turn to the companion study of his musical style, and those who want the works in their order to the catalogue article; this article has owned the life, and the life, on the evidence, is remarkable enough in its documented plainness to need no legend's assistance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky?

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky, born in 1840 and dead in 1893, was a Russian composer who became the first musician of his country to sustain a fully professional career of the Western kind. Trained originally as a lawyer, he resigned from the Ministry of Justice in 1863 to study at the new St Petersburg Conservatory, taught for twelve years at the Moscow Conservatory, and was then freed to compose full time by an allowance from the patron Nadezhda von Meck. His output includes six symphonies, the operas Eugene Onegin and The Queen of Spades, the ballets Swan Lake, The Sleeping Beauty and The Nutcracker, three piano concertos and a violin concerto. He conducted his own works across Europe and in the United States, dying in St Petersburg days after premiering his Sixth Symphony.

### Q: Where and when was Tchaikovsky born?

Tchaikovsky was born on 7 May 1840, New Style, in Votkinsk, a settlement built around an ironworks in the Vyatka province of the Russian Empire, east of Moscow. His father, Ilya Petrovich Tchaikovsky, was a mining engineer who managed the Kamsko-Votkinsk ironworks, and his mother, Alexandra Andreyevna, came from a family of French descent. He was the second son in a large household. The family left Votkinsk as his father's postings changed, and in 1850 the boy was taken to St Petersburg to prepare for the Imperial School of Jurisprudence. His mother died of cholera in 1854, when he was fourteen, a bereavement his letters returned to for the rest of his life, resurfacing in them at moments of crisis across the following four decades.

### Q: What did Tchaikovsky do before becoming a composer?

Before composition, Tchaikovsky completed a full professional training in law and practised it. He spent nine years at the Imperial School of Jurisprudence in St Petersburg, an elite institution that prepared civil servants for the Russian state, graduated in 1859, and was appointed to the Ministry of Justice, where he worked as a clerk for four years, rising to the rank of titular counsellor. The work was clerical: files, drafts and departmental routine. Music during those years was an evening pursuit, pursued with growing seriousness through concerts, the opera and eventually formal theory classes. The legal career was not a false start imposed by his family; it was a qualification he earned, used and then deliberately resigned, and its habits of documentation shaped the way he later ran his musical affairs.

### Q: Why did Tchaikovsky leave the civil service?

He left because the musical study he had begun in the autumn of 1861, in Nikolai Zaremba's theory classes under the Russian Musical Society, had become the centre of his life and could not be combined with a ministry desk. The decision came in two stages: at twenty-one he began serious study while still serving, and in April 1863, aged twenty-two, he resigned and enrolled full time at the St Petersburg Conservatory, which had opened the previous year. His father, consulted about the change, declined to oppose it while doubting that music would support him. The resignation cost him a salary and a pensionable future, and for several years he lived on very little, giving lessons while he studied. His letters show no regret about the exchange, only anxiety to make up for the late start.

### Q: When was the St Petersburg Conservatory founded?

The St Petersburg Conservatory was founded in 1862 by the pianist Anton Rubinstein, working through the Russian Musical Society he had established in 1859 with the patronage of the Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna. It opened its classes in the autumn of 1862, with Rubinstein as director, offering graded professional training in composition, theory and instrumental performance on the model of the Western European conservatories. Tchaikovsky was among its first students, studying composition with Nikolai Zaremba and then with Rubinstein himself, and graduated at the end of 1865 with a silver medal. It was the first institution of its kind in Russia. Its Moscow counterpart, directed by Anton's brother Nikolai, followed in 1866 and employed Tchaikovsky as its professor of theory.

### Q: Why did Russia lack music conservatories before the 1860s?

The lack was structural. Professional music in imperial Russia was supplied by other institutions for other purposes: the imperial theatres trained their own singers and dancers and imported composers and conductors from Italy, France and Germany; the church trained its choirs; great estates maintained serf orchestras until emancipation in 1861 removed the basis of that system. None of these produced a Russian professional class of composers. Social classification reinforced the gap, since a working musician ranked as a servant or tradesman, and gentlemen such as Glinka composed as amateurs rather than as professionals. A talented Russian boy therefore had no educational route into composition as a career. The Russian Musical Society and the conservatories of 1862 and 1866 were founded to close exactly that gap.

### Q: How did the von Meck allowance work?

The allowance was a private, regular income settled on Tchaikovsky by the widowed railway heiress Nadezhda von Meck from 1877. It amounted to six thousand roubles a year, paid in monthly instalments and administered like a household disbursement, at a time when that sum was roughly three times a senior professor's salary. No compositions were commissioned in return, no attendance was required and no duties attached except the correspondence itself, which both parties treated as the substance of the relationship. One condition governed the whole arrangement: the two were never to meet or speak, a rule von Meck proposed and Tchaikovsky embraced, maintained even when they were in the same city or staying, in sequence, in the same house. The allowance let him resign his Moscow teaching post in 1878 and compose full time, and it ran until she withdrew it in October 1890.

### Q: Did Tchaikovsky and von Meck ever meet?

No. Across fourteen years of correspondence, money and intimate confession, Tchaikovsky and Nadezhda von Meck never met face to face and never spoke, by their own standing agreement. The rule was actively managed: she lent him her Moscow house and her estate at Brailov for his holidays and absented herself before he arrived, and when their travels placed them in the same city, in Florence and in the countryside near her estates, their movements were coordinated so that the rule held. On a couple of occasions they saw one another at a distance, unacknowledged, in the manner of strangers passing. There was no quarrel behind the arrangement and no barrier imposed by others. Both parties regarded the distance as the condition that made the relationship possible, and both kept it for the whole fourteen years.

### Q: Why did the von Meck arrangement end?

It ended in October 1890 with a letter from von Meck stating that her family's finances were ruined, the estates in disorder and the allowance therefore impossible to continue, and asking to be remembered with the finality of a farewell. Her financial difficulties were genuine: the railway fortune had been damaged by mismanagement within the family, and economies were being imposed across her households. But the sum involved was small against that fortune, she was ill and withdrawing from the world, and she broke the correspondence as completely as the payments, never answering his letters again. David Brown's assessment, that the financial explanation was true but insufficient, represents the documentary position. He kept her portrait, spoke of her with gratitude, and named the loss of the correspondence, rather than the money, as the wound.

### Q: Who did Tchaikovsky marry and what happened?

In July 1877 Tchaikovsky married Antonina Ivanovna Milyukova, a former Moscow Conservatory student who had written to him declaring her love. The marriage collapsed within weeks. His letters to his brothers record a misery so complete that he fled Moscow in the autumn, spent time under his sister's care at Kamenka and then recovered in Switzerland, on medical advice to live permanently apart from his wife. Antonina's own later account presents the same events from the other side: she had idolised him, understood the marriage as her fulfilment, and experienced his flight as inexplicable cruelty. The marriage was never legally dissolved, since she would not consent to a divorce, and she remained his wife in law until his death and his widow afterwards, dying in psychiatric care in 1917.

### Q: What do Tchaikovsky's letters say about his private life?

His letters, particularly those to his brothers Anatoly and Modest and to his nephew Vladimir Davydov, discuss his private life with a frankness that settles the central questions. They document that he was homosexual, record his relationships and his struggles with his nature under a legal code that criminalised it, and show him weighing marriage as social protection years before he attempted it. The passages that establish this are precisely the passages his editors removed: Modest's editions cut them, and the Soviet collected edition of the letters cut them again with political authority. Alexander Poznansky's archival work, published from the late 1980s, restored the uncensored texts to the centre of the biography. Read whole, the letters show the private life occupying a modest share of a correspondence dominated by work, money, family and music.

### Q: Did Tchaikovsky conduct his own music?

Yes, extensively, though never willingly at first and never without fear. In his early conducting appearances he was haunted by the conviction that his head might fall from his shoulders and held his chin with one hand while beating time with the other, a detail his own letters and family recollections preserve. Nikolai Rubinstein conducted most of his Moscow premieres. From 1887, however, foreign engagements required the composer in person, and he re-trained himself on the podium, conducting the Moscow premiere of his opera The Cherevichki that year and then undertaking European tours from Leipzig in January 1888 onward. He conducted in Hamburg, Prague, Paris, London and New York, and conducted the premieres of the Fifth and Sixth Symphonies himself. The fear diminished with practice but, by his diaries' testimony, never disappeared.

### Q: Did Tchaikovsky visit the United States?

Yes, once, in the spring of 1891, when he crossed the Atlantic as the principal foreign guest at the opening of the new Music Hall in New York, the hall built by Andrew Carnegie that soon took Carnegie's name. He conducted his own works across the opening festival's concerts, including the Festival Coronation March at the first concert on 5 May 1891, and also conducted in Baltimore and Philadelphia. He kept a diary of the expedition that records his astonishment at the scale, noise and energy of the country, his exhaustion under its hospitality and his longing for home, alongside a professional's recognition of what such halls and fees meant for music's future. He was the first Russian composer engaged in America as a festival's representative attraction, and he never returned.

### Q: What was the recorded cause of Tchaikovsky's death?

The recorded cause of Tchaikovsky's death was cholera. He died in St Petersburg on 6 November 1893, New Style, nine days after conducting the premiere of his Sixth Symphony, during a period when cholera was active in the city. The physicians who attended him, Drs Bertenson, entered the diagnosis at the time, his family recorded an illness and a death consistent with it, and the funeral followed with state honours. That is the cause the contemporary documents state, and it is the cause the principal modern documentary biographers, including David Brown, accept as supported by the evidence. A different account, published in the late twentieth century, disputes the recorded cause; it is a claim about the evidence rather than an established alternative, and it is examined on its merits in the sections of this article devoted to the dispute.

### Q: Why is the account of Tchaikovsky's death disputed?

The dispute rests on a claim published by the musicologist Alexandra Orlova after her emigration in 1979, attributed to an oral tradition among alumni of the School of Jurisprudence: that a complaint about the composer's private life was about to reach the Tsar, and that former schoolfellows, acting as a court of honour, compelled him to end his own life, with cholera announced as a cover. The claim was never charged, tested or proved, and no document supporting it has emerged from archives that have since been intensively worked. Alexander Poznansky dismantled its chain of testimony and chronology from 1988 onward, and David Brown rested the contrary case on the contemporary medical record. The dispute endures partly because a century of editorial suppression of the private record taught readers to suspect official accounts.

### Q: How did Soviet writers handle Tchaikovsky's biography?

Soviet treatment combined maximal honour for the music with systematic deletion of the man. The state installed Tchaikovsky as its supreme musical classic: his works anchored conservatory teaching, his birth centenary in 1940 was a state festival, and his house at Klin became a shrine museum. At the same time, the Soviet collected edition of his letters cut the passages documenting his private life, his religious doubts and his despair, and official biographies presented a patriot and professional whose marriage failed vaguely and whose nature required no discussion. The same custodians, to their credit, preserved the archive itself, maintained Klin and published the complete works, so that the documents from which the record was later restored survived under the custody that censored them in print.

### Q: Which Tchaikovsky biography should I read first?

David Brown's four-volume Tchaikovsky: The Early Years, The Crisis Years, The Years of Wandering and The Final Years, published between 1978 and 1992, remains the fullest documentary account in English and the natural first choice for a reader who wants the life whole, since Brown worked substantially from archival documents the standard editions had cut. Alexander Poznansky's Tchaikovsky: The Quest for the Inner Man, published in 1991, is the essential companion, strongest exactly where the older record is weakest, on the private life and the legends. Roland John Wiley's biography and his studies of the stage works offer the most careful documentary handling of the working life. Modest Tchaikovsky's three-volume Life of 1900 to 1903 should be read as a primary document of the family's presentation, not as a neutral source.

### Q: Should I read Tchaikovsky's letters alongside a biography?

Yes, and the combination is more valuable for this composer than for almost any other, because the letters are the evidence against which every biography in this field must be checked. His correspondence with Nadezhda von Meck, running to more than a thousand letters across fourteen years, shows the working life from inside, score by score and fee by fee. The family letters show the private life that the editions long concealed. Two cautions govern the reading. First, ask which edition is in hand, since the older collected editions, including the Soviet one, cut passages without marking the cuts. Second, remember that a letter is a performance for its reader: the confessions to von Meck, the business letters to his publisher and the jokes to his brothers are all true in different registers, and the biography exists to price the registers against each other.

### Q: How do I check a claim made about Tchaikovsky's private life?

Apply three tests in order. First, ask what document the claim rests on: a dated letter or diary entry in an uncensored text outranks a memoir, which outranks an oral tradition published generations later, and a claim whose chain of transmission cannot be produced, as with the court-of-honour story of his death, should be priced accordingly. Second, ask which edition supplied the document, since the editions of the letters prepared under his brother and under Soviet editorship removed the private passages, and a claim drawn from a cut text may be evidence only of the cutting. Third, ask what the claim's keeper stood to gain: the family protecting its honour, the state protecting its emblem and the legend-maker explaining a silence all had interests, and naming the interest does not settle the claim but tells you where to press.

### Q: Which recordings suit a listener new to Tchaikovsky?

Begin with the works in the forms in which he reached the public first. The First Piano Concerto and the Violin Concerto give the virtuoso manner at full stretch; the Fifth Symphony gives the orchestral rhetoric in its most approachable scale, and the Sixth, approached after it, gives the final statement that he conducted nine days before his death. For the stage, a complete Swan Lake rather than a suite shows what he did to ballet music, and Eugene Onegin, heard whole, shows the lyric opera he considered his most honest work. Listeners drawn to smaller forms should try the Souvenir de Florence and the string serenade. The works article in this series takes up the catalogue piece by piece; this biography's only advice is to meet the public works first, since the public is where he built the career.
