An enacted statute states what the law commands in words that passed both chambers of Congress and reached the President for signature. A disputed phrase inside that text, however, often resists settlement by the text alone, because the same words can bear two readings, because a term can carry a specialized meaning drawn from the field under regulation, or because the relation between a clause and the sections around that clause can leave the reach of the clause uncertain. At that point a researcher turns away from the bare enacted words and toward legislative history, the documentary record that the process of enactment generated while the words took shape, tested, amended and defended in public. The turn is natural and hazardous at once. The record exists in abundance, and abundance invites selection. A passage can be lifted from a debate, a report or a hearing and offered as though the passage settled the question, even when the passage spoke for a single member, addressed an earlier draft, or belonged to a document that a court in the forum under consideration would give little weight or no weight at all. Research that begins without a method therefore ends with quotation rather than with knowledge, and the difference between the two outcomes is the subject of the pages that follow.

![Researching legislative history in committee reports, bill versions and the Congressional Record - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-32.webp)

The subject of this article is legislative history itself considered as a body of evidence: the documentary record produced by the legislative process, comprising bill versions, committee reports, hearings, committee prints, floor proceedings and related executive branch documents. Each class in that record came into existence for a legislative purpose rather than for a later interpretive purpose. A bill version froze a proposal at a stage of consideration. A committee report explained a measure that a committee had ordered reported. A hearing gathered testimony and material that a committee wished to place before itself. A print served the internal preparation of a committee. Floor proceedings recorded the consideration of a measure by a chamber. None of those documents was produced as a commentary on a finished statute, because none of those documents was written after the statute had taken final form and entered the Code. The record therefore preserves the process in motion, and a researcher reads the record as evidence of a process, with attention to the stage at which each document appeared, the body that issued each document and the text to which each document referred.

Against that record stands a named discipline that governs every stage of the work described in this article: find it and then discount it. The first half of the discipline concerns location. A document that cannot be found cannot be weighed, and location in legislative research is rarely a matter of a single search in a single collection, because the collections that hold the record begin their coverage in different years for different classes of material, because the record for one enactment can scatter across official systems, commercial databases, bound volumes and library compilations, and because an informal starting point, such as a popular name or a codified section, must be converted step by step into the formal citations that unlock the underlying documents. The second half of the discipline concerns weight. A document that has been found must then be discounted, assessed against the institutional authority of the body that issued the document, the distance between the document and the final enacted text, the incentives of the speaker or author, and the practice of the forum in which the document will be offered. Discounting is not dismissal. A source can carry great value for one purpose and little authority for another, and the discipline requires the researcher to hold both judgments at once rather than allowing the effort spent in finding a document to inflate the authority assigned to the document after discovery.

The two halves depend on each other, and the dependence explains why neither half can be practiced safely in isolation. Location without discounting produces a dossier, an accumulation of passages assembled without a principle for ranking the passages against each other, and a dossier can mislead a reader more completely than a thin record can, because bulk creates an impression of proof. Discounting without location produces the opposite failure, a confident ranking of sources that the researcher has not actually retrieved, tested against the enacted text or placed in sequence. Sound work moves between the halves repeatedly. A document found early in a search suggests where a stronger document might lie. A document retrieved late in the search can force a revision of a judgment formed earlier, when a report explains language that debate left obscure or when a version comparison shows that an explanation quoted with confidence referred to words that never survived into law.

Three cautions run through the whole discipline and require statement at the outset, before the detailed treatment of each source begins. The first caution concerns chronology. Documents in a legislative history belong to moments in a sequence, and a document speaks to the text that existed at the moment of production rather than to a text that emerged later through amendment, negotiation or enrollment. The second caution concerns authorship. A statement carries the authority of the capacity in which the statement was made, and the record mixes institutional explanations, individual representations, witness testimony and material inserted for publication, each with a different claim on a reader. The third caution concerns the forum. Interpretive method differs among decision makers, and a document set assembled with care can meet different receptions in different settings, so research prepared without regard to the receiving forum can be thorough in coverage and misdirected in emphasis at the same time.

Readers who hold those cautions in view possess the frame within which the detailed sections arrange themselves. The sections that follow examine the document classes one by one, the reports that explain measures to the chambers, the published account of floor proceedings and the limits on treating that account as a transcript, the order in which sources are ranked when sources conflict, the tools that locate material for each historical period, the long dispute over whether the material deserves any place in interpretation at all, and the path that leads from a popular name or a Code section to a citable source whose weight can be defended. Practiced as a whole, the discipline converts a mass of paper generated by a legislature at work into evidence that a reader can trust in proportion and use with precision.

## The Documents Themselves: Versions, Hearings and Prints

A legislative history rests first on documents, and the most reliable documents in the set are the ones produced by the legislative process itself as a measure moved through it. Commentary written about a bill, whether friendly or hostile, describes decisions taken elsewhere. The successive texts of the bill record those decisions directly. Each version froze the measure at a defined stage, under a defined responsibility, and a researcher who lays the versions side by side works from evidence generated by the process rather than from recollection or advocacy about the process. That distinction explains the order in which a careful search proceeds. The versions come first, the hearings and prints supply the surrounding record, and explanations offered by participants take their place behind the texts that the chambers actually handled, amended and passed.

The sequence begins with the bill as introduced. The introduced text embodied the proposal in the form in which a sponsor placed it before a chamber, before committee examination, before floor amendment and before any negotiation with the other chamber. Its importance lies in that priority. Language included at introduction and absent afterward disappeared at some identifiable point, and language absent at introduction and included afterward entered at some identifiable point, so the introduced version provides the baseline against which every later alteration becomes visible. Without a baseline, a change cannot be described as a change at all, and an argument that a provision was narrowed, broadened, qualified or abandoned has nothing firm to rest on. The introduced text also preserves proposals that failed, and failed proposals form part of the documentary record even though they form no part of the enacted law.

The next stage produced the bill as reported. A committee that examined a measure and ordered it reported returned the text to its chamber in an amended form, a form unchanged from introduction, or a form accompanied by a recommendation for a substitute, and the reported version captured the measure after the most concentrated scrutiny the process applied. Comparison between the introduced and reported versions therefore isolates the committee stage as a source of alteration. Definitions rewritten in committee, exceptions added in committee and obligations removed in committee all stand out against the introduced baseline, and the timing matters as much as the substance, because the committee acted after receiving testimony and after considering the measure in the form in which the sponsor had offered it. The reported version does not explain itself, and explanation belongs to the report that accompanied it, but the text alone establishes what the committee chose to send forward and what the committee chose to withhold.

Passage by each chamber produced further distinct versions, and the distinction between the two carries weight of its own. A bill as passed by the House and a bill as passed by the Senate represented the positions of two separate institutions acting on related but not necessarily identical texts. Where the two versions agreed, the agreement itself documented a settled point. Where the two versions diverged, the divergence marked the questions that remained open when the measure left the separate chambers and entered the stage at which a single text had to emerge. A researcher tracing a disputed phrase benefits from locating the chamber in which the phrase first appeared, the chamber that resisted or revised it, and the form in which it survived the reconciliation of the two positions, because attribution of language to a chamber and a stage is firmer ground than attribution of language to a general congressional intention that no document states in those terms.

### What do the successive versions of a bill show?

The successive versions show the measure changing in public, stage by stage, with additions, deletions and substitutions fixed to identifiable actions. Comparison reveals what drafters kept, what committees altered, what each chamber accepted, and what survived agreement, supplying dated evidence of choices that commentary describes only at second hand.

The value of that comparison grows when the changes are read as a connected sequence rather than as isolated differences. A definition broadened at the reported stage and narrowed again before passage tells a different story from a definition that moved in one direction only, and a qualification that appeared after a specific amendment was offered tells a different story from a qualification included from introduction onward. Sequence converts a static text into a record of movement. The final enacted words remain the law, and no earlier version displaces them, but the path toward those words shows which formulations were available to the drafters, which formulations the drafters set aside, and which formulations survived repeated opportunities for removal. A court or a researcher weighing two plausible readings of an ambiguous phrase can ask whether one reading matches a formulation that the process considered and discarded, and the versions alone can answer that question with documentary certainty.

Deleted language deserves particular attention, and also particular caution. A provision struck between one version and the next proves that the provision stood in the measure at one stage and stood outside the measure at a later stage, and the documentary fact of removal is beyond dispute once the versions are authenticated. The reason for removal is a separate matter, and the versions by themselves do not supply it. Removal could reflect rejection of the idea, acceptance of the idea in different words, a judgment that the idea belonged in another measure, or a bargain in which the provision was traded away for reasons unrelated to its merits. Comparison establishes the event and fixes its place in the sequence. Interpretation of the event requires corroboration from the surrounding record, and a disciplined researcher separates the two operations rather than allowing a clear documentary fact to carry an unclear inference about motive at the same weight.

Added language carries a parallel discipline. An exception, a definition or a savings clause that entered the text at a late stage entered a measure whose other provisions had already taken shape, and its placement, its cross references and its relationship to the surrounding sections often reveal the problem the addition addressed more precisely than a general statement of purpose could. Late additions also interact with the earlier record in a limiting way. Testimony taken and debate conducted before an addition existed addressed a measure that lacked the addition, so material generated at an early stage speaks to late language only indirectly, and the chronology of the versions marks the boundary of that material with exactness. Ignoring the boundary produces a common error in legislative research, the error of explaining final language by statements made about different language, and the version sequence exists in large part to prevent that error from passing unnoticed.

The sequence ends with the enrolled bill. The enrolled bill was the final version passed identically by both chambers, authenticated by the presiding officers and presented to the President. Enrollment therefore marked the point at which the separate institutional texts converged into a single authoritative document, and the enrolled text, rather than any earlier version, became the measure on which presidential action rested. For the researcher, the enrolled bill closes the comparative chain. Every earlier version is measured against it, every amendment is judged by whether it reached it, and every document in the surrounding record is classified by its distance from it. The chain from introduction through report, passage in each chamber and enrollment gives the history its skeleton, and the remaining documents supply context around a structure that the versions have already established.

The enrolled bill also carries a legal rule that fixes the limits of inquiry into its own authenticity. Under the enrolled bill rule, stated in Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), courts do not look behind a duly authenticated enrolled bill to the journals to dispute that it passed. The rule treats the authenticated enrolled document as conclusive on the question of passage, so a challenge arguing that the text presented to the President differed from the text a chamber approved cannot be sustained by resort to the internal journals of the House or the Senate. The strength of the rule lies in its finality. Litigation over the meaning of a statute proceeds from a settled text whose enactment the courts accept on the authority of the enrollment itself, and the researcher compiling a history works under the same settlement, treating the enrolled bill as the fixed endpoint of the version sequence rather than as one more document open to contradiction by less formal records.

Hearings form the second great class of documents in a legislative history, and the class differs from the versions in a fundamental respect. A hearing recorded testimony of witnesses and material submitted for the record, gathered by a committee as the committee informed itself about a subject or a pending measure. The participants whose words fill a hearing volume were, in the main, not legislators at all. Agency officials, affected interests, subject specialists and members of the public appeared to persuade, to warn, to supply data or to place grievances before the committee, and the published hearing preserved their contributions together with the questions and exchanges through which committee members tested them. A hearing volume therefore reads as a record of inquiry rather than a record of decision, and everything in the interpretation of hearings follows from that character.

The contents of a published hearing reward a researcher for reasons that have little to do with judicial weight. Witnesses frequently supplied the factual foundation on which legislation rested, in the form of statistics, technical descriptions, accounts of conditions under an existing law and projections of the effect of a proposed change. Exhibits and statements submitted for the record extended that foundation beyond the oral testimony, and the questions from committee members revealed which facts the committee doubted, which consequences the committee feared and which alternatives the committee wished explored. A researcher reconstructing the problem that a statute addressed, the vocabulary that specialists used for that problem or the range of solutions under consideration at the time of the inquiry finds in hearings a density of material available nowhere else in the record, because no other document class was designed to assemble outside knowledge in bulk before decisions hardened into text.

Against that research value stands a practical limitation on availability. Not every hearing is published. A committee could hear witnesses and leave the proceeding without a published volume, so the absence of a hearing volume from a library or a database establishes only that no published record circulated, not that no inquiry occurred. The limitation shapes search strategy. A researcher who finds no published hearing for a measure turns to other document classes rather than treating the silence as evidence about the measure, and a researcher who finds a published hearing for a related measure on the same subject weighs whether testimony addressed to a different bill can illuminate the bill under study at all. Publication gaps also widen for older material and for proceedings that a committee regarded as routine, so the surviving hearing record represents a selection shaped by publication decisions as well as by the inquiries themselves.

Judicial treatment of hearings sits at the lower end of the interpretive scale, and the set of reasons is consistent. Testimony precedes the final text, often by a considerable interval, so witnesses addressed proposals that later drafting may have transformed. Witnesses are not legislators, so a witness statement expresses the understanding or the hope of a person outside the body whose action made the law. A committee hears conflicting witnesses without endorsing the testimony received, so presence in a hearing volume signals only that a view reached the committee, not that the committee adopted the view. For those reasons courts give hearings low weight as interpretive evidence while researchers value them for the factual record Congress assembled, and the two judgments coexist without contradiction, because the questions differ. A court asks what the enacting body decided. A researcher asks what the enacting body knew, heard and considered, and hearings answer the second question better than the first.

Committee prints form the third class, and the class is defined by what the documents are not. A committee print was a document printed at a committee's direction for the committee's own use, such as a staff study, a compilation of existing law, or a draft measure circulated for consideration. Committee prints are not committee reports, carry no report number in the House Report or Senate Report series, and were never voted on as explanations of a reported bill. The distinctions are bibliographic, and bibliographic distinctions in legislative research carry interpretive consequences, because a document's authority in a history depends on the capacity in which the document issued. A print issued for internal use, however learned its contents, spoke for its authors rather than for a committee acting institutionally, and citation practice preserves that difference by identifying prints as prints rather than allowing the prestige of a report to attach to a document that never passed through the reporting process.

The typical contents of prints explain why researchers consult them despite their limited authority. A staff study could assemble the background of a subject in greater depth than a report format allowed, tracing the administration of an existing law, comparing the law of different jurisdictions or presenting options that a committee had under consideration without recommending among them. A compilation of existing law gathered the scattered statutory material bearing on a subject into a single working document, sparing the committee repeated resort to separate volumes and sparing later researchers the same labor. A draft measure printed as a committee print exposed proposed language to scrutiny before introduction or before a reporting decision, and such drafts occasionally supply an additional point of comparison for the version sequence, since language tested in a print and language later introduced can be set beside each other. In each case the print served the committee's preparation, and preparation documents illuminate the range of material before the committee even where the documents cannot establish what the committee decided.

Confusion between prints and reports is the standing hazard of the class, and the hazard has practical consequences for citation and for argument. A report accompanied a measure ordered reported and explained that measure to the parent chamber. A print explained nothing on a chamber's behalf, arrived at no institutional conclusion and bound no member. A researcher who cites a staff study as though a committee had adopted the study attributes institutional weight to individual work, and a court given that attribution has reason to discount the citation entirely. Accurate identification also protects the genuine value of prints. A compilation prepared by committee staff may be the most convenient accurate statement of prior law available for a subject, and a study may preserve data collected nowhere else, and those virtues survive honest labeling, since the virtues rest on quality and convenience rather than on an authority the documents never claimed.

Related executive branch documents stand at the edge of the history rather than within it, and one class illustrates the boundary. Signing statements, issued by the President when signing a bill, are executive documents, not produced by Congress and not passed through bicameral procedure, and are therefore generally classified outside legislative history proper, though litigants sometimes offer them as evidence of the executive understanding of a statute and any weight is contested. Transmittal material arriving with a presidential proposal occupies a comparable position on the other side of the process, originating outside the chambers whose documents compose the history itself.

Taken together, the versions, the hearings and the prints give a legislative history its documentary spine and its documentary bulk, and the classes answer different questions. The versions show what the process did to a text, stage by stage, ending in an enrolled bill whose authentication the courts accept as conclusive on passage. The hearings show what the process heard while the text was forming, preserved unevenly because publication never covered every proceeding. The prints show what the process prepared for its own work, internal in character, unnumbered in the report series and unvoted on in any institutional sense. A history that keeps the classes distinct and assigns each document the weight its origin supports rests on the documents themselves rather than on the loudest voice found among them.

## The Committee Report and the Conference Report

A researcher tracing the meaning of a statutory provision encounters many kinds of paper, and the order in which that paper is read shapes the quality of the answer that emerges. Floor debate sprawls across days and speakers. Hearings gather testimony from witnesses who addressed a measure before its final language settled. Amendments appear, fail, or pass in sequences that can be difficult to reconstruct after the fact. Against that scattered record, the committee report stands apart because a committee that investigated a problem, heard evidence, and amended a text then explained the resulting measure in a single institutional document. For that reason, the committee report has long been treated as the single most useful document for understanding what a provision was intended to do, and research into legislative intent that begins elsewhere risks building an interpretation on fragments before the central explanation has been absorbed.

The standing of the report rests on proximity to the drafting decision. A committee report is the considered institutional explanation closest to the drafting, produced by the body charged with studying the measure in detail rather than by an individual member speaking in the pressure of floor consideration. Colleagues who lacked a seat on the reporting committee relied on that explanation when deciding how to vote, and courts and practitioners reading the statute after enactment inherited the same reliance. The report does not float free of the text. It accompanies a specific version of a measure at the moment the measure leaves committee, and its analysis follows the structure of that version section by section. That tight correspondence between explanation and text gives the report a discipline that general debate lacks, because a reader can move from a disputed phrase in the bill to the committee discussion of the same section and test whether the explanation actually addresses the language under examination.

Placement within a larger hierarchy reinforces the same point without diminishing the report in the run of ordinary research. A conference report, when one exists, carries greater weight because a conference document speaks for both chambers on the final text. In the far more common situation in which no conference stage occurred, or in which the disputed provision never reached conference in altered form, the committee report supplies the fullest explanation available. Sponsor and floor manager statements rank behind it, hearings and general debate rank behind those statements, and material produced after enactment carries little weight in the hierarchy described for the series. The practical consequence for research method is straightforward. The report is read first, read in full, and read against the exact text it explained, and other sources are then used to confirm, qualify, or challenge the account the report provides rather than to substitute a looser account gathered from scattered remarks.

### What does a committee report contain?

A committee report contains a statement of the measure's purpose, a section by section analysis, a cost estimate, a showing of changes to existing law, and any dissenting, minority or supplemental views, with Senate reports also recording roll call votes taken on ordering the measure reported to the chamber for action.

Each element in that inventory performs a distinct function, and a careful reading treats the elements as parts of one argument rather than as interchangeable background. The purpose statement frames the problem the committee believed the measure addressed and the object the committee believed the measure pursued. A purpose statement read in isolation can mislead, because statements of purpose are written at a level of generality that many applications can claim to serve. Read together with the operative analysis, however, the purpose statement disciplines interpretation by identifying the mischief the committee placed at the center of deliberation. When a later dispute concerns an application far from that stated problem, the distance itself becomes an interpretive fact worth weighing. When the application sits squarely within the stated problem, the purpose statement strengthens a reading grounded in the section analysis. The value lies in the combination, and research that quotes a purpose clause without testing it against the detailed discussion that follows has used only the thinnest part of the document.

The section by section analysis carries the greatest share of the explanatory burden. In that portion, the committee walks through the measure provision by provision and explains what each part does, how each part interacts with law already in force, and what change, if any, each part effects in practice. For a researcher pursuing the meaning of one disputed sentence, the corresponding passage in the section by section analysis is the passage most likely to address the sentence directly, in the order in which the sentence appears, with attention to the terms the sentence employs. Definitions receive attention there. Cross references receive attention there. Effective date provisions, conforming amendments, and rules of construction that attract little notice during floor debate receive attention there as well. The analysis also reveals structure, because a committee explaining a measure sequentially discloses how the parts were understood to fit together, and structural understanding often resolves ambiguity that a single sentence, extracted from context, appears to present.

Production of the report followed the committee decision to order a measure reported, and that sequence matters for the weight the document carries. The path from hearing room deliberation to a filed report ran through the markup process described in [Committees, Markups and Gatekeeping](/2022/03/01/committee-system-and-markups/), where text was amended and ordered reported before an explanation was prepared for the chamber. Because the explanation followed the amendment decisions, the report describes the measure as amended rather than the measure as introduced, and a researcher comparing versions gains a check against a common error. Explanations attached to an introduced draft may describe language that markup removed, softened, or relocated, and reliance on such explanations without attention to sequence can transfer intent from discarded language to enacted language that carries a different reach. The report, anchored to the ordered text, reduces that risk and supplies a fixed point against which earlier and later versions can be measured.

The showing of changes to existing law gives the report a comparative function that no other portion performs in the same visual form. Many measures amend statutes already codified, and the legal effect of an amendment depends entirely on the relationship between new language and old language. A showing that places the two side by side converts an abstract description of change into a direct comparison. Deletions become visible as deletions. Insertions become visible as insertions. A researcher can observe whether a contested term entered the statute through the measure under study, survived from earlier law without alteration, or replaced a different term that carried a settled meaning. Each observation points interpretation in a different direction. Language carried forward unchanged invites a different inference from language deliberately substituted, and the comparative showing allows that distinction to be drawn from the face of the report rather than reconstructed laboriously from separate printings of the statute at different dates.

Two chamber specific rules govern that comparative showing, and each rule carries the name of the legislator associated with its adoption or its practice. In the House of Representatives, the Ramseyer rule, at House Rule XIII, clause 3(e), was adopted on January 28, 1929, and named for Representative Christian W. Ramseyer of Iowa. The rule provides that a report on a measure amending or repealing existing law must print the text proposed to be repealed and a comparative print showing omissions in stricken-through type and insertions in italics. The typographic convention deserves attention from any researcher consulting older reports in print or in scanned form. Stricken-through type marks language that the measure would remove from existing law, and italics mark language that the measure would insert. A reader unfamiliar with the convention can invert the meaning of a page entirely, treating language slated for removal as language the committee proposed to retain, and familiarity with the Ramseyer presentation therefore belongs to basic competence in report research.

In the Senate, the Cordon rule, at Senate Rule XXVI, paragraph 12, named for Senator Guy Cordon of Oregon, imposes the Senate equivalent comparative-print duty. The parallel structure reflects a shared institutional judgment across both chambers that amendments to existing law ought to be presented comparatively, so that members voting on a measure and readers consulting the record afterward can perceive the change without independent reconstruction. The Cordon rule contains one qualification that the Ramseyer statement lacks. A committee may dispense with the comparative-print duty only by stating in the report that doing so is necessary to expedite the business of the Senate. That qualification carries research significance beyond its procedural function. An absent comparative print in a Senate report, accompanied by the required statement, signals that expedition displaced a normal safeguard, and a researcher confronting ambiguity in such a measure lacks a tool that the chamber rules ordinarily guarantee. The absence cannot supply intent, but the absence explains a gap in the record and warns against treating silence in the report as a deliberate substantive choice by the committee.

Cost estimation in committee reports follows a uniform frame that supports comparison across measures and across Congresses. Congressional Budget Office estimates cover the first fiscal year affected and the following four years, under section 308(a) of the Congressional Budget Act and the report requirements of House Rule XIII. The five year presentation window gives a researcher more than a single figure for a single year. A pattern across the window can show whether a committee expected costs to rise as a program reached full operation, to fall as a transition completed, or to remain level as a steady administrative expense. Estimates also locate responsibility. Because the estimates come from the Congressional Budget Office rather than from the reporting committee alone, the figures represent an external assessment placed inside the committee document, and later readers can distinguish the committee argument about a measure from the budgetary assessment the committee was required to confront and include.

Views beyond the majority account form the final standard element, and those views often prove disproportionately valuable to interpretation. A report may include dissenting, minority or supplemental views, and Senate reports also record roll call votes taken on ordering the measure reported. Dissenting views identify the points at which members of the committee itself rejected the majority explanation, and a dissent that predicts a particular application of disputed language, met by silence or by rebuttal in the majority discussion, furnishes material that courts have weighed in assessing how a provision was understood inside the committee. Minority views can also perform the opposite service, confirming that a reading later advanced as controversial was in fact the reading shared across a divided committee. Supplemental views occupy a middle position, adding emphasis, qualification, or additional reasoning from members who supported the decision to report. The roll call record in a Senate report adds a further datum, the margin by which the committee acted, and a narrow margin paired with extensive dissenting views presents a different institutional picture from a unanimous report carrying no additional views at all.

The conference report stands at the summit of the hierarchy because the conference stage, when a measure reaches that stage, produces the version on which both chambers finally act. A conference report reconciles the two chambers' versions of a measure after each chamber has passed a differing text. Accompanying the report is a joint explanatory statement prepared by the managers of both chambers explaining the agreement. The managers, also termed conferees, negotiate the resolution of differences between the House-passed and Senate-passed texts, and the joint explanatory statement records the reasoning by which the managers settled each difference that the conference addressed. Because the statement explains the final text rather than a text that one chamber alone approved, and because the explanation issues from representatives of both chambers acting together, courts and researchers assign the conference report and its joint explanatory statement the heaviest weight among legislative history documents.

The explanatory value of a joint statement follows from the nature of compromise itself. A provision that survived conference often represents a midpoint, a trade, or a deliberate selection of one chamber approach over the other, and none of those origins can be inferred reliably from the bare text. The joint explanatory statement can identify which chamber position prevailed on a given point, which position yielded, and what understanding accompanied the selection. For a researcher, that identification converts speculation about bargaining into documented institutional explanation. The statement also addresses the text in its final arrangement, after amendments adopted in conference have renumbered, consolidated, or recast provisions that appeared in different form in the separate committee reports. Reliance on a committee report for a provision that conference substantially recast risks attributing to the enacted text an explanation written for language that never became law in that form, and consultation of the joint statement guards against exactly that error.

Given that weight, the rarity of the conference document in the closing decades of the twentieth century and the opening decade of the twenty-first century altered the practical character of legislative history research. Formal conference committees, once a routine method for resolving differences between chambers, met far less often as an alternative practice of amendment exchange handled a growing share of bicameral resolution. Amendment exchange, under which chambers pass a measure back and forth with amendments rather than convening a formal conference, resolves disagreement without producing a conference report or a joint explanatory statement. The researcher consequence is direct. Measures resolved through amendment exchange leave committee reports, floor statements, and the texts of successive amendments as the principal record, and the single document that speaks for both chambers on the final text never comes into existence for those measures at all.

The contraction has been counted rather than merely asserted, and the counts define the scale of the change. Formal conferences fell from 38 in the 106th Congress, covering 1999 to 2000, to 9 in the 110th Congress, covering 2007 to 2008, per counts compiled from House and Senate records in an analysis by the Center for Congressional and Presidential Studies at American University. A decline of that magnitude over the span between those Congresses means that a method of research built around locating a conference report succeeds for a shrinking fraction of enactments. The scarcity of that bicameral explanation, examined in [Conference Committees and Amendments](/2022/03/15/conference-committees-explained/), followed a measurable contraction in formal conferences between the 106th Congress and the 110th Congress. Amendment exchange, the practice that filled much of the resulting space, resolved roughly 11 to 24 percent of public laws from 1992 to 2007, according to CRS Report RL34611. That range, spanning fifteen years of enactments, places the alternative method at a substantial and persistent level rather than at the margin of occasional use.

Research method adapted to the documented pattern without any change in the underlying hierarchy of weight. Absence of a conference report for a given enactment conveys no negative inference about the enactment itself, because the route a measure traveled depended on chamber strategy, timing, and the scale of disagreement rather than on the importance of the measure. Major enactments and minor enactments alike traveled the amendment exchange route during the period covered by the CRS figures. The researcher task therefore begins with a threshold determination of the path the measure followed. Bill records and action histories establish whether a conference occurred, whether a conference report was filed, and whether a joint explanatory statement accompanies the report in the record. A search for a conference document that was never produced wastes effort and can generate a false impression of an incomplete archive, while a failure to locate a conference document that was produced forfeits the most heavily weighted explanation available for the final text.

The two report forms together thus supply the documentary core of a compiled legislative history, and mastery of the two forms separates reliable research from quotation gathering. The committee report contributes purpose, sequential analysis, cost estimation across a defined five year window, a comparative showing governed in the House by the Ramseyer rule and in the Senate by the Cordon rule, and the preserved disagreement of dissenting, minority and supplemental views, supplemented in the Senate by the roll call record. The conference report contributes reconciliation of chamber differences and a joint explanatory statement from the managers of both chambers, carrying the heaviest weight in the hierarchy for the provisions the conference resolved. The documented decline in formal conferences, from the count recorded for the 106th Congress to the count recorded for the 110th Congress, means that the committee report bears the principal explanatory burden for a large share of enactments within the period studied by CRS Report RL34611 and by the Center for Congressional and Presidential Studies analysis. Competence in reading that burden bearing document, in full and in proper sequence, remains the foundation on which every other source in a legislative history is arranged and judged.

## The Congressional Record and the Limits of the Transcript

The Congressional Record offers the most complete published account of proceedings in the House of Representatives and the Senate arranged by legislative day. A researcher tracing a bill through debate, amendment, and final passage turns to the Record to reconstruct the sequence of consideration and to locate statements by sponsors, floor managers, supporters, and opponents. The value of that account depends on a precise understanding of its status. By law and long practice, the Congressional Record is a substantially verbatim account of proceedings rather than a strictly verbatim transcript. The adjective carries the research problem. A substantially verbatim account preserves the substance and order of debate in a form close to delivery, but the published page does not guarantee that every sentence appeared in the chamber in exactly the printed form, or that every printed sentence was spoken in the chamber at all. A citation to the Record therefore identifies a published congressional document with a defined relationship to floor proceedings, and careful research preserves that distinction.

The distinction matters because legislative history research often treats floor statements as evidence of how a measure was understood during consideration. A statement by a sponsor or floor manager may receive weight because colleagues heard explanations, relied on representations, and voted against the background of an announced understanding. That chain of reasoning depends on delivery. If a passage entered the Record through revision, extension, or insertion, the passage forms part of the documentary record created by a member, but the passage lacks the same direct connection to the exchange on the floor. Other members had no opportunity to hear a qualification that reached print after debate, to question an inserted document during consideration, or to answer an argument that arrived through the Extensions of Remarks section. The researcher who ignores the production method of the Record risks assigning floor significance to material that functioned as a written supplement, and the error compounds when a later argument quotes a passage without identifying placement, typeface, or status as delivered debate, revised debate, or undelivered insertion.

Revision and extension describe two related but distinct alterations. Revision concerns remarks delivered during proceedings, rendered in print in a form shaped by member review. Extension concerns the enlargement of remarks beyond the delivered statement, so that the printed version contains material that extends past the spoken exchange. Insertion reaches further. With permission, a member may insert material never delivered on the floor, and the Record may therefore contain speeches, documents, and supporting matter that belong to the publication history of a measure without belonging to its oral debate. None of those practices turns the Record into a fabrication, because the practices operate under chamber rules and permissions and the publication signals several categories of inserted matter through typography and placement. The practices do, however, define the limits named in the heading of this section. The Record is a record of proceedings prepared through an authorized editorial process that includes member participation after delivery. A transcript in the strict sense would close the text at the moment of utterance, subject to correction of transcription error. The Record leaves the text open through recognized channels, and a researcher reads the result with attention to the channel that produced a given passage.

### How can a reader tell which remarks were actually spoken?
A reader compares typeface and placement within the daily Record. Material printed in the distinctive typeface reserved for insertions was not part of the substantially verbatim account of floor debate, and material placed in Extensions of Remarks was submitted for publication rather than delivered during proceedings on the floor itself.

House Practice, citing the House Rules and Manual at section 687, states the governing requirement for the House. Material inserted under permission to extend remarks must be clearly distinguishable, by different typeface, from the substantially verbatim account in the daily Record. The rule attaches to the daily edition because the daily edition is the publication closest to proceedings, the form in which debate reaches readers on the following day and in which the distinction between delivered debate and inserted matter carries immediate research significance. The different typeface operates as a visible boundary inside the text. On one side of the boundary, the Record presents the substantially verbatim account of proceedings. On the other side, the Record presents matter admitted by permission. A researcher who reads across that boundary without noticing the change in typeface treats two kinds of authority as one. A researcher who records the boundary preserves a fact about the document that may later determine whether a passage supports an argument about floor understanding, member intention, or public explanation after debate.

The requirement carries institutional enforcement. The Speaker has instructed the Official Reporters of Debates to adhere strictly to the requirement that inserted material appear in a distinguishable typeface. That instruction matters to research method because the signal does not rest on the preference of an individual member or on an informal printing habit. The Official Reporters of Debates stand at the point where spoken proceedings become published proceedings, and the instruction directs those reporters to preserve the visual separation between the two categories of content. Strict adherence gives the reader a basis for reliance, provided the reader consults a form of the Record that preserves the typography. A facsimile page, a paginated file, or a printed daily issue carries the signal directly. A plain transcription may carry the words while discarding the feature that identifies the status of the words. Legislative research on a disputed passage therefore returns to the paginated Record before drawing a conclusion about delivery, because the conclusion depends on physical features of the publication that a stripped text may fail to reproduce.

Timing supplies a second signal that works alongside typeface. Extensions submitted on the day of consideration appear immediately after general debate in the different typeface described by section 687. Proximity to general debate in the daily Record does not convert an extension into delivered debate, and the typeface prevents that inference. The placement instead reflects the day of submission and keeps the extension near the proceedings to which the extension relates. Material submitted later follows a different route. Later material goes to the Extensions of Remarks section, a separate part of the House Record reserved for matter submitted after the day of consideration or otherwise placed apart from the sequence of debate. The two routes reward attention to chronology. An extension printed after general debate in distinctive typeface belongs to the day of consideration as a submission, even though the words may never have sounded in the chamber. An item in Extensions of Remarks belongs to a later submission track, and the separation from the debate sequence reinforces the understanding that the item served publication rather than oral persuasion during the pending question.

The Extensions of Remarks section has a defined character of its own. The section carries speeches never delivered and extraneous material such as articles and letters, and everything in the section appears in a distinctive typeface, as described in CRS Report RL30136, The Congressional Record. The breadth of eligible material explains the breadth of the section. A member may place a tribute, a policy statement, a constituent letter, a reprinted article, or a speech prepared but not delivered into Extensions of Remarks, creating a public document associated with the member and with the legislative day of publication. For a biographer or political historian, that association may be the point of the research, because the section preserves positions members wished to publish under congressional authority. For a statutory interpreter, the association cuts the other way. An article reprinted in Extensions of Remarks records a member decision to republish the article, not a chamber decision to adopt the reasoning in the article. A letter printed in the section records the contents of the letter as submitted, not testimony heard by a committee or argument answered on the floor. The distinctive typeface and the separate section give fair notice of that difference, and a citation that omits the section name withholds context from any reader who later evaluates the passage.

The daily edition has carried a lettered pagination system since 1967 that makes section identification part of every page reference. Daily edition page numbers carry letter prefixes, with S for Senate proceedings, H for House proceedings, E for Extensions of Remarks, and D for Daily Digest. The prefix performs real research work. A page beginning with H belongs to House proceedings, a page beginning with S belongs to Senate proceedings, a page beginning with E belongs to Extensions of Remarks, and a page beginning with D belongs to the Daily Digest. The E prefix in particular reinforces the typeface signal, because a citation to an E page announces before a reader reaches the text that the passage sits outside the main proceedings sequence. A citation without the prefix, copied from a source that dropped the letter, loses that announcement and may mislead a reader about the character of the material. Researchers therefore preserve the full daily citation, letter included, whenever a note relies on the daily edition, and a bibliography that normalizes away the prefixes erases information that the publication deliberately encoded in pagination.

The permanent bound edition repaginates the Record continuously, drops the letter prefixes, and may incorporate further editing. Repagination creates a second citation system for the same proceedings, and the two systems do not map page for page onto one another. A daily page and the corresponding bound page carry different numbers, and a researcher moving between editions must relocate a passage by date, chamber, and context rather than by page number alone. The disappearance of the letter prefixes in the bound edition also removes one quick signal of section identity, even though the bound edition retains the organization of proceedings and sections through headings and sequence. Further editing adds a third consideration. The bound edition represents the settled published form of the Record after an opportunity for additional correction, and that settled quality explains the citation preference that follows. The same settled quality means a researcher investigating the exact state of the daily publication, including typeface distinctions applied on the day after debate, consults the daily edition as the primary artifact. Edition choice follows the research question, and neither edition excuses a reader from identifying the section and status of a quoted passage.

Citation practice prefers the bound edition once the bound edition exists. The preference gives scholars, courts, and agencies a stable reference point, with continuous pagination across a volume and a text settled after the daily issues have passed through further editing. A citation to the bound edition can be located in any complete set of the bound volumes without reference to the daily issues or to the lettered pagination of a single day. Stability, however, does not erase the methodological distinctions developed through the daily edition. A bound citation identifies where a passage may be found in the permanent publication, and the passage retains the character the passage held at insertion or delivery. An extension does not become delivered debate through republication in bound form. Material in Extensions of Remarks does not move into the proceedings sequence through repagination. The preference for the bound edition governs citation form, not evidentiary character, and a careful writer pairs a bound citation with a description of section and status whenever that status bears on the weight assigned to the passage.

The Record did not begin the published history of congressional debate, and the predecessor publications extend the same source problems backward across the first decades of Congress. The Congressional Record dates from 1873, in the 43rd Congress, and a researcher seeking debate before that year works through a sequence of earlier publications with different origins and different claims on accuracy. The Annals of Congress cover 1789 to 1824. The Register of Debates covers 1824 to 1837. The Congressional Globe covers 1833 to 1873. The Congressional Record follows from 1873. The sequence contains an overlap between the Register of Debates and the Congressional Globe from 1833 to 1837, and the overlap itself teaches a lesson about the era before a single official record. Successive publications reported debate for overlapping years, and the researcher of the early republic reads debate through publications assembled under conditions far removed from an official reporting staff bound by instructions from the Speaker. HeinOnline carries the debate set in full, including the Annals of Congress, the Register of Debates, the Congressional Globe, and the Congressional Record, and digital availability of the full set can obscure the differences among the components unless a researcher attends to the origin of each title in the sequence.

The Annals of Congress demand the greatest caution in the sequence. Joseph Gales and William Seaton compiled the Annals retrospectively between 1834 and 1856, drawing on newspaper accounts to reconstruct debate for the period from 1789 to 1824. Retrospective compilation separates the Annals from any transcript made during proceedings. No reporter captured the debates of the first Congresses for the Annals at the moment of delivery with later publication in view. Instead, editors working decades after the earliest debates assembled a record from newspaper reporting, a source base shaped by the practices, space limits, political sympathies, and reporting resources of early newspapers. A speech in the Annals therefore represents a reconstruction of remarks as filtered through newspaper publication and later editorial assembly. Some debates survive in fuller form because newspapers printed extended accounts, and other debates survive in summary or in fragments because newspaper coverage was brief. A researcher quoting the Annals identifies the publication as a reconstruction, treats exact wording with reserve, and corroborates important passages against journals, bill texts, and other contemporary evidence where corroboration is possible. The Annals remain indispensable because no fuller debate record exists for the period covered, and indispensability does not cure the defects of the source.

The Register of Debates covers 1824 to 1837 and brings the researcher closer to the period of debate without removing the need for caution. The Register belongs to an era when published debate depended on reporting and editing practices that preceded the official structure of the later Record, and the set appeared before Congress settled debate publication into the form the Record later supplied. A passage in the Register reports debate through the mediation of the publication that produced the volume, and a researcher weighs the passage accordingly. Questions that arise for the Record, including whether a member revised a statement for print and whether material entered the publication without delivery, arise in a less standardized setting for the Register, without a settled typographic code comparable to section 687 to answer them. The absence of a uniform signal system is itself a research finding. Where the daily Record marks insertion through typeface and separates Extensions of Remarks from proceedings, the earlier publications offer fewer internal markers, and a researcher compensates through corroboration, comparison across sources from the same period, and restraint in drawing conclusions from a single reported sentence.

The Congressional Globe covers 1833 to 1873 and carries debate publication to the threshold of the Record era. The Globe offers a far more continuous account of debate than the reconstructions and earlier reports that precede the Globe in the sequence, and researchers rely on the Globe for the debates of the middle nineteenth century. Continuity does not equal official verbatim status. The Globe predates the Congressional Record and the reporting regime that the Record embodied from 1873, and a researcher treats Globe debate as reported debate rather than as a transcript certified under the practices governing the later publication. Practices of revision, condensation, and reporting judgment belong to the history of the Globe as a publication, and the researcher who needs the exact language of an amendment, a motion, or a vote turns from debate reporting to the journals and bill records that document formal action. The transition from Globe to Record in 1873 marks a genuine change in the documentary basis of legislative research, with the Record presenting debate as an official and substantially verbatim account of proceedings from the 43rd Congress forward. Even after that change, the central caution of the Record era applies in stronger form to the Globe era, because the printed word and the spoken word stand at a greater distance from one another in a publication assembled without the later official framework.

## The Hierarchy of Weight in Legislative History

A legislative history rarely speaks with one voice. A single enactment can leave behind a conference report, committee reports, hearings held before the text took final shape, hours of floor debate, and statements made after passage. The hierarchy of weight sorts that uneven record by institutional authority rather than by convenience or by how neatly a passage supports a preferred reading. The ranking runs from the conference report and its joint explanatory statement at the top, through the committee report, through statements by the sponsor or floor manager, through hearings and general floor debate, and down to statements by opponents and to anything said after enactment, which receive little weight.

The logic of the hierarchy rests on three questions. The first asks whose explanation a document carries, since an explanation offered by a body that acted on the measure carries a different status from an observation offered by a witness, a critic, or a later observer. The second asks how close the document stands to the final text, and the third asks what the speaker had at stake. A document rises when it speaks institutionally, addresses the enacted text, and comes from participants responsible for that text, and a document falls when it speaks individually, addresses a version that changed, or comes from participants whose purpose was to stop the measure or to reshape its meaning afterward.

At the top stands the conference report, accompanied by its joint explanatory statement, treated as the most heavily weighted legislative history document. A conference report reconciles the two chambers' versions of a measure after the House and the Senate have passed different texts, and the joint explanatory statement, prepared by the managers of both chambers, explains the agreement that the conference reached. That pairing holds a quality no earlier document possesses, since the conference document speaks for both chambers rather than for one chamber acting alone, and the document speaks on the final text rather than on a proposal that one chamber favored before negotiation altered the proposal. The conference document explains the settlement that both chambers accepted as the basis for final passage, and interpretation seeks exactly that settlement when the enacted words leave a question open.

The committee report occupies the second tier, below the conference report and above individual statements of any kind, as the considered institutional explanation closest to the drafting. A report is institutional because a committee issues the report as a body reporting a measure to a chamber, and a report is considered because the report assembles the committee's account in structured form rather than leaving that account scattered across debate. A standard committee report contains a statement of the measure's purpose, a section by section analysis, a cost estimate, a showing of changes to existing law, and any dissenting, minority, or supplemental views, and Senate reports also record roll call votes taken on ordering the measure reported.

The committee report's proximity to the drafting adds a further advantage, since the committee stage is where a measure takes systematic shape and where the reporting committee settles the language that the chamber will debate. The comparative print required under the Ramseyer rule in the House, House Rule XIII, clause 3(e), adopted on January 28, 1929 and named for Representative Christian W. Ramseyer of Iowa, illustrates the report's documentary discipline, since a report on a measure amending or repealing existing law must print the text proposed to be repealed and a comparative print showing omissions in stricken-through type and insertions in italics. Cost estimates prepared by the Congressional Budget Office, covering the first fiscal year affected and the following four years, add a further institutional component to the report's authority.

Below the committee report stand statements by the sponsor or floor manager, and the gap between the tiers is a gap between an institutional document and an individual voice. A sponsor nevertheless holds a defined place above hearings and general debate, because the sponsor speaks for the measure, introduces and carries the bill, presents the bill's purposes to colleagues, and answers for the bill's provisions during consideration, while the floor manager performs the same function during debate on the floor. When colleagues vote, colleagues rely on the sponsor's representations about what the measure does and does not do. That reliance is the foundation of the sponsor's weight.

The limits on a sponsor's statement follow from the same reasoning that grants the weight, since a sponsor speaks as an individual, however central the role, and an individual statement lacks the structured, collective character of a committee report and the bicameral character of a conference report. Statements that explain the text under consideration or clarify the effect of an amendment stand at the strongest end of the tier, while statements ranging beyond the pending language sit lower within the tier. Placement in the Congressional Record adds a caution across the floor tiers, since the Record is by law and long practice a substantially verbatim account of proceedings rather than a strictly verbatim transcript, and members may revise and extend remarks and, with permission, insert material never delivered on the floor.

### Why does a sponsor's statement outweigh an opponent's?

A sponsor's statement outweighs an opponent's because colleagues rely on the sponsor's representations when voting, while an opponent describes a measure in the terms most likely to defeat it. The sponsor speaks for the measure. The opponent speaks against it, often at its most alarming.

The contrast between sponsor and opponent illustrates the incentive principle that runs through the whole hierarchy. The sponsor's incentive aligns with accurate description in a specific and limited way, because passage depends on persuading colleagues that the measure does what supporters claim, within limits that colleagues will accept, and the sponsor's representations form part of the basis on which colleagues cast votes carrying political and legal consequences. A sponsor's clarification of ambiguous language therefore receives weight that a casual remark by an uninvolved member does not, because the clarification purports to state the meaning on which assent was solicited.

The opponent's position reverses every element of that arrangement, since an opponent describes a bill that the opponent is trying to defeat, and opponents often characterize a measure at its most alarming. A legislator seeking to defeat a bill gains nothing by presenting its provisions in the most moderate or most probable application, and the rhetorical task runs toward the broadest plausible consequence, the hardest case, and the application most likely to alarm undecided colleagues. The hierarchy responds by discounting the statement rather than by sorting sincere warnings from tactical warnings passage by passage, because opponents' descriptions given equal weight would tilt interpretation toward the reading offered by the side that lost.

The low placement does not erase a narrow use for opponents' statements, since debate records the objections placed before a chamber, and a majority that passes a measure after hearing a stated objection has proceeded with that objection in view. A researcher can use an opponent's statement to establish that a concern was aired, that a particular consequence was predicted, or that an alternative reading was presented and left unadopted. The statement cannot reliably establish the meaning of the enacted text, because the speaker's purpose was to characterize that text in the manner most damaging to the text's prospects. The distinction between evidence of what was said and evidence of what a statute means is among the most useful disciplines the hierarchy imposes, since the whole record remains available for historical purposes while interpretive authority is reserved for documents whose production circumstances support that authority.

Hearings sit in the tier below sponsor and floor manager statements for reasons tied to authorship and timing. Hearings record the testimony of witnesses and material submitted for the record, and witnesses are not legislators, while testimony also precedes the final text, since hearings inform the drafting and revision that produce the language later reported and passed. A witness may address a problem with precision while addressing a version of the solution that a committee later revises, narrows, or abandons, and not every hearing is published, which adds further unevenness to the source. Courts accordingly give hearings low weight as interpretive evidence, while researchers value hearings for the factual record that Congress assembled, since a hearing can show what information reached the legislature and what concerns witnesses pressed, without any assumption that a witness's preferred construction became the construction of the enacting chambers.

General floor debate occupies the same low region for reasons tied to volume, variety, and production. Debate includes the sponsor and the floor manager, whose statements the hierarchy lifts into a higher tier, and debate also includes every other member who speaks, supportive, hostile, tangential, or focused on a local concern, so a transcript contains careful explanations of pending language beside objections framed for effect and remarks addressed to audiences outside the chamber. That heterogeneity limits the weight of any isolated passage, since a single speech represents one member's intervention in a proceeding where the chamber acted by vote rather than by adopting the reasoning of any one speaker.

At the bottom of the hierarchy, receiving little weight alongside statements by opponents, stands anything said after enactment, on a ground concerning time and institutional identity, since a later Congress cannot speak for the enacting one. Members who explain a statute years after passage, in debate on an unrelated measure, in a report on proposed amendments, or in statements offered during later oversight, did not cast the votes that enacted the language under interpretation, and many held no seat in the enacting Congress at all. Whatever the quality of the later statement as commentary, the statement lacks the feature that gives the higher tiers authority, namely participation in the decision that made the text law, a decision taken through a specific alignment of members under the constitutional requirement that identical text pass both chambers and be presented to the President.

The post enactment category reaches more than stray remarks, since explanations offered in later reports, debates over later amendments that failed, and characterizations of an existing statute offered during consideration of new proposals share the same structural defect when offered as evidence of an earlier statute's meaning. The discount therefore protects the distinction between interpreting a statute and revising a statute. The enrolled bill, the final version passed identically by both chambers, authenticated by the presiding officers and presented to the President, marks the act that made law, and under the enrolled bill rule associated with Field v. Clark, courts do not look behind a duly authenticated enrolled bill to the journals to dispute that the bill passed.

The hierarchy disciplines research practice as directly as the hierarchy disciplines judicial reasoning, and the discipline can be stated as a reading order in which a researcher reads downward from the top. The conference report and joint explanatory statement come first, because those documents address the final text with the authority of both chambers behind the explanation, committee reports come next, sponsor and floor manager statements follow, and hearings and general debate come after, used for context, for the factual record, and for the objections and alternatives that the process aired. Opponents' statements and post enactment material come last and carry the least, consulted mainly to establish what was argued rather than what a statute means.

Reading downward carries a companion rule about stopping, since when the top tier documents answer the question under investigation, research into meaning can stop, and the lower tiers become background rather than authority. A joint explanatory statement that addresses a disputed provision directly, in terms that fit the enacted text, leaves little for a floor speech to add, and a committee report that explains the same provision in consistent terms closes the inquiry further. The stopping rule conserves effort, but the deeper function is protective, because weaker material cannot strengthen a conclusion already supported by stronger material and can only complicate or dilute that conclusion by offering competing formulations that the hierarchy ranks as less reliable.

The hierarchy also imposes a specific caution about negative evidence, the argument that rejection of a proposal reveals the meaning of the text that passed. A proposal can nevertheless fail for many reasons that the record rarely disentangles, since members may vote against an amendment because the amendment states a rule that members oppose, because the amendment duplicates language already in place, because the amendment creates drafting problems, because leadership prefers to settle the point in conference, or because the amendment serves as a vehicle for delay. A defeat recorded without a reliable account of the grounds for defeat cannot bear the weight of a precise interpretive conclusion, since the rejected language was never enacted, never explained in a report adopted by both chambers, and never made part of the final text.

Defeated amendments require the same caution in sharper form, because a defeat at least occurred on the floor in connection with the pending text, yet the inference from defeat to meaning depends on assumptions that debate seldom validates. Sponsors of a measure sometimes oppose an amendment on the express ground that the amendment is unnecessary because the text already accomplishes the amendment's stated purpose, and in that posture defeat supports the opposite inference from the one that a negative evidence argument usually draws, while at other times sponsors oppose an amendment as harmful, and defeat then supports the conventional inference. The two postures produce identical vote outcomes with contradictory interpretive implications, and a researcher relying on the vote alone cannot tell which posture governed. Statements by the floor manager during consideration of the amendment can resolve the ambiguity in a given instance, which is one reason the manager's statements hold a separate tier, but the need for that resolution proves the caution, and negative evidence functions safely as a question to investigate rather than as a conclusion to assert.

The hierarchy states a general order of weight, and the order operates within forums that receive the material differently, a subject that another section of the article develops in full. Some courts treat clear text as the end of the inquiry and give legislative history no weight at all, while other courts consult the material routinely as part of interpretation, so the identical document set can be decisive in one forum and excluded in another. The tiers matter most in forums that admit the material, where the tiers govern the relative authority of documents competing within an admitted record, and a researcher preparing work for an unknown or contested forum consequently preserves the full hierarchy in the research file even while expecting that a given decision maker may stop at the text or admit only the highest tiers.

Taken together, the tiers form a single instrument for reading a record that was never designed as a unified explanation. Congress produces reports, hearings, debates, and statements for the purposes of legislating, in sequence, under pressure, by many hands, and the hierarchy converts that production sequence into an order of interpretive trust, under which trust tracks each document's connection to the final agreement, to the drafting, and to the reliance of voting colleagues. Hearings and debate earn limited trust because witnesses held no vote and because debate mixes explanation with advocacy in a record that members could revise and extend, while opponents and later commentators earn little trust because advocacy against a measure and commentary after enactment arise outside the reliance and responsibility that give the higher documents standing. A researcher who internalizes the reasons can sort a new source by authorship, timing, and incentive, and that capacity is what the hierarchy finally teaches.

## Finding the Record: Tools by Period

The search for legislative history changed character according to the period under examination, and the coverage dates of the principal finding tools drew the practical boundaries of research as sharply as any rule of interpretation. A measure enacted after the mid nineteen nineties left a trail that an official online system could display in connected form, with bill records, texts, amendments, reports, and floor proceedings gathered behind a single bill number. A measure enacted during the nineteen seventies or nineteen eighties left a divided trail, part of it summarized in official online records and part of it reachable only through indexes, library collections, and commercial databases. A measure enacted during the nineteenth century left a trail that ran through bound volumes and specialized digitized collections, where the first problem was locating the material at all and the second problem was reading it in sequence. Period therefore governed method. The researcher who understood what each tool covered, and where each coverage stopped, could plan a search that moved from one system to another at the exact point where the first system ceased to carry the record.

Coverage also governed confidence. A negative result meant one thing inside a period of systematic coverage and something quite different outside that period. Failure to locate a committee report in a system that carried committee reports from 1995 onward carried real weight for a bill from the late nineteen nineties, because the absence arose inside a collection built to hold that class of document. The same failure carried far less weight for a bill from 1980, because committee reports from that year sat outside the stated coverage of the official online system altogether. The distinction protected research from a common error, the treatment of an empty search result as proof that a document never existed. Every tool in this section had a stated beginning point, and discovery depended on matching the document sought to a tool whose coverage began before the document was created. Where no single tool spanned the whole inquiry, the search became an exercise in joining partial collections without mistaking a seam between collections for a gap in the legislative record itself.

### What does the official online system cover?

The official online system operated by the Library of Congress provided bill records, actions, summaries, and status from 1973, public law records from 1973, bill texts from 1989, amendment records from 1981, amendment texts from 1995, committee reports from 1995, and the Congressional Record from 1995 for researchers.

That pattern of staggered starting dates defined the architecture of the system known in prose as Congress.gov, the legislature's own online system operated by the Library of Congress. The deepest layer reached back to 1973 and covered bill records, actions, summaries, and status, together with public law records from the same year. A researcher tracing a measure from the ninety third Congress onward could therefore establish the formal skeleton of its progress, including the actions taken on the measure and its status at each stage, even for years in which the system carried no bill text and no committee report. The skeleton had genuine value in its own right. It fixed the bill number, identified the sequence of actions, and showed whether a measure became a public law, and those facts formed the signposts that guided every later search in other collections. Research on an older statute often began with the skeleton for precisely that reason, because a verified bill number and a verified public law record converted a vague inquiry about a statute into a precise inquiry about identified documents.

The textual layer began considerably later. Full bill texts entered the official online system from 1989, at the opening of the one hundred first Congress, so a measure from the nineteen eighties occupied an uneven position in which the record of what happened to the bill extended sixteen years further back than the text of the bill itself. Amendment records began in 1981, with the ninety seventh Congress, while amendment texts began only in 1995, with the one hundred fourth Congress. The gap between an amendment record and an amendment text mattered in practice. For an amendment offered between 1981 and 1994, the system could show that the amendment existed and could record action on it, while the words of the amendment had to be sought in another source. For an amendment offered in 1995 or in a later year, record and text sat together in the same system, and the researcher could move from the fact of proposal to the language proposed without changing tools. Committee reports and the Congressional Record shared the 1995 starting point, so the one hundred fourth Congress marked the point at which the official system first assembled the principal classes of legislative history material around a single measure in one place.

The years from 1973 to 1994 thus demanded a layered search even when the official system supplied the starting point. A bill from 1985, for example, fell within the coverage of bill records, actions, summaries, and status, and within the coverage of amendment records, and the public law records reached that year as well. The same bill fell outside the coverage of bill texts, which began in 1989, outside the coverage of committee reports, which began in 1995, and outside the coverage of the Congressional Record in the official system, which began in the same year. A bill from 1991 reversed part of that picture, because its text fell inside the system coverage while its committee report and its floor debate remained outside the official collection. Understanding those boundaries prevented wasted effort and pointed the search toward the complementary systems built by the Government Publishing Office and toward the commercial and library routes that carried the earlier material. The official system functioned, for the decades before 1995, less as a repository of the full record than as an index of verified starting points from which the full record could be assembled elsewhere.

The Government Publishing Office system, known in prose as GovInfo, supplied a second official route whose coverage overlapped the first without duplicating it exactly. Published bill versions appeared in that system from 1993, at the opening of the one hundred third Congress, four years after bill texts began in the legislature's own system. The Congressional Record daily edition appeared from 1994, beginning with volume one hundred forty, one year before the Record began in the legislature's own system. The committee reports collection began in 1995, matching the starting point in the official system operated by the Library of Congress. Hearings coverage followed a different pattern again. Selected hearings reached back to the ninety ninth Congress in 1985, while systematic hearings coverage began with the one hundred fourth Congress in 1995. The staggered pattern repeated the central lesson of the official system. No single date described the reach of the Government Publishing Office collection, because each class of document entered the collection on its own schedule, and the researcher had to ask the coverage question separately for bills, for debate, for reports, and for hearings.

The overlap between the two official systems from the mid nineteen nineties onward gave research on measures from that period a useful redundancy. A committee report from 1996 fell within the stated coverage of both systems, a bill version from the same period could be checked in both collections, and the Congressional Record for a debate after 1995 sat inside the coverage of both routes, with the Government Publishing Office carrying the daily edition from 1994. Cross checking between official sources helped confirm that a document was complete and correctly associated with a particular bill.

Hearings illustrated the distance between selected survival and systematic coverage with unusual clarity. The Government Publishing Office collection held selected hearings reaching back to 1985, a full decade before systematic coverage began in 1995, and the word selected carried the whole weight of the distinction. A hearing from 1987 might appear in the collection because it had been chosen for inclusion, while another hearing from the same Congress might not appear at all, and the absence of the second hearing proved nothing about whether the hearing occurred or whether a printed record existed in a library collection. Systematic coverage from 1995 changed the meaning of a search result, because a collection built to gather hearings as a class supported stronger inferences from both presence and absence. The decade between 1985 and 1994 therefore required the researcher to treat the official hearings collection as a partial source, valuable for what it held and unreliable as a measure of what it lacked. That treatment matched the handling required for every selective collection, and it explained why the commercial index route remained essential for hearings from the nineteen eighties and for congressional publications of every kind from the decades before systematic official coverage began.

The commercial route to earlier material rested on an index that began in 1970. The Congressional Information Service index and abstracts of congressional publications, known by the initials CIS, gave researchers a structured path into the publications of Congress from 1970 onward, covering the very decades in which the official online systems carried records and summaries without carrying the underlying reports, hearings, and debates in full. An index performed a different service from a repository. It identified documents, described them in abstracts, and organized them so that a researcher who began with a statute or a bill number could discover which reports, hearings, and related publications existed and where the trail led next. For the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, that discovery function filled the most serious gap in the official systems, because the problem was rarely a shortage of printed material. Committees had printed reports and hearings in abundance during those decades, and the material sat in library collections, but finding the right items among the output of every committee required an index built for the purpose. CIS supplied that apparatus from 1970, and research on statutes enacted between 1970 and the start of systematic online coverage in the mid nineteen nineties habitually passed through it.

The successor database to the CIS index extended the commercial route far beyond the reach of the official online systems. That successor, ProQuest Congressional, carried full text bills from 1789 and hearings from 1824, together with digitized material from the Serial Set and the Congressional Record. The depth of the bill coverage transformed research on early statutes, because the text of a bill from the first decades of the Republic could be read in the same database environment that held hearings reaching back to 1824. The depth of the hearings coverage carried a similar consequence for the nineteenth century, a period in which hearings formed a growing part of the congressional record and in which official online systems offered no coverage at all. Commercial legal databases provided additional routes into the same territory through library subscriptions, so the boundary between commercial and library research blurred in practice.

For the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the bound route remained fundamental. The United States Congressional Serial Set began in 1817, during the fifteenth Congress, and bound House and Senate reports and documents in numbered volumes. The Serial Set served as the standard route to committee material from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and its numbered volumes gave the material a stable physical and citational structure that persisted across generations of research. A committee report from the eighteen sixties or the nineteen hundreds was sought in the Serial Set as a matter of course, because the set gathered reports and documents that had originally appeared as separate publications into a single ordered sequence. The bound route demanded skills that online research did not. The researcher worked through finding aids keyed to the numbered volumes, identified the volume that held a particular report or document, and then read the item in the context of the surrounding material from the same Congress. Libraries that held deep runs of the Serial Set functioned as the indispensable infrastructure for research on early statutes, and the set anchored the documentary record for the long stretch of American legislative history that predated every electronic system described in this section.

Digitization carried parts of the bound record into a form that researchers could reach without traveling to a library that held the volumes. The Library of Congress collection known as A Century of Lawmaking for a New Nation digitized selections from the Serial Set, together with House bills from 1799 to 1873, Senate bills from 1819 to 1873, and selected reports from 1833 to 1917. The coverage dates rewarded close attention. House bills began in 1799, while Senate bills began twenty years later in 1819, so the digitized bill record for the opening decades of the nineteenth century was a House record only. Selected reports ran from 1833 to 1917, extending the digitized report coverage well into the twentieth century while remaining, by the terms of the collection itself, a selection rather than a complete run. A report from 1850 might sit inside the digitized selection or might require the bound volume, and only a check against the Serial Set itself could settle the question when the selection came up empty.

The period before 1817, and the many items from later periods that fell outside every digitized selection, kept the older collections in active service alongside the databases. The successor database carried full text bills from 1789, which covered the earliest Congresses for bill text, and hearings from 1824, which left the earliest decades of committee activity to other sources. The Serial Set began in 1817, which left the publications of the first fourteen Congresses outside the set. Each starting date marked a frontier, and the frontiers did not align. Bills could be traced to 1789 in one source, reports could be followed in bound volumes from 1817 in another, and hearings reached 1824 in a commercial database and 1985 in selective official coverage.

One further library route served researchers who wanted a compiled history of a major statute rather than the raw sequence of separate documents. The United States Code Congressional and Administrative News, a long running commercial compilation published by West, printed each public law together with selected committee and conference reports for major enactments. The compilation functioned as a standard library route to a compiled history for major statutes, and its value lay in the gathering. A researcher who opened the volume for the year of an enactment found the text of the public law and the principal reports assembled in one place, without a preliminary search through indexes or databases to identify and retrieve each document separately. The compilation carried selected reports for major enactments, so it served the major statutes thoroughly while offering less for minor measures, and a researcher whose statute fell outside the selection returned to the general routes through the indexes, the databases, and the official systems. For the statutes it covered, the compilation shortened the distance between identifying an enactment and reading the documents that explained it.

The tools by period thus formed a single connected system when read through their coverage dates. The legislature's own online system anchored research from 1973 for records and public laws, from 1989 for bill texts, from 1981 for amendment records, and from 1995 for amendment texts, committee reports, and the Congressional Record. The Government Publishing Office system reinforced the record from 1993 for published bill versions, from 1994 for the daily edition of the Record, from 1995 for committee reports, and from 1985 for selected hearings. The Congressional Information Service index opened the publications of Congress from 1970, its successor database carried full text bills from 1789 and hearings from 1824, and the Serial Set bound the reports and documents from 1817. A search that respected those boundaries moved across the centuries without confusion, treating every coverage date as a fact about the tool rather than a fact about the history itself.

## The Fight Over Whether Any of It Counts

A researcher gathered committee reports, conference explanations, sponsor statements, hearing testimony, and floor debate because a court, an agency, or a litigant might treat part of that material as evidence of what an enacted statute meant. The dispute over legislative history asked whether that expectation was justified. One side treated the material as a necessary aid to understanding enacted language, produced by the institution that had drafted and debated the language. The other side treated the material as an illegitimate substitute for enacted language, produced outside the constitutional process for making law. Both sides assumed that the documents existed in great quantity. The disagreement concerned authority, reliability, and the relation between a text that had become law and the explanations that had surrounded the text before enactment.

The textualist critique began with the structure of lawmaking under Article I of the Constitution. A bill became law only after passage by both chambers in identical form and presentment to the President. The enacted text had survived that process. A committee report had not been passed by both chambers as law. A floor statement had not been passed by both chambers as law. A hearing exchange had not been passed by both chambers as law. The critique therefore drew a sharp line between law and material about law. The enacted words were the product of the procedure that the Constitution prescribed. The surrounding explanations were commentary on that product, sometimes close to the drafting and sometimes distant from it.

Authorship deepened the objection. Committee reports were typically drafted by committee staff and were never voted on as explanations by the chamber that issued the report, let alone by both chambers. A member could vote to report a bill without voting on every sentence in the accompanying report. The document nevertheless entered the interpretive record with an institutional voice, as if a committee position on a contested phrase could stand for the understanding of the legislature as a whole. Textualists regarded that step as a central fiction in the use of legislative history. A report looked collective because it bore a committee name and a report number. Staff drafting served the legislative process, but staff drafting did not pass through bicameral passage and presentment. A document created under those conditions could not acquire the authority of enacted text through later judicial respect.

Statements made during debate presented a related problem, and staged colloquies presented that problem in its sharpest form. A sponsor statement carried weight in the conventional hierarchy because colleagues were understood to rely on sponsor representations about a measure. A staged colloquy between a sponsor and a friendly member could be arranged to manufacture an interpretation of disputed language. The substance, under the critique, could be strategic placement rather than persuasion of colleagues at the moment of decision. A participant who expected litigation over a provision had an incentive to create favorable interpretive material and to phrase that material as an authoritative clarification. The more a court rewarded such material, the stronger the incentive became to produce material aimed at a later court rather than at members deciding a vote. Sponsors could describe compromise language broadly to one audience and narrowly to another, and opponents could characterize a measure at its most alarming. The printed record preserved each characterization without reconciling the characterizations.

Scale compounded the objections from authorship and strategy. The material was vast and contradictory. A major statute could generate reports from more than one committee, dissenting and minority views, and hearing testimony supporting conflicting accounts of a problem. A provision could be described in one document as a narrow response to a defined problem and in another document as part of a broad program. A term could receive a careful explanation in a report and a looser description in debate. A judge consulting that mass of material could find support for almost any reading. The objection was therefore also an objection about judicial discretion. Legislative history, in this account, expanded the range of materials from which an interpreter could select. Selection among contradictory materials could conceal a choice that the enacted words had not made.

Judge Harold Leventhal of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit captured the selection problem in an image of a crowded gathering, in which a person scanned a room full of guests and picked out familiar faces as allies for a position held in advance. Justice Antonin Scalia repeated the image in later writing on statutory interpretation. Scalia's concurrence in Conroy v. Aniskoff, 507 U.S. 511 (1993), used the image in a concrete statutory dispute. Reading Law, published in 2012 and written with Bryan A. Garner, carried the image into a broader account of interpretation. A Matter of Interpretation, published in 1997, supplied Scalia's systematic statement of the position. The question was whether a particular document proved an authoritative collective purpose on the disputed point, or proved only that some participant had expressed a view where expression required no majority vote on the explanation itself.

The critique also addressed the incentives that judicial reliance created inside the legislative process. If courts treated reports and floor statements as authoritative explanations, participants had reason to shape those explanations apart from the enacted language. Committee staff, agency officials, and members could seek favorable sentences in a report while knowing that report language did not face the same obstacles in the other chamber or in presentment. Enacted text had to attract majority support in two chambers and survive the possibility of presidential objection. A regulated party could comply with the enacted words and face an interpretation drawn from a statement that had never been enacted. The strongest form of the critique did not rest on a single defective document. The critique addressed the category and asked what principled basis allowed a court to accept the careful report, reject the planted colloquy, and treat one passage in a voluminous record as representative while dismissing another passage as unrepresentative.

### What do defenders of the material say in reply?

Defenders answered that legislative history served concrete functions in interpretation, helped a court avoid unreasonable outcomes and drafting mistakes, clarified specialized language, identified a reasonable purpose, and assisted in choosing among competing purposes, and that misuse of the material argued for discipline in its handling rather than abandonment of the material altogether.

That answer received a classic formulation from Stephen Breyer in an article published in 1992, when Breyer served as a judge of the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit. The article, titled On the Uses of Legislative History in Interpreting Statutes, appeared in the Southern California Law Review, volume 65, at page 845. The title identified the method of the defense. Breyer defended uses, in the plural, and evaluated legislative history by the work the material could perform in specific interpretive situations. The defense did not claim that a court could substitute a discovered purpose for language that the legislature had enacted. The defense instead asked whether a blanket refusal to consult the material would deprive an interpreter of useful evidence. The comparison was between informed use, subject to discrimination among documents, and a rule that treated the entire category as unusable even when a document answered the precise question before a court.

The first use concerned avoidance of absurd outcomes. Enacted language could produce a result so disconnected from the problem addressed by a statute that a court had reason to suspect a mismatch between literal application and legislative design. Legislative history could help a court examine that suspicion by showing the setting in which the language had been adopted. Language operated in a context, and the documents surrounding enactment could reveal whether an apparently literal reading rested on a false assumption. A statute aimed at one kind of transaction could contain words broad enough to reach a different kind of transaction that no participant had discussed. A court faced with that application could consult the history to test whether the broad reading produced an outcome outside the reasonable scope of the measure.

A second use concerned correction of drafting errors. Legislation passed through committees, floor amendments, conference negotiation, and enrollment of a final text. That process could leave a cross reference wrong, a word misplaced, or a phrase inconsistent with surrounding provisions. A court that refused all legislative history had fewer tools for deciding whether an apparent command reflected a considered choice or a mistake. Committee reports and conference explanations could show the intended placement of a provision or the relation between a new section and an existing section. That material could support a reading that treated an obvious error as an error instead of compounding the error through literal enforcement. Defenders regarded that function as practical rather than theoretical. Drafting occurred under time pressure, in technical fields, and through successive versions of a measure.

A third use concerned specialized terms. Statutes governing taxation, securities, labor relations, communications, public lands, and health programs often used language drawn from an industry, a profession, an agency practice, or an earlier statutory scheme. A word that appeared ordinary in general usage could carry a specialized meaning in the field that Congress regulated. Hearings, reports, and committee explanations could preserve the setting in which such language had been used. That use did not turn every witness statement into legislative meaning. Hearings ranked low in the hierarchy because witnesses were not legislators and testimony preceded the final text. A committee report could connect that vocabulary to the reported bill. An interpreter who ignored the record risked importing a general dictionary meaning into a statute whose words had been chosen from a narrower vocabulary.

A fourth use concerned identification of the reasonable purpose of a provision. Many statutory disputes involved language that could bear more than one reading, each compatible with the words in isolation but different in effect. Legislative history could help a court identify the purpose that fit the provision and the problem that had prompted the provision. Purpose in that sense did not float free of the text. Purpose disciplined a choice among readings that the text allowed by asking which reading advanced the objective that the enacted scheme appeared designed to serve. A report could explain why a remedy had been added or why enforcement authority had been assigned to one institution rather than another. Defenders acknowledged that statutes often embodied compromises and that a compromise could lack a single elegant purpose. A court choosing between plausible readings had reason to consult the best available evidence of the problem and design behind the provision.

A fifth use concerned choice among competing purposes. A statute could pursue more than one objective, and the objectives could point in different directions in a hard case. A measure could seek to expand a benefit and control cost, or to encourage an activity and police abuse of the encouragement. Enacted language reflected the balance struck in the text, but ambiguous language could fail to reveal how that balance applied to an unaddressed situation. Legislative history could show which purpose had been emphasized during consideration of the disputed provision. The history could show which purpose had been connected to the specific language under review. That evidence could help a court avoid elevating a general statement of one objective over a specific explanation tied to the disputed clause. Defenders treated the exercise as an inquiry into relative weight within the enacted scheme. The history mattered because the choice among purposes often determined the choice among readings.

Those five uses led to the central distinction in the purposivist defense, the distinction between abuse and use. Breyer's position in the 1992 article was that the problem with legislative history was abuse rather than use. A court could treat a planted statement as a collective understanding or search a large record for a favorable passage while ignoring context. Those errors argued for better judicial practice. Those errors did not prove that a conference explanation, a committee report, or a sponsor statement could never assist interpretation. A document had provenance, timing, authorship, institutional status, and a relation to the final text. A careful reader could ask whether the document came from the committee that reported the bill and whether the document addressed the disputed language. Defenders treated the hierarchy of weight as learned conventions for separating stronger evidence from weaker evidence. A staged colloquy was a reason to discount staged colloquies under those conventions, not a reason to discard a conference explanation that spoke for both chambers on the final text.

The drafting quality argument extended the defense from courts to Congress. Breyer argued that discounting legislative history degraded the technical quality of statutes because Congress drafted against the expectation that its reports would be read. Committees and staff used reports to explain choices that could not be expressed fully in statutory language without making the language unwieldy or excessively detailed. A report could give examples, state assumptions, and identify the intended treatment of recurring situations. If courts refused to read those explanations, Congress had less reason to prepare explanations with care. Drafters had reason to force explanatory material into the enacted text or to leave questions unexplained. The result, in that argument, was not cleaner law. A court method that dismissed reports regardless of quality weakened the incentive to produce careful reports and reduced the information available to every later reader.

Judge Robert A. Katzmann defended informed judicial use in Judging Statutes, published in 2014, drawing on experience of the legislative process from inside that process. That experience shaped the character of the defense. Katzmann's treatment did not present legislative history as a code hidden behind enacted language. The defense rested on familiarity with how legislation was assembled, how committees worked, and how staff and members divided labor. Informed use meant use by a reader who understood that a report differed from a hearing and that a conference explanation differed from a floor exchange. Such a reader could neither accept the record uncritically nor dismiss the record as inherently unreliable. Katzmann's defense also answered the charge that reliance on legislative history necessarily expanded judicial power. A judge who consulted a report could be constrained by the report, because the report could identify a specific problem, compromise, or limitation that narrowed the range of plausible readings. A judge who refused the report retained discretion to choose among readings of ambiguous text, but exercised that discretion with fewer external checks.

Justice Elena Kagan's observation at Harvard Law School in 2015 entered the dispute from a different angle. Kagan observed that on questions of statutory method the Court had converged considerably toward text first reading. Both sides quoted the observation because both sides could draw support from the terms in which convergence was described. A textualist could emphasize movement toward text first reading as evidence that sustained criticism of legislative history had changed judicial practice. A defender of legislative history could emphasize that text first did not necessarily mean text only. A method beginning with text could leave room for context, structure, purpose, and legislative history as a check on a proposed reading. The observation described a shift in emphasis rather than a settlement of every question about weight. It did not identify a single rule for committee reports, sponsor statements, hearings, or conference explanations. Each side heard a confirmation of a different part of the dispute, the centrality of enacted text on one side and the survival of contextual inquiry on the other side.

Neither side surrendered the core premise from which the argument began. The textualist premise remained that authority followed enactment, and that a search for meaning outside the enacted text risked displacing the only material that had satisfied Article I. The purposivist premise remained that enacted text operated in an institutional and factual setting, and that a refusal to consult the best evidence of that setting risked error. Scalia's writings gave the first premise a forceful judicial and scholarly expression, with Leventhal's image supplying the memorable warning about selective consultation. Breyer's 1992 article and Katzmann's book gave the second premise a defense grounded in identifiable uses and in knowledge of the legislative process. The dispute persisted as a dispute over method, evidence, institutional competence, and the limits of judicial choice.

Forum variation gave that persistence a practical consequence for research. In some forums, the material received no weight at all, and a court treated clear text as the end of the inquiry without turning to reports, statements, or hearings for interpretive support. In other forums, the material was consulted routinely, with courts examining committee reports, conference explanations, and sponsor statements as part of the ordinary work of resolving a statutory question. The identical document set could be decisive in one forum and excluded in another. The value of a compiled history therefore depended on the forum in which the history would be used. A researcher had to know which forum the work was aimed at before deciding how much effort to devote to each class of document. The fight over whether any of it counted ended, for research purposes, in that forum-specific question. The answer determined whether the record assembled through bills, reports, debates, and hearings would be treated as authority, as context, or as material that the decision maker would not consult at all.

## From a Popular Name to a Citable Source

Research into legislative history often begins with a name that carries no citation value at all. A statute enters public discussion under a popular name, a shortened title, or a label attached by journalists, agencies, and litigants. The working method therefore treats the popular name as a point of departure rather than as a destination, and each step converts an informal reference into a formal one. A researcher who follows the chain in order moves from a familiar label to a public law number, from a public law number to a session law citation and a bill number, and from a bill number to the documentary record generated during consideration.

The first conversion relies on the Popular Names Table. The table converts a statute's popular name to its public law citation, matching the name by which an enactment is commonly known against the formal designation assigned at enactment. That matching matters because a popular name may cover an entire act, a major title within an act, or an amendment that altered an earlier act, and each possibility leads to a different public law. Careful use of the table therefore requires attention to the entry itself, to any cross references the table supplies, and to the distinction between an original enactment and a later amendment that acquired a popular name of its own. The table resolves ambiguity by tying each name to a specific public law, and the public law citation then anchors every later step. Without that anchor, a search built on a popular name alone gathers material indiscriminately, mixing documents that belong to the original enactment with documents that belong to amendments enacted years afterward under a different Congress.

A public law number carries two pieces of information in a compact form. The number identifies the Congress that enacted the measure and the sequence of the law within that Congress, in that order. From the public law number, the trail leads to the Statutes at Large citation, which gives the volume and page where the enacted text appears as a session law, and to the bill number of the measure that became the law. The Statutes at Large citation fixes the text in the chronological series of enacted laws, preserving the act as Congress passed it and as the President approved it, before any editorial arrangement into the Code. The bill number performs a different function, identifying the vehicle that moved through committee consideration, floor debate, and final passage. That vehicle generated most of the legislative history a researcher seeks, because reports attach to a bill number, debate concerns a bill number, and hearings examine a bill number or the subject a bill number later embodies. Conversion from a public law to a bill number therefore converts a finished enactment into a researchable proceeding.

The Statutes at Large citation also protects against a quieter error, the confusion of a codified arrangement with the enacted arrangement. Codification rearranges session law into titles and sections organized by subject, and a single act may scatter across several titles while a single Code section may combine material from several acts. The public law citation and the Statutes at Large citation together restore the original unit, allowing a researcher to read the act as a whole and to see which provisions Congress enacted together. Commercial compilations reinforce the same point of access. The United States Code Congressional and Administrative News, published by West, prints each public law with selected committee and conference reports for major enactments, and libraries have long treated that compilation as a standard route to a compiled history for major statutes.

The reverse path begins in the Code itself. A codified section carries a credit line, and that credit line names the public laws that enacted and amended the section. The discipline described in [US Code vs Statutes at Large](/2022/06/01/us-code-vs-statutes-at-large/) concerns tracing a codified provision back to its enacting act, and the credit line supplies the raw material for that tracing because each public law named in the line marks one legislative action that shaped the section. Reading the line in order separates original enactment from amendment. The earliest public law in the line identifies the act that created the provision, while each later public law identifies an act that altered the provision, whether by substitution, by addition, or by repeal of part of the earlier language. From each public law in the line, the same conversions apply again, since the public law number leads to a Statutes at Large citation and to a bill number, and the bill number leads to reports, debates, and hearings for that particular enactment or amendment. Conflating the several trails that a credit line generates produces a familiar failure, the attribution of an explanation written for an amendment to language enacted decades earlier, or the attribution of an original committee explanation to words that a later amendment replaced entirely.

The chain matters because legislative history explains a text that has independent legal force. The guidance set out in [How to Read a Federal Statute](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/) concerns the enacted text the history explains, and that relationship imposes an order on research. The enacted words come first in authority, and the history acquires value only insofar as the history illuminates words that survived bicameral passage and presentment. The enrolled bill marks the endpoint of that process, as the final version passed identically by both chambers, authenticated by the presiding officers, and presented to the President. Under the enrolled bill rule associated with Field v. Clark, decided in 1892, courts do not look behind a duly authenticated enrolled bill to the journals to dispute that the bill passed. A citation chain that ends with an unenacted draft or a statement detached from the enrolled text has therefore departed from the object that legislative history exists to explain.

Citation form preserves the chain in a way other researchers can test. A sound citation does more than name a document, because the form identifies the kind of document, the Congress that produced the document, and the place where the document sits in an official series. Another researcher can then retrieve the identical page and can judge whether the document bears the weight assigned to it. Loose citation defeats that testing function, since a reference to a report without a Congress, a reference to debate without a volume, or a reference to a hearing without a committee leaves retrieval to guesswork and leaves weight to assertion. Standard forms answer that problem by fixing the elements each source type requires, and the elements differ because the sources differ in origin and in authority.

A committee report citation identifies the chamber, the Congress, and the report number. The chamber element distinguishes a House Report from a Senate Report, a distinction that matters because the two chambers considered the same bill through separate committees and often explained the bill in different terms. The Congress element fixes the report in time and ties the report to the bill number in use during that Congress, since bill numbers begin again with each Congress and a bare number without a Congress identifies nothing. The report number places the document in the official report series for that chamber and Congress, allowing retrieval through the Congressional Serial Set for earlier periods and through committee report collections for later periods. A complete report citation therefore allows a reader to move directly from a footnote to the document, to verify the quoted language in context, and to see the standard contents that accompany the explanation, including any dissenting, minority, or supplemental views printed with the report.

A Congressional Record citation gives the volume and the page. Citation practice prefers the permanent bound edition once that edition exists, and the preference rests on more than formality, because the daily edition and the bound edition do not share pagination. Since 1967, daily edition page numbers have carried letter prefixes, with S for Senate proceedings, H for House proceedings, E for Extensions of Remarks, and D for Daily Digest. The bound edition repaginates continuously, drops the letter prefixes, and may incorporate further editing. A page number taken from the daily edition therefore does not locate the same passage in the bound edition, and a citation that gives a daily page as though the page were a bound page sends a reader to the wrong place or to no place at all. Recognition of the two editions also affects substance, because the Record is by law and long practice a substantially verbatim account of proceedings rather than a strictly verbatim transcript, and members may revise and extend remarks and, with permission, may insert material never delivered on the floor.

A bill citation identifies the chamber, the number, and the Congress. The chamber element appears in the designation itself, distinguishing a House bill from a Senate bill, and the number identifies the measure within that chamber's sequence for a particular Congress. The Congress element completes the identification because numbering restarts, so the same number in two Congresses designates two entirely different measures. Precision about version belongs with bill citation as well, even though version is a matter of description rather than of the bare citation. A measure exists in successive forms across a legislative life, including introduction, committee report, passage by one chamber, passage by both chambers, and enrollment, and each form may differ from every other form, sometimes in a single phrase and sometimes in entire titles. A citation that identifies a bill without identifying the version consulted leaves a reader unable to determine whether quoted language ever survived to enactment, while analysis tied to the enrolled version or to the public law itself treats earlier versions only as evidence of development.

A hearing citation identifies the committee and the Congress, together with the measure or subject under examination. The committee element matters because hearings are committee proceedings, conducted to gather testimony and material for the record rather than to state the position of a chamber. The Congress element matters because committees return to the same subjects across many Congresses, and testimony taken in one Congress about one proposal cannot be transferred silently to a different proposal considered years later. Hearing material consists of testimony by witnesses and material submitted for the record, and not every hearing results in publication. Those facts explain the limited interpretive weight courts assign to hearings, since witnesses are not legislators and testimony precedes the final text. A correctly cited hearing nevertheless preserves the factual record Congress assembled, including data, expert accounts, and agency explanations that shaped committee choices, and a citation naming the committee and the Congress permits a reader to place testimony at the correct point in a bill's development.

### What mistakes produce a wrong citation?

Worst errors arise from mistaking a convenient copy for an authoritative source. A citation fails when a daily Record page stands in for bound pagination, when an introduced bill stands in for enacted text, or when a committee print, inserted remark, or later statement is treated as a contemporaneous explanation.

The daily and bound editions of the Congressional Record supply the most mechanical trap and the easiest trap to avoid. A researcher working soon after a debate consults the daily edition, records a page bearing a letter prefix, and later presents that page in a form that implies bound edition pagination. The citation looks complete because a volume and a page appear in sequence, yet the page belongs to a different pagination system. Retrieval fails, or worse, retrieval succeeds at the wrong page and a reader attributes language from an unrelated proceeding to the debate under study. The error also conceals the editorial history of the passage, because the bound edition may incorporate further editing beyond the daily text, so a quotation verified against the daily edition alone may differ in wording or in placement from the bound text that a court or a library treats as the permanent record.

Version error in bill citation produces a substantive failure rather than a merely mechanical one. A bill as introduced may contain language that a committee removed, that a floor amendment replaced, or that a conference agreement abandoned. Citing that language as though Congress enacted the language reverses the meaning of the legislative process, treating a proposal as a decision. The same failure occurs when a researcher cites the version passed by one chamber as though both chambers had agreed to that version, even though bicameral agreement on identical text is the condition of enactment and the enrolled bill embodies that agreement. Conference reports, with the joint explanatory statement prepared by the managers of both chambers, carry the heaviest weight among legislative history documents precisely because the conference document speaks for both chambers on the final text. Sound practice compares the cited version against the public law, accounts for every material difference between the two, and uses superseded language only to show a considered and rejected alternative, never as a description of enacted law.

Confusion between a committee print and a committee report produces a failure of authority. A committee print is a document printed at a committee's direction for the committee's own use, and prints include staff studies, compilations of existing law, and draft measures. A print is not a committee report, carries no report number in the House Report or Senate Report series, and was never voted on as an explanation of a reported bill. A citation that presents a print in the form reserved for a report therefore invents an institutional endorsement that never occurred. A staff study may be learned and influential, and a compilation may be an excellent research tool, yet neither document speaks for a committee in the way a report speaks after a committee orders a measure reported. Correct description names a print as a print, identifies the committee that directed publication, and assigns the document weight as staff or compiled material rather than as a committee explanation, a distinction that also governs retrieval through report series and print collections.

Inserted material in the Congressional Record creates a trap about speech itself. The Extensions of Remarks section of the House Record carries speeches never delivered and extraneous material such as articles and letters, and everything in that section appears in a distinctive typeface. House Practice, citing section 687 of the House Rules and Manual, records that material inserted under permission to extend remarks must be clearly distinguishable, by different typeface, from the substantially verbatim account in the daily Record, and that the Speaker instructed the Official Reporters of Debates to adhere strictly to that requirement. A researcher who ignores typeface and placement may quote an inserted article as though a member delivered the article on the floor, may treat a letter from a constituent as a legislator's own statement, or may assign a speech written for publication the weight of remarks addressed to colleagues during debate. Each error inflates the material, because colleagues in the chamber never heard the inserted text, never responded to that text, and never voted with that text in mind.

Two final traps concern statements that originate outside the enacting process or outside the enacting Congress. A signing statement is issued by the President at the signing of a bill, and the statement is an executive document, not produced by Congress and not passed through bicameral procedure. Classification practice therefore places signing statements generally outside legislative history proper, even though litigants sometimes offer a statement as evidence of executive understanding of a statute. Treating a signing statement as congressional intent commits a category error, attributing to legislators an interpretation that legislators never considered, never debated, and never adopted, and weight for any signing statement remains contested. The related trap involves statements by a later Congress about an earlier act, since post enactment legislative history, including statements made after enactment and explanations offered by subsequent Congresses, receives little weight because a later Congress cannot speak for the enacting Congress. Relying on such statements to contradict the reports and debates of the enacting Congress inverts the chronology that gives legislative history its claim to attention, and the contemporaneous record, produced by the legislators who drafted, debated, and passed the text, remains the record a careful citation reflects.

## Putting the Two Halves Together

A reader who has worked through the document classes, the reports, the published account of debate, the ranking of sources, the tools arranged by period, the dispute over method and the citation path possesses more than a set of research techniques. Such a reader possesses a test that can be applied to any claim about a statute encountered anywhere else. Series articles on particular enactments quote committee explanations, repeat sponsor assurances, describe hearings and invoke conference settlements, and each of those invocations carries an implicit promise that a document exists, that the document says the words attributed to the document, and that the document deserves the authority the invocation assigns. The research foundation laid in this article allows that promise to be checked rather than accepted. A claim can be followed back through a bill number to a report, through a report to a passage, and through a passage to a judgment about weight, and a claim that survives that journey has earned a place in an argument in a way that a claim repeated from a secondary summary has not.

Verification of that kind changes the relationship between a reader and the rest of the series. An article on a contested statute no longer asks for trust in the selection of quotations made by an author working under space limits and deadline pressure. Verification returns the selection to the record, where the omitted context sits beside the quoted sentence, where dissenting views printed in the same report qualify the majority explanation, and where a version comparison can show whether the explained language matches the enacted language at all. A reader equipped to perform that return can also detect the characteristic failures cataloged in the sections on citation and on the published debate record: an introduced draft treated as enacted law, an inserted remark treated as a floor exchange, a committee print treated as a committee report, a later statement treated as the voice of the enacting Congress. Detection requires no special talent beyond the habits the article has described, applied in order and without exception for a favored conclusion.

The discipline that organizes those habits bears restatement in the plain form in which the introduction named the discipline: find it and then discount it. Finding came first throughout the article because authority cannot attach to a document that exists only as a rumor of a document, and the finding half demanded conversions from popular name to public law, from public law to bill number, from bill number to reports, debates and hearings, with each conversion checked against a collection whose coverage actually reached the period and the document class in question. Discounting followed because discovery settles existence and nothing more, and the discounting half demanded a ranking by institutional authorship, proximity to the final text and incentive, together with a clear view of the forum that would receive the material and of the dispute that could lead that forum to set the material aside entirely. Held together, the two halves describe a complete act of judgment, neither a scavenger hunt for favorable passages nor a reflexive skepticism that treats every explanation as worthless because some explanations were planted, exaggerated or produced after the fact.

The onward route for a reader follows from that judgment. A statute under study can be approached through the study framework set out in [A Study Guide to US Legislation Since 1950](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/), which places individual enactments inside the larger sequence of postwar lawmaking and supplies the chronological setting within which a single history acquires meaning. Work on a particular provision then proceeds by the methods of the sections in sequence: identification of the enactment through the Popular Names Table or through a Code credit line, retrieval of the versions in order, reading of the committee report in full and of the conference explanation where a conference occurred, inspection of the published debate with attention to typeface, placement and edition, and a written ranking of every document retained, with the reason for the ranking stated beside the citation. A file assembled in that manner can be defended document by document, and a conclusion drawn from that file can be separated cleanly from the weaker material that the file deliberately set aside.

Organization of the file deserves a final word, because legislative research accumulates quickly and because a conclusion is only as reliable as the record kept of the path taken toward that conclusion. A researcher can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) so that a chain from a Code section to a report passage remains retrievable long after the search that produced the chain has ended. A student preparing for examination or classroom discussion can [practice and revise US government and civics material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) to test command of the institutions and procedures that generated the record under study. Neither aid substitutes for the record itself, which remains the ground of every claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you find legislative history?

A search begins by identifying the enactment and the bill that carried the enactment, because reports, debates and hearings attach to a bill number rather than to a subject. Congress.gov supplied bill records, actions, summaries and status from 1973, public law records from 1973, bill texts from 1989, committee reports from 1995 and the Congressional Record from 1995. GovInfo added published bill versions from 1993, the Record daily edition from 1994 and committee reports from 1995. For earlier decades, the Congressional Information Service index from 1970, ProQuest Congressional, the Congressional Serial Set and library compilations supplied the routes described in the period sections of the article.

### Q: Which legislative history source carries the most weight?

The conference report, together with the joint explanatory statement that accompanies the report, carries the most weight in the hierarchy used throughout the article. A conference report reconciled the differing versions passed by the House and the Senate, and the joint explanatory statement was prepared by the managers of both chambers to explain the agreement reached. The document therefore spoke for both chambers and spoke on the final text, a combination no earlier source possessed. The committee report ranked second, followed by statements of the sponsor or floor manager, then hearings and general floor debate, with statements by opponents and material produced after enactment receiving little weight.

### Q: Is the Congressional Record a verbatim legislative history transcript?

The Congressional Record was, by law and long practice, a substantially verbatim account of proceedings rather than a strictly verbatim transcript. Members could revise and extend remarks and, with permission, insert material never delivered on the floor. House Practice, citing section 687 of the House Rules and Manual, required material inserted under permission to extend remarks to appear in a different typeface from the substantially verbatim account in the daily Record, and the Speaker instructed the Official Reporters of Debates to adhere strictly to that requirement. Material submitted later went to the Extensions of Remarks section, which carried speeches never delivered and extraneous material such as articles and letters in a distinctive typeface.

### Q: Where do you find legislative history before 1973?

Material from before 1973 lay outside the bill record coverage of Congress.gov, which began with the 93rd Congress in 1973, so research moved to indexes, commercial databases, bound volumes and digitized library collections. The Congressional Information Service index and abstracts began in 1970. ProQuest Congressional carried full text bills from 1789 and hearings from 1824, together with digitized Serial Set and Record material. HeinOnline carried the Annals of Congress, the Register of Debates, the Congressional Globe and the Congressional Record in full. The United States Congressional Serial Set, begun in 1817, bound House and Senate reports and documents and served as the standard route to nineteenth and early twentieth century committee material.

### Q: Do textualist judges use legislative history?

The textualist critique described in the article denied legislative history authority as an interpretive source, on the ground that only the enacted text survived bicameral passage and presentment under Article I. Committee reports were typically drafted by committee staff and never voted on, floor statements could be planted strategically, and the material was vast and contradictory. Forum practice varied in the account given: some courts treated clear text as the end of the inquiry and gave the material no weight at all, while other courts consulted the material routinely. The article described the dispute at full strength and offered no resolution of the contest between the textualist critique and the purposivist defense.

### Q: What is a committee print in legislative history?

A committee print was a document printed at the direction of a committee for the use of that committee, with staff studies, compilations of existing law and draft measures given as the standard examples in the fact record. A print was not a committee report, carried no report number in the House Report or Senate Report series and was never voted on as an explanation of a reported bill. The distinction mattered because a print spoke for the authors of the study, compilation or draft rather than for a committee acting as an institution, and citation practice preserved that difference by identifying a print as a print instead of allowing report authority to attach to the document.

### Q: Do signing statements count as legislative history?

Signing statements were generally classified outside legislative history proper. A signing statement was issued by the President at the signing of a bill, so the document was an executive document, not produced by Congress and not passed through bicameral procedure. Litigants sometimes offered a signing statement as evidence of the executive understanding of a statute, and any weight assigned to the document remained contested. Treating a signing statement as congressional intent committed a category error, because legislators never considered, debated or adopted the interpretation that the President attached to the measure at signature, and the contemporaneous record produced by the enacting Congress remained the record to which a careful citation referred.

### Q: How do you trace a code section to its legislative history?

Tracing began with the credit line attached to the codified section, because the credit line named the public laws that enacted and amended the section. Each public law number identified the Congress and the sequence of the law within that Congress, and the number led to the Statutes at Large citation and to the bill number of the measure that became the law. The bill number then led to committee reports, conference material, debates and hearings for that enactment or amendment. Reading the credit line in order separated original enactment from later amendment, and that separation prevented an explanation written for an amendment from being attributed to language enacted in an earlier Congress.

### Q: What is the difference between a committee report and a conference report?

A committee report explained a measure that one committee had ordered reported to a single chamber, and a standard report contained a statement of purpose, a section by section analysis, a cost estimate, a showing of changes to existing law and any dissenting, minority or supplemental views. A conference report performed a different task at a later stage, reconciling the versions that the House and the Senate had passed in differing forms. Managers from both chambers prepared a joint explanatory statement to accompany the conference report, and that bicameral authorship on the final text placed the conference document above the committee report in the hierarchy of interpretive weight.

### Q: How can you tell whether remarks in the Congressional Record were actually delivered on the floor?

A reader examined typeface and placement in the daily Record. Material inserted under permission to extend remarks had to be clearly distinguishable, by different typeface, from the substantially verbatim account, under the requirement recorded in House Practice from section 687 of the House Rules and Manual. Extensions submitted on the day of consideration appeared immediately after general debate in that different typeface, while material submitted later went to the Extensions of Remarks section. Since 1967, daily edition page numbers carried letter prefixes, with E marking Extensions of Remarks, and the permanent bound edition repaginated continuously, dropped the prefixes and could incorporate further editing, so edition and section both formed part of a reliable check.

### Q: What is a joint explanatory statement?

A joint explanatory statement was the explanation prepared by the managers of both chambers to accompany a conference report. The managers, also termed conferees, negotiated the resolution of differences between the House passed and Senate passed texts, and the statement recorded the reasoning by which the managers settled each difference that the conference addressed. The statement could identify which chamber position prevailed on a point, which position yielded and what understanding accompanied the selection. Because the statement explained the final text rather than a text approved by one chamber alone, courts and researchers assigned the conference report and the joint explanatory statement the heaviest weight among legislative history documents.

### Q: Where are committee hearings published and how far back do they reach online?

Hearings appeared as committee publications gathering testimony of witnesses and material submitted for the record, although not every hearing was published. GovInfo held selected hearings reaching back to the 99th Congress in 1985, with systematic hearings coverage from the 104th Congress in 1995. ProQuest Congressional, the commercial successor to the Congressional Information Service index, carried hearings from 1824 together with full text bills from 1789. The difference between selected survival and systematic coverage governed the meaning of a search result, because absence from a selected collection established only that no copy circulated in that collection, while absence after systematic coverage began carried greater significance for a researcher.

### Q: Why do courts give statements by a bill's opponents so little weight?

An opponent described a bill that the opponent sought to defeat, and opponents often characterized a measure at the most alarming application available. A legislator arguing against passage gained nothing from presenting a provision in the most moderate or most probable form, so debate from that side ran toward the broadest plausible consequence and the hardest case. The hierarchy discounted such statements rather than sorting sincere warnings from tactical warnings passage by passage. Opponent statements retained a narrower use, establishing that an objection was aired, a consequence predicted or an alternative reading presented and left unadopted, without reliably establishing the meaning that the enacting majority gave to the enacted text.

### Q: What is an enrolled bill and why does it matter to a researcher?

An enrolled bill was the final version of a measure passed identically by both chambers, authenticated by the presiding officers and presented to the President. Enrollment marked the point at which the separate institutional texts converged into a single authoritative document, and the enrolled text became the measure on which presidential action rested. Under the enrolled bill rule, stated in Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), courts did not look behind a duly authenticated enrolled bill to the journals to dispute that the measure passed. A researcher therefore treated the enrolled bill as the fixed endpoint of the version sequence, against which every earlier version and surrounding document was measured.

### Q: How is a committee report cited?

A committee report citation identified the chamber, the Congress and the report number. The chamber element distinguished a House Report from a Senate Report, a distinction that mattered because the two chambers considered the same bill through separate committees and often explained the bill in different terms. The Congress element fixed the report in time and tied the report to the bill number in use during that Congress, since bill numbers began again with each Congress and a bare number identified nothing. The report number placed the document in the official report series for that chamber and Congress, allowing retrieval through the Congressional Serial Set for earlier periods and through committee report collections for later periods.

### Q: What is the United States Congressional Serial Set?

The United States Congressional Serial Set began in 1817, during the 15th Congress, and bound House and Senate reports and documents in numbered volumes. The set served as the standard route to committee material from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, gathering reports and documents that had first appeared as separate publications into a single ordered sequence with a stable physical and citational structure. A researcher sought a committee report from the eighteen sixties or the nineteen hundreds in the Serial Set as a matter of course. The Library of Congress collection A Century of Lawmaking for a New Nation digitized Serial Set selections, together with House bills from 1799 to 1873, Senate bills from 1819 to 1873 and selected reports from 1833 to 1917.

### Q: Can a rejected amendment prove what a statute means?

A rejected amendment supplied weak proof of meaning, because a proposal could fail for many reasons that the record rarely disentangled. Members could vote against an amendment because the amendment stated a rule that members opposed, duplicated language already in place, created drafting problems, belonged in conference settlement or served as a vehicle for delay. Sponsors sometimes opposed an amendment as unnecessary on the express ground that the text already accomplished the stated purpose of the amendment, a posture that supported the opposite inference from the conventional reading of a defeat. Defeat therefore functioned safely as a question for investigation, supported where possible by statements of the floor manager during consideration, rather than as a conclusion that a researcher could assert from the vote alone.

### Q: What records survive for a bill that never became law?

A measure that never became law left records at each stage the measure reached before failure. The introduced version preserved the proposal in the form in which a sponsor placed the measure before a chamber, and failed proposals formed part of the documentary record even though no part became enacted law. Hearings held on the measure preserved testimony and material submitted for the record, and committee prints preserved staff studies, compilations and draft measures prepared for committee use. Where a committee ordered the measure reported, a reported version and an accompanying committee report survived, and floor proceedings survived where a chamber considered the measure. No enrolled bill, public law or conference settlement closed the sequence for such a measure.

### Q: What do the different versions of a bill show a researcher?

The successive versions showed a measure changing in public, stage by stage, with additions, deletions and substitutions fixed to identifiable actions. The introduced version supplied the baseline, the reported version isolated changes made in committee, the versions passed by the House and by the Senate marked agreement and divergence between the chambers, and the enrolled bill fixed the final text passed identically by both chambers. Comparison revealed which formulations drafters kept, which formulations committees altered and which formulations survived repeated opportunities for removal. Deleted language proved an event of removal without proving the reason for removal, so interpretation of motive required corroboration from reports, statements or other material in the surrounding record.

### Q: What should a student remember about legislative history for an exam?

A student answered best by holding the two halves of the discipline together: finding the documents and discounting the documents by weight. The hierarchy placed the conference report and joint explanatory statement first, the committee report second, sponsor and floor manager statements next, hearings and general debate lower, and statements by opponents and post enactment material at the lowest level. The Congressional Record counted as a substantially verbatim account rather than a strict transcript. Coverage dates governed every database search, with Congress.gov bill records from 1973 and committee reports from 1995 serving as anchor points, and the dispute between textualist and purposivist accounts remained a dispute to describe accurately rather than a question to settle.
