## The Agricultural Act as Permanent Law

The Agricultural Act of 1949 occupies a position unlike that of an ordinary authorization statute. Congress enacted it without an expiration date, and the generations of farm legislation that followed did not repeal it. Each successive farm bill instead suspended its operation for a defined period, leaving the text in place while a different commodity program governed in its stead. The consequence of that arrangement appears only when the sequence breaks. If a farm bill lapses and no successor or extension takes its place, the suspension lapses with it, and the older statute revives by its own force. Revival would not restore a modest safety net written for contemporary conditions. It would restore mandatory price supports calculated from parity, a standard anchored to the relationship between prices farmers received and prices farmers paid during the years from 1910 to 1914, before the First World War transformed American agriculture. A failure of legislative timing would therefore produce a substantive change in farm policy, returning the Department of Agriculture to formulas, allotments, quotas, and purchase obligations that Congress had kept dormant for decades precisely because their operation would be so disruptive.

The formality of that identity matters because the statute is often treated in debate as a background abstraction rather than as a specific enactment with a citation that can be checked. The Agricultural Act of 1949 is Public Law 81-439, published at 63 Stat. 1051, signed by President Harry S. Truman on October 31, 1949, and enacted by the 81st Congress. Its provisions are codified principally at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following, a placement explained by the location of its general and Commodity Credit Corporation provisions in title IV of the act, even though its price support titles sit at 7 U.S.C. 1441 and following. The 1949 act does not stand alone as permanent law. Together with the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, Public Law 75-430, and the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948, Public Law 80-806, it forms the permanent law from which farm commodity program authority derives, a formulation the Congressional Research Service used in describing the commodity provisions of the farm bill enacted in 2014. Those three enactments supply the enduring statutory foundation, and each farm bill builds a temporary structure on top of that foundation rather than replacing the foundation itself.

That layered structure explains why expiration carries a meaning in farm legislation that expiration does not carry in most other fields of federal law. In an ordinary program statute, the end of an authorization period leaves a gap. Agencies lose authority to undertake new obligations under the expired provisions, programs wind down or wait for appropriations and reauthorization, and the legal condition after expiration is an absence. Under the farm bill structure, the condition after a lapse is not an absence but a substitution in reverse. The temporary program recedes and the permanent program advances, because the permanent program never left. The suspension language had held it back, and the suspension language was written to last only as long as the farm bill that contained it. What returns is not a general authority to support farm income by whatever means the Secretary of Agriculture considers sensible. What returns is a detailed and mandatory code, written for the commodity economy of the middle of the twentieth century, prescribing support levels as percentages of parity and attaching production controls to some of the highest levels of support.

### What does suspension actually do to a provision that Congress leaves in the statute?

Suspension leaves the provision in the statute book, word for word, while a separate provision declares that it shall not be applicable to named crops, commodities, and years. The text survives, the authority sleeps, and the farm bill program operates in its place until the stated period ends.

The mechanics deserve close attention because the word suspension can suggest an informal pause, a decision by an agency to hold a program in abeyance while circumstances change. Nothing of that kind occurs under the farm bill arrangement. Suspension is itself a statutory act. Congress enacts language declaring that specified provisions of the 1938 act and the 1949 act shall not be applicable to specified crops of covered commodities, to cotton and sugar, and to milk for a specified period, and that declaration has the force of law for exactly the period it names and no longer. The suspended provisions are not amended into a new form and they are not repealed subject to a later revival statute. They remain where the Code places them, with their mandatory verbs intact, while the nonapplicability language directs readers and administrators to pass over them for the crops and years covered by the farm bill. When the period named in the nonapplicability language ends, no further act of Congress is required to restore the older provisions to operation. Their restoration is the default consequence written into the structure from the beginning.

The codified home of the modern form of that device is 7 U.S.C. 9092. In the farm bill enacted in 2014, the suspension appeared as section 1602 of that act. The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, signed on December 20, 2018, took a shorter path. Its section 1702 simply extended the existing suspension by striking the year 2018 and inserting the year 2023. That drafting choice captures the relationship between the two layers of law with unusual clarity. A single substitution of one year for another in the suspension provision moved the revival date for the entire permanent law edifice, extending the period during which the 2018 act commodity programs would govern the crops through 2023 and milk through December 31, 2023. The permanent provisions underneath did not change at all. Only the duration of their enforced inactivity changed, and that change was accomplished by amending the switch rather than the machine the switch controls.

The text of the suspension in its 2014 form shows how completely Congress intended the older law to be held in check during the period of a farm bill. The language provided that the listed provisions shall not be applicable to the 2014 through 2018 crops of the covered commodities, cotton, and sugar, and shall not be applicable to milk during the period from enactment through December 31, 2018. The 2018 act extended the same lists through the 2023 crops and through December 31, 2023 for milk. The lists themselves run across both permanent statutes. From the 1938 act, the suspension reaches parts II through V of subtitle B of title III, section 377 for upland cotton, subtitle D of title III, title IV, and the joint resolution of May 26, 1941 concerning corn and wheat marketing quotas. From the 1949 act, the suspension reaches section 101, section 103(a), section 105, section 107, section 110, section 112, section 115, section 201, title III, title V, title VI, and title IV with three stated exceptions. A reader who follows those citations encounters the core of the old support system, its dairy provision, its loan and purchase authorities, and its related titles, each one identified and each one switched off by name.

The exceptions to the suspension are as instructive as the list of provisions suspended. Sections 404, 412, and 416 of the 1949 act were excepted from the suspension of title IV, and those sections operate through every farm bill rather than sleeping between enactments. Section 416, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1431, supplies authority for the donation of surplus commodities. Its continuous operation demonstrates that permanent law is not a single block that Congress treats in a single way. Parts of the 1949 act never sleep, because Congress has reason to keep them available under all conditions, while the price support machinery beside them is held inactive because Congress has no intention of allowing that machinery to govern while a farm bill program exists. The distinction between the excepted sections and the suspended sections therefore marks a deliberate legislative judgment about which parts of the older statute remain useful as standing authority and which parts survive only as the consequence that follows a failure to legislate.

Suspension in this form was not the only technique Congress used, and the history of the technique clarifies what the modern language accomplishes. Each farm bill since the farm bills of the 1960s and 1970s contained a temporary suspension of permanent law in one form or another. Earlier enactments sometimes proceeded differently, supplanting permanent law from within the permanent statutes themselves, as the farm bill enacted in 1970, Public Law 91-524, did in part. From the farm bill enacted in 1977 onward, farm bills used direct suspension or nonapplicability language, stating in terms that identified provisions of the older acts would not apply for the period and the commodities the new act covered. The section numbers carrying the suspension moved from bill to bill, appearing in sections 409 and 410 and several companion sections in 1977, in a consolidated section 171 in 1996, and in section 1602 in each of the farm bills enacted in 2002, 2008, and 2014, before section 1702 of the 2018 act extended the arrangement. The continuity lies not in the numbering but in the function. Whatever section carried it, the language performed the same task of holding a complete older program in reserve beneath a complete newer one.

Understanding the device also requires understanding the vehicle that carries it, because suspension has no independent existence apart from the farm bill whose period it shares. A farm bill is a temporary, multi-year statute that revises commodity support, conservation, nutrition, credit, research, and related programs for a defined coverage period, and then gives way to a successor. The [history and structure of the farm bill](https://insightcrunch.com/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/) supply the setting in which a permanent statute can be switched off for five years at a time by a law that is itself expected to expire. The paradox is built into the pairing. The expiring law is the one that governs in ordinary times, and the law without an expiration date is the one that governs only in the extraordinary circumstance of a lapse. Longevity and practical authority run in opposite directions, and the suspension clause is the hinge on which that inversion turns.

The contrast with an ordinary authorization lapse can be drawn more sharply by considering what administrators would face on the morning after each kind of expiration. After an ordinary lapse, an agency confronts missing authority and must determine which operations it may continue with appropriated funds, which obligations it may honor, and which activities must stop until Congress acts again. After a farm bill lapse that carries the suspension down with it, the Department of Agriculture confronts the opposite problem. Authority would not be missing. Authority would be excessive, mandatory, and badly fitted to the markets it would govern. The Congressional Research Service has explained that, if the suspension expires, the Department of Agriculture is required to begin implementing permanent law, although the department may need time to write and publish new regulations, and market effects may develop gradually over weeks or months rather than arriving on a single day. Implementation would therefore be a process rather than an instant transformation, but the direction of the process would be fixed by law. The department would be building regulations to carry out parity supports, allotments, and quota referenda, not waiting in a legal vacuum for Congress to supply a program.

What the department would be required to build explains why the dormant statute retains such force in negotiation. Permanent law support levels run between 50 and 90 percent of parity depending on the commodity, according to the Congressional Research Service. Parity itself is defined in section 301(a) of the 1938 act, at 7 U.S.C. 1301(a), under which the parity price for a commodity equals its adjusted base price multiplied by the parity index. The index measures the relationship between prices farmers receive and prices farmers pay, including inputs, interest, taxes, and wage rates, against the base period from 1910 to 1914. Because agricultural productivity grew enormously across the century that followed that base period while the formula preserved the older relationship as its benchmark, parity prices for the supported commodities sit at multiples of market prices in the modern era, and support at even the lowest levels in the permanent ranges exceeds market prices for every supported commodity. A revived statute would therefore not merely inconvenience administrators. It would oblige the government to support prices at levels the market had not sustained, through nonrecourse loans, purchases, acreage allotments, and marketing quotas, for dairy products, wheat, rice, cotton, corn, and other feed grains.

The commodity detail confirms that revival would be a change of system rather than a change of rates. For milk and butterfat, section 201 of the 1949 act mandates purchases at 75 to 90 percent of parity. For wheat, the permanent provisions supply nonrecourse loans and purchases at levels that vary with the status of marketing quotas, descending to 50 percent of parity where quotas are disapproved. Upland cotton carries nonrecourse loans at 65 to 90 percent of parity together with acreage allotments. Corn carries nonrecourse loans at 50 to 90 percent of parity without authorized acreage allotments, and the other feed grains, sorghum, barley, oats, and rye, are set by feeding value relative to corn. At the same time, the revived law would give no parity support to soybeans and other oilseeds or to sugar, even though farm bills in the modern era cover those commodities along with many others that permanent law omits. The map of supported production would contract to the commodities of an earlier agricultural economy while the support offered to those commodities rose to levels drawn from that same earlier economy. Both halves of that result follow from the age of the statute, and both halves make revival an outcome that commodity producers, administrators, and budget officials have reason to avoid for different and sometimes opposing reasons.

The selective character of the device extends beyond commodities to the programs that a farm bill lapse would leave untouched. Federal crop insurance rests on permanent authority of its own and does not expire with the farm bill. Food assistance rests on a separate permanent authorization, and an appropriations act or continuing resolution can carry its operations through a lapse. The provisions that actually stop when a farm bill expires are principally programs whose authority expires and that have no other funding route, above all smaller programs without a budget baseline. Permanent law revival is therefore not a general collapse of agricultural government, and it is not a general continuation of it either. It is a narrow, intense event concentrated in the commodity title, where an old and powerful statute waits beneath a new and temporary one. That concentration gives the device its political precision. The pressure of a lapse falls on the specific programs, producers, and committees responsible for writing the next farm bill, rather than dispersing across the whole field of agricultural and nutrition policy.

Congress has had repeated opportunities to remove that pressure by repealing the older statutes, and the record of those opportunities forms part of the device itself. Proposals to repeal permanent law have been rare across the decades in which suspension became routine, and only a small number advanced beyond introduction. The furthest advance came in the middle of the 1990s, when repeal appeared in versions of budget reconciliation legislation and in versions of the farm bill enacted in 1996, only to be dropped in conference in favor of continued suspension. In 2013, the farm bill passed by the House of Representatives would have repealed the permanent laws of 1938 and 1949 and made its own commodity program the new permanent law, but the enacted legislation of 2014 continued suspension instead, as did the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018. The Congressional Research Service records that some observers regard the existence of permanent law, and the policy and budget consequences of restoring it, as assurance that Congress will revisit the farm commodity programs when a farm bill expires. Critics of the arrangement characterize it as a manufactured crisis, while defenders characterize it as necessary discipline. The competing descriptions agree on the underlying mechanism even as they dispute its legitimacy. Each side recognizes that the dormant statute changes the incentives surrounding expiration.

For that reason, the continuing existence of the 1949 act cannot be explained adequately as legislative inertia, as an oversight preserved by the difficulty of cleaning up the Code. A statute that Congress reexamines every few years, that Congress lists provision by provision in every suspension clause, that Congress excepts in part and suspends in part, and that Congress has repeatedly declined to repeal when repeal was squarely presented is not a statute Congress has forgotten. It is a statute Congress has chosen, farm bill after farm bill, to keep in reserve. The choice is renewed each time a suspension passes, because passage of a suspension is itself an acknowledgment that the underlying law would otherwise operate. Every legislator who votes for a farm bill votes for a text that names the older provisions and declares them inapplicable for a period, and the naming is the acknowledgment. To suspend a statute is to concede that the statute lives, and to fix the date on which its life would resume if the suspension were allowed to run out.

The namable claim of the statute, stated plainly, is therefore that the Agricultural Act of 1949 functions as a deliberate legislative doomsday machine. It is a law Congress maintains precisely because the prospect of its operation compels the enactment of a new farm bill before the suspension expires. Its formulas are obsolete for the agriculture they would govern, its support levels are detached from the markets they would command, and its controls belong to an era of allotments and referenda that farm policy left behind, and none of those defects weakens its function. The defects are its function. Because revival would be so costly, so disruptive, and so difficult to administer, expiration ceases to be a neutral option and becomes a deadline with consequences attached, and a statute enacted in 1949 to support farm prices ends up governing the legislative calendar of every generation that refuses either to use it or to let it go.

## The Road to a Permanent Farm Law

The Agricultural Act of 1949 did not arrive as an isolated invention. It arrived at the end of a long argument about how the federal government should stand between farmers and the market, an argument that had already produced one invalidated statute, one rebuilt statute, a wartime system of high guarantees, and a postwar disagreement about whether those guarantees should harden into permanence or soften into flexibility. The machinery of 1949 came largely from 1938. The price philosophy came from the parity idea that both statutes shared. The immediate political shape came from a three way contest in 1949 among an administration plan that Congress refused, a House bill that wanted supports kept high and rigid, and a Senate bill that wanted them flexible within a defended range. Understanding the statute therefore begins well before its signing, with the collapse of the first attempt and the careful reconstruction that followed it.

### Why did the earlier farm program have to be rebuilt?

The scheme created by the act of 1933 rested on a processor tax that did not survive the decision in United States v. Butler in 1936, leaving Congress to build a successor financed from federal funds and placed on a different statutory footing altogether.

That single sentence carries most of what the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 was. Congress did not abandon federal farm support after the invalidation of the 1933 act. Instead, Congress rebuilt the program on another basis, and the rebuilding shaped American farm policy for generations. The 1938 act, Public Law 75-430, was signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on February 16, 1938, and codified principally at 7 U.S.C. 1281 and following. Its constitutional companion was the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act of 1936. Where the 1933 scheme had drawn its money from a tax on processors, the 1938 act funded its programs federally. That change in financing was more than an accounting detail. A program paid for through general federal funding rested on the spending choices of Congress as a whole, and it could be presented as a national policy for agriculture rather than as a levy transferred from one part of the food economy to another. The processor tax had tied the fate of farm payments to a narrow and contested revenue device. Federal funding loosened that tie and gave the successor statute a broader foundation on which to stand.

The tools assembled in 1938 became the permanent vocabulary of farm support. One tool was the ever-normal granary, the idea that abundance and scarcity should be balanced across seasons rather than left to collide in a single market year. In a year of heavy harvests, a crop could be stored and held off a glutted market instead of being sold at once into falling prices. In a leaner year, stored supplies could meet demand and moderate the opposite extreme. The granary concept treated surpluses not simply as waste or failure but as a reserve to be managed, and it treated storage itself as a form of public policy. That idea required an instrument to make it work, because a farmer facing immediate expenses could not always afford to hold a crop and wait for a better price. The nonrecourse loan supplied the instrument. At harvest, a producer could pledge the crop as collateral for a loan at the loan rate. If market prices stayed below the loan rate, the producer could forfeit the crop to the government and keep the principal. The loan thus set a floor under the transaction without ordering any buyer to pay a particular price, and forfeiture turned the stored crop into a public holding when the market would not clear it at the supported level.

Parity supplied the measure by which those floors were set. The parity price for a commodity, defined at section 301(a) of the 1938 act, equaled its adjusted base price multiplied by the parity index. Parity measured the relationship between the prices farmers received and the prices farmers paid for inputs, interest, taxes, and wage rates against the base period from 1910 to 1914, years often described as a golden age of American farm purchasing power. The ambition embedded in the formula was restoration. A farmer was not merely to receive a price that cleared the market on a given day. A farmer was to receive a price that bore a defined relationship to the costs of farming, modeled on a period when that relationship had been favorable. Parity payments and supports expressed in percentages of parity therefore carried a historical claim inside a technical calculation. To support a crop at a stated share of parity was to say that the federal government accepted some responsibility for closing the distance between market outcomes and a benchmark of fairness drawn from an earlier agricultural economy.

Supply management supplied the discipline that accompanied the guarantee. The 1938 framework used acreage allotments and marketing quotas, devices meant to keep production from overwhelming the very supports meant to stabilize it. A support price without any attention to supply invited larger harvests, larger forfeitures, and larger public stocks. Allotments limited the acreage on which supported production could occur, and quotas went further by restricting the quantity that could be marketed. Under the permanent framework, quotas for commodities such as wheat and cotton required a producer referendum and a two-thirds affirmative vote of eligible producers, and approved quotas brought the highest support levels together with mandatory restrictions, while producers who marketed above quota faced penalties and lost loan eligibility. The bargain was plain. Producers who accepted collective restraint received the strongest federal guarantee, and producers who stood outside the restraint could not claim its benefits on the same terms. Support and control were not separate policies. Each was the condition of the other.

### What did the wartime experience change about farm supports?

Wartime demand turned a program designed to restrain surpluses into a program expected to encourage production, and supports held at 90 percent of parity gave producers a firm guarantee while the emergency lasted, raising the practical question of what guarantee should replace it afterward.

The wartime years reversed the ordinary problem of farm policy. A system built in the shadow of surplus, built to store crops, restrict acreage, and keep prices from collapsing under abundance, was asked during wartime to call forth as much production as the land and its operators could deliver. The same guarantee that in peacetime might have encouraged overproduction became in wartime an assurance that additional effort would not be ruined by a price collapse. Supports ran at 90 percent of parity, a high level that told producers the federal commitment behind their output was strong and predictable. High supports had been lived experience for producers across the basic commodities, and any postwar reduction would be felt not as a return to an ordinary baseline but as the withdrawal of a guarantee that had organized real decisions for years.

That memory made the postwar transition the central difficulty. Ending high supports abruptly risked a sharp fall in farm income at the very moment producers were adjusting from wartime demand to peacetime markets. Keeping high supports indefinitely risked the opposite disorder, because guarantees set for emergency production could stimulate output that peacetime demand could not absorb, filling storage, swelling federal holdings, and converting a price policy into a surplus policy. Neither course commanded agreement, and the disagreement was not really about whether the federal government belonged in farm markets. The programs of 1938 had settled that question in practice, and wartime experience had deepened the settlement. The disagreement concerned the form of the federal presence. Should supports be rigid, fixed at a high percentage of parity regardless of supply, so that producers could rely on a known figure and plan around it? Or should supports be flexible, moving within a range as supply conditions changed, so that the guarantee itself carried a signal about abundance and scarcity? The first answer privileged income certainty. The second privileged adjustment. The legislation of 1948 and 1949 was the arena in which those two answers contended.

The Agricultural Act of 1948, known as the Aiken Act and enacted on July 3, 1948, chose adjustment, but chose it on a delayed schedule. The 1948 act scheduled a move to flexible supports from 1950, which meant that the high wartime pattern would not be displaced at once and that the new philosophy would govern only after a further interval. Delay was itself a policy choice. It gave producers time, it gave Congress time, and it postponed the confrontation between the two philosophies until the flexible system was about to take effect. The 1948 act also carried the modernized parity formula, with titles II and III effective January 1, 1950, under which the adjusted base price used the average prices received for a commodity over the most recent ten years, rescaled against the parity index. That technical change mattered because it altered what the guarantee was anchored to. Parity computed through a moving recent average behaved differently from parity tied more tightly to older relationships, and a flexible support applied to a modernized parity price was a different promise from a rigid support applied to an older measure. By the time Congress returned to the question in 1949, therefore, two separate issues were entangled. One was the level and rigidity of support. The other was the definition of the benchmark from which support percentages were calculated. A percentage could sound unchanged while the underlying price it produced moved, and every participant in the 1949 debate understood that the formula and the percentage had to be read together.

The scheduled arrival of flexible supports in 1950 also created the deadline that forced the 1949 contest. Without further action, the 1948 settlement would govern, and the flexible approach would move from enacted text to operating policy. Opponents of that approach had one legislative season in which to displace it with something else, and supporters of flexibility had the same season in which to defend or revise it. Into that opening stepped the Truman administration with a proposal that reframed the entire question, and its defeat narrowed the field to the two congressional alternatives between which the final compromise was eventually struck.

### Why did the Brannan Plan fail while a compromise succeeded?

The Brannan Plan asked Congress to separate consumer prices from producer income, letting markets clear while the Treasury paid the difference, and the House rejected that approach in the Pace bill, leaving rigid and flexible support bills as the remaining paths to a settlement.

Secretary of Agriculture Charles F. Brannan proposed that market prices be allowed to fall to clearing levels and that producers be paid the difference as income payments. The logic of the plan separated two things that the loan and purchase system had fused together. Under the older machinery, supporting farm income meant supporting the market price itself, holding it above the level an unaided market would have reached and accepting the storage, forfeiture, and supply problems that followed. Under the Brannan approach, the market price could do its ordinary work of clearing supply against demand, food could move to consumers at the price the market set, and the federal commitment to farm income would be honored through a direct payment calculated against the gap. The producer would be made whole without the government having to buy, store, or hold the physical crop in order to make the price. In political terms the plan asked Congress to replace a familiar guarantee, embedded in loans and supported prices that producers could see, with a payment system whose size depended on the distance between a market outcome and a standard set by law.

The House refused the reframing. A House bill embodying Brannan features, the Pace bill, was voted down, and with that vote the administration alternative left the field. The defeat deserves attention because it defined what the Agricultural Act of 1949 would not be. The finished statute would not abandon price support as its central mechanism. It would not let market prices clear freely and settle the federal obligation afterward in cash. It would remain inside the tradition that ran from 1938 through the wartime years, using loans, purchases, percentages of parity, allotments, and quotas. Whatever flexibility the final law contained would be flexibility within that tradition, a movement of support levels inside a range, rather than a replacement of the tradition itself. The Brannan Plan survived the 1949 contest as an idea about how farm income might be supported by other means, but as legislation it was finished, and the remaining question was the older and narrower one of how high supports should be and how firmly they should be fixed.

That narrower question divided Congress deeply, because a rigid support and a flexible support distributed risk in opposite ways. A rigid high support placed the risk of surplus largely on the government, which would stand behind a fixed percentage of parity and absorb the consequences in loans taken, crops forfeited, and stocks accumulated. A flexible support placed more of the risk on producers, whose guarantee would fall as supply rose, but it also used the guarantee itself as a steering device, offering stronger support when supplies were restrained and weaker support when production pressed against demand. Behind the technical vocabulary stood different judgments about what had gone wrong in American agriculture between the wars and what might go wrong again in peace. One judgment feared price collapse above all and treated a firm floor as the essential protection. The other feared chronic surplus above all and treated adjustability as the essential safeguard. Both judgments could claim descent from the experience of 1938 to 1945, and both were represented in the bills that Congress took up after the Brannan Plan fell.

The House passed the Gore bill, sponsored by Representative Albert Gore Sr., continuing high rigid supports. The Gore bill answered the scheduled flexibility of the 1948 act by holding the line at the level producers had known during wartime, treating the high percentage not as an emergency expedient to be retired but as the proper standing terms of federal farm policy. Its appeal rested on certainty. A producer under a rigid support knew the floor around which planting and borrowing decisions would be made, and rural credit, land values, and local trade all organized themselves more easily around a guarantee that did not move with administrative judgments about supply. The Senate passed the Anderson bill, sponsored by Senator Clinton P. Anderson of New Mexico, a former Secretary of Agriculture, which modified title II of the 1948 act toward flexible supports with a somewhat higher lower limit than the 1948 settlement had provided. The Anderson bill accepted the principle of movement within a range while softening its lower edge, so that flexibility would not mean exposure to the full depth of a price fall. Anderson brought to the argument the perspective of an administrator who had operated farm programs rather than merely debated them, and his bill read as an attempt to keep the steering function of flexible supports while conceding that the floor beneath them needed to be raised if the system was to command durable support in the Senate and in farm country.

Between the Gore and Anderson bills lay the material for a settlement, because the two bills differed in degree along a shared scale rather than in kind. Both operated through parity, both preserved the support machinery inherited from 1938, and both covered the same basic commodities, the group that under the 1938 and 1949 acts comprised corn, wheat, cotton, rice, tobacco, and peanuts. The conference therefore had to choose a path across time, deciding how long the high wartime level would be prolonged and how wide the band of flexibility would be once movement began, and the compromise followed exactly that logic.

As enacted, the act supported the basic commodities at 90 percent of parity through the 1950 crops, preserving the familiar wartime figure for the first full cycle under the new law. For 1951 it set a permissive minimum of 80 percent, except for tobacco, easing the level downward by a single step rather than dropping it to the bottom of a range at once. Thereafter it provided flexible supports of 75 to 90 percent of parity, keyed to supply, with the Secretary of Agriculture setting the level within the range under statutory supply stipulations. Each element of that sequence answered a party to the negotiation. The 90 percent figure answered the House demand that the postwar settlement not begin with retreat. The 80 percent step for 1951 answered the need for a visible transition, a year in which the level would soften without yet becoming variable. The range of 75 to 90 percent answered the Senate insistence that support respond to supply, while its floor of 75 percent embodied the higher lower limit that the Anderson bill had sought, ensuring that flexibility operated between defended boundaries. Even the discretion left to the Secretary was bounded discretion, exercised inside a statutory band and under stipulations tied to supply, so that administrative judgment moved the level without rewriting the policy.

President Harry S. Truman signed the Agricultural Act of 1949 on October 31, 1949. The statute, Public Law 81-439, enacted by the 81st Congress and codified principally at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following, carried no expiration date. That absence attracts little attention in the context of its enactment and a great deal of attention in every later context, and the difference between those two reactions is the heart of the matter. Farm statutes of the period were working law, amended, adjusted, and revisited as conditions changed, and the 1949 act was in fact adjusted soon enough, with the acts of 1954 and 1956 altering its support ranges as it operated as the farm law of the early 1950s. A Congress that expected to keep legislating about agriculture had no particular reason to dwell on the theoretical consequence of the text remaining in the statute books beneath later laws. The text was not drafted as a trap for a distant future. It was drafted as the governing program for the crops immediately ahead, by legislators resolving a concrete dispute about support levels in the first postwar decade.

That ordinariness is precisely what gives the later history its strange character. Nobody in the 1949 conference set out to design a doomsday machine. The conferees were settling the price of political agreement between a House that wanted rigidity and a Senate that wanted flexibility, using delay, a stepped transition, and a defended range to make the settlement acceptable to both. The producers whose incomes the law addressed were concerned with the distance between 90 percent of parity and 75 percent, a distance measured in the viability of farms in the seasons directly before them. All of those concerns belonged to the law's own moment, and the law answered them in the vocabulary of that moment. Its survival as a suspended backstop under later farm bills, held in reserve by a practice that belongs to the farm bills of the 1960s and 1970s onward rather than to the design of 1949, was an afterlife that its authors had not planned and its text had not announced. The strangeness lies in that mismatch. A statute written to govern its own time, through tools built for the problems of surplus and wartime demand, remained on the books after the system around it had been repeatedly replaced, so that the ordinary farm law of 1949 became, by suspension rather than by intention, the law waiting underneath every temporary successor.

## The Architecture of the Two Permanent Statutes and the Suspension Lists

Permanent law has a physical shape on the page, and that shape explains much of the behavior attributed to the device in political debate. Two statutes enacted eleven years apart carry the commodity support system between them. The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 came first, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on February 16, 1938, enacted by the Seventy-fifth Congress as Public Law 75-430 at 52 Stat. 31, and classified principally to 7 U.S.C. 1281 and following. The Agricultural Act of 1949 followed, signed by President Harry S. Truman on October 31, 1949, enacted by the Eighty-first Congress as Public Law 81-439 at 63 Stat. 1051, and classified principally to 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following. The earlier statute supplies definitions, a measurement system, and machinery for controlling production, while the later statute supplies a large share of the operative price support commands. Modern farm bills suspend provisions drawn from both texts in a single breath, because the working program was never contained within a single enactment.

The division of labor reflects the circumstances of enactment. The 1938 act replaced the scheme of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 after the Supreme Court invalidated that scheme in United States v. Butler in 1936. The replacement dropped the processor tax that had funded the earlier design and funded programs federally instead, and the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act of 1936 stood as a constitutional companion. The 1949 act arrived after wartime supports had run at 90 percent of parity and after the Agricultural Act of 1948 had scheduled a move toward flexible supports from 1950, and the later text reads as working instructions layered over foundations the earlier statute had poured. A farm bill cannot suspend the 1949 commands alone, because the definitions and production controls of 1938 would remain awake underneath, and it cannot suspend the 1938 foundations alone, because the 1949 commands would lack a coherent basis for calculation.

### The Earlier Statute and the Machinery of Title Three

Title III of the 1938 act forms the center of gravity for the older text, and its compound subject matter joins four elements a modern reader might expect to find scattered across separate laws. Loans, parity payments, consumer safeguards, and marketing quotas sit together in a single title because the design treated the four elements as parts of one transaction between the government and the producer. A loan supported the price of a stored crop. A parity payment supplemented returns toward a measured standard of purchasing power. Consumer safeguards limited the reach of the program at the point where support for producers might press too hard on purchasers. Marketing quotas restrained the volume that could move to market when abundance threatened to defeat the support effort. The loan rate meant little without a benchmark, the benchmark meant little without a payment or purchase mechanism, and the support structure meant little if unrestricted production could overwhelm the supported price season after season.

The benchmark sits at section 301(a) of the 1938 act, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1301(a). The parity price for a commodity equals its adjusted base price multiplied by the parity index. Behind that formula stands a comparison between the prices farmers receive and the prices farmers pay, with the second set embracing inputs, interest, taxes, and wage rates. The comparison is anchored to the period from 1910 to 1914, years often described as a golden age of American farm purchasing power, and parity expresses any later relationship between received and paid prices as a proportion of the relationship that held during the base period. The adjusted base price draws on the average prices received for the commodity over the most recent ten years, rescaled, and that element reflects the modernized parity formula introduced by the Agricultural Act of 1948, with titles II and III of that act effective January 1, 1950, and carried through the era of the 1949 act. Parity prices are computed under section 301(a) as amended by the Agricultural Acts of 1948, 1949, and 1956, and the National Agricultural Statistics Service estimates and publishes parity prices monthly in Agricultural Prices.

The same title houses the machinery of acreage allotments and marketing quota referenda. For wheat and cotton, permanent law requires the Department of Agriculture to announce acreage allotments and to hold producer referenda on marketing quotas, so the governed producers vote on the restraint that would govern them. Quotas require a two-thirds affirmative vote of eligible producers, and approved quotas bring the highest support levels and mandatory restrictions. Wheat illustrates the gradient, with nonrecourse loans and purchases at 75 to 90 percent of parity where marketing quotas are not announced, at 65 to 90 percent where quotas are approved, and at 50 percent where quotas are disapproved, with acreage allotments attached. A producer who markets above an approved quota pays penalties and loses loan eligibility. Each step depends on statutory text that suspension must reach if the system is to sleep as a whole.

Subtitle D of title III stands within the 1938 act as a discrete block, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1379a and following, and the suspension lists name the subtitle as a unit rather than picking through its provisions one at a time. Other portions of title III receive finer treatment, with parts II through V of subtitle B named separately. Title IV of the 1938 act, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1401 and following, closes the older statute for suspension purposes, and the lists take the whole title in a single reference. The pattern across the 1938 references mixes the surgical and the sweeping, a few named fragments alongside entire titles.

### The Later Statute and Its Titles

Title I of the Agricultural Act of 1949 carries price support for the basic commodities, and the basic commodities under the 1938 and 1949 acts together comprise corn, wheat, cotton, rice, tobacco, and peanuts. The support provisions run from section 101 to section 115, and the suspension lists identify the principal provisions among them by their homes in the Code. Section 101 sits at 7 U.S.C. 1441. Subsection (a) of section 103 sits at 7 U.S.C. 1444(a). Section 105 sits at 7 U.S.C. 1444b, section 107 at 7 U.S.C. 1445a, section 110 at 7 U.S.C. 1445e, section 112 at 7 U.S.C. 1445g, and section 115 at 7 U.S.C. 1445k. The sequence of Code citations, marching through the 1440s in step with the section numbers of the original act, preserves the architecture of the 1949 text inside the arrangement of the Code. As enacted in 1949, the title supported the basic commodities at 90 percent of parity through the 1950 crops, set a permissive minimum of 80 percent for 1951 except for tobacco, and provided flexible supports of 75 to 90 percent thereafter, keyed to supply, with the Secretary of Agriculture setting the level within the range under statutory supply stipulations. Across permanent law more generally, support levels run between 50 and 90 percent of parity depending on the commodity, according to the Congressional Research Service.

The support technique behind the title I commands recurs across the permanent system. Support runs through nonrecourse loans. At harvest a producer pledges the crop as collateral for a loan at the loan rate, and if market prices remain below the loan rate, the producer may forfeit the crop to the government and keep the principal, so the loan operates as a floor under the market rather than as ordinary credit. A permanent repayment provision, added by section 1009 of the Food Security Act of 1985 and codified at 7 U.S.C. 1308a, lets producers repay at less than the loan rate, in the manner of modern marketing loans. Upland cotton takes nonrecourse loans at 65 to 90 percent of parity, with acreage allotments. Corn takes nonrecourse loans at 50 to 90 percent, with no acreage allotments authorized. Other feed grains, namely sorghum, barley, oats, and rye, are set by feeding value relative to corn. Rice stands at 50 to 90 percent, and rice permanent authority was repealed by the 1981 farm bill and restored by the 1996 farm bill. Honey takes purchases at 60 to 90 percent. No parity support exists for soybeans and other oilseeds or for sugar, and parity supports once existed for wool, mohair, and peanuts but were repealed, with peanut support ending through the buyout in the farm bill of 2002.

Section 201 of the 1949 act, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1446, carries the dairy provision, placed at that section number and Code citation in the territory following title I. The provision makes support mandatory for whole milk, butterfat, and the products of such commodities, at the level between 75 and 90 percent of parity that will assure an adequate supply. Dairy received separate classification because milk is perishable and must be supplied daily, a physical fact that shaped both the substance of the provision and the later politics of expiration. The standing program built on the provision is the Dairy Price Support Program, under which the Department of Agriculture, through the Commodity Credit Corporation, stands ready to purchase cheddar cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk at announced purchase prices calculated to drive the farm price of milk up to the support level. Manufactured products serve as the purchase vehicle because fluid milk cannot be stockpiled, and purchases of storable products transmit the support price back to the farm price of milk. Farm bills suspend section 201 alongside the crop provisions even though milk moves on a calendar year rather than a crop year, a difference in timing that gives dairy a distinctive place when a suspension lapses.

Title III of the 1949 act, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1447 and following, enters the suspension lists as a whole title, taken in one reference rather than through an enumeration of internal provisions. Title V, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1461 and following, and title VI, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1471 and following, receive the same wholesale treatment. The contrast with title I, whose provisions are gathered section by section, and with title IV, whose suspension carries express exceptions, shows a drafter calibrating the instrument to the target. Where provisions within a title must be separated into those that sleep and those that operate, the list slows down and names names. The 1949 act also amended sections of the 1938 act and repealed 7 U.S.C. 1302, so the later statute edited the earlier statute even as the two began their joint service as permanent law.

Title IV of the 1949 act holds a special place, because title IV contains the general provisions and the Commodity Credit Corporation provisions, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following. That placement explains a citation practice that puzzles readers who meet the statute through the Code. The Agricultural Act of 1949 is cited as classified principally to 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following even though its price support titles sit at 1441 and following, because the general and Corporation provisions of title IV were placed ahead of the commodity titles in the Code arrangement. Section 404 sits at 7 U.S.C. 1424, section 412 at 7 U.S.C. 1429, and section 416 at 7 U.S.C. 1431, all three inside the title IV sequence, and all three matter disproportionately because the lists suspend title IV except for those three items.

### The Corporation That Operates Every Program

The Commodity Credit Corporation functions as the operating instrument of every support program in the permanent system, and an account of the architecture that stopped at statutory commands would miss the institution that turns commands into transactions. Together with the 1938 act and the 1949 act, the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948 stands as one of the three permanent laws from which farm commodity program authority derives, a grouping recorded by the Congressional Research Service in analysis of the 2014 farm bill. The placement of the Corporation provisions inside title IV of the 1949 act reflects the same reality, since the machinery served every commodity title rather than any one crop. A loan to a corn producer, a purchase of butter under the dairy provision, and the holding of a forfeited crop all pass through the same corporate instrument.

The transactions show why a corporate operator was necessary. A nonrecourse loan requires an entity that lends against a pledged crop, holds or disposes of collateral, and absorbs the forfeited commodity when market prices remain below the loan rate. A purchase program requires an entity that buys cheddar cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk at announced prices and holds the purchased products. The Dairy Price Support Program states the relationship expressly, with the Department of Agriculture acting through the Commodity Credit Corporation to stand ready with purchases calculated to lift the farm price of milk to the support level. Section 416, the surplus donation authority discussed among the surviving exceptions below, likewise presupposes an operator holding commodities that can be donated. Title IV is suspended along with the commodity titles, while the three excepted items preserve the pieces of the title that Congress chose to keep in operation.

### How does a suspension clause switch off permanent law without repealing it?

A suspension clause leaves the permanent text in the United States Code and directs that named provisions shall not be applicable for stated crops and stated periods. Repeal would erase the text. Suspension preserves the text in inactive form, so expiration of the suspension restores operation without new enactment by Congress.

The modern form of the device appears in section 1602 of the Agricultural Act of 2014, codified at 7 U.S.C. 9092. Each farm bill since the farm bills of the 1960s and 1970s has contained a temporary suspension of permanent law, and from the farm bill of 1977 onward, farm bills have used direct suspension or nonapplicability language. Earlier practice differed. The farm bill of 1970, Public Law 91-524, for example, sometimes supplanted permanent law from within the permanent statutes themselves. The mature technique, refined across the suspension items in the farm bills of 1977, 1981, 1985, and 1990 and then in section 171 of the farm bill of 1996, section 1602 of the farm bill of 2002, and section 1602 of the farm bill of 2008, works by enumeration. The drafter lists the provisions that are to sleep, one by one, and attaches a period of nonapplicability to the list. That drafting technique rewards close attention to operative verbs and exception clauses, a discipline explained in [a guide to reading a federal statute](https://insightcrunch.com/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/), because a single omitted citation leaves the omitted provision awake while neighbors sleep.

Section 1602 in its 2014 form directs that the listed provisions shall not be applicable to the 2014 through 2018 crops of covered commodities, cotton, and sugar, and shall not be applicable to milk during the period from enactment through December 31, 2018. Two clocks run inside the single sentence. Crops move on crop years, so crop provisions sleep by reference to the sequence of crops from 2014 through 2018, while milk moves on the calendar, so the dairy item sleeps by reference to a terminal calendar date. The enumeration reaches first into the 1938 act. Parts II through V of subtitle B of title III, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1326 and following, are named as a block. Section 377, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1377, is named for upland cotton. Subtitle D of title III, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1379a and following, is named as a subtitle. Title IV, classified at 7 U.S.C. 1401 and following, is named as a title. The joint resolution of May 26, 1941, concerning corn and wheat marketing quotas and classified at 7 U.S.C. 1330 and 1340, completes the older statute portion of the list, gathering a quota authority that lives outside the two principal acts yet belongs to the same system of corn and wheat control.

The enumeration then turns to the 1949 act and slows to a section by section pace. Section 101 at 7 U.S.C. 1441 is named, and subsection (a) of section 103 at 7 U.S.C. 1444(a) is named at the finer grain of a subsection, a detail that leaves the remainder of section 103 outside the suspension item. Section 105 at 7 U.S.C. 1444b follows, then section 107 at 7 U.S.C. 1445a, section 110 at 7 U.S.C. 1445e, section 112 at 7 U.S.C. 1445g, and section 115 at 7 U.S.C. 1445k. Section 201, the dairy provision at 7 U.S.C. 1446, is named on its own line of the enumeration, carrying the December 31, 2018 terminal date for milk rather than the crop sequence. Title III, at 7 U.S.C. 1447 and following, is then taken as a whole title. Title IV, at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following, is taken with the express exception of sections 404, 412, and 416, at 7 U.S.C. 1424, 1429, and 1431 respectively. Title V, at 7 U.S.C. 1461 and following, and title VI, at 7 U.S.C. 1471 and following, close the enumeration as whole titles.

Extension in 2018 demonstrated how economical the mature technique had become. Section 1702 of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, signed December 20, 2018, extended the suspension by striking 2018 and inserting 2023 in the operative places. Two years changed, and nothing else in the list required alteration. The same provisions slept for the sequence running through the 2023 crops, and the same dairy item slept through December 31, 2023 for milk, stated as enacted schedule. A farm bill that suspends by enumeration creates a text that a later farm bill can prolong with a pair of date substitutions, because the gathering of every fragment across two statutes and a joint resolution was performed once and then inherited by each successor enactment. The list at 7 U.S.C. 9092 thus operates as a standing inventory of permanent law.

### The Provisions That Never Sleep

Against the long enumeration of sleeping provisions stand three short exceptions, and the exceptions complete the anatomy of a suspension. Sections 404, 412, and 416 of the 1949 act are never suspended. The suspension of title IV expressly excepts the three items, so the general title sleeps around three operating provisions that continue through every farm bill. Section 416, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1431, carries the surplus commodity donation authority, and that authority works through every farm bill, moving surplus commodities held under the support system into donation channels under a grant of authority that no farm bill in the suspension sequence has switched off. Parts of the 1949 act never sleep, and the donation authority is the clearest demonstration, because donations presuppose the acquisition and holding functions of the support system and yet continue in periods when the support commands rest in suspension. Inactivity describes the suspended commodity commands that would revive on a lapse, while sections 404, 412, and 416 conduct a narrower band of business without interruption.

The structure that emerges is a layered settlement between permanence and revision. Congress in 1938 built definitions and machinery, Congress in 1949 layered commodity commands and a general title over the foundations, and Congress in each farm bill from the 1970s onward chose enumeration over repeal, listing the sleeping provisions with enough precision to separate a subsection that sleeps from a remainder that operates and a title that sleeps from three items that operate. The 2018 extension, accomplished by changing two years in an inherited list, shows the settlement in its most settled form, and the surviving exceptions show the settlement from the opposite side. The consequences of any future lapse will follow the lines drawn by the titles, subtitles, parts, and items that the suspension lists gathered, and by the three items left awake.

## The Parity Formula and the Flexible Scale

Parity entered the farm statutes as a measurement before it became a support level, and the distinction matters for understanding permanent law. A slogan could promise fairness in the abstract, but the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 supplied a definition that administrators could compute, publish, and apply commodity by commodity. Section 301(a) of the 1938 act, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1301(a), defined the parity price for a commodity as its adjusted base price multiplied by the parity index. The definition turned a political idea about restoring farm purchasing power into an arithmetic operation with two moving parts, each tied to a different side of the farm economy. One part looked backward to prices received for the commodity itself. The other part measured what farmers paid for the goods and services required to produce and live, including interest, taxes, and wage rates. Support under the Agricultural Act of 1949 then took a percentage of the resulting parity price, so the percentage alone never told the whole story. A support level of 75 to 90 percent sounded restrained beside a guarantee of full parity, yet the underlying parity price could stand far above a market price because the benchmark relationship belonged to an earlier agricultural economy.

### How is a parity price actually calculated?

A parity price is calculated by multiplying a commodity adjusted base price by the parity index. The adjusted base price reflects average prices received over a ten year span, rescaled to the benchmark relationship. The parity index tracks prices paid, interest, taxes, and wage rates against the base period established by statute.

The benchmark behind the calculation was the period from 1910 to 1914. Those years supplied the relationship against which later prices received and prices paid were compared, and the choice of benchmark carried an economic judgment as well as a statistical one. The period from 1910 to 1914 has often been described as a golden age of American farm purchasing power, a span in which the exchange relationship between what farmers sold and what farmers bought was treated as sufficiently favorable to serve as the standard of equity for later policy. Parity therefore did not ask simply whether a bushel or a hundredweight fetched more dollars than in some earlier year. Nominal prices could rise while farm purchasing power fell if the costs embedded in the parity index rose faster. Parity asked whether the price of a commodity, adjusted through the formula, preserved the purchasing relationship embodied in the base period. A parity ratio of 100 expressed that benchmark relationship. A ratio below 100 expressed a shortfall from it, while a commodity price at parity expressed the price that, under the statutory comparison, would restore the base period balance between receipts and outlays.

The adjusted base price prevented the formula from freezing each commodity forever at an unadjusted price drawn only from 1910 to 1914. Under the modernized parity formula, the adjusted base price used the average prices received for the commodity over the most recent ten years, rescaled so that the commodity entered the calculation in relation to the benchmark and to the broader movement of farm prices. The modernized approach was introduced by the Agricultural Act of 1948, with titles II and III effective on January 1, 1950, and parity prices were thereafter computed under section 301(a) of the 1938 act as amended by the Agricultural Acts of 1948, 1949, and 1956. The change was called modernized because a ten year moving average allowed the base for an individual commodity to shift gradually as the pattern of prices received changed across a decade. The formula nevertheless preserved the older benchmark through the parity index, so modernization altered the commodity side of the multiplication without abandoning 1910 to 1914 as the standard for prices paid. The result was a hybrid measure, part moving average and part historical anchor, and that hybrid character explained much of the later controversy over revival.

The parity index formed the second factor in the multiplication and represented the cost side of the farm economy. The index covered prices paid by farmers and also incorporated interest, taxes, and wage rates, so it reached beyond seed, feed, fertilizer, and equipment in concept even where the fact of definition was stated more compactly. Interest mattered because farm production rested on borrowed capital and land obligations. Taxes mattered because land and property carried public charges that did not move automatically with commodity receipts. Wage rates mattered because hired labor formed part of the cost of bringing a crop or a livestock product to market. By placing those items inside the index, the statute defined purchasing power broadly rather than as a narrow comparison between one crop price and one input price. When the index rose, the parity price produced by the formula rose as well, provided the adjusted base price held steady. When prices received for a commodity lagged across the ten year span used for its adjusted base price, that weakness also entered the result. Administrators therefore had to maintain both series with care, because an error or revision on either side changed the support price that a statutory percentage would generate.

Publication gave the formula a public and recurring presence. The National Agricultural Statistics Service of the Department of Agriculture estimated and published parity prices monthly in Agricultural Prices. Monthly publication translated the statutory definition into prices for particular commodities at particular times. The monthly rhythm also exposed the widening distance between parity and market outcomes in the modern era described by the Congressional Research Service. Parity ratios in that era sat far below 100 because farm productivity had grown much faster than the prices farmers paid had fallen relative to prices received. The formula measured a relationship from 1910 to 1914, while the productive system being measured had been transformed.

The Agricultural Act of 1949 used parity through flexible supports rather than through a single permanent percentage for every year. Wartime supports had run at 90 percent of parity, and the Agricultural Act of 1948 had scheduled a move toward flexible supports from 1950. As enacted in 1949, the compromise preserved a high level at the outset and then introduced flexibility in stages. For the basic commodities, support stood at 90 percent of parity through the 1950 crops. For 1951, the statute set a permissive minimum of 80 percent of parity, excepting tobacco from that minimum. Thereafter, flexible supports ran from 75 to 90 percent of parity, keyed to supply, with the Secretary of Agriculture setting the level within the range under statutory supply stipulations. The design linked generosity to abundance. A smaller supply could justify a level nearer the top of the range, while a larger supply pointed toward a lower level inside the same statutory band. Flexibility in that sense did not mean discretion without a standard. The Secretary operated inside a range written by Congress, under stipulations that tied the chosen percentage to supply conditions for the commodity in question.

The staged structure of 1949 also revealed the political settlement embedded in the formula. Support at 90 percent through the 1950 crops continued the high wartime relationship for a defined transition, while the later band from 75 to 90 percent conceded that a rigid guarantee could accumulate surpluses and burden the government with commodities acquired through support operations. The permissive minimum of 80 percent for 1951, with tobacco excepted, formed a bridge between those positions. After the transition, supply became the organizing consideration. The Secretary had to translate estimates of stocks, production, and need into a percentage point inside the band, and each choice carried consequences for loan activity, purchases, storage, and producer income. A percentage near the top of the range announced that the government would defend a higher return. A percentage near the bottom retained a support operation while allowing market prices to perform more of the work of clearing supply.

Permanent law as it would revive did not apply one identical band to every commodity. The Congressional Research Service summarized support levels under permanent law as running between 50 and 90 percent of parity depending on the commodity, and the commodity detail showed why a single revival statute would produce very different interventions across farm markets. Milk and butterfat stood under section 201 of the 1949 act, which required mandatory purchases at 75 to 90 percent of parity at the level that would assure an adequate supply. Dairy support therefore operated through purchases of products rather than through a loan secured by a storable crop held on the farm. The range for milk began comparatively high, at 75 percent, and reached 90 percent. No lower revival band applied to milk under section 201. The mandatory character of the purchases also distinguished dairy from a permissive program that an administration might choose not to operate. Where the statutory conditions for revival were met, section 201 directed support for whole milk, butterfat, and the products of such commodities within the stated range.

Wheat illustrated the connection between price support and production control more completely than any other commodity in the permanent law schedule. Under the Congressional Research Service summary of permanent law as it would revive, wheat would receive nonrecourse loans and purchases at 75 to 90 percent of parity where marketing quotas were not announced. Where quotas were approved, the range would run from 65 to 90 percent of parity. Where quotas were disapproved, support would fall to 50 percent of parity. Wheat also carried acreage allotments. The three postures made the referendum outcome and the administrative posture toward quotas part of the price itself. The highest band applied before quotas were announced, the middle band applied after approval, and the sharply lower level applied after disapproval. That ordering showed that approval did not simply switch support on or off. Approval accepted mandatory restrictions in exchange for a program whose floor, at 65 percent, remained well above the 50 percent level attached to rejection.

Upland cotton followed a related but simpler pattern. Permanent law would provide nonrecourse loans for upland cotton at 65 to 90 percent of parity, with acreage allotments. Cotton therefore shared with wheat the pairing of loans and allotments, but the summarized revival schedule did not divide cotton into the three quota postures stated for wheat. The floor for cotton, at 65 percent, stood above the floors for corn, rice, and disapproved wheat, while the ceiling matched the common ceiling of 90 percent found across much of the schedule. Corn received nonrecourse loans at 50 to 90 percent of parity, the widest band among the principal field crops named in the summary, and permanent law authorized no acreage allotments for corn. The breadth of the corn band gave the Secretary a larger span within which supply conditions and statutory stipulations could operate. The absence of allotment authority also meant that corn support lacked one of the direct acreage controls attached to wheat and cotton, even though corn stood at the center of livestock feeding and therefore influenced markets well beyond the farms that grew it.

Other feed grains entered the schedule through corn rather than through independent parity bands of their own. Sorghum, barley, oats, and rye were to be set by feeding value relative to corn. That method recognized substitution in livestock rations, where feeders compared grains by the nourishment delivered for the price paid. A support relationship based on feeding value sought to keep the supported prices of the several feed grains in a workable relation to one another and to corn, instead of allowing statutory percentages to push one grain far out of line with a close substitute. Rice stood on its own band of 50 to 90 percent of parity. The history of that authority contained an interruption absent from the other major commodities in the schedule. Rice permanent authority was repealed by the 1981 farm bill and restored by the 1996 farm bill, so a revival after the restoration would again include rice within the 50 to 90 percent range. Honey completed the commodity list in the summary, with purchases at 60 to 90 percent of parity. The use of purchases for honey, like the use of purchases for dairy products, reflected a support technique suited to the commodity rather than a universal reliance on loans alone.

The percentages acquired force through machinery that reached the farm as loans, purchases, allotments, quotas, and penalties. A nonrecourse loan allowed a producer at harvest to pledge the crop as collateral for a loan at the loan rate. The crop secured the obligation, and the nonrecourse feature defined the producer choice if the market failed to rise above the loan rate. Where market prices remained below the loan rate, the producer could forfeit the crop to the government and keep the principal. Forfeiture converted a private marketing problem into a public inventory, because the government took the commodity instead of repayment in cash. The loan therefore placed a floor under the harvest decision without requiring an immediate government purchase of every bushel or bale. A permanent repayment provision altered one part of that calculation. Section 1009 of the Food Security Act of 1985 added a provision, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1308a, that allowed producers to repay at less than the loan rate, as under marketing loan practice. Repayment below the loan rate reduced the pressure toward forfeiture in some market conditions while preserving a benefit tied to the difference between the loan rate and the lower repayment level.

Acreage allotments and marketing quotas supplied the control side of the bargain for wheat and cotton. Permanent law required the Department of Agriculture to announce acreage allotments and to hold producer referenda on marketing quotas for those commodities. An allotment addressed the land base from which a supported crop could be produced, while a marketing quota addressed the quantity that could be marketed within the program rules. The referendum requirement gave eligible producers a direct vote on whether quota discipline would apply. Quotas required a two thirds affirmative vote of eligible producers, a supermajority that demanded broad assent before mandatory restrictions attached. Approved quotas brought the highest support framework described for the quota system and brought mandatory restrictions with it. Producers who marketed above quota paid penalties and lost loan eligibility. The penalty and the loss of eligibility worked together, one imposing a charge on excess marketing and the other removing access to the program benefit that made compliance valuable. The structure therefore joined consent, restraint, and support in a single sequence. Producers could reject quotas, but rejection carried the lower support consequence stated in the wheat schedule and the loss of the order that quotas were designed to impose.

The interaction between the formula and the machinery explained why cost estimates for revival reached such large figures even though the statutory percentages appeared to promise only a fraction of parity. Official studies examined the consequences before any modern revival occurred. A study by the Economic Research Service of the Department of Agriculture in 1985, Possible Economic Consequences of Reverting to Permanent Legislation, estimated that the Department of Agriculture would need to remove 13 to 17 percent of milk production to lift prices to mandated levels and that permanent dairy law could cost about 6.5 billion dollars per year in 1990. The dairy figure belonged to a single commodity sector, yet it exceeded the scale of many whole farm programs because the mandated support price would have applied across a large and continuous flow of milk production. The Congressional Budget Office also studied the dairy consequences of permanent law in 1979, an examination that formed part of the official record through which Congress and the executive branch assessed revival long before the suspension debates of later farm bills. The existence of studies from both an executive department research agency and the Congressional Budget Office showed that the fiscal exposure of permanent law had been measured from more than one institutional vantage point.

The reason the numbers grew so large lay in the distance between the benchmark economy and the productive economy to which the formula would return. The Congressional Research Service concluded that, because parity remained anchored to relative prices from 1910 to 1914 while productivity had transformed production, parity prices for the supported commodities sat at multiples of market prices in the modern era. Support at the bottom of the permanent ranges exceeded market prices for every supported commodity, according to the Congressional Research Service. That finding removed the reassurance that a Secretary could soften revival simply by choosing the lowest percentage in each band. For milk, the lowest percentage was 75 percent. For wheat after quota approval, the lowest percentage was 65 percent, and even disapproved wheat stood at 50 percent. Cotton began at 65 percent, corn and rice at 50 percent, and honey at 60 percent. Each floor was a percentage of a parity price that already stood at a multiple of the market price, so a floor expressed as one half or two thirds of parity could command the government to defend a price far above the price at which buyers and sellers were clearing the market. The multiplication came first, and the percentage came second. Reversing that order in public debate understated the intervention by treating 50 percent as if it meant one half of a market price rather than one half of a parity price derived from 1910 to 1914.

Productivity growth supplied the central explanation for that gap. In the base period, a given quantity of food and fiber required far more land, labor, and animal power relative to output than production required after mechanization, improved varieties, fertilizer, pesticides, irrigation, animal breeding, and the other advances that accumulated across the twentieth century. The Congressional Research Service stated the point in direct terms: permanent law did not recognize productivity gains or technological advances, and parity ratios sat far below 100 because farm productivity had grown much faster than prices paid had fallen relative to prices received. When output per unit of input rises dramatically, the market can clear at prices that leave the parity ratio low while calling forth abundant supply. A statute that then requires support at a stated share of parity overrides that market signal across the whole supported sector. The government must buy, lend against, store, donate, or otherwise absorb the consequences of production encouraged by a defended price and restrained only where allotments and quotas operate effectively.

The flexible scale of 1949 was designed for a world in which those consequences could be managed year by year through supply judgments, allotments, quotas, loans, and purchases administered as an operating program. Revival under a later farm economy would instead switch the scale on as a suspended statute, with parity prices computed from a benchmark more than a century old at the time of the December 1, 2020 vantage date and with floors that the Congressional Research Service found to exceed market prices for every supported commodity. The range from 50 to 90 percent therefore described discretion inside an intervention, not a choice between intervention and restraint. That was the working meaning of the formula in permanent law: adjusted base price multiplied by parity index, converted by a commodity percentage into loans and purchases, disciplined for some crops by allotments and referenda, and separated from ordinary market outcomes by the distance between 1910 to 1914 purchasing power and the productive capacity of the agriculture that would receive the support.

The suspension lists gathered provisions from two statutes, a joint resolution, and three excepted sections into a single inventory, and the pattern of treatment across that inventory shows which parts of permanent law sleep, which parts operate, and which programs stand outside the device altogether. Crop provisions are measured by crop years and marketing years, the dairy provision is measured by the calendar year, and the excepted sections of title IV of the 1949 act operate in every period, a set of distinctions that the enacted farm bills of 2014 and 2018 both preserved when each bill carried the suspension forward on the lists inherited from its predecessor.

## The Dairy Problem

Permanent law does not fall on every commodity at the same moment, and it does not fall with the same force on every commodity it reaches. A crop harvested once in a year meets the old statute on a schedule set by a crop year and a marketing year, with time between the expiration of a farm bill and the point at which the suspended provisions would govern a new harvest. Milk meets the old statute almost at once, and it meets a statute written to intervene in the market directly, through purchases, at a level set far above the prices recorded in 2013. The combination explains why discussion of a farm bill lapse so often became discussion of one commodity before it became discussion of any other. The date arrived earlier, the machinery required no loan transaction with an individual producer before the government entered the market, and the product reached nearly every household in a form whose price could be quoted in a single figure per gallon.

The provision at the center of the problem is section 201 of the Agricultural Act of 1949, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1446. Section 201 makes support mandatory for whole milk, butterfat, and the products of such commodities. The mandatory character matters as much as the level. For several crop commodities, permanent law works through nonrecourse loans, instruments that depend on a producer pledging a harvested crop as collateral and that operate within the rhythm of harvest, storage, and forfeiture. The dairy provision commands support itself, and it directs that support toward a perishable product and toward the storable products made from that product. The statute therefore does not wait for a producer to choose a loan or for a crop to move into storage after a single harvest. It places the Department of Agriculture, acting through the Commodity Credit Corporation, in the position of a standing purchaser of manufactured dairy products, ready to take quantities off the commercial market until the farm price rises to the level the statute prescribes.

### Why does milk reach the reversion date before any crop?

Milk reaches the reversion date first because production never pauses for a season and the dairy program runs on the calendar year. When a suspension ends on December 31, permanent law governs milk on January 1, while crop commodities wait for their separate crop and marketing years to turn months later.

The calendar point deserves emphasis because the suspension language itself drew the same distinction. The suspension carried in the farm bill of 2014 listed provisions that were not to apply to crops of covered commodities, cotton, and sugar for the stated crop years, and it stated the treatment of the dairy provision in calendar terms, running from enactment through December 31, 2018. The farm bill of 2018 extended the same structure, carrying the crop coverage through the 2023 crops and the dairy coverage through December 31, 2023, as an enacted schedule. A suspension written in two different kinds of time produces two different kinds of deadline. The crop deadline is diffuse, spread across commodities whose years begin at different points. The dairy deadline is a single date that every producer, processor, retailer, and consumer could circle, and January 1 follows the end of the suspension by a single day.

Production reinforces the calendar. A wheat grower or a corn grower produces a crop that is harvested and then marketed over a period that the statute can measure as a marketing year, and permanent law for those commodities carries its own apparatus of allotments, referenda, quotas, and loans tied to that rhythm. A dairy herd produces on December 31 and produces again on January 1, and the output of the second day cannot be held back in anticipation of a better statute in the way that a stored grain crop can be held in a bin. The flow is continuous because the animals are continuous producers and because the product spoils. The 1949 Congress confronted a commodity that arrived every morning whether the market wanted it or not, and the program built for that commodity inherited the same daily pressure that had shaped its enactment.

The separate classification of dairy in the 1949 act followed from exactly those properties. The Agricultural Act of 1949 treated milk and butterfat apart from the basic commodities, and the reason recorded for the separate treatment is that milk is perishable and must be supplied daily. Perishability changes what support has to mean. A support program for a storable crop can lend against inventory and allow time to do part of the work, because the crop exists as a stock that can be pledged, held, and released. A support program for a fluid product that deteriorates cannot lend against the fluid product in the same way and cannot allow a season to pass before acting, because the product of one week has ceased to exist in its original form long before the next season arrives. The statute answered the problem by supporting the fluid product through its manufactured forms, which is the design choice from which the purchase program, the government stock position, and the peculiar politics of the dairy provisions all descend.

The level of support that section 201 prescribes converts the timing problem into a price problem. The provision requires support at the level between 75 and 90 percent of parity that will assure an adequate supply. Parity, as defined in section 301(a) of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, measures the relationship between the prices farmers receive and the prices farmers pay against the base period from 1910 to 1914, a period often described as a golden age of American farm purchasing power. Because farm productivity grew across the intervening century far faster than the relationship embedded in the parity index adjusted, parity prices for the supported commodities stood at multiples of market prices in the modern era, and the Congressional Research Service recorded that support even at the bottom of the permanent ranges exceeded market prices for every supported commodity. A range that begins at 75 percent of such an index does not begin near the market. It begins well above the market, and every point within the range sits above the prices at which the commercial trade had cleared in 2013.

The phrase in the statute that ties the choice of level to an adequate supply also deserves close reading. The Secretary of Agriculture does not receive discretion to select a level that the market can absorb without disruption, nor discretion to decline support in a year when support would be expensive. The discretion runs only within the band, from 75 to 90 percent of parity, and the criterion for placing the level within the band is the assurance of an adequate supply. In a market already producing abundantly at prices near 20 dollars per hundredweight, a support level near twice that figure could not be defended as the minimum needed to call forth supply, yet the statutory structure left no route to a lower figure outside the band.

The standing machinery through which section 201 operates is the Dairy Price Support Program. Under that program, the Department of Agriculture, through the Commodity Credit Corporation, stands ready to purchase cheddar cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk at announced purchase prices. The choice of products is the practical heart of the design. Fluid output cannot be bought and held by the government in its original form, so the government buys the manufactured products into which surplus fluid output can be converted. Cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk can be stored, transported, and held in government inventory, and purchases of those products draw raw supply away from commercial channels. A processor deciding where the output of a day should go can direct it toward the products the Corporation buys, secure in the knowledge that a purchaser exists at the announced price, and that decision transmits the government's price back toward the farm.

The announced purchase prices are not arbitrary figures posted for their own sake. The design, as summarized by the Congressional Research Service, is that the purchase prices are calculated to drive the farm price of milk up to the support level. The calculation works backward from the statutory target. The Corporation sets prices for the manufactured products at levels intended to make those products worth enough, in the hands of processors, that processors can pay farmers the supported price for the raw product and cover the conversion. If the purchase prices are set correctly against the support target, the commercial price has nowhere to settle below the target, because any quantity that the commercial market would take only at a lower price can instead move into the manufactured products and out through the government's standing offer. The support level is therefore enforced not by a payment that compensates a farmer for a low market price, but by a bid that removes the low market price as an available outcome.

That mechanism makes the government the buyer of last resort in form and something more powerful in effect. A buyer of last resort in ordinary usage takes what others refuse, at the margin, after the commercial market has cleared what it can. A standing purchase offer calibrated to raise the entire farm price cannot remain at the margin, because the offer reprices every transaction that precedes it. A commercial buyer who wants cheese, butter, or powder must match or exceed the value that the same raw supply commands through the government channel, or lose the supply to that channel. The Corporation thus becomes the buyer that outbids the commercial market, not by attending an auction and raising a hand, but by publishing prices that every other buyer must treat as the floor of negotiation. The government bid does not merely support the price from below. It sets the price, and commercial buyers compete above a level that a public corporation has announced for reasons of statute rather than reasons of demand.

The Congressional Research Service, describing permanent law in connection with the farm bill of 2014, stated the translation plainly. Purchases under permanent law would raise the farm price to the equivalent of 75 to 90 percent of the parity index anchored in the period from 1910 to 1914, resulting in support prices near 40 dollars per hundredweight. Against that figure the Service placed the average all milk farm price of about 20 dollars per hundredweight during 2013. The comparison requires no adjustment and no model to interpret. The statutory floor, at the bottom of its range or near it, stood at roughly double the price the market had actually paid during 2013. A support program ordinarily narrows a gap between a target and a market that have drifted apart by degrees. The permanent dairy law presented a gap of one hundred percent, to be closed by government purchases, beginning on a date fixed by the calendar rather than by any movement in the market itself.

A second official estimate, prepared on a slightly different basis, reached a figure in the same region and attached a budget cost to it. A White House report of November 2013 estimated permanent law for dairy at a support price near 37 dollars per hundredweight, calculated on 2013 prices and production, and put the cost at between 10 billion and 12.5 billion dollars per year, with milk prices doubling. The estimate's components each perform distinct work. The support price near 37 dollars shows that the conclusion did not depend on the highest plausible reading of the parity calculation, since a figure several dollars below the Congressional Research Service's near 40 dollars stood close to double the market. The production basis shows that the cost was not a conjecture about a distant market structure, but a calculation run on the quantities the industry was actually producing in 2013. The annual cost range, running into eight figures of billions, shows why the provision could not be treated as a symbolic survival in the statute books. A dormant provision whose activation carries a federal cost between 10 billion and 12.5 billion dollars in a single year is a fiscal event in its own right, apart from any effect on retail prices.

The doubling of prices in the White House estimate also supplied the consumer translation of the parity arithmetic. A farm price is quoted per hundredweight, a unit that few shoppers use, and a parity percentage is an abstraction anchored in prices paid and received a century earlier. A doubling requires no conversion. The report's conclusion that prices would double told a household exactly what the statute's percentages meant at the point of purchase.

Contemporaneous reporting during the earlier lapse episode put the figures in a form that reached an even wider audience. In December 2012, reporting on the prospective operation of permanent law placed the support level near 38 dollars per hundredweight against a market near 18 dollars, and estimated that retail milk could reach about 7 dollars per gallon. The three figures together form the complete anatomy of the scare. The support level near 38 dollars stated what the statute would pay, in effect, at the farm gate. The market near 18 dollars stated what the trade was paying without the statute, so that the distance between the two figures measured the size of the intervention rather than the size of a routine adjustment. The retail estimate near 7 dollars per gallon carried the intervention out of the trade press and into the grocery aisle, where the consequence of a farm bill's expiration could be pictured as a single familiar container at an unfamiliar price. No crop commodity in the permanent law structure offered an equivalent translation, because no crop commodity combined a support level at twice the market with a retail product whose price every household already knew.

It was during the December 2012 episode that the phenomenon acquired the name by which it has been known in political discussion since. The Congressional Research Service recorded that the phrase dairy cliff entered political vocabulary in December 2012, coined alongside the simultaneous budgetary fiscal cliff. The coinage was exact in a way that political phrases often are not. A cliff describes a discontinuity, a point at which conditions change not by slope but by drop or, in this case, by sudden ascent, and the dairy provisions supplied a genuine discontinuity of that kind. On December 31 the suspension governed and the support level had no operation. On January 1 the suspension had ended for the dairy provision and the statute commanded support at a level near double the market. Nothing in the market moved between those two dates. Only the law moved, and the phrase captured the experience of standing at a date on the calendar beyond which prices would be set by a statute enacted in 1949 for an industry organized around daily delivery to local markets.

The phrase also performed a sorting function within the larger farm bill debate. The expiration of a farm bill affects many programs in many different ways, and the effects do not arrive together. Some programs cease new operations when authority expires, others continue under appropriations acts, crop commodity programs continue to cover a crop year already in progress, and programs without a budget baseline lose operations entirely, as the extension enacted in January 2013 demonstrated by leaving such programs without new mandatory funding. To describe all of that as a single expiration is accurate and unintelligible at once. The dairy cliff isolated the one consequence that was dated, priced, and visible, and gave the press and the public a handle for the whole tangle.

Against the dairy provisions of permanent law stands a neighboring body of dairy regulation that a lapse does not touch at all, and the contrast clarifies what kind of authority section 201 is. Federal milk marketing orders rest on the Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act of 1937, a permanent authority that sits outside the farm bill structure. The Congressional Research Service noted the distinction directly: the marketing orders continue regardless of any lapse. A marketing order is a standing regulatory instrument, not a program suspended and revived by successive farm bills, and its authority does not pass through the suspension lists codified for the 1938 and 1949 acts at all. When the suspension of section 201 lapsed in law on January 1, 2014, the marketing order system did not lapse, was not revived, and required no rescue, because nothing about its legal foundation had been scheduled to expire on December 31, 2013. The two systems govern the same commodity through entirely different legal postures, one continuous and regulatory, the other discontinuous and based on purchases, and only one of them generates a cliff.

The distinction matters for understanding what a revival would and would not change. The marketing orders would go on operating within a market whose price floor had been seized by the purchase program, and the order system would neither restrain the Corporation's announced purchase prices nor substitute for them. Modern farm bill dairy policy illustrates the opposite pole from section 201 in a different way. The Dairy Margin Coverage program under the farm bill of 2018 does not set a support price at all, addressing dairy income through the margin between prices and costs rather than through a government bid for manufactured products. Between the continuous regulation of the marketing orders and the margin approach of the modern farm bill, section 201 occupies a category of its own: a mandatory, purchase based, parity linked support that sleeps under suspension and wakes on a calendar date. The modern instruments were each designed, in their different eras, to avoid governing the market by standing in it as the dominant buyer. The permanent provision governs by exactly that method, which is why its revival could not be absorbed as a routine continuation of dairy policy under another name.

The dairy problem, stated whole, is therefore a problem of alignment among four facts that the statute itself supplies. The first fact is classification: the Agricultural Act of 1949 set milk and butterfat apart because the product is perishable and must be supplied daily, and it made support for them mandatory. The second fact is level: the mandatory support runs from 75 to 90 percent of a parity index anchored in the period from 1910 to 1914, which placed the support price near 37 to 40 dollars per hundredweight in the official estimates of 2013, against market prices near 18 to 20 dollars. The third fact is machinery: the support is delivered by a standing government offer to buy cheddar cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk at announced prices calculated to pull the farm price up to the statutory level, an offer that turns the buyer of last resort into the buyer whose bid every commercial purchaser must beat. The fourth fact is time: the program runs on the calendar year, production never pauses, and the reversion date of January 1 arrives months before the crop and marketing years of the other supported commodities turn. Together they make the dairy provisions the part that revives first, costs the most visibly, and reaches the consumer fastest, which is why the fate of a statute enacted on October 31, 1949 has so often been argued, in December debates and January deadlines, as a question about the price of a gallon at the store.

## The Near Misses

The history of permanent law in the modern farm bill era is in large part a history of dates that approached, passed, or nearly passed without the suspended provisions returning to operation. The episodes differed in length, in the commodities they placed at risk, and in the legislative response they produced, yet they followed a recurring structure. A farm bill or an extension reached its stated end. The suspension of the Agricultural Act of 1949 and of the corresponding provisions of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 reached its stated end with it. For an interval, measured in one case in weeks and in others in months, the governing framework depended on appropriations, on the crop year already covered by expired authority, on administrative preparation, and on the expectation that Congress would enact either another extension or a successor farm bill before the Department of Agriculture put permanent law into operation.

That structure explains why an expiration and a revival were never the same event. Expiration ended the temporary authority and the temporary suspension together. Revival required more than the calendar. For crops, permanent law attached to crop and marketing years that began months after a fiscal year expiration in the autumn, and a bill that had expired for fiscal purposes could have covered the crop already harvested or already planted. For milk, the calendar ran differently, because the dairy program operated on the calendar year and milk moved from farm to market each day. The dairy trigger therefore fell on January 1 following a lapse, and dairy became the commodity around which each modern near miss turned, even when the underlying expiration had covered the entire farm bill.

The extension habit came first in the sequence, and it set the pattern for the later episodes. Congress had repeatedly chosen short continuations of an expired farm bill over an interval in which permanent law governed in fact. An extension preserved the provisions of the expiring act, including its suspension of permanent law, for a further defined period. It did not settle the policy questions that a new farm bill had to answer, and it did not amend the permanent statutes waiting underneath. It moved the date. The record from the end of 2007 into the spring of 2008 showed that device used repeatedly, in quick succession, until a successor act was ready for enactment.

The 2002 farm bill expired at the end of 2007. Portions of that bill were then extended six times in the spring of 2008, for less than a year in total across the sequence. The first extension appeared in Public Law 110-161, at Division A, section 751. Five further measures followed, enacted as Public Law 110-196, Public Law 110-200, Public Law 110-205, Public Law 110-208, and Public Law 110-231. The extensions mostly continued the 2002 provisions, including the provisions for dairy and for sugar. The sequence ended when the 2008 act was enacted over vetoes, replacing the series of short continuations with a new farm bill and a new suspension.

### What changed on the first day after the farm bill expired in the autumn lapse?

On October 1, 2012, some farm bill programs ceased new operations, while others continued under appropriations acts. The commodity programs of the expired act covered the 2012 crop year, so the crop trigger had not arrived. For dairy, the governing trigger date was January 1, 2013.

The 2008 farm bill, enacted as Public Law 110-246, expired on September 30, 2012. The expiration was complete as a matter of the farm bill calendar, and October 1, 2012 opened a period in which no new farm bill and no extension was in place. The effect, however, was divided rather than uniform. Some farm bill programs ceased new operations after October 1, 2012. Others continued under appropriations acts, which supplied a separate legal basis for operations even though the authorizing farm bill had expired. The lapse therefore did not produce a single day on which every program ended, and it did not produce a single day on which permanent law replaced every program either.

The divided effect followed from the different legal foundations on which farm programs rested. Appropriations could carry a program forward for a period without resolving the expiration of its farm bill authorization. A program with expiring mandatory authority and no other funding route stood in a different position from a program that an appropriations act continued. That distinction returned in the design of the extension enacted at the start of 2013, and it returned again in 2018, when appropriations carried many programs forward in the absence of any extension at all. In the autumn of 2012, the distinction meant that the expiration date was decisive for some operations and largely administrative for others, with the commodity programs presenting the most consequential case because of the permanent law waiting behind them.

For the commodity programs, the crop year supplied a cushion that the calendar alone did not reveal. The commodity programs of the 2008 act covered the 2012 crop year, so the permanent law trigger for crops had not arrived when the act expired on September 30, 2012. A crop harvested under the authority of the expired bill remained within that bill's coverage through its marketing year, and permanent law for wheat, corn, cotton, and the other supported commodities attached to later crop years rather than to the crop already covered. The interval between September 30, 2012 and the start of a new crop year gave Congress time in which an extension or a new bill could restore the suspension before acreage allotments, marketing quotas, nonrecourse loans, and purchases under permanent law had to be organized for a new crop.

Dairy had no comparable cushion. Milk was produced daily, the dairy program ran on the calendar year, and the suspension of section 201 of the 1949 act, the dairy provision codified at 7 U.S.C. 1446, ran on calendar dates for milk. The dairy trigger following the September 30, 2012 expiration was January 1, 2013. Between the expiration and that trigger stood a single quarter of the calendar, October through December 2012, and the legislative question in that quarter was whether an extension or a new farm bill would be enacted before the first day of the new calendar year. The commodity at immediate risk was therefore the one commodity whose support mechanism under permanent law was mandatory purchase of manufactured dairy products.

Under section 201, support for milk and butterfat was mandatory at a level between 75 and 90 percent of parity, set at the level that would assure an adequate supply. The standing mechanism was purchase by the Department of Agriculture, through the Commodity Credit Corporation, of cheddar cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk at announced purchase prices calculated to drive the farm price of milk up to the support level. A revival would not have been a continuation of the dairy policy then in place under the farm bill. It would have substituted a purchase program anchored to parity for the policies of the suspended period, and the parity anchor pointed to support prices at multiples of market prices for supported commodities in the modern era.

In September 2012, before the 2008 act expired, the Department of Agriculture published its implementation outline under the title "The Effects of Failure to Enact a New Farm Bill: Permanent Law Support for Commodities and Authorization Lapse of Other USDA Programs." The memorandum set out how permanent law would be implemented if Congress did not enact a new farm bill. Its timing placed administrative preparation ahead of the expiration itself, and its contents separated two questions that public discussion often combined. One question concerned the support operations that permanent law would require for commodities. The other concerned the lapse of authorization for other department programs, a lapse whose consequences depended on the funding and authority available to each program apart from the farm bill.

The memorandum also showed how a revival would proceed in practice. The Congressional Research Service recorded the general point that, if a suspension expired, the Department of Agriculture was required to begin implementing permanent law, but the department could need time to write and publish new regulations, and market effects could be gradual, taking effect over weeks or months. The September 2012 outline was the first statement of that implementation approach in the modern episodes. A revival, on that account, began as rulemaking and administrative preparation before it appeared as purchases in a market. The obligation attached on the trigger date, while the machinery to discharge the obligation had to be assembled under the procedures that governed federal rulemaking.

That sequence had consequences for how the December 2012 debate unfolded. The legal date was fixed and near, and the administrative path from that date to changed prices ran through implementation steps that took time. Secretary of Agriculture Tom Vilsack warned publicly through December 2012 that milk prices could roughly double under permanent law. Contemporaneous reporting in December 2012 placed the prospective support level near 38 dollars per hundredweight against a market price near 18 dollars, and estimated that retail milk could reach about 7 dollars per gallon. The phrase dairy cliff entered political vocabulary in December 2012, coined alongside the simultaneous budgetary phrase fiscal cliff, and the pairing fixed the farm bill expiration to a wider end of year legislative deadline in public attention.

The warnings described a prospective outcome under a statute that had not operated for dairy in the suspended period, and the figures attached to the warnings were estimates of support levels and prices rather than prices that any buyer or seller had paid. No permanent law purchases were made during the 2012 lapse. The Department of Agriculture made no purchases of cheese, butter, or nonfat dry milk under section 201 between October 1, 2012 and the enactment of the extension, and no acreage allotments or marketing quotas under permanent law were put into operation for a new crop. The episode closed by legislation, as the 2008 episode had closed, before implementation under permanent law began.

Congress passed the American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012 on January 1, 2013, and the President signed the act on January 2, 2013, as Public Law 112-240. Title VII of that act supplied the farm bill extension. It extended all provisions in effect on September 30, 2012 for fiscal 2013 and for the 2013 crop year. The extension therefore reached back to the state of the farm bill on the day before its expiration and carried that state forward through a defined fiscal year and a defined crop year. For dairy, enactment on January 2 followed the January 1 trigger date by one day on the calendar of passage and signature recorded for the act, and the extension displaced the prospect of permanent law purchases before any such purchases had begun.

The design of the extension contained a limitation that determined which programs it actually continued. The extension was designed to be budget neutral, and it provided no new mandatory funding for programs that lacked a budget baseline. Programs without a budget baseline did not continue under the extension, for that reason. The distinction between a provision extended in form and a program funded in operation ran through the whole episode. Title VII extended the provisions in effect on September 30, 2012, and at the same time the smaller programs without a baseline lost the funding basis for operations, a pattern that identified the programs most exposed in any lapse as the mandatory programs with expiring authority and no other funding route.

The one year extension enacted in January 2013 expired on September 30, 2013. Congress did not renew it, because a conference agreement on a new farm bill was near. The expiration therefore differed from the expiration of the year before in one governing respect. In 2012, expiration had opened an uncertain interval whose end depended on legislation that had not been completed. In the autumn of 2013, expiration opened an interval during which the two chambers were completing a conference agreement, and no extension was enacted to cover the weeks between the end of the extension and the expected completion of the new bill. The suspension of permanent law lapsed with the extension, subject to the same crop year and calendar year triggers that had governed in 2012.

For crops, the cushioning operated again. The extension had covered fiscal 2013 and the 2013 crop year, and permanent law for the supported crops attached to a later crop year. For dairy, the trigger was again January 1, falling on January 1, 2014. The calendar gave the conference process a quarter of the year, from October 1, 2013 to the end of December, before the dairy provision of permanent law became the governing statute for milk. As December advanced without enactment of the conference agreement, the question narrowed to whether a short extension would be enacted to cover January, or whether the dairy suspension would be allowed to lapse in law while implementation remained incomplete.

On December 12, 2013, the House passed a further short extension, which would have extended the provisions to January 31. The Senate did not take up the House measure. The measure therefore never became law, and no extension covered the turn of the year. The record of that stopgap attempt is part of the precise chronology of the episode, because it distinguishes the lapse that followed from a lapse in which no extension was proposed. A chamber passed a dated extension, the other chamber did not act on it, and the existing extension remained expired as the dairy trigger approached.

On January 1, 2014, the suspension of the dairy provision lapsed in law, and permanent law again became the governing statute for milk. Section 201 of the 1949 act, with its mandatory support at 75 to 90 percent of parity and its purchase mechanism through the Commodity Credit Corporation, stood as the operative dairy statute on paper. No new farm bill had displaced it, and no extension had continued the suspension past December 31, 2013. Of the modern episodes, this was the only one in which the dairy trigger date passed without a successor statute or an extension in place, and the Congressional Research Service described the result as the closest the device had come to operating in the modern era.

The department did not begin purchases on January 1, 2014. The Congressional Research Service recorded that permanent law for dairy was delayed, citing the need for rulemaking to implement it. The delay followed the implementation sequence that the department had outlined in September 2012 and that the general implementation point described. Before purchases at announced prices could begin, regulations had to be written and published to carry a support program that had been suspended for decades into operation, covering the products to be purchased, the prices to be announced, and the procedures by which the Commodity Credit Corporation would stand ready to buy. The governing statute had changed on January 1, and the administrative apparatus for the restored statute did not exist in completed form on that date.

Secretary Vilsack stated publicly in December 2013 and in January 2014 that an immediate price increase in January was unlikely, because implementation steps had to come first. The statements aligned the public expectation for January prices with the rulemaking position recorded by the Congressional Research Service. Milk moved through the market in January 2014 without a permanent law purchase price in operation, and no support level near the parity based figures discussed in December 2012 was announced for January purchases. The lapse in law and the absence of an operating program coexisted for the entire interval between the trigger date and the enactment of the new farm bill.

The Agricultural Act of 2014 was signed on February 7, 2014, as Public Law 113-79. Section 1602 of that act suspended permanent law again, in the form that listed the suspended provisions of the 1938 and 1949 acts and made them inapplicable to the 2014 through 2018 crops of the covered commodities and to milk through December 31, 2018. Enactment ended the interval in which section 201 had governed milk in law. From February 7, 2014, dairy policy rested again on the farm bill, and the purchase authority of permanent law returned to its suspended and inactive position in the statute.

The verdict on the episode can be stated with precision, and the precision is the point of the chronology. The revival existed in law for five weeks, from January 1, 2014 to the enactment of the new farm bill on February 7, 2014, without existing in the market. No permanent law purchases were ever put into operation in the episode. No acreage allotments were put into operation, and no marketing quotas were put into operation. No producer referendum on quotas was held under permanent law, no penalty for marketing above a quota was imposed under permanent law, and no nonrecourse loan at a parity based rate was made under the restored provisions. The statute governed, the market continued under the arrangements already in place, and the successor farm bill arrived before the two met.

Four years later, the expiration pattern recurred without either an extension or a lapse of the dairy suspension in law. The fiscal year provisions of the 2014 farm bill began expiring on October 1, 2018. Congress passed no extension. The absence of an extension distinguished 2018 from both 2008, when six short extensions had bridged the interval, and 2013, when an extension had been enacted after expiration and had then been allowed to expire in turn. In 2018, the bridge consisted of appropriations rather than an extension of the farm bill itself.

Regular appropriations acts allowed many programs to continue operations without addressing the expiration. The arrangement repeated, in a different legislative form, the divided effect recorded on October 1, 2012, when some programs had ceased new operations and others had continued under appropriations. Programs that appropriations carried forward operated through the autumn of 2018 under that authority, while the expired farm bill provisions awaited a successor act. The approach left the permanent law question to be resolved by the timing of the new farm bill against the January 1 dairy trigger, rather than by an extension that moved the trigger itself.

The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 was enacted on December 20, 2018, as Public Law 115-334. Section 1702 of that act extended the suspension by striking 2018 and inserting 2023 in the suspension provision, carrying the listed suspensions through the 2023 crops and, for milk, through December 31, 2023, as an enacted schedule. Enactment fell eleven days before January 1, 2019, the date on which permanent law would have taken effect for dairy. The margin was narrower than the margin in January 2013, when an extension was signed one day after the trigger date on the recorded calendar, and wider in practical effect, because enactment preceded the trigger and the dairy suspension never lapsed in law in the 2018 episode.

Across the three principal episodes, the outcomes formed a graded series. In 2012, expiration was followed by an extension that continued the provisions in effect at expiration, funded the programs that held a baseline, and left the programs without a baseline unfunded, with no permanent law transaction at any point. In 2013 and 2014, expiration was followed by no extension, a House passed stopgap that the Senate did not take up, a five week revival of the dairy provision in law, a rulemaking delay, and a new farm bill that restored the suspension before implementation began. In 2018, expiration was followed by no extension, continued operations under appropriations for many programs, and a new farm bill enacted eleven days before the dairy trigger. No episode produced a permanent law purchase.

The record left by the near misses is therefore a record of a device that repeatedly approached operation without operating. Permanent law was prepared for in a department memorandum in September 2012, warned about in public statements through December 2012, restored for milk in law on January 1, 2014, delayed in implementation for want of rulemaking, and displaced by new farm bills in February 2014 and December 2018. The purchases, allotments, and quotas that permanent law prescribed remained unexecuted in every episode, and the support prices at 75 to 90 percent of parity for milk, wheat, cotton, and the other supported commodities remained figures in estimates and warnings rather than prices announced for a transaction.

## The Selective Effect of a Lapse

A lapse in farm bill authority has a reputation for totality that the statute does not support. Expiration ends a set of temporary arrangements and exposes the older permanent law underneath, but exposure is not a general shutdown of federal agricultural activity. Whole programs rest on authorities that the farm bill never supplied, and those programs continue on their own foundations while the suspended provisions of the Agricultural Act of 1949 and the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 return to view. Other programs depend entirely on the expiring bill, and those programs face a very different fate. The selective character of the effect reflects decades of choices about placement on permanent authority, periodic reauthorization, and baseline funding.

### What actually stops when a farm bill expires?

Very little of the largest machinery stops at all. Crop insurance, nutrition assistance, and the permanently authorized disaster programs continue under their own statutes and appropriations, while commodity programs for crops already harvested run to their marketing year ends. What stops is narrower: smaller mandatory programs without another funding route or a budget baseline.

Crop insurance supplies the clearest example of continuity. The program is permanently authorized and funded under the Federal Crop Insurance Act, codified at title 7, section 1501 and following of the United States Code. That authorization does not expire with the farm bill, and a lapse does not affect it. Public discussion often treats the farm bill as a single container holding every agricultural program at once, and the container image misleads. As [the permanent structure of federal crop insurance](https://insightcrunch.com/2021/01/01/federal-crop-insurance-law/) shows, Congress placed crop insurance on a footing designed to survive the periodic expiration that defines the farm bill cycle. The lapse that revives parity based support for a limited list of commodities leaves the insurance program untouched.

Nutrition assistance rests on a comparable but distinct foundation. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program rests on the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008, a permanent authorization. The program involves mandatory spending authorized but not appropriated by the farm bill, so an appropriations act or a continuing resolution allows operations to continue through a farm bill lapse. The Agricultural Act of 1949 has no direct effect on food assistance, and food assistance does not rest on the 1949 act. Disaster assistance adds a third layer of continuity. The four livestock and tree disaster programs identified by the Congressional Research Service by their initials, LIP, LFP, ELAP, and TAP, are permanently authorized under title 7, section 9081. The Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program has permanent authority as well, under section 196 of the 1996 act, codified at title 7, section 7333. Federal milk marketing orders supply a further example, since those orders rest on the Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act of 1937, a permanent authority outside the farm bill, and continue regardless of any lapse. Every use of permanent authority narrows the practical reach of a farm bill expiration.

The narrowing continues on the commodity side, because revival would not restore a modern commodity program in full. Permanent law would support dairy, wheat, rice, cotton, corn, and other feed grains, through parity, nonrecourse loans, purchases, acreage allotments, and marketing quotas. The modern farm bill covers a far broader field. Permanent law would not support soybeans and other oilseeds at all. Permanent law would not support peanuts, wool, or mohair, commodities for which parity supports once existed before repeal, with peanut support ending through the buyout enacted in the farm bill of 2002. Permanent law would not support sugar beets and sugarcane, and permanent law would not support dry peas, lentils, and chickpeas. One group of producers would face a revived support system at levels that the Congressional Research Service describes as exceeding modern market prices even at the bottom of the permanent ranges. Another group, producing commodities that modern farm bills cover as a matter of course, would face the absence of any permanent law support at all.

That division corrects the claim that everything stops on expiry. Programs on permanent authority do not stop, and commodity programs for crops already harvested under the expiring bill continue to the ends of their marketing years. At the same time, programs without permanent authority and without a funding bridge face a genuine end. [The durable myths that gather around farm bill expiration](https://insightcrunch.com/2021/03/15/farm-bill-myths/) survive because a single expiration date is easier to describe than a staggered set of consequences spread across titles, statutes, crop years, and appropriations measures. Each consequence follows from the location of the relevant authority, and the location differs by program.

What genuinely stops deserves precise statement because the affected programs lack the scale that draws headlines. The programs at risk are mandatory farm bill programs with expiring authority and no other funding route, above all the many smaller programs without a budget baseline. A baseline matters because an extension designed to remain budget neutral can carry forward programs that the budget process already counts, while a program without a baseline receives no new mandatory funding under such an extension and loses operations entirely. The episode of 2012 proved the point. The American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012, passed on January 1, 2013 and signed on January 2, 2013, extended the provisions in effect on September 30, 2012 for fiscal year 2013 and the 2013 crop year, and programs without a budget baseline did not continue under the extension because no new mandatory funding was provided for them. Timing adds a further selectivity. Dairy reverts first because milk is produced daily and the dairy program runs on the calendar year, with a trigger date of January 1 following a lapse. Crop commodities revert on their crop and marketing years, months later. The Congressional Research Service also records that permanent law is generally considered inconsistent with modern farming practices, marketing systems, and obligations of the United States in the World Trade Organization, and that the law does not recognize productivity gains or technological advances.

## The Politics of Repeal

The selectivity of a lapse raises an obvious political question. If revival produces consequences that legislators describe as unacceptable, repeal offers the direct remedy. Congress could strike the 1938 and 1949 provisions, remove the parity based support structure, and free each future farm bill debate from the pressure of a revived statute waiting behind a suspension. Proposals to repeal permanent law have nevertheless been rare, in the assessment of the Congressional Research Service, and only some bills containing repeal passed one chamber. The history of those proposals, concentrated in the middle of the 1990s with later episodes in 2012 and 2013, shows advance and retreat. Repeal attracted support when broader reform promised to move farm policy away from the old support model, and repeal lost support when conference negotiations forced a choice between a clean break and a familiar suspension. Legislators considered repeal, wrote repeal into bills that passed a chamber, carried repeal into conference, and then chose suspension instead.

The furthest advance came in the sequence running from 1995 to 1996, when budget politics and farm bill politics converged. The Senate passed a version of the budget reconciliation package of 1995 that included repeal, in S. 1357, section 1101. The conference agreement adopted the Senate approach, in H.R. 2491, section 1109, passed both chambers, and was vetoed for reasons outside the farm provisions. Repeal in that vehicle reached the President and failed only as part of a larger measure rejected on other grounds. A parallel track ran through the House Agriculture Committee. The initial House Agriculture Committee bill in 1995, H.R. 2195, title IV, would have continued suspension, and the text of that bill was folded into the House passed reconciliation package as H.R. 2491, section 1105.

The farm bill of 1996 supplied the second and more durable test. Repeal held a saliency in that debate because the 1996 act, the measure known as Freedom to Farm, was expected to wind supports down after 2002, and a statute built to wind support down sat uneasily beside a permanent law built to restore support at parity based levels. The House passed a farm bill, H.R. 2854, section 109, that would have repealed permanent law. The Senate reported a bill, S. 1541, section 19, that also carried repeal. The Senate passed version suspended instead, in S. 1541, section 109, and the conference agreement followed the Senate, in H.R. 2854, section 171, choosing suspension. Repeal commanded enough support to enter a House passed bill and a Senate reported bill, and repeal then gave way where a final text required agreement between the chambers. Readers tracing that retreat benefit from [the full account of the Freedom to Farm settlement of 1996](https://insightcrunch.com/2020/12/15/freedom-to-farm-act-1996-guide/), because the suspension decision belonged to a larger bargain about the direction of farm support after 2002.

Outside the two vehicles of 1995 and 1996, repeal mostly lived and died at introduction. The Congressional Research Service lists standalone bills introduced from 1995 to 2001, none of which advanced beyond introduction and referral to committee. H.R. 1354 and S. 586 proposed abolishing the Department of Agriculture and eliminating price support authorities. H.R. 1923 placed repeal inside a government wide restructuring. H.R. 2010 proposed phasing down supports and then repealing the authorities. H.R. 2523 and H.R. 2787 followed in 1995. H.R. 502 and S. 2573 followed in the period from 1997 to 1998. H.R. 328 followed in 1999, and S. 1571 followed in 2001. Across seven years, members returned to repeal in different forms, and in every case the proposal stopped at referral. The gap between introduction and advancement is the political space in which permanent law survives.

The pattern repeated in compressed form in 2012. In the 112th Congress, Senate amendment 2379 to S. 3420 was submitted to replace suspension with repeal, and the amendment was never actually offered on the floor. The text entered the debate, the intention was recorded, and the confrontation that an offer would have forced never occurred, in a year when the dairy trigger dominated discussion and the Department of Agriculture published an implementation outline for permanent law. The next Congress produced the most structurally ambitious proposal of the modern era. In 2013 the House passed a farm bill, H.R. 2642, section 1602, that would have repealed the permanent laws of 1938 and 1949 and made the commodity program in the House bill itself the new permanent law, applying to the crop year of 2014 and to each succeeding crop year. Repeal under H.R. 2642 did not contemplate a world without permanent law. The proposal replaced one permanent law with another, substituting a modern commodity title for a parity based structure anchored to relative prices from 1910 to 1914. The Senate bill, S. 954, continued suspension, as did the first House bill, H.R. 1947, and the enacted farm bills of 2014 and 2018 continued suspension as well. A third option, replacement, would have preserved the discipline of a permanent default while changing the content of the default, and conference chose the familiar option instead.

Two characterizations of the surviving device run through the debate, and each belongs to identifiable holders. Critics of the arrangement, including members who proposed repeal in the bills and amendments traced in the preceding paragraphs, characterized the device as a manufactured crisis, a deadline engineered to produce panic over milk prices and commodity markets at the moment a farm bill expires. Defenders of the arrangement, including members who repeatedly chose suspension over repeal in conference and on the floor, characterized the device as necessary discipline, a guarantee that an expired farm bill returns to the congressional agenda instead of drifting into neglect. The Congressional Research Service records a version of the defender position in measured terms, noting that some observers see the existence of permanent law, and the policy and budget consequences of restoring permanent law, as assurance that Congress will revisit the farm commodity programs when a farm bill expires. The assurance argument turns severity into the point of the device.

## A Revival That Would Arrive in Stages

Against both characterizations, the operational record supports a cooler reading, beginning with a concession. Revival would be real. A lapse makes the 1938 and 1949 provisions the governing statute, the Department of Agriculture comes under a duty to implement permanent law, and support levels in that law exceed modern market prices for every supported commodity even at the bottom of the statutory ranges. Nothing in the record converts revival into a bluff. The timing of revival nevertheless deserves the same precision as the coverage of revival, because a statute becoming law again and a statute operating in markets are two events separated by administrative work. The dairy cliff phrase, which entered political vocabulary in December 2012 alongside the simultaneous budgetary fiscal cliff, captures the legal edge with accuracy and the administrative slope beyond the edge with very little accuracy. The record of 2012, and the conduct of the Department of Agriculture from 2013 to 2014, describes a slope, steep in eventual effect and gradual in arrival.

Implementation requires regulations written and published. The Congressional Research Service states the point directly. If the suspension expires, the Department of Agriculture is required to begin implementing permanent law, but the department may need time to write and publish new regulations, and market effects may be gradual, taking effect over weeks or months. Permanent law in a suspended state lacks the operating apparatus of a working program. Purchase prices must be calculated and announced for cheddar cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk under the Dairy Price Support Program. Support levels must be set. Acreage allotments must be announced for wheat and cotton, and referenda on marketing quotas must be held, with quotas requiring a two thirds affirmative vote of eligible producers. Each step takes time that the trigger date does not compress.

The Department of Agriculture supplied evidence for the lag in advance. In September 2012, with the farm bill of 2008 set to expire on September 30 of that year, the department published an implementation outline titled The Effects of Failure to Enact a New Farm Bill, setting out how permanent law would be implemented. A program that revived automatically, by the mere passage of a date, would require no such outline. Secretary of Agriculture Tom Vilsack warned publicly through December 2012 that milk prices could roughly double under permanent law, a warning that described a consequence at the end of an implementation process rather than a consequence scheduled for the first morning of January. Congress acted on January 1, 2013, and the President signed the extension on January 2, 2013, so the full sequence never ran in that episode. The episode from 2013 to 2014 completed the proof by running the legal trigger without the market consequence. The one year extension expired on September 30, 2013, Congress did not renew the extension because a conference agreement was near, and the House passed a further short extension on December 12, 2013 that the Senate did not take up. On January 1, 2014, the suspension of the dairy provision lapsed in law. The department did not begin purchases. The Congressional Research Service records that permanent law for dairy was delayed, citing the need for rulemaking, and Secretary Vilsack stated publicly in December 2013 and January 2014 that an immediate price increase in January was unlikely because implementation steps had to come first. The Agricultural Act of 2014 was signed on February 7, 2014, and reimposed the suspension. For five weeks, revival existed in law without existing in the market, and no purchases, allotments, or quotas were ever put into operation.

Discretion inside the statute adds a second source of gradation. The Secretary sets support levels inside statutory ranges rather than at a single explosive point. Dairy support under section 201 of the 1949 act runs from 75 to 90 percent of parity, at the level that will assure an adequate supply. Wheat support runs from 75 to 90 percent where marketing quotas are not announced, from 65 to 90 percent where quotas are approved, and at 50 percent where quotas are disapproved. Upland cotton support runs from 65 to 90 percent, corn support runs from 50 to 90 percent, and the general run of levels extends from 50 to 90 percent of parity depending on the commodity. The ranges do not rescue the statute from severity, since support at the bottom of the ranges exceeds modern market prices for every supported commodity. The ranges do place consequential choices in administrative hands, and those choices shape how quickly prices respond. The commodity calendar adds a third gradation, since dairy turns on the calendar year and crops turn on marketing years months after dairy. Estimates of eventual scale belong to that later stage. The Economic Research Service, in a study from 1985, estimated that the department would need to remove from 13 to 17 percent of milk production to lift prices to mandated levels and that permanent dairy law could cost about 6.5 billion dollars per year in 1990. A White House report of November 2013 estimated a support price near 37 dollars per hundredweight on prices and production from 2013, costing between 10 billion and 12.5 billion dollars per year, with milk prices doubling. The cliff metaphor overstates the suddenness of revival while understating nothing about the eventual scale.

## What the Device Does Not Do

An honest account ends with limits, because the pressure that permanent law exerts has encouraged claims that the statute cannot sustain. Permanent law does not operate the modern farm program. Revival would restore parity based supports for a limited list of commodities through nonrecourse loans, purchases, allotments, and quotas, and revival would leave soybeans, oilseeds, peanuts, wool, mohair, sugar beets, sugarcane, dry peas, lentils, and chickpeas without support, leave crop insurance to a separate statute that never lapsed, and leave nutrition assistance to a separate statute continued through appropriations. Permanent law does not guarantee repeal, replacement, or timely suspension. The record from 1995 to 2013 shows repeal reaching conference and failing, bills stopping at referral across seven years, an amendment submitted and never offered, and a replacement plan passing one chamber and yielding to suspension. Permanent law does not even guarantee speed on its own behalf, as five weeks of governing dairy law without a purchase demonstrated at the start of 2014. The device was never built, in the form that survived, to run a modern agricultural economy. The device performs a narrower task, and the narrower task explains both the limits and the longevity.

The narrower task is compulsion by prospect. Congress keeps the Agricultural Act of 1949 in the statute books, suspended beneath each successive farm bill, because the consequences of operating the 1949 act are consequences that Congress, producers, and markets all wish to avoid, and the shared wish disciplines the legislative calendar. The discipline produced extensions in the spring of 2008, an extension signed on January 2, 2013, a completed farm bill in February 2014 after five weeks of lapsed dairy suspension, and a completed farm bill on December 20, 2018 before permanent law would have taken effect on January 1, 2019. The closing assessment returns to the claim that names the whole arrangement. Congress keeps a statute nobody wants to operate because its operation is the guarantee. The guarantee is not that milk will double in price on a January morning, a claim the record from 2013 to 2014 disproved in practice. The guarantee is that a lapsed farm bill cannot rest in lapse, because behind the lapse stands a parity based system at multiples of market prices, a department under a duty to build the machinery of that system, a staggered calendar that brings dairy first and crops in sequence, and smaller programs already losing operations for want of a baseline.

A reader who follows that history faces a documentary problem as much as a legal problem, since the evidence sits in codified sections, conference agreements, department memoranda, and budget reports produced across seven decades. Permanent law endures as a text, sleeps as a program, and works as a threat, and the three conditions explain a statute retained through December 1, 2020 as a law from 1949 that almost no participant wishes to see in force and that every participant can rely upon to force a decision before inaction becomes operation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why does the Agricultural Act of 1949 never expire?

The Agricultural Act of 1949 was enacted without an expiration date, and Congress has never repealed it. President Harry S. Truman signed the act on October 31, 1949, as Public Law 81-439. Later farm bills did not remove its commodity support provisions from the statute. Each farm bill instead suspended those provisions for a defined period, leaving the text in place while a temporary program governed. Because no expiration date appears in the act and no repeal has been enacted, the provisions remain in the United States Code in suspended and inactive form, and operation revives by the terms of the suspension when that suspension ends without a successor farm bill or extension in place.

### Q: What is permanent law under the Agricultural Act of 1949?

Permanent law means the non-expiring commodity support provisions of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949. Under each modern farm bill, those provisions remain in the statute, suspended and inactive, while the farm bill program operates in their place. The suspended provisions prescribe mandatory price supports calculated as percentages of parity, delivered through nonrecourse loans, purchases, acreage allotments, and marketing quotas. Support levels under permanent law run between 50 and 90 percent of parity depending on the commodity, according to the Congressional Research Service. If a farm bill lapses and the suspension lapses with it, no new enactment is required for the older provisions to govern again.

### Q: What is parity pricing in the Agricultural Act of 1949?

Parity pricing is a statutory measure of farm purchasing power defined at section 301(a) of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1301(a). The parity price for a commodity equals its adjusted base price multiplied by the parity index. The adjusted base price uses the average prices received for the commodity over the most recent ten years, rescaled, under the modernized parity formula introduced by the Agricultural Act of 1948. The parity index measures the relationship between prices farmers receive and prices farmers pay, including inputs, interest, taxes, and wage rates, against the base period from 1910 to 1914, years often described as a golden age of American farm purchasing power. The National Agricultural Statistics Service publishes parity prices monthly.

### Q: What is the dairy cliff under the Agricultural Act of 1949?

The dairy cliff is the sudden revival of dairy support under section 201 of the Agricultural Act of 1949 when a farm bill suspension lapses. Section 201 requires mandatory purchases at 75 to 90 percent of parity. Milk is produced daily and the dairy program runs on the calendar year, so the trigger date is January 1 following a lapse, months before crop commodities revert. The Congressional Research Service stated that purchases would produce support prices near 40 dollars per hundredweight, about double the average all-milk farm price of about 20 dollars per hundredweight during 2013. Contemporaneous reporting in December 2012 estimated retail milk could reach about 7 dollars per gallon. The phrase dairy cliff entered political vocabulary in December 2012, alongside the fiscal cliff.

### Q: Has the Agricultural Act of 1949 ever actually taken effect again?

Permanent law came closest to operating again in the episode of 2013 to 2014. The one-year extension of the farm bill expired on September 30, 2013, and Congress did not renew it. On January 1, 2014, the suspension of the dairy provision lapsed in law, and permanent law again became the governing statute for milk. The Department of Agriculture did not begin purchases. The Congressional Research Service records that permanent law for dairy was delayed, citing the need for rulemaking to implement it, and Secretary Tom Vilsack stated that an immediate price increase in January was unlikely. The Agricultural Act of 2014, signed on February 7, 2014, re-suspended permanent law. No permanent law purchases, allotments, or quotas were put into operation, so the revival existed in law for five weeks without existing in the market.

### Q: Why has Congress not repealed the Agricultural Act of 1949?

Congress has considered repeal repeatedly and has chosen suspension instead. Proposals to repeal permanent law have been rare, and the furthest advance came from 1995 to 1996, when repeal appeared in budget reconciliation versions and in versions of the 1996 farm bill, before the conference agreement followed suspension. In 2013, the House-passed farm bill would have repealed the 1938 and 1949 permanent laws and made its own commodity program the new permanent law, but the enacted 2014 act continued suspension, as did the 2018 act. The Congressional Research Service records that some observers see permanent law, and the consequences of restoring it, as assurance that Congress will revisit farm commodity programs when a farm bill expires. Critics characterize the device as a manufactured crisis, and defenders characterize it as necessary discipline.

### Q: Does the Agricultural Act of 1949 affect food assistance?

The Agricultural Act of 1949 has no direct effect on food assistance. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program rests on the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008, a permanent authorization separate from the 1949 act. Food assistance involves mandatory spending authorized but not appropriated by the farm bill, so an appropriations act or a continuing resolution allows operations to continue through a farm bill lapse. A lapse therefore does not end nutrition assistance by reviving the 1949 act, and the dairy and commodity support provisions of permanent law do not govern the program. The separation follows from the location of the authority for each program, which differs by statute and by funding route, rather than from a single expiration date applying to every program at once.

### Q: How do modern bills suspend the Agricultural Act of 1949?

Modern farm bills suspend permanent law by enacting language that declares listed provisions of the 1938 and 1949 acts shall not be applicable for stated crops, commodities, and periods. The modern suspension is codified at 7 U.S.C. 9092. In the Agricultural Act of 2014, the suspension appeared as section 1602, providing that the listed provisions shall not be applicable to the 2014 through 2018 crops of covered commodities, cotton, and sugar, and shall not be applicable to milk from enactment through December 31, 2018. Section 1702 of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 extended the suspension by striking 2018 and inserting 2023, carrying the same lists through the 2023 crops and through December 31, 2023 for milk, as an enacted schedule.

### Q: What is the public law number of the Agricultural Act of 1949, and where is it codified?

The Agricultural Act of 1949 is Public Law 81-439, published at 63 Stat. 1051. President Harry S. Truman signed the act on October 31, 1949, and the 81st Congress enacted it. Its provisions are codified principally at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following. That placement follows from title IV of the act, which contains the general provisions and the Commodity Credit Corporation provisions classified at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following. The price support titles of the act sit at 7 U.S.C. 1441 and following, beginning with section 101 at 7 U.S.C. 1441 and including section 201, the dairy provision, at 7 U.S.C. 1446. The act also amended sections of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and repealed 7 U.S.C. 1302.

### Q: What is the difference between the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949?

The two statutes perform different work within a single permanent law system. The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, Public Law 75-430, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on February 16, 1938, and codified principally at 7 U.S.C. 1281 and following, replaced the scheme of the 1933 act after United States v. Butler in 1936. The 1938 act dropped the processor tax, funded programs federally, defined parity at section 301(a), and supplied acreage allotments, marketing quotas, and loan machinery. The Agricultural Act of 1949, enacted eleven years later, layered commodity support commands over those foundations, supporting the basic commodities at 90 percent of parity through the 1950 crops and then through flexible supports of 75 to 90 percent, keyed to supply. Farm bills suspend provisions from both statutes together.

### Q: Which commodities would permanent law support if a farm bill lapsed?

Permanent law would support dairy, wheat, rice, cotton, corn, and other feed grains. Milk and butterfat would receive mandatory purchases at 75 to 90 percent of parity under section 201. Wheat would receive nonrecourse loans and purchases at ranges that vary with marketing quotas. Upland cotton would receive nonrecourse loans at 65 to 90 percent, corn at 50 to 90 percent, and sorghum, barley, oats, and rye would be set by feeding value relative to corn. Rice would stand at 50 to 90 percent, and honey at purchases of 60 to 90 percent. Permanent law would not support soybeans and other oilseeds, peanuts, wool, mohair, sugar beets and sugarcane, or dry peas, lentils, and chickpeas, all of which modern farm bills cover.

### Q: What happened when the farm bill expired and Congress extended it in the American Taxpayer Relief Act?

The farm bill enacted in 2008, Public Law 110-246, expired on September 30, 2012. Some farm bill programs ceased new operations after October 1, 2012, while others continued under appropriations acts. The commodity programs of the 2008 act covered the 2012 crop year, so the permanent law trigger for crops had not arrived, while the dairy trigger was January 1, 2013. In September 2012, the Department of Agriculture published an implementation outline setting out how permanent law would be implemented, and Secretary Tom Vilsack warned through December 2012 that milk prices could roughly double. Congress passed the American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012 on January 1, 2013, and the President signed it on January 2, 2013, as Public Law 112-240. Title VII extended the provisions in effect on September 30, 2012 for fiscal 2013 and the 2013 crop year. No permanent law purchases were made.

### Q: What did the Department of Agriculture do during the lapse when permanent law governed milk on paper?

The Department of Agriculture did not implement permanent law dairy purchases during the lapse of 2013 and 2014, even after the suspension ended in law. The one-year extension expired on September 30, 2013, and Congress did not renew it because a conference agreement on a new farm bill was near. The House passed a further short extension on December 12, 2013, extending provisions to January 31, which the Senate did not take up. On January 1, 2014, the suspension of the dairy provision lapsed and permanent law became the governing statute for milk. The Congressional Research Service records that implementation was delayed, citing the need for rulemaking. Secretary Tom Vilsack stated publicly in December 2013 and January 2014 that an immediate price increase in January was unlikely because implementation steps had to come first. The Agricultural Act of 2014 was signed on February 7, 2014, and re-suspended permanent law before any purchases began.

### Q: Would milk prices really double under permanent law?

Official estimates prepared in 2013 supported the conclusion that farm milk prices would roughly double, although no permanent law purchases ever put those estimates into operation. The Congressional Research Service stated that purchases under permanent law would result in milk support prices near 40 dollars per hundredweight, about double the average all-milk farm price of about 20 dollars per hundredweight during 2013. A White House report of November 2013 estimated a support price near 37 dollars per hundredweight on 2013 prices and production, costing between 10 billion and 12.5 billion dollars per year, with milk prices doubling. Contemporaneous reporting in December 2012 placed the prospective support level near 38 dollars per hundredweight against a market near 18 dollars and estimated retail milk could reach about 7 dollars per gallon. Each estimate rested on support at 75 to 90 percent of parity anchored to 1910 to 1914.

### Q: What does the Commodity Credit Corporation do under the Agricultural Act of 1949?

The Commodity Credit Corporation is the operating instrument through which the Department of Agriculture carries out support under the Agricultural Act of 1949. Title IV of the 1949 act contains the general and Commodity Credit Corporation provisions, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following. Through the Corporation, the department stands ready under the Dairy Price Support Program to purchase cheddar cheese, butter, and nonfat dry milk at announced purchase prices calculated to drive the farm price of milk up to the support level. For crops, support runs through nonrecourse loans in which a producer pledges the crop as collateral and may forfeit the crop to the government if market prices remain below the loan rate. Together with the 1938 and 1949 acts, the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948 forms one of the three permanent laws from which farm commodity program authority derives.

### Q: What are acreage allotments and marketing quotas under permanent law?

Acreage allotments and marketing quotas are the production controls that accompany price support for wheat and cotton under permanent law. The Department of Agriculture is required to announce acreage allotments, which address the land base for a supported crop, and to hold producer referenda on marketing quotas, which address the quantity that may be marketed. Quotas require a two-thirds affirmative vote of eligible producers. Approved quotas bring the highest support levels and mandatory restrictions. For wheat, support runs at 75 to 90 percent of parity where quotas are not announced, at 65 to 90 percent where quotas are approved, and at 50 percent where quotas are disapproved, with acreage allotments attached. Producers who market above an approved quota pay penalties and lose loan eligibility, joining consent, restraint, and support in a single sequence.

### Q: What was the Brannan Plan, and why did Congress enact the Agricultural Act of 1949 instead?

The Brannan Plan was the alternative proposed by Secretary of Agriculture Charles F. Brannan under the Truman administration. The plan would have let market prices fall to clearing levels and paid producers the difference as income payments, separating consumer prices from producer income. A House bill embodying Brannan features, the Pace bill, was voted down. The House then passed the Gore bill, sponsored by Representative Albert Gore Sr., continuing high rigid supports, while the Senate passed the Anderson bill, sponsored by Senator Clinton P. Anderson of New Mexico, modifying the Agricultural Act of 1948 toward flexible supports with a somewhat higher lower limit. The conference compromise between the Gore and Anderson bills became the Agricultural Act of 1949, signed on October 31, 1949, supporting basic commodities at 90 percent of parity through the 1950 crops and then through flexible supports keyed to supply.

### Q: When would permanent law take effect for each commodity after a lapse?

Permanent law would take effect on different dates for different commodities because the programs run on different calendars. Dairy arrives first. Milk is produced daily, the dairy program runs on the calendar year, and the trigger date is January 1 following a lapse of the suspension. Crop commodities revert on their crop and marketing years, months later, and a crop already covered by an expiring farm bill continues through its marketing year. Timing after the trigger also matters. The Congressional Research Service records that, if a suspension expires, the Department of Agriculture is required to begin implementing permanent law, but the department may need time to write and publish new regulations, and market effects may be gradual, taking effect over weeks or months rather than on the trigger date itself.

### Q: What should a student know about the Agricultural Act of 1949 for an exam?

A student should hold five connected points together. The Agricultural Act of 1949 is Public Law 81-439, signed on October 31, 1949, codified principally at 7 U.S.C. 1421 and following, and enacted without an expiration date. Together with the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, it supplies permanent law for farm commodity programs. Parity, defined at section 301(a) of the 1938 act, equals an adjusted base price multiplied by the parity index, anchored to the period from 1910 to 1914. Farm bills suspend permanent law rather than repealing it, under the suspension codified at 7 U.S.C. 9092. Dairy under section 201 revives first, on January 1, at mandatory purchases of 75 to 90 percent of parity. Repeal proposals, including the advance of 1995 to 1996 and the House proposal of 2013, ended in continued suspension.

### Q: Where is the suspension of permanent law codified?

The suspension of permanent law is codified at 7 U.S.C. 9092. Section 1602 of the Agricultural Act of 2014 carried the suspension in that farm bill, listing provisions of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949 that shall not be applicable to the 2014 through 2018 crops and to milk through December 31, 2018. Section 1702 of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 extended the same suspension by striking 2018 and inserting 2023. Earlier farm bills carried suspension in different sections, including section 171 in 1996 and section 1602 in each of the farm bills enacted in 2002 and 2008, after direct suspension or nonapplicability language became the practice from the farm bill enacted in 1977 onward.
