The plan for how to stop Son Heung-min begins while you have the ball, which is the sentence most coaches will find hardest to accept and the one everything else in this article depends on. What it accepts giving up is territory and the settled duel: he will receive the ball in decent positions, he will beat defenders occasionally, and you will not prevent that. What it refuses to give up is the transition, the four-second window between your side losing possession and your side re-establishing shape, because that window is where almost every dangerous action from this profile begins and where almost no defensive plan is actually operating.

![How to stop Son Heung-min, a rest-defense counter-plan for a two-footed transition runner - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-41.webp)

Two facts define the entire problem and both of them break the standard defensive toolkit.

The first is that his threat is created by your attacking shape rather than by your defensive shape. A forward who hurts you primarily in transition is a forward whose danger level was set before the ball was lost, by where your full-backs were standing, by whether your holding midfielder was ahead of or behind the ball, and by how many bodies you committed to the final third. By the time your defenders are reacting to him, the important decisions have already been made and they were made by your attackers.

The second is that the single most common individual defensive tactic in football is unavailable here. Showing an attacker onto his weaker foot is the instruction every defender learns before any other, and it is the instruction most opposition meetings are built around. Against a genuinely two-footed attacker who finishes with either foot from either side, the instruction has nothing to point at. There is no weaker side to show him onto, which means the forcing decision has to be relocated from feet to space, and that relocation is a different skill that has to be coached as one.

## The no-weak-side answer

That relocation is the framework this article names and applies throughout.

When an attacker has no weak foot, the defender's forcing objective changes from side selection to contact and congestion. Instead of asking which direction is less dangerous, which has no answer, the defender asks which direction has more bodies in it and less ground beyond it, and forces there. The target is not a foot, it is a population: the defender is trying to deliver the attacker into a zone containing more of your players and less open grass, and he is trying to do it by making contact available rather than by angling his body toward one side.

This sounds like a semantic difference and it is a large practical one. A defender showing an attacker onto a weak foot stands off, angles his hips, and waits, because the plan is to let the attacker execute a lower-quality action. A defender forcing into congestion does something different: he closes the space on the open side, accepts that he may be beaten on the crowded side, and makes his body available for a collision that a second defender can arrive into. The first is a passive tactic. The second is an active one, it feels riskier, and against a two-footed attacker it is the only one that works.

### What replaces showing an attacker onto his weaker foot?

Forcing him toward bodies rather than toward a foot. The defender closes the open side, concedes the crowded side, and makes contact available so a covering defender can arrive into the duel. The objective moves from producing a low-quality action to producing a congested one, which is a different coaching point and a different rehearsal.

## Rest defense: the section that matters most

Almost every amateur coach and a surprising number of professional ones have no rest-defense plan, and the absence is invisible until a transition punishes it, at which point it is diagnosed as a defensive error committed by whoever happened to be closest.

Rest defense is the arrangement of the players who are not directly involved in your attack, held while you have the ball, specifically to control what happens when you lose it. It is a possession-phase decision with an entirely defensive purpose, which is why it falls between the two halves of most coaching and gets addressed by neither.

The concept can be stated in one sentence: the shape you hold while attacking determines the shape you will defend in for the first four seconds after losing the ball, and the first four seconds are when a transition runner does his damage.

Against this profile, the rest-defense question has a specific form. He starts wide or in the half-space, high, and stays there while his side defends. He is not part of their defensive block in any meaningful sense, which is a coaching decision on their part and a permanent condition you must plan around. The moment his side wins the ball, he is already goal-side of at least one of your players and running.

Your rest-defense arrangement therefore has to answer three questions before every attacking sequence. How many of your players are behind the ball at the moment of the turnover. Where is your holding midfielder relative to the ball. And which of your defenders is responsible for the space he starts in.

Most sides answer the first, ignore the second, and have never considered the third.

### The three rest-defense structures and what each concedes

There are three standard arrangements and each is right in some circumstances, which is why the choice is a decision rather than a rule.

The first is the two-plus-one, meaning two centre-backs and one holding midfielder behind the ball, with both full-backs advanced. This is the most aggressive attacking arrangement, it produces the most attacking players in the final third, and it is close to unusable against this profile. Against a transition runner starting wide, a two-plus-one leaves the wide channel entirely uncovered at the moment of the turnover, which means the first pass finds him with no defender within fifteen yards and a centre-back forced into a sprinting duel across the pitch. If you use a two-plus-one against a side with a transition runner, you should understand you have chosen to concede a specific kind of chance in exchange for attacking numbers, and that trade is sometimes correct against a weak side and never correct against a good one.

The second is the three-plus-one, meaning two centre-backs, one full-back held back, and one holding midfielder. This is the standard elite arrangement and it is the recommendation for most sides facing this profile. The held-back full-back is the one on his side, which is the whole point: the defender responsible for his starting space is present at the moment of the turnover rather than fifty yards away. It costs one attacking player, which is a real cost and a smaller one than the alternative.

The third is the four-plus-one, meaning a full back four plus a holding midfielder, with only five players committed to the attack. This is a conservative arrangement that essentially removes the transition threat and removes most of your attacking threat with it. It is correct when protecting a result, correct for a weaker side that intends to attack only on set pieces and counters, and wasteful for a side that expects to control the match.

The decision between the second and third arrangements is the single most consequential one a coach makes against this profile, and it is made in the week rather than in the match.

## Why dropping deep is not the answer
### Does a deep block remove a transition threat?

No, and it usually makes things worse. Dropping deep removes your counter-press, which is the only mechanism that prevents a transition rather than defending one, and it moves the break closer to your goal, cutting your recovery time from around six seconds to around three.


The instinctive response to a transition threat is to defend deeper, and it is worth dismantling properly because it is the plan most sides default to and it costs more than it saves.

A deep block does reduce the space behind your defensive line, and space behind the line is the raw material a transition runner uses. That much is true and it is where most analysis of the question stops.

What a deep block also does is remove your own counter-pressing capability, and counter-pressing is the primary defence against a transition. When you lose the ball with a compact shape near the opposition box, you have five or six players within twenty yards of the ball and you can win it back before the transition begins. When you lose the ball with a deep block, your players are already retreating, nobody is near the ball, and the opposition has an uncontested first pass. You have converted a situation you could have prevented into one you have to defend.

There is a second cost. A deep block invites the opposition into your half, which means the transition, when it comes, starts closer to your goal. A break that begins sixty yards from your goal gives your recovering players six seconds. A break that begins forty yards from your goal gives them three. Against a runner who covers ground quickly, the difference between six seconds and three seconds is the difference between a shot and a clearance.

The verdict is conditional and should be stated as one. A deep block is correct for a side whose counter-pressing is genuinely poor, because such a side gets no benefit from the compact-shape option and only pays its costs. It is wrong for any side capable of pressing the ball immediately after losing it, which is most organized sides above a basic level. The general principle, that you should defend a transition threat with your attacking structure rather than with your defensive depth, is the argument this article exists to make, and dropping deep is the polite way of declining to make the plan at all.

## The individual duel: forcing without a weak side

Now the defender's half of the problem, which is where the no-weak-side answer applies.

The situation is a recovering defender facing a carrier who is running at him with space behind. The defender's traditional toolkit contains three tools: show him onto his weaker foot, delay until support arrives, and force him wide. The first is unavailable. The second is available and insufficient on its own. The third is available and needs modification.

Start with what forcing wide means against a two-footed attacker, because the usual version of this instruction is wrong here.

Forcing wide normally works because the touchline is an extra defender and because a wide position reduces shooting angles. Against an attacker comfortable finishing with either foot from either side, the second benefit largely disappears: a wide-left position is a shooting position for a right-footed strike across goal, and a wide-right position is a shooting position for a left-footed one. The touchline benefit remains and it is the one to use.

The practical instruction is therefore to force him toward the touchline on whichever side has more of your players in it, and to accept that the shooting angle is not being meaningfully reduced. That is a smaller gain than a defender is used to, and it is the honest one.

The larger gain comes from contact and congestion. The objective is a duel in which he cannot accelerate cleanly, which means a duel with a body in front of him and a second body arriving, rather than a duel in open space where his advantages are maximal. The defender's job is to survive long enough for the second body, and surviving means staying attached rather than winning the ball.

The specific technique adjustment is the angle of the retreat. A defender retreating in a straight line backward is running the race the attacker wants. A defender retreating at a diagonal, angling toward the crowded side, is compressing the space the attacker has to work in and shortening the distance the covering player has to travel. That diagonal retreat is the single most useful individual adjustment available here, and it is under-coached because it feels like conceding ground on one side.

The duel technique in its full form, including footwork, the moment of engagement, and the body position at contact, belongs to [the dedicated treatment of the one-on-one duel against a wide attacker](/2021/08/15/defending-inverted-wingers-1v1/), and this article covers only the adjustment specific to an attacker with no weak side.

## The plan in sequence, phase by phase

Here is the plan as it unfolds in a match, in order, so it can be delivered as a narrative rather than as a set of rules.

The sequence begins in your attacking phase, which is where a plan against a transition runner has to begin. Your near-side full-back, meaning the one on his side, is held. Your holding midfielder is positioned behind the ball rather than beside it, which is a subtle instruction and a critical one: a holding midfielder level with the ball is an attacking player, and at the moment of the turnover he is running the wrong way. Your far-side centre-back has an instruction to hold the central lane rather than to drift toward the ball, because when the break comes it will come centrally even if it starts wide.

Phase two begins at the turnover. The first three seconds decide everything and they are decided by a single choice: press or retreat. The rule is a distance rule rather than a judgment. If three or more of your players are within roughly fifteen yards of the ball, press immediately and aggressively, because you can win it back before the break begins and preventing the transition is worth more than any defensive arrangement. If fewer than three are within that distance, do not press at all; the pressing attempt will be beaten with one pass and the presser will be behind the ball for the remainder of the sequence.

That rule needs stating in the meeting as a number rather than as a principle, because players making the judgment in the moment will always choose to press, since pressing feels like effort and retreating feels like giving up.

Phase three is the first pass. Assume the press did not apply or did not work. The ball is now travelling toward him and your held full-back is the nearest defender. His instruction is to delay rather than to intercept. An interception attempt on a pass into a fast runner has a low success rate and a catastrophic failure mode, since the full-back is then behind the play with the runner accelerating.

The full-back's delay has a specific shape. He does not sprint at the receiver, he closes to about five yards and matches his speed, angling his body to the diagonal described above, showing toward the touchline and toward whichever side has his own recovering teammates. He does not attempt to win the ball. He is buying time in units of one second, and every second is worth roughly ten yards of recovery for somebody else.

Phase four is the covering arrival. Your holding midfielder is taking the inside lane, meaning he is not chasing the ball but running to a position between the carrier and the centre of the pitch, so that the pass inside is unavailable and the carrier's only options are outside or forward into the full-back. Your near-side centre-back is covering depth, positioned so that if the full-back is beaten, the carrier meets a second defender rather than open space. Your far-side centre-back is holding the central lane against the runner arriving from the other side, who is the player most sides forget entirely.

Phase five is the resolution, and it has three branches.

If the carrier is contained and turns back, the sequence is over and you have won. Recover shape immediately rather than pressing, because a side that has just survived a transition is disorganized and a second turnover is fatal.

If the carrier commits to a strike from a wide angle, that is the intended outcome. The goalkeeper's job is described below and the defenders' job is to close standing rather than to block sliding.

If the carrier beats the full-back and reaches the covering centre-back, the plan is now a straightforward last-line duel and the instruction is delay again rather than tackle, because a second covering player is always closer than the defender thinks and a tackle attempt that fails leaves nothing.

Phase six is the tactical foul decision, which needs its own treatment.

## The tactical foul decision, honestly
### Where on the pitch is a tactical foul actually worth it?

In the central lane, outside the final third, using the body rather than the legs. That location stops a break and concedes a free kick with almost no direct threat. A foul in the wide channel of your own defensive third trades a break for a delivery, which is often the worse outcome.


Every serious discussion of stopping a transition runner arrives at the professional foul, and it needs handling carefully rather than either endorsing or avoiding it.

The decision is a trade between three variables: the location of the offence, the card risk, and the danger of the situation being prevented. It is not a decision about whether to be cynical, and framing it that way produces worse decisions, because a defender who has decided fouling is wrong will fail to stop a break and a defender who has decided fouling is fine will accumulate cards for no benefit.

The location variable is the most important and the most misunderstood. A foul in the central lane outside the final third stops a break and produces a free kick from a position with almost no direct threat, and that is the highest-value place to commit one. A foul in the wide channel in your defensive third stops a break and produces a delivery into your box from a set position, which against a good delivery side is a worse outcome than the break you prevented. A foul inside the penalty area is obviously catastrophic. Defenders should be told the zones rather than the principle.

The card risk variable is about the manner of the challenge and this is where the honest safety statement belongs. A challenge made from behind, at speed, into the back of a sprinting player's legs is dangerous to the opponent and is the kind of action that injures people. It should not be coached, should not be tolerated, and no tactical advantage justifies it. The challenges that are appropriate are the ones that use the body rather than the legs: a shoulder-to-shoulder contact that unbalances the carrier, a step into his running line, a shirt held for a fraction. Those stop a break, they carry a card risk that is manageable, and they do not put anyone in hospital.

The danger variable is a judgment about what is being prevented, and the practical version is a simple test: would the situation, allowed to continue, produce a shot within the next four seconds. If yes, and if the location is the central lane outside the box, the foul is the correct football decision. If no, it is not, and the defender should keep defending.

The final point on this is a coaching one rather than a tactical one. Decide as a staff, in advance, which players are permitted to make that decision, and tell them. A team where every player believes he might be the one to make a professional foul is a team that accumulates cautions from midfielders in the first twenty minutes for offences that prevented nothing. Nominate the holding midfielder and the near-side full-back, and instruct everyone else to defend.

## The goalkeeper's job

A transition runner arriving at goal is a goalkeeping problem more than a defending one, and the goalkeeper's preparation against a two-footed finisher is different from his preparation against a strong-footed one.

The core adjustment is the timing of the set. Against a strong-footed forward, a goalkeeper can commit slightly earlier, because the finishing distribution is narrower and the disguised alternative is less likely. Against a two-footed finisher, the goalkeeper must hold his set a fraction longer, because both the strike across the body and the strike with the near foot are genuine, and committing early to either is being wrong half the time.

The second adjustment is depth. Against a runner attacking space behind a defensive line, the goalkeeper's starting position should be higher than his default, because his most valuable action is not the save but the interception of the pass before it becomes a chance. Sweeping is the highest-return goalkeeping behaviour against this profile and it is a positional decision made before the ball is played rather than a reaction.

The third is the strike from distance. A forward who shoots early and from range creates a specific goalkeeping problem, which is that the shot arrives before the goalkeeper has fully set. The instruction is to set earlier and further out, accepting that a chip becomes marginally more available, because an unset goalkeeper facing a struck ball from twenty-five yards is in a worse position than a set goalkeeper facing a lifted one.

The fourth is communication and it is the most valuable. The goalkeeper is the only player who can see the whole transition develop, and his instruction to the recovering defenders about which runner is unmarked is worth more than any positional adjustment he makes himself. The call has to name the zone rather than the player, because in a transition nobody has time to identify a shirt number.

## The defender profile that copes

The selection question against this profile has an answer that surprises coaches, because the obvious quality is not the decisive one.

Recovery pace matters and it is not the top requirement, because the situations that hurt are the ones where the runner already has a head start and pace merely determines how far he gets before help arrives. What matters more is deceleration and change of direction, because the moment that decides a transition duel is not the chase but the point where the defender has to stop running backward and start defending forward. A defender with excellent straight-line speed and poor deceleration will catch the runner and then be beaten by the first change of direction, which is a worse outcome than never catching him.

The second quality is the willingness to delay rather than tackle, which is a temperament question as much as a technical one. A transition duel is decided by whether the last defender buys three seconds or loses the ball in one, and defenders who are good at winning tackles are frequently bad at this, because their instinct is to engage.

The third is comfort defending in a wide channel. Against this profile the covering defender will frequently be a centre-back defending twenty yards from the touchline, which is unfamiliar ground for a centre-back and a situation in which many otherwise excellent defenders are poor. If you have a centre-back who is uncomfortable there, the rest-defense arrangement should be adjusted so he is never the one who has to do it, which usually means holding a full-back on that side permanently rather than situationally.

The fourth, and the one that decides more transitions than any of the above, is the holding midfielder's positioning discipline. The single most useful player against a transition threat is a midfielder who stays behind the ball during his own side's attacks. That is a positional habit rather than a physical quality, it is available to any player at any level, and sides that have it concede dramatically fewer transition goals than sides that do not, regardless of who their defenders are.

## Comparing this plan with the acceleration case

It is worth naming the closest comparison in this series explicitly, because coaches frequently treat all transition forwards as one category and the plans differ in a specific and important way.

The acceleration-based transition forward, whose threat is a burst of speed over the first fifteen metres from a standing or rolling start, is defended primarily by depth. Against that profile the line height is the decisive variable, because the burst needs space and removing the space removes most of the threat. The counter-plan is therefore a line-height plan, and this series develops it fully in [the counter-plan for an acceleration-first forward attacking depth](/2020/02/15/how-to-stop-kylian-mbappe/), which owns that material.

The profile in this article is different in a way that changes the plan. The threat is not primarily a burst into space behind a line; it is a run into space that has already been vacated by your own attacking shape, finished early and from range with either foot. Depth helps less, because the space he uses was created by your full-backs being high rather than by your line being high, and dropping the line does not bring your full-backs home.

The practical distinction for a coach preparing both is this. Against the acceleration profile, adjust your defensive line. Against this profile, adjust your attacking shape. Both instructions are about space and they are executed by completely different players in completely different phases, and applying the wrong one produces a side that has prepared diligently for the wrong problem.

There is a second difference in the duel itself. Against an acceleration forward, a defender's best hope is to be goal-side and to force him to beat you from a standing start, since the burst is what hurts. Against this profile, being goal-side is less protective, because the finish comes early and from range rather than after a run into the box. The defender's objective shifts from preventing the run to preventing the strike, which means closing the shooting distance rather than protecting the depth behind him. Defenders who apply the acceleration-case instruction here will drop off, concede twenty-five yards, and watch the shot.

## What to concede deliberately

The concession list against this profile is shorter than most and it needs to be stated because two items on it are counterintuitive.

Concede possession and territory in the middle third. A plan that holds a conservative rest-defense arrangement will have fewer players in the final third and will therefore concede the ball more often in advanced areas. That is the price of the arrangement and it should be understood as the price rather than as a performance problem.

Concede the settled duel. He will get the ball in wide areas with your defence set, he will take on a defender occasionally, and he will beat one sometimes. Against a set defence with cover behind, that is a low-value situation, and a side that panics about it will start doubling in settled positions, which pulls a defender out of the rest-defense arrangement and creates the transition exposure the plan exists to remove.

Concede the wide strike. If the plan forces him toward the touchline and he shoots from a wide angle with a set goalkeeper, that is the intended outcome and it should be praised.

Do not concede the central run at a retreating defence with the shooting distance closing, and do not concede the situation where your near-side full-back is fifty yards from his own goal at the moment of a turnover. Those two are the plan's actual failure conditions.

## The fallbacks

Three fallbacks, in ascending order of cost.

The first is to change the pressing rule. If your side is losing the ball in advanced areas repeatedly and the counter-press is failing, tighten the distance rule from three players within fifteen yards to four, which means you will press less often and retreat more often. This is a cheap adjustment, it can be made from the touchline, and it usually fixes a side that is being repeatedly caught.

The second is to hold both full-backs, moving from a three-plus-one to a four-plus-one rest-defense arrangement. This effectively removes the transition threat and removes most of your width in attack. Make it when the transitions are producing chances rather than when they are merely occurring.

The third is a personnel change: a second holding midfielder. This is the structural version of the same decision and it is more stable than holding both full-backs, because a midfielder screening centrally covers both channels while a held full-back covers one. It costs an attacking midfielder and it is the adjustment most sides should make at half time if the first two have not worked, rather than the shape change to a back five that coaches reach for instinctively.

The back five is available and it is the wrong fallback here, which is worth saying because it is the right fallback against most of the other profiles in this cluster. A back five defends width and depth, and the threat here is created by your own attacking shape rather than by width or depth, so a back five spends five defenders solving a problem that two extra behind-the-ball players would solve better. Sides that go to a back five against a transition threat frequently find they are still being countered, and they conclude the runner is unstoppable when the actual problem is that their wing-backs are still advanced at the turnover.

## Reading the debate

Five positions recur about this profile and each has something in it.

The position that he is only dangerous on the counter gets the emphasis right and overstates the conclusion. The transition is where the highest-value chances come from and it is not the only source; a wide reception with space to run at a defender produces genuine danger too, and a plan that defends only the transition will be beaten in settled play by a side patient enough to wait for it. What is true and useful is that the ratio is heavily weighted toward transition, and that a coach with limited preparation time should spend it there.

The position that a deep block removes him is wrong for the reasons already argued at length, and the reason it persists is that it produces a visible reduction in one thing, the ball over the top, while producing an invisible increase in another, the number of transitions you concede because you cannot counter-press. Coaches see the first and not the second.

The position that two-footedness is overrated is the most interesting of the five and it deserves a real answer. The argument is that most attackers finish with their strong foot the overwhelming majority of the time regardless of their weak-foot competence, so the practical difference is small.

That argument is right about the frequency and wrong about the consequence. What genuine two-footedness changes is not the distribution of finishes; it is the defender's ability to pre-commit. A defender facing a strong-footed attacker can angle his body, position his covering teammate, and set his goalkeeper against a narrow distribution, and all three of those decisions have positive expected value. Against a two-footed attacker every one of those decisions becomes a coin flip. The cost is not paid in the finishes he takes with the weaker foot; it is paid in the quality of every defensive decision made before the finish, which is exactly the kind of cost that never shows up in a statistic and always shows up in a scoreline.

The position that he is dependent on a creator behind him is half true in a way that matters for planning. A transition runner needs a first pass, and the quality of the first pass determines whether the run becomes a chance or a chase. That means the highest-leverage defensive intervention is not on the runner at all; it is on the passer, in the second after the turnover. This is the strongest practical argument for the counter-press rule stated above, and it is why the pressing decision belongs at the top of the plan rather than in the section on individual duels.

The position that he is more effective centrally than wide is worth engaging because it changes the plan. A central starting position shortens the distance to goal and puts him between the lines rather than outside them, which increases the number of receptions in dangerous areas and decreases the number of receptions with a full field of space to run into. Against a defence that sits deep, central is better for him. Against a defence that holds a high line and can be run behind, wide is better, because the wide start produces the diagonal run into the channel that is the hardest thing in football to defend. Both are true, they apply in different circumstances, and the practical instruction for a defending coach is to know which condition your own plan creates: if you are holding a high line, expect the wide version; if you are dropping, expect the central one.

## Scouting the transition threat

The analysis method belongs to other articles in this series and the narrow version needed for this plan is short and specific.

Watch his side's transitions rather than his touches. Count how many seconds elapse between their recovery and the first forward pass, because a side that plays forward within two seconds is a genuine transition side and a side that takes four is a possession side with a fast forward, and the plans differ.

Count how many of their transitions begin from a specific zone. Most transition sides recover the ball in one favoured area, usually the middle third on one side, and knowing which one tells you where your own attacking play should avoid losing possession.

Note his starting position at the moment of their recovery, over ten transitions. If it is consistently on the shoulder of your full-back, your rest-defense arrangement is the whole plan. If it is consistently deeper and central, the plan shifts toward the holding midfielder's screening position.

Note the first pass. Is it played over the top, through the lines, or wide. Each has a different counter: the ball over the top is countered by the goalkeeper's starting depth, the ball through the lines by the holding midfielder's position, and the wide ball by the held full-back. Knowing which one your opponent prefers lets you weight the plan rather than spreading it evenly.

Keeping those four observations in a form you can find again is the part that most often fails, because transition notes are made in a hurry and lost by Thursday. ## The amateur version

Rest defense is an elite concept with an amateur application, and the translation is more direct than most.

Most amateur sides have no rest-defense arrangement whatsoever, which means both full-backs are usually advanced, the holding midfielder is usually ahead of the ball, and every turnover is a three-versus-two against two centre-backs. That is not a subtle failure and it is the single largest source of conceded goals in amateur football, well ahead of individual defensive errors, and it is fixable in one conversation.

The conversation is short. Name one player who stays behind the ball during every attack, and name him by position rather than by situation. In a side playing with four at the back, hold the full-back on the side where the opposition's dangerous attacker starts. If you do not know which side that is, hold the one on your weaker centre-back's side. Tell the holding midfielder that he does not go past the ball, ever, and be prepared for him to dislike it.

The second amateur adjustment is the pressing rule, and it should be simplified further than the elite version. Rather than counting players within fifteen yards, use a visual rule: press if you can see three of your own shirts near the ball. Amateur players cannot count distances under pressure and they can count shirts.

The third is the diagonal retreat, which is teachable in a single session and which most amateur defenders have never been shown. Set up a defender retreating against a carrier in a wide channel with a covering player arriving late, and coach the retreat angle rather than the tackle. Score it on whether the covering player arrived in time, not on whether the ball was won.

The fourth is the honest one. Do not attempt to show a two-footed attacker onto a weaker foot, because there is not one, and a defender attempting it will end up square and passive. Tell him to force toward his own teammates and to make contact available. That instruction is comprehensible at every level and it is the same instruction the elite version gives.

## The line-height question, answered properly

Coaches ask which line height works against this profile more often than they ask anything else, and the honest answer has two parts that pull in opposite directions.

The first part is that line height is less decisive here than against a pure depth runner, for the reason developed above: the space he uses is created by your attacking commitment rather than by your defensive line, and no line height brings a full-back home from the opposition byline.

The second part is that line height still matters, in a specific and narrower way than usual. It determines the distance your recovering players have to cover, and therefore how many of them arrive in time. A high line with a compact shape means your midfielders are close to your defenders and the whole unit recovers together. A deep line means your midfielders are ahead of your defenders at the turnover and recover into an already-formed defensive block, which sounds fine and produces the specific problem of a large gap between the lines that a transition runner receives in.

The recommendation is a high-to-medium line with genuine compactness, meaning no more than roughly twenty-five yards between the deepest defender and the highest midfielder during your own attacks. Compactness matters more than absolute height, and it is the variable most sides ignore in favour of the one they can see.

There is a condition that changes the recommendation. If your centre-backs cannot defend space behind, meaning they are slow in recovery and poor in a turn, a high line is dangerous regardless of the analysis above, because a single ball over the top ends the argument. Sides in that situation should drop the line, accept the counter-pressing cost, and compensate by holding both full-backs, which is the four-plus-one arrangement. That is a coherent plan with a known price, and it is much better than the incoherent plan of a high line defended by players who cannot defend it.

The goalkeeper's starting depth is the other half of the line-height decision and it is usually treated separately, which is a mistake. A goalkeeper positioned high behaves as an additional defender and permits a line five yards higher than the same defenders could otherwise hold. A goalkeeper positioned on his line removes that permission. The line height and the goalkeeper's depth should be set together, in the same conversation, and against a transition threat the goalkeeper's willingness to sweep is often the variable with the most room to improve.

## The full-back's recovery angles

The full-back on his side is the plan's most important individual and his job splits into two distinct situations that require different behaviour.

The first is the situation where he is held in the rest-defense arrangement and is therefore present at the turnover. His job is the delay described in the sequence: close to five yards, match speed, angle the retreat diagonally toward the crowded side, do not attempt the interception, do not attempt the tackle. The measure of his performance is seconds bought, not duels won.

The second is the situation where he was advanced, the arrangement broke down, and he is recovering from behind. This is the situation that decides matches and it is the one nobody rehearses.

A full-back recovering from an advanced position has a choice of line and the choice is usually made wrongly. The instinct is to run at the ball, taking the shortest path to the carrier, which is a ball-side recovery. It feels productive and it achieves nothing, because the carrier is ahead of him and moving away, so he arrives behind the play permanently.

The correct line is a goal-side diagonal, meaning he runs toward his own goal at an angle that will intersect the attack rather than toward the ball. He is not trying to catch the carrier. He is trying to arrive at the point where the attack will be, which is usually the edge of his own penalty area, and to arrive there before the ball does. A full-back who runs that line will frequently make an interception or a block that looks fortunate and was geometric.

There is a third situation worth naming, which is the recovery when the transition starts on the opposite flank and he is the far-side full-back. His job is to tuck to the far post, and the instruction is to do it immediately rather than after checking whether he is needed. The far-post arrival on a transition is the least-tracked runner in football, and the far-side full-back is the only player positioned to take him.

## Defending him when your side is chasing a goal

The match-state question is the one that produces the most conceded goals and the least planning, because a side chasing a goal has abandoned its rest-defense arrangement by definition.

Start with the honest statement. If you need a goal and you commit players forward, you will concede transitions, and no arrangement prevents it. The question is not how to have both but how to choose the exposure you can live with.

The best available answer is to concede width rather than the centre. Push both full-backs high, accept that the wide channels are open at every turnover, and keep two centre-backs and one holding midfielder behind the ball with an explicit instruction to defend the central lane and nothing else. A transition down a wide channel that ends with a cross into a box containing two centre-backs is survivable. A transition through the central lane against a scrambling defence is not.

That instruction has a specific consequence for the covering players and it must be said clearly: when the break comes down the wide channel, the centre-backs do not go out to it. They hold central. The wide carrier is allowed to run, allowed to reach the byline, allowed to cross, and is met by nobody until he enters the box. Every defensive instinct rejects this and it is correct, because a centre-back who steps out to a wide carrier while chasing a goal leaves a central lane with one defender in it and a runner arriving.

The second element is the substitution plan. A side chasing a goal against a transition threat should consider bringing on a defensive midfielder rather than an attacker at some point, which sounds like a contradiction and is not: the extra screening player permits the full-backs to stay high, which produces more attacking width than the attacker would have contributed. That is a counterintuitive substitution and it is one of the most effective in this situation.

The third is the set-piece discipline. A side chasing a goal sends bodies forward for corners, and a transition threat left up the pitch is the reason to keep three back rather than two. Two back against a fast forward with a partner is a two-versus-two in open space, and that is a chance rather than a clearance.

## The wide-or-central question

Whether this profile is more dangerous from a wide or a central starting position is a genuine analytical question rather than a settled one, and the answer depends on what your own defence is doing, which is why it belongs in a counter-plan rather than a profile.

From a wide start, the threat is the diagonal run into the channel between full-back and centre-back, taken at speed, arriving in the box from an angle. That run is the hardest single movement in football to defend, because it attacks the seam between two defenders with different responsibilities while running away from both. It requires space behind the defensive line, which means it is available against a high line and unavailable against a deep one.

From a central start, the threat is the reception between the lines with the ability to turn either way, followed by an early strike. This does not require space behind, which makes it available against a deep block, and it is less devastating when it works because the finish comes from further out.

The practical consequence for a defending coach is a matching rule. If you are holding a high line, you should expect and prepare for the wide diagonal, which means your centre-backs' primary instruction is to defend the channel rather than the line and your full-backs' primary instruction is to stay narrow enough that the seam is small. If you are dropping deep, you should expect the central reception, which means your holding midfielder's primary instruction is to occupy the space in front of the centre-backs and your centre-backs' primary instruction is to step to a receiver rather than dropping with him.

Sides that give both instructions give neither, which is the most common failure in preparing for a flexible forward. Choose your line height first, then give the instruction that matches it, and change both together or not at all.

## The long pass that releases him

One specific action deserves its own treatment because it is the sequence that produces the most goals and the one defensive plans address least directly: the pass from deep, played first time, into the space behind or beside your defensive line, with him already moving.

The sequence has three components and you can attack any of them.

The first is the passer. Almost every side has one or two players who supply this pass, usually a centre-back with range or a deep midfielder, and the pass requires time and a clear sight line. Denying either prevents the sequence entirely, which makes pressure on that passer the highest-return intervention available. The practical instruction is that your nearest forward's pressing angle should be set to block the diagonal rather than to win the ball, meaning he approaches from the side that closes the passing lane to the runner rather than head on. Most forwards press head on because that is what pressing looks like, and a head-on press leaves both diagonals open.

The second is the runner's timing, which is not something you can influence directly and which you can exploit. A transition runner has to start his run before the pass is played, which means he is committed a fraction before the passer is. That fraction is what your defensive line can use, and the way to use it is a coordinated step forward at the moment of the run rather than a drop. Stepping to the run rather than dropping with it converts the run into an offside or into a reception facing your goal, and both are wins.

That instruction is dangerous and it must be conditional. Stepping requires a back line that steps together and a goalkeeper positioned to cover the ball played early. A line that steps raggedly plays the runner onside and hands him a clean run. If your defence cannot step as a unit, do not use this, and instead use the third component.

The third is the reception. Assume the pass has been played and he is running onto it. The defensive objective is now to control the direction of his first touch, and the mechanism is body position rather than speed. A recovering defender should aim to arrive on the side that pushes his first touch toward the touchline and toward your covering players, which is the no-weak-side answer applied at speed. The defender is not trying to reach the ball; he is trying to be on a specific side of the runner at the moment the ball arrives, and that is a much more achievable objective that most recovering defenders never attempt because they are chasing the ball rather than choosing a side.

The goalkeeper's part in this sequence is the sweep, and the decision rule is a distance rule: if the ball will arrive closer to the goalkeeper than to the runner, he goes, decisively and early, and he goes to clear rather than to claim. Goalkeepers who hesitate on that decision produce the worst outcome available, which is an arrival at the same moment as the runner outside the penalty area.

## The set-piece and second-phase dimension

Two dead-ball considerations attach to this plan.

The first is your own attacking corners and free kicks, which are the highest-risk moments in the entire match against a transition threat. A side attacking a corner has nine or ten players in the opposition box and a forward waiting on the halfway line. The standard arrangement of two players back is inadequate against a fast forward, because two back against a forward and one supporting runner is a two-versus-two in open space with fifty yards to attack.

The correct arrangement is three back, positioned as a triangle rather than a line: two players spread across the halfway line and one deeper, and the deeper one is the important addition, because he is the player who turns a two-versus-two into a covered situation. It costs an attacking body in the box, which is a real cost on corners and a smaller cost than the alternative.

The second consideration is the second phase after your defensive set piece. When you clear a corner, the ball frequently arrives in a wide area with your defensive shape unformed, and a transition runner in that moment is attacking a side that has no shape at all. The instruction is that the first player to recover shape is the holding midfielder rather than the full-backs, because the central screen is what a broken shape needs first.

There is a third and narrower point about free kicks conceded in wide areas of your own half. The tactical foul discussion above concluded that the wide channel is the wrong place to commit one, and this is why: the resulting delivery is a set-piece chance against an unformed defence, which is a higher-value situation for the attacking side than the transition you stopped.

## Six ways this plan fails

Collecting the failure modes gives a coach a review checklist, which is more useful after a conceded goal than a narrative.

The first is the absent rest-defense arrangement. Both full-backs advanced at the turnover, holding midfielder ahead of the ball, and the first pass reaching him with nobody within fifteen yards. Cause: rest defense treated as a possession-phase topic and therefore never covered in the defensive meeting. Fix: name the held full-back and the screening midfielder by position in the same breath as the defensive instructions.

The second is the wrong full-back held. The more defensive of the two is kept back regardless of which side the threat starts on, which pays the attacking cost and receives none of the defensive benefit. Cause: selecting for quality rather than for geography. Fix: hold the full-back on his side.

The third is the partial press. Two players press, the pass beats them, and both are now behind the ball. Cause: pressing treated as a matter of effort rather than a matter of numbers. Fix: the distance rule, stated as a number and rehearsed.

The fourth is the ball-side recovery. A recovering full-back or midfielder runs at the ball rather than toward the point of arrival, and arrives permanently behind the play. Cause: the shortest path to the ball is the intuitive path. Fix: coach the goal-side diagonal explicitly and score it in practice independently of the outcome.

The fifth is the interception attempt on the releasing pass. A defender gambles on the long diagonal, misses, and the runner has fifty yards. Cause: the pass looks interceptable and the failure mode is invisible until it happens. Fix: an absolute instruction that the pass into a runner is not contested, only the reception is.

The sixth is the sliding block in the final duel. A last defender goes to ground against a carrier with a covering player arriving, and the carrier goes past him into a space that is now empty. Cause: the tackle feels decisive and the delay feels passive. Fix: the instruction that the last defender never leaves his feet unless the ball is genuinely loose.

Five of the six are decision failures rather than ability failures, which is the recurring finding in this series and the reason the plan is written as a set of prior decisions rather than a set of qualities to select for.

## Measuring transition exposure from your own attacking shape

The most useful analytical exercise a coach can run against this profile is not about the opponent at all, and it takes one viewing of your own side.

Watch a recording of your own match and stop it at every moment you lose possession in the opposition half. Count three things at the freeze frame: how many of your players are behind the ball, where your holding midfielder is relative to the ball, and where your two full-backs are. Write the three numbers down for each turnover.

After twenty turnovers you will have a profile of your own transition exposure, and it will almost certainly be worse than you believed, because the exposure is invisible in matches where the opponent lacks the personnel to punish it. Sides discover their rest-defense problem in the fixture against a fast forward and conclude the forward was exceptional, when the exposure had been there all season.

The number that matters most is the holding midfielder's position, and the specific measurement is what proportion of turnovers occur with him ahead of the ball. Above roughly one in three, you have a structural problem that no defensive coaching will fix. Below one in five, your transition defending is probably adequate regardless of who you face.

The second most useful measurement is the height of your near-side full-back at the turnover, expressed simply as whether he is in the opposition half. Against a wide transition runner this is close to a binary predictor of whether the sequence becomes a chance.

The third is the time from turnover to your first defensive contact, which you can count in seconds off the recording. Under two seconds means your counter-press is functioning. Over four means it is not, and you should switch from the pressing option to the retreating option as a default rather than deciding in the moment.

None of that requires software, a subscription, or an analyst. It requires one viewing and a piece of paper, and it will change more about your defending than a week of work on defensive shape, because it addresses the phase where the goals actually come from.

## The plan across ninety minutes

The fatigue and match-state profile of this plan is different from most, because the load is concentrated in a phase rather than on a player.

The player carrying the most load is the held full-back, and the load is unusual: he is performing repeated maximal recovery sprints separated by long periods of low activity, which is the most fatiguing pattern in football and the least visible one, because his total distance will look modest. A full-back doing that job will decline sharply after roughly seventy minutes and the decline will show up first in his retreat angle rather than in his speed, meaning he starts running straight backward rather than diagonally. That is the substitution signal and it is visible from the touchline.

The second player carrying load is the holding midfielder, whose job is positional rather than physical and whose decline shows up as drifting forward. A holding midfielder who has been disciplined for an hour will start joining attacks in the seventieth minute, usually because his side is chasing the match, and that is precisely the moment the exposure becomes fatal. Assign somebody on the bench to watch for it specifically.

The match-state profile has three states.

Leading, the plan tightens by moving from the three-plus-one arrangement to the four-plus-one, which removes the transition threat almost entirely at the cost of your attacking play. Make that change with a substitution rather than an instruction, because players told to be more conservative interpret it inconsistently.

Level, the plan runs as written.

Trailing, the plan is partially abandoned as described in the match-state section above, with the specific instruction that the centre-backs hold the central lane and do not step out to a wide carrier.

The opposition substitution to prepare for is a change in the supporting personnel rather than a change to him, because the transition threat depends on the first pass and the second runner. A fresh central midfielder capable of the releasing pass is a more significant change than a fresh forward, and sides that watch only the attacking substitutions miss the one that matters.

## The second fixture

The plan will be studied and answered, and the likely answers are predictable enough to prepare for.

The response to a held full-back is to move his starting position to the opposite flank, which nullifies the geographic advantage of holding that specific full-back. The counter to that is not to hold both, which is expensive, but to move the screening midfielder's default position to a genuinely central one rather than a side-weighted one, so that the screen covers either channel.

The response to a functioning counter-press is to play the first pass long rather than short, bypassing the press entirely. The counter is the goalkeeper's starting depth and the centre-backs' willingness to defend the ball over the top, which is a personnel-dependent decision.

The response to a defence that steps to the run is to delay the run, arriving late rather than early, which defeats the offside mechanism. The counter is the holding midfielder tracking the late runner, which is a different assignment and needs to be named rather than assumed.

The instruction for the second fixture is the one that recurs across this series: change the condition, keep the concession. If you held a full-back in the first fixture, use a second screening midfielder in the second. The rehearsed answer will be aimed at an arrangement you are no longer using, and the time it takes to recognize that is worth more than any individual improvement.

## The verdict

The plan against this profile is a rest-defense plan and everything else is secondary.

Hold the full-back on his side, keep the holding midfielder behind the ball at all times during your own attacks, and instruct the far-side centre-back to hold the central lane rather than drifting toward the ball. Use a distance rule for the counter-press: three or more players within fifteen yards means press immediately, fewer means do not press at all. When the transition comes, the held full-back delays rather than intercepts, retreating on a diagonal toward your own recovering players, and the covering midfielder takes the inside lane rather than chasing the ball.

Do not attempt to show him onto a weaker foot, because there is not one. Force him toward bodies rather than toward a side, close the open side, concede the crowded one, and make contact available for a second defender to arrive into. That is the no-weak-side answer and it is a different skill from the one every defender was taught first, which is why it has to be rehearsed rather than assumed.

Do not defend the threat by dropping deep, because a deep block removes your counter-press, invites the transition closer to your own goal, and does nothing about the full-backs whose advanced positions created the space in the first place.

Concede territory in the middle third, concede the settled duel, and concede the wide strike at a set goalkeeper. Refuse the central run at a retreating defence and refuse the arrangement where your near-side full-back is in the opposition final third at the moment of a turnover.

If it fails, tighten the pressing rule first, hold both full-backs second, and add a second screening midfielder third. Do not reach for a back five, which spends five defenders on a problem two behind-the-ball players would solve.

The reason this article is built the way it is, rather than around a marking scheme, is the claim it has argued throughout: against a transition runner, the defending starts while you have the ball, and a plan that begins at the moment of the turnover has already conceded the only decision that mattered. ## Counter-pressing as the primary defence

The pressing rule stated in the sequence deserves a fuller treatment, because it is the single highest-value behaviour against a transition threat and because it is usually discussed as an attacking idea rather than a defensive one.

The logic is arithmetic. A transition becomes dangerous when the attacking side gets a clean first pass forward. Everything downstream, the runner's speed, the covering angles, the goalkeeper's sweep, is an attempt to survive a situation that has already gone wrong. Preventing the first pass prevents all of it, and the only moment at which the first pass can be prevented is the two seconds immediately after the turnover, while the recovering side still has bodies around the ball.

That window is short and it is the reason the pressing decision has to be a rule rather than a judgment. Two seconds is not enough time for eleven players to assess a situation and reach a consensus, so the assessment has to have been made in advance and encoded as something a player can execute without thinking.

The distance rule does that. Three players within fifteen yards means the ball can be surrounded before the pass is made. Fewer than three means the first presser will be bypassed and will end the sequence behind the ball, which converts a four-versus-five defensive situation into a four-versus-four.

Two refinements make the rule work in practice.

The first concerns who presses. The nearest player presses the ball and the second nearest presses the most dangerous forward pass rather than the ball, which is the detail that turns a press into a trap. Two players both pressing the ball leave every passing lane open, and the ball carrier under pressure from two players will find one of them. The second presser's job is to be in a lane, not on a shirt.

The second concerns what happens when the press fails. A failed press is not a disaster if the failure is anticipated, and the anticipation is a single instruction: the third player nearest the ball does not press and instead drops immediately into the space behind the two who did. That player is the insurance, and sides that press without insurance concede exactly the kind of transition this article exists to prevent.

There is a limit to how far this can be pushed and it should be stated. Counter-pressing requires proximity, and proximity requires that your attacking shape was compact, which conflicts with the width most attacking plans want. A side that attacks with genuine width across the whole pitch cannot counter-press effectively, because its players are too far apart at the moment of the turnover. That is a real trade and it means the rest-defense arrangement and the counter-pressing plan are the same decision seen from two angles: a compact attacking shape gives you the press and costs you width, and a wide attacking shape gives you width and requires the held full-back and the screening midfielder instead.

Coaches should choose one and commit. A side that attacks with width and also expects to counter-press will do neither, and it will conclude that the opposition forward is unplayably fast when the real finding is that the attacking shape was incoherent.

## Installing rest defense with a squad that has never heard the term

Most of the sides that need this material most have never encountered the concept, and installing it has to work around that rather than assuming it away.

The first obstacle is that rest defense sounds like a defensive topic and gets introduced in a defensive session, where the players who most need to hear it, the full-backs and the holding midfielder, are thinking about defending. It has to be introduced in a possession session, framed as part of how you attack rather than as part of how you defend, or it will not stick.

The framing that works is ownership of the counter. Tell the side that every attack has two possible endings, a chance for you or a chance for them, and that the shape you attack in decides which one is more likely. That framing gets attacking players interested in a defensive idea, which is the whole difficulty.

The second obstacle is that the concept is invisible in a normal training match, because the practice restarts when possession is lost and the exposure never gets punished. The fix is a rule change: when the defending side wins the ball, they get thirty seconds to score in a full-size goal, and a goal scored in transition counts double. That single scoring rule teaches rest defense faster than any explanation, because the attacking side will lose three matches in a row and will start organizing itself without being told.

The third obstacle is the holding midfielder, who will find the instruction unrewarding and will drift. The way to hold him is to make his position visible and countable. Give him a physical reference, usually the ball, with the rule that he does not go past it, and have somebody count violations during the practice. Counting works where explanation does not, and a player who has been told he went past the ball eleven times will fix it.

The fourth obstacle is the full-backs, who will resist being held because attacking is the enjoyable part of their job. The honest handling is to alternate: the held full-back is the one on the dangerous side and that is a match-by-match decision, so neither player is permanently restricted across a season. Framing it as a rotating assignment rather than a permanent demotion removes most of the resistance.

The fifth obstacle is that the concept has to survive contact with a match in which nothing goes wrong, because a side that holds its arrangement for ninety minutes and concedes nothing will conclude the arrangement was unnecessary. Tell them in advance that success looks like nothing happening, which is the same problem this series encounters with every containment plan, and review the transitions afterwards rather than the result.

## What each player hears in the meeting

Distilled instructions, one position at a time, because a meeting that gives everyone everything gives nobody anything.

The near-side full-back hears three sentences. You stay behind the ball when we attack. When they win it, you delay and you do not tackle. Retreat diagonally, toward your own teammates, never straight backward.

The far-side full-back hears one. When the break goes down the other side, you tuck to the far post immediately, without checking whether you are needed.

Both centre-backs hear two. The far-side one holds the central lane and does not drift to the ball. The near-side one covers depth behind the full-back and does not step out to a wide carrier when we are chasing a goal.

The holding midfielder hears two, and they are the most important instructions in the meeting. You do not go past the ball while we are attacking. When the break comes, you take the inside lane, which means you run to a position, not to the ball.

The nearest forward at the moment of a turnover hears one. Press the passer from the side that blocks the diagonal, not head on.

Every outfield player hears the pressing rule as a number. Three shirts near the ball, press. Fewer, drop.

The goalkeeper hears three. Start higher than your default whenever they have possession in our half. If the ball will reach you first, go early and clear rather than claim. Call the free runner by zone, not by name.

The nominated foul-makers, and they should be nominated by name, hear the location rule. Central lane, outside the box, body rather than legs, and never from behind into a sprinting player's legs.

And the whole side hears one sentence about the forward himself, which is the one that replaces the instruction they are expecting: there is no weaker foot, so nobody is showing him anywhere. Force him toward your teammates and make him take contact.

## What genuine two-footedness looks like, and how to check it

Coaches describe a lot of players as two-footed and very few of them are, so the plan should not be built on the label without checking the thing the label refers to.

There are three distinct levels and they call for different defensive treatment.

The first level is functional on both sides, which describes a large minority of professional attackers. The weaker foot can control, move, and pass the ball reliably, and can strike it cleanly with no pressure. What it cannot do is finish under pressure at speed or strike with power from distance. Against this level, showing an attacker onto his weaker side still works, because the action he produces there is materially lower quality even though it is not a mistake.

The second level is dangerous on both sides in specific actions. The weaker foot can finish inside the box and can deliver a cross, but the striking action from distance and the first touch under pressure still favour the stronger side. Against this level, showing onto the weaker foot works in some zones and not others, and the defensive instruction has to be zonal: show him weak-side outside the box, do not bother inside it.

The third level is genuinely symmetrical, meaning the finishing action, the striking distance, the first touch under pressure, and the shooting angles are close to equivalent on both sides. This is rare, it is what makes the no-weak-side answer necessary, and it is the level to verify before adopting the plan in this article, because the plan gives up a real defensive tool and giving it up unnecessarily is expensive.

The check takes one match and it has three markers.

The first marker is the distribution of shots by foot, which is the obvious one and the least informative on its own, because a wide attacker's shot distribution reflects the side he plays on as much as his ability. A left-sided attacker shooting mostly with his right is doing what the geometry requires and tells you nothing about his left.

The second marker is more diagnostic: the direction of the first touch out of a reception under pressure. A player with a genuine strong-side preference will move the ball to that side under pressure almost every time, because pressure removes deliberation and reveals the default. A genuinely symmetrical player will move it away from the pressure regardless of which side that is. Ten pressured receptions are enough to see it.

The third marker is the striking action from distance with the weaker foot, which is the last thing to develop and the first thing to disappear. If a player strikes from twenty-five yards with both feet, without adjusting his run-up or taking an extra touch to set, the symmetry is real.

There is a fourth marker worth watching for and it is the one that changes the plan most: the ability to finish across the body with the far foot from a wide position on both flanks. That is the specific action that removes the defender's angle advantage entirely, because it means every position on the pitch is a shooting position, and a defender cannot buy safety by forcing him to one side of the goal.

If the player you are facing sits at the first or second level, do not use the plan in this article's individual-duel section. Use the standard weak-side forcing instruction, because it will work and because it is a tool your defenders already have and can execute under fatigue. Reserve the no-weak-side approach for the third level, where the standard instruction has nothing to aim at.

The wider point generalizes beyond this profile. Defensive plans are built from a small number of tools, and each tool has preconditions. A coach who applies a tool whose precondition is absent will get a defender doing something that looks like defending and achieves nothing, and will blame the defender. Checking the precondition takes one viewing and it is the difference between a plan and a ritual.

## The structural signature of successful containment

Across the sides that handle transition forwards well, the common features are structural rather than personal, which is the encouraging finding for coaches without elite defenders.

They hold a screening midfielder behind the ball at all times, without exception, and they do it in matches where the opponent poses no transition threat, which is why the habit holds when the opponent does.

They hold the full-back on the dangerous side rather than the better defender.

They make the pressing decision by numbers rather than by enthusiasm, which means they sometimes decline to press in situations where pressing looks obligatory.

They coach recovery as a geometric problem, so recovering players run toward the point of arrival rather than toward the ball.

They keep three back on attacking corners rather than two.

And they instruct their last defender to delay rather than tackle, which means they concede more territory and fewer shots.

None of those six requires a fast centre-back or a physically dominant full-back. All six are decisions, they cost nothing but agreement and rehearsal, and the sides that make them concede fewer transition goals than sides with better individual defenders who have not made them.

## Reading whether the plan is working, in-match

Judging a transition plan by whether the forward has scored is judging it by an outcome with enormous variance, and it will mislead you in both directions inside the first half hour. Four leading indicators are visible from the technical area without any equipment.

The first is the position of your holding midfielder at the moment your side loses the ball. This is the single most diagnostic thing on the pitch and you can watch for it directly: fix your eyes on him rather than on the ball during your own attacks and note whether he is in front of it or behind it. If he is drifting in front, nothing else in the plan matters, because the arrangement has already failed before the transition begins.

The second is the location of your near-side full-back at the same moment. He should be in your own half. If he is repeatedly in the opposition final third when possession turns over, either the instruction has not landed or he has decided the attacking contribution is worth more, and both are fixed by a direct conversation rather than by a shape change.

The third is what happens in the two seconds after each turnover, and the specific thing to watch is whether your players are moving as a group or as individuals. A press with three players converging and a fourth dropping behind them is the plan working. Two players chasing while everyone else retreats is the plan failing, and it is failing at the decision layer rather than the effort layer, so the fix is to restate the rule as a number rather than to demand more running.

The fourth is the angle of your recovering defenders. A recovering full-back running at the ball is a plan failure you can see from fifty yards away, and it is the most correctable one in-match, because a single instruction shouted at the right moment will change it.

There is a fifth indicator that is about your own confidence rather than the players' behaviour, and it is worth naming. A plan that concedes territory deliberately will look, for the first twenty minutes, like a side being pushed back. If nobody on the bench has agreed in advance what success looks like, somebody will decide the side needs to press higher, the instruction will go out, and the arrangement will dissolve into the exact exposure the plan was built to prevent. Write the success markers down before kick off and check them against the list rather than against the feeling, because the feeling is unreliable and it is unreliable in a predictable direction.

The corresponding failure markers are equally short. Two or more transitions in which the first pass reached him with no defender inside fifteen yards. Any transition in which your near-side full-back was in the opposition half. Any sequence in which a recovering defender chased the ball rather than the point of arrival. One of each in a half is normal. Two of any is an adjustment.

## Why this plan is cheaper than it looks

A coach reading the arrangement above will price it as expensive, because the visible cost is an attacking full-back held back and a midfielder told not to join. That pricing is wrong, and correcting it matters, because the plan gets abandoned by sides who believe they cannot afford it.

The held full-back is not removed from the attack; he is repositioned within it. A full-back holding a deeper starting position is still available for the switch, still able to receive and circulate, and still the outlet when the attack has to be rebuilt. What he loses is the overlap and the arrival in the box, which are the two most visible parts of his attacking contribution and among the least productive per repetition. Sides that hold a full-back typically lose a small number of crossing situations and gain a structure that lets the rest of the attack take more risk, which is a trade in their favour rather than against them.

The screening midfielder is the same argument in a different position. A holding midfielder who stays behind the ball is not absent from the attack; he is the player who recycles the attack when the first entry fails, which is most of the time. Sides that push him forward gain one extra body in the final third and lose the ability to sustain pressure, because every failed entry becomes a turnover rather than a reset.

There is a third saving that almost nobody counts. A side that has a rest-defense arrangement can commit its remaining attackers more aggressively, because the consequence of losing the ball has been contained. A side without one has to be cautious everywhere, since any turnover anywhere is a potential chance conceded, and that caution suppresses the attack far more than one held full-back does.

The net position is that a coherent rest-defense arrangement usually costs less attacking output than it appears to and buys a great deal of defensive stability, which is why the sides with the best attacking records are also, more often than not, the sides with the most disciplined behind-the-ball structures. That is not a coincidence and it is not a paradox. Controlling the counter is what makes sustained attacking possible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Son Heung-min?

With a rest-defense plan rather than a marking plan. Hold the full-back on his side behind the ball while you attack, keep your holding midfielder behind the ball at all times, and instruct the far-side centre-back to hold the central lane rather than drifting toward the ball. Use a numerical rule for the counter-press: three or more of your players within roughly fifteen yards of the turnover means press immediately, fewer means retreat instead. When the break comes, the held full-back delays rather than intercepts, retreating diagonally toward his own recovering teammates, while the screening midfielder takes the inside lane. Concede territory and the settled duel; refuse the central run at a retreating defence.

### Q: What is Son Heung-min's biggest weakness?

The threat is structurally dependent on the first pass, which is the most exploitable feature of the profile and the one that changes plans. A transition runner cannot create the situation himself; somebody has to release him within a second or two of the recovery, and if that pass is delayed, blocked, or forced backward, the run is wasted and he arrives in a settled defensive shape where his advantages are much smaller. That makes pressure on the passer, not on the runner, the highest-leverage intervention available. The secondary point is that a settled duel against a covered defender is a low-value situation for him, as it is for almost every wide attacker, which is why sides that keep their shape and decline to panic concede far less.

### Q: How do you stop Son Heung-min on the counter-attack?

Prevent the counter rather than defending it. The two-second window after your turnover is when the sequence is decided, and the decision is whether you can surround the ball before the forward pass is played. If three of your players are near it, press immediately, with the nearest man on the ball and the second nearest in the most dangerous passing lane rather than also on the ball. The third nearest drops into the space behind them as insurance. If you cannot get three near it, do not press at all, since a beaten presser is a player permanently behind the play. Everything after that first pass is damage limitation: delay rather than tackle, take the inside lane, and retreat diagonally.

### Q: Can you show Son Heung-min onto a weaker foot?

Not if the symmetry is genuine, and that is the fact that breaks the standard defensive toolkit against him. Showing an attacker onto his weaker foot is the first instruction every defender learns, and it needs a weaker side to point at. When the finishing action, the striking distance from range, and the first touch under pressure are close to equivalent on both sides, the instruction has no target and a defender attempting it ends up square and passive. What replaces it is forcing toward bodies rather than toward a foot: close the open side, concede the crowded side, and make contact available so that a covering defender can arrive into the duel. That is an active tactic where weak-side forcing is a passive one.

### Q: Which defender profile copes with Son Heung-min?

Not the fastest. Deceleration and change of direction matter more than straight-line speed, because the moment that decides a transition duel is where the defender stops retreating and starts defending forward, and a quick defender with poor braking will catch him and then be beaten by the first change of direction. The second requirement is the temperament to delay rather than tackle, which good tacklers are often bad at. The third is comfort defending in a wide channel, since your covering defender will frequently be a centre-back twenty yards from the touchline. The fourth matters most and is not a defender at all: a holding midfielder with the positional discipline to stay behind the ball during your own attacks prevents more of these situations than any centre-back resolves.

### Q: How do you defend the run in behind from Son Heung-min?

Attack the pass rather than the run. The nearest forward presses the deep passer from the side that blocks the diagonal rather than head on, since a head-on press leaves both diagonals open. If the pass is coming anyway, a coordinated forward step by the defensive line at the moment the run starts converts it into an offside or a reception facing your own goal, but only if your line steps as a unit and your goalkeeper is positioned high enough to cover the ball played early; a ragged step plays him onside with a free run. If the pass has been played, the recovering defender stops chasing the ball and instead aims to arrive on the side that pushes his first touch toward the touchline and toward your covering players.

### Q: What line height works against Son Heung-min?

A high-to-medium line with genuine compactness, meaning no more than about twenty-five yards between your deepest defender and your highest midfielder while you attack. Compactness matters more than absolute height here, because line height is less decisive against this profile than against a pure depth runner: the space he uses was created by your full-backs being advanced, and no line height brings a full-back home. What the line does control is how far your recovering players have to travel and whether they arrive together. The condition that changes the answer is centre-back recovery quality. If yours cannot defend space behind, drop the line, accept the loss of counter-pressing, and compensate by holding both full-backs.

### Q: How do you stop the long pass that releases Son Heung-min?

Three points of attack. Pressure the passer with an angled approach that closes the diagonal lane, since the pass needs time and a clear sight line and denying either kills the sequence. Step the defensive line forward at the moment the run commits, which is available only to a line that steps together. And if the ball has been played, control the direction of his first touch by arriving on a chosen side rather than chasing the ball, which is a far more achievable objective than reaching it. Your goalkeeper's part is a distance rule: if the ball will arrive closer to him than to the runner, he goes early and decisively to clear rather than claim, since arriving simultaneously outside the box is the worst outcome available.

### Q: How do goalkeepers prepare for Son Heung-min finishing?

By holding the set a fraction longer and starting a good deal higher. Against a strong-footed forward a goalkeeper can commit slightly early because the finishing distribution is narrow; against genuine symmetry both the strike across the body and the near-foot strike are live, so early commitment is being wrong half the time. Starting depth matters more than the save itself, because the highest-value goalkeeping action against this profile is sweeping the pass before it becomes a chance, which is a positional decision made before the ball is played. Against the early strike from range, set earlier and further from goal, accepting that a chip becomes marginally more available, since an unset goalkeeper facing a struck ball from twenty-five yards is in a worse position than a set one facing a lifted ball.

### Q: Is Son Heung-min more dangerous wide or central?

It depends on what your own defence is doing, which is why the question belongs in a plan rather than a profile. From a wide start the threat is the diagonal run into the channel between full-back and centre-back, which is the hardest single movement in football to defend and which requires space behind your line, so it is available against a high line and unavailable against a deep one. From a central start the threat is the reception between the lines with a turn either way and an early strike, which does not need space behind and is therefore available against a deep block, while being less devastating because the finish comes from further out. Set your line height first, then give the matching instruction, and change both together or not at all.

### Q: How do you defend Son Heung-min when your side is chasing a goal?

Choose the exposure you can live with, because committing players forward guarantees transitions. Concede width rather than the centre: push both full-backs high, and keep two centre-backs and a holding midfielder behind the ball with one instruction, defend the central lane and nothing else. When the break comes down a wide channel, the centre-backs do not go out to it. He is allowed to run, allowed to reach the byline, allowed to cross, and is met by nobody until he enters the box, because a cross into two centre-backs is survivable and a central run at a scrambling defence is not. Consider a defensive-midfield substitution rather than an attacking one, since the extra screen is what lets your full-backs stay high.

### Q: What do you accept conceding in order to contain Son Heung-min?

Territory and possession in the middle third, which is the price of a conservative rest-defense arrangement and should be understood as a price rather than a performance failure. The settled duel, meaning he will receive in wide areas against a set defence and will beat a defender occasionally, which is a low-value situation whenever cover is present. And the wide strike at a set goalkeeper, which is the intended outcome of forcing him toward the touchline and should be praised rather than tolerated. Two things are never conceded: the central run at a retreating defence with the shooting distance closing, and any arrangement that leaves your near-side full-back in the opposition final third at the moment of a turnover.

### Q: What is the fallback if the plan against Son Heung-min fails?

Tighten the pressing rule first, raising the threshold from three players within fifteen yards to four, so you press less and retreat more. That is a touchline adjustment and it usually fixes a side being repeatedly caught. Second, hold both full-backs, moving to a four-plus-one arrangement, which removes most of the transition threat and most of your width. Third, introduce a second screening midfielder, which is more stable than holding both full-backs because a central screen covers both channels while a held full-back covers one. Do not reach for a back five, which is the instinctive change and the wrong one, since it spends five defenders on a problem created by your attacking shape rather than by width or depth.

### Q: How should an amateur side defend a two footed wide attacker like Son Heung-min?

Fix the rest defense first, because most amateur sides have none and it is the largest single source of conceded goals at that level. Name one player who stays behind the ball during every attack, by position rather than by situation, and hold the full-back on the side where the dangerous attacker starts. Tell the holding midfielder he does not go past the ball, and expect him to dislike it. Simplify the pressing rule to something countable under pressure: press if you can see three of your own shirts near the ball. Coach the diagonal retreat in one session, scoring it on whether the covering player arrived in time rather than on whether the ball was won. And do not ask anyone to show him onto a weaker foot, because there is not one; tell them to force him toward teammates and make him take contact.

### Q: What do analysts track when scouting Son Heung-min?

Transitions rather than touches. Count the seconds between his side's recovery and their first forward pass, since under two seconds means a genuine transition side and four means a possession side with a fast forward, and the plans differ. Identify the zone where they most often recover the ball, which tells you where your own attacks should avoid losing it. Log his starting position at the moment of recovery across ten transitions, because consistently on your full-back's shoulder makes rest defense the whole plan while consistently deep and central shifts it to the screening midfielder. And classify the first pass as over the top, through the lines, or wide, since each has a different counter and knowing the preference lets you weight the plan instead of spreading it evenly.

### Q: Which sides have coped with Son Heung-min and what did they do?

The sides that cope share a structural signature rather than a personnel one. They keep a screening midfielder behind the ball in every match, including the ones where the opponent poses no transition threat, which is why the habit survives when it matters. They hold the full-back on the dangerous side rather than the better defender. They make the pressing decision by numbers rather than by enthusiasm, and they sometimes decline to press where pressing looks obligatory. They coach recovery as a geometric problem, so recovering players run toward the point of arrival rather than toward the ball. They keep three back on their own attacking corners. And they instruct the last defender to delay rather than tackle, conceding more territory and fewer shots.
