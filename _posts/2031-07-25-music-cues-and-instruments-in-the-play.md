## Music Cues and Instruments in Romeo and Juliet: A Score in Three Evidences

The music cues in Romeo and Juliet are few enough to count without a second hand, and the count is the argument. The play that posterity wrapped in symphonic scores and ballet suites carries, inside its own early texts, one great burst of dance music at a ball, a wedding band that is hired and then forbidden to play, and a long final act in which the loudest sound is a boy's whistle in a churchyard. Everything else that audiences associate with the sound of this tragedy, the funeral marches, the love themes, the dirges over the tomb, was composed later and pasted on. The text's own music is smaller, stranger, and more exact: it is social music, hired for occasions, bound by contract, and dismissed the moment the occasions collapse. Restoring the cues to their printed and spoken forms shows a score that works by summons, performance, and refusal, in that order.

Any honest catalogue of the play's music has to begin by separating three kinds of evidence that editions and productions habitually blur. Directed music is what a stage direction in an early text orders to be played. Spoken music is what the dialogue commands, promises, reports, imagines, or uses as a figure of speech. Supplied music is what editors and theatre companies add, from a bracketed direction in a classroom text to a full incidental score. The three classes carry different weights. A printed direction is the closest thing the period offers to an instruction from the playhouse. A line of dialogue proves that the fiction hears or wants music, and it may imply a sound the stage owed the audience, but it binds no player. A supplied cue proves only that later centuries could not bear the silence. Kept apart, the three classes tell a clean story: the directed score grows slightly between the early texts, the spoken score is rich from the start, and the supplied score is where the famous sound of Romeo and Juliet actually lives.

The census of directed music can be stated in one paragraph, because the documents leave so little to count. The Second Quarto of 1599, the text on which modern editions of the play rest, prints exactly one direction that orders music: at the Capulet ball, "Musick playes and they dance" (1.5). The First Folio of 1623 keeps that direction in variant words and adds three more, each tiny: a drum commanded at the end of the street scene before the feast (1.4), a "Play Musicke" as the bridegroom approaches on the wedding morning (4.4), and a "Whistle Boy" in the churchyard (5.3). The First Quarto of 1597, the short reported text, prints none of the four. Every other musical event in the tragedy, and the tragedy is saturated with musical language, arrives through speech: a host's summons, a servant's begging, a bridegroom's promise, a father's metaphor, a page's agreed signal. F. W. Sternfeld, whose Music in Shakespearean Tragedy (1963, revised 1967) was the first full-length study of its subject, argued that Shakespeare used song and instrumental music as an integral part of dramatic structure to a degree his contemporaries did not attempt. The cue record of this play supports Sternfeld's standard and refines it: the integration is real, but it is achieved less by playing than by placing music under contract and then breaking the contract in public.

A catalogue earns its keep when it can be checked, argued with, and extended, and researchers who keep cue records of this kind do well to preserve the collation behind every row alongside the row itself, in a private research vault such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where a note on a single quarto reading stays attached to the claim it supports. Read down the class column, the catalogue states the article's thesis in miniature. The directed column is short and grows by accretion, one cue in the Second Quarto, four by the Folio, dozens in any modern prompt book. The spoken column is long from the beginning, because the play's people live inside a culture that hires music the way it hires cooks, and talks about sound the way it talks about honour. The drama happens where the two columns collide: at the wedding that becomes a funeral, the directed and spoken scores are both cancelled, and the band that embodies them is left standing onstage with its instruments in their cases.

A word on reading the table is owed before the argument proceeds. The rows stand in performance order, and the class column is the argument: read down it, and the score's shape appears as a rhythm of classes, directed once at the feast, spoken almost everywhere, supplied at the points where later ages could not bear the silence. The texts column is the discipline, since a cue that lives only in the Folio and a cue that lives only in dialogue cannot support the same sentence in criticism, however identical they feel in a theatre. Two absences in the rows deserve the notice an entry would give them. There is no row for a serenade, because Romeo never sings beneath the window, and no row for a lament at the tomb, because the text never orders one; the table's empty places are findings, and a reader who scans the sixteen rows for what a famous tragedy ought to contain will feel the argument before reaching the prose.

## The Ball Consort: What the Direction Orders and What It Withholds

The ball is the one place where the play's music is beyond argument, and the argument begins with five printed words. In the Second Quarto the direction "Musick playes and they dance" stands at line 602 of the text, set between Capulet's spoken summons, "come, musicians, play", and his cry for floor space, "A hall, a hall, give room, and foot it, girls". The order of the printing is itself a small staging problem, as the dance scholar Emily Winerock observes in her study of the scene's choreography: the music is apparently already playing before the host has cleared the room for it, which suggests either that the direction sits a breath early in the copy, or that playing and dancing were understood as separate starts, the band striking up while the tables were still being turned up and the fire quenched. Either way, the direction is a door left open. It orders sound and movement and specifies nothing else: no tune, no dance by name, no instrument, no count of players, no station for the band.

The dialogue around the direction is more forthcoming about the event than about the music. Capulet's welcome jokes turn on dancing: ladies whose toes are unplagued with corns will not refuse, and the old men retire to reminisce about masking thirty years before. Benvolio has already named the dance in the street, punning that the masquers will "measure them a measure and be gone" (1.4), and Romeo, watching from the floor's edge with his torch, times his approach by the dance's unit: "The measure done, I'll watch her place of stand, / And touching hers, make blessed my rude hand" (1.5). The measure, the stately dance unit of the period, is the only choreographic fact the text vouchsafes, and it arrives through speech. Romeo does not dance. His refusal is set in the street, "Give me a torch, I am not for this ambling" (1.4), and the ball's music therefore does double duty in the scene's design: it animates the room and it excuses the one man standing still inside it, so that the sonnet the lovers share is spoken under the cover of a festivity that a fuller treatment of [staging the ball, its music, masks, and dance](/2027/12/24/staging-the-ball-music-dance/) examines as a problem of space and sightlines.

The First Quarto sharpens the point by subtraction. In Q1's shorter text of the scene, Capulet welcomes the guests, jokes about corns, reminisces, and the dance happens, attested by the same "measure done" and by Juliet's parting joke about a rhyme learned from a dancing partner. What Q1 never prints is a summons to musicians or a direction for music. The reported text remembers the dancing and forgets the band, which is the opposite of the modern instinct, and a useful reminder that a direction's presence in Q2 marks a decision in the copy, not a law of the scene. The ball had music because the fuller text says so, in so many words, once.

### Does the ball scene name a single instrument?

No instrument is named anywhere in the scene, in any early text. The Second Quarto's direction says only that music plays; Capulet's summons names only the players, never their tools; and the dialogue that surrounds the dance speaks of measures, torches, and corns, but never of a viol, a lute, or a pipe.

The silence is worth dwelling on, because this play names instruments eagerly elsewhere when it wants them, in puns, in surnames, in a fiddlestick brandished as a sword. At the ball the band is a function, not a personnel list. A great Veronese house keeping a feast would engage the best dance band the city offered, and the fiction leaves the engagement exactly as blank as a steward's ledger line: musicians, so many, paid so much, instruments unspecified. Whatever stood in the musicians' gallery or at the hall's end in the first performances, the text declines to record it, and the decline is the evidence. Claims about hautboys at the Capulet ball, a favourite of programme notes, have no warrant in any early text of this play: the word belongs to the stage directions of Macbeth and to the subterranean music of Antony and Cleopatra, and importing it into Verona is the supplied score colonizing the directed one.

### Who were the waits, and why do they matter to a feast like Capulet's?

The waits were a town's official band, descended from watchmen who sounded the hours, grown by Shakespeare's lifetime into trained municipal musicians who attended the mayor and aldermen, played for civic ceremony, and hired themselves out for private feasts and weddings, with occasional work in the playhouses.

For the fiction, the waits are the obvious model: a standing civic consort, retained by the city, available by the evening to great households, and practiced in exactly the two services this play demands of its band, dancing after supper and bride-leading in the morning. For the playhouse, the question is harder, and it is here that the scholarship divides. The best surviving snapshot of a London consort in the play's own decade is a printed one: Thomas Morley's First Booke of Consort Lessons (1599), whose title page promises pieces "for six Instruments to play together, the Treble Lute, the Bandora, the Cittern, the Base-Violl, the Flute & Treble-Violl". That mixed or broken consort, plucked wire and gut strings under bowed strings and a flute, is the band that Mary Chan describes as most suitable for dance music and popular song, and Morley dedicated the book to the civic authorities whom London's waits served. Andrew Gurr's standard history of the Shakespearean stage long drew a line between the amphitheatres, where music stayed comparatively plain, and the Blackfriars with its famous consort; revisionist work on the company's instrumental resources answers that the line is too clean, pointing to the instruments the players themselves owned, among them the bass viol that the sharer Augustine Phillips disposed of by his will in 1605. David Lindley, in Shakespeare and Music (2006), stays guarded: instrumental playing by the adult companies before the Blackfriars years is possible, he allows, but a full Morley-style mixed consort is a claim the evidence will not carry.

The adjudication has to split a question that the debate keeps merging. What played inside the fiction is one question; what the Lord Chamberlain's Men could field at the Theatre in the middle 1590s is another. For the fiction, a hired civic band of waits is as close to certain as an unstated premise can be, since the play hires the same band twice and treats the hiring as routine. For the playhouse, the graded verdict is a small strings-led consort: viols and plucked instruments were in the company's hands, a dance band of some kind was a nightly necessity across the repertory, and the indoor house's famous consort belongs to the years after 1608. The broken consort in its full printed glory of 1599 remains, for the amphitheatre stage, a plausible ceiling rather than a documented floor. What neither side of the debate may do is quote the ball direction as evidence for its band. The direction says the music played. The rest is reconstruction, and reconstruction labelled as such is scholarship; reconstruction costumed as the text is the supplied score again.

## Music Heard Only in Words: Lark, Nightingale, and the Silver-Sweet Tongue

Between the ball and the wedding morning, the play's music goes underground into language, and the catalogue's spoken-as-metaphor rows carry two of the most analysed passages in the tragedy. In the orchard, Romeo measures speech against an instrument that is not there: "How silver-sweet sound lovers' tongues by night, / Like softest music to attending ears" (2.2). The compliment depends on music as the period's standard of tuned sweetness, and its metal is not decorative. Silver returns at the tragedy's hinge, in the song Peter quotes over the supposedly dead Juliet, where music with her silver sound is asked why silver is her epithet and three tradesmen answer in coin. The orchard's metaphor and the chamber's riddle are the same figure at two temperatures: in the garden, silver is what love sounds like; in the house of mourning, silver is what musicians are paid in, and the distance between those two silvers is the distance the play travels.

The dawn scene in Juliet's chamber (3.5) conducts the tragedy's most consequential argument entirely in the vocabulary of music, over a sound no direction orders and no production can neutrally supply. The lark is winning the dispute about whether day has come, and Juliet, losing, attacks the bird as a performer: "It is the lark that sings so out of tune, / Straining harsh discords and unpleasing sharps". Romeo offers the conventional defence, and Juliet overturns it on a technicality that is also a prophecy: "Some say the lark makes sweet division; / This doth not so, for she divideth us". Division, in the period's usage, is the art of breaking a melody into rapid ornamental notes, the performer's licensed embroidery. Juliet's pun confiscates the ornament and returns it as separation. A staging that pipes birdsong into the scene has to decide, before a word is weighed, whether the sound is lovely or harsh, and the text carefully declines to decide it: the only performance of the lark on record is the argument itself. The nightingale, the bird Juliet needs, never sings in any text. It is the score's most eloquent entry, a cue for a musician who does not exist.

### What does the dawn argument owe to the music treatises?

Nearly every term of the lovers' dawn argument comes from the period's music pedagogy: division for a melody broken into quick ornamental notes, sharps and discords for clashing pitches, tune itself for a bird's fitness to sing in measure. Morley's Plaine and Easie Introduction (1597) taught such words to amateur readers.

That a pair of teenagers in a bedroom can fence with the terms is the point. Shakespeare writes for an audience whose ears had been schooled by part-singing, by consort lessons, by the waits on civic duty, to hear concord and discord as social facts and not as abstractions. When Juliet convicts the lark of unpleasing sharps she is using the shared technical language of that audience the way the feud uses the shared language of honour: fluently, and to kill. The imagined sounds of the play are scored with the same precision. Mercutio's Queen Mab catalogue gives the dreaming soldier a sound effect at three removes from any stage: "and then anon drums in his ear, at which he starts and wakes" (1.4). A drum sounded by a production at that line would collapse the removes and turn a dream inside a speech inside a jest into a noise in the theatre. Juliet in the potion soliloquy imagines the vault's acoustics with a connoisseur's dread, fearing "shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth, / That living mortals, hearing them, run mad" (4.3). No direction ratifies either sound. The play's inner ear is better stocked than its band, and the catalogue keeps the classes apart precisely so that this richness is credited to the writing rather than smuggled into the directions.

## The Duel's Imaginary Fiddle: Consort as Insult in 3.1

The play's single most famous instrument is one that never exists. Tybalt, greeting Mercutio in the square, uses the period's ordinary verb for keeping company: "Mercutio, thou consort'st with Romeo" (3.1). Mercutio hears the other sense of the word, the musical one, and takes offence as a professional insult: "Consort? What, dost thou make us minstrels? An thou make minstrels of us, look to hear nothing but discords. Here's my fiddlestick; here's that shall make you dance. Zounds, consort!" The fiddlestick is a fiddle's bow, and the fiddle it belongs to is Mercutio's sword. In one gesture the duel is recast as a dance engagement, the weapon as an instrument, and Tybalt as a partner who will be made to move. Every element of the joke depends on the social fact the musicians' scene will later price: a minstrel is a hireling, a man paid to sound for other men's occasions, and to be classed with minstrels is a demotion that a gentleman answers with steel. Mercutio's answer is to accept the demotion and conduct it, promising a programme of discords with himself as the entire consort.

### Why does the text give the duel a fiddle that never appears?

Because the fiddle is the insult, not the equipment. Tybalt's verb consort demotes Mercutio to a hired minstrel; Mercutio accepts the demotion as a weapon, promising discords and a dance, and the sword in his hand becomes the bow of an instrument the stage never has to produce.

The street's other instrument makes the same point from the opposite side. The Folio, alone among the early texts, sends the masquers toward the feast to a spoken drumbeat, Benvolio's closing order "Strike, drum" (1.4), where the Second Quarto simply marches them about the stage. Martial percussion is the feud's native sound in the period's theatre, the noise that belongs to entries, alarms, and armed companies, and this play rations it to a single Folio line and a dream. The brawl that opens the tragedy is directed with swords and bucklers in the Folio's opening direction and with no sound cue at all; the duel in the square is fought, in every early text, unscored. How the blades themselves were built and rehearsed belongs to the fight's own record; the soundscape's contribution is simpler and more severe. Verona's violence happens in the open air of the afternoon stage, accompanied by nothing, because the feud has no band. Bands are for households at peace with their occasions. The Montagues and Capulets can hire music for a feast and a wedding; the street, where their real business is done, gets no musicians, only the memory of a drum inside a drunkard's dream of war.

## The Wedding-Morning Music: A Folio Direction and an Editor's Bracket

The last music the play ever directs is four words long in one text and absent in the other two, and its history is a parable of how scores accrete. The wedding morning in Capulet's house (4.4) is a scene of noise without music: cooks wanted, logs fetched, a bridegroom imminent, and over the bustle a single reported sound, the curfew bell that Capulet hears and reads like a clock: "The curfew bell hath rung, 'tis three o'clock". The bell is spoken, not directed; the fiction hears it, the playhouse is not told to ring it. Then Capulet announces the bridegroom's approach in the scene's famous promise: "The County will be here with music straight, / For so he said he would. I hear him near." Paris is observing the custom of the bridegroom's morning music, the hired band that fetches a bride to church with playing, a daylight serenade in reverse, and Capulet's ear is already enjoying it in anticipation.

In the Second Quarto, that is all: a promise and a claim to hear. The Folio upgrades the report into an event. Midway through Capulet's speech, after "good faith, 'tis day" and before the promise itself, the Folio prints the direction "Play Musicke" (4.4), instructing the house band to sound as Paris approaches. No such direction stands in Q2, and Q1's compressed version of the scene ends with Capulet's "The Countie will be heere with musicke straight. Gods me hees come", again without direction or summons. The modern editorial tradition then takes the Folio's four words and improves them out of recognition: editions commonly print a bracketed "[Music within]" after "I hear him near", relocating the cue to the moment of claimed hearing, renaming it in the period's stage vocabulary, and, in school editions that drop the brackets, graduating a Folio supplement into what looks like an instruction of the copy-text. Three centuries of readers have consequently studied a wedding-morning cue that exists in exactly one early document, in different words, at a different point in the speech. The cue is genuine evidence, but it is Folio evidence, and the catalogue's class system exists to keep that distinction visible.

The pathos of the moment needs no editorial help. Whichever text a company follows, the bridegroom's band plays, or is promised, for a wedding that the potion has already annulled. Paris approaches a house in which the bride lies in a counterfeit death, attended by the man who counterfeited it, and the musicians who cross the threshold with him walk into the one scene in the play where their trade will be formally stood down. The wedding music and the funeral refusal are a single engagement, and the play is careful to let the audience watch the contract signed in the morning and voided within the hour.

The bracket's little preposition deserves a sentence of its own, because "within" does interpretive work that "Play Musicke" does not. A direction to play music states an event; a direction for music within states a place, behind the doors of the house, in the street or the passage beyond the chamber, and it converts the sound into a countdown the audience can measure. Paris is approaching through architecture, nearer with every bar, and the household onstage hears the wedding arrive before it sees the bridegroom. The Folio's bare imperative knows nothing of that suspense, and the copy-text's silence knows even less. Three mornings once more: one in which music interrupts, one in which it approaches, and one in which the only sound of the bridegroom's arrival is a father's report of a sound that the text never lets its audience hear.

## The Musicians' Scene: The Band That Never Enters

The most substantial music scene in Shakespeare's tragedy begins with an entrance that no early text prints. When the Friar and Paris reach the chamber in 4.5, the Second Quarto directs only "Enter Frier and the Countie", and the Folio repeats the direction unchanged. The musicians who dominate the scene's last movement are never brought on. The Second Quarto establishes their presence by remainder and by label: its direction "Exeunt manet" (line 2632 of the quarto text) dismisses the mourners and keeps the band onstage, and its speech prefixes, "Musi." and, for the first of them, "Fid.", for Fidler, acknowledge the men the entrance forgot. Modern editions repair the omission with a supplied entrance, "Enter Friar Laurence and Paris, with Musicians", a reasonable repair that becomes unreasonable only when it is read as the text's own stagecraft. The play's boldest musical stroke is entrusted to men the documents never introduce.

The fiction's economy implies one band, not two. The household that engaged musicians for the feast engages musicians for the wedding; the First Musician's opening line assumes a routine engagement ending in the usual disappointment: "Faith, we may put up our pipes and be gone". The pipes are the text's one concrete word for the band's equipment in this scene, and its vagueness is diagnostic: the word can denote wind instruments outright, or stand loosely for a player's gear of whatever family, and editors divide on how hard to press it. Either way the gesture is the meaning. Putting up the pipes is the visible form of a cancelled contract, the instruments returned to their cases while the Nurse, who has found the body, begs them through the formality of dismissal: "Honest good fellows, ah, put up, put up! / For, well you know, this is a pitiful case". The case pun runs through the exchange and lands on the First Musician's reply, "Ay, by my troth, the case may be amended": a case is an instrument's box, a circumstance, and a thing that can be mended, and the tradesman's calm treats the catastrophe as all three. The patterned lamentations that precede the dismissal, grief performed in formal quatrains by the family, are the subject of [the mourning of the 'dead' Juliet in Act 4 Scene 5](/2023/11/17/act-4-scene-5-mourning-juliet/); the band's prose begins where the family's verse ends, and the register shift is the sound of hired feeling replacing performed feeling.

Then Peter arrives, and the play stages its only request programme. "Musicians, O musicians, 'Heart's ease', 'Heart's ease'! O, and you will have me live, play 'Heart's ease'". The request is a pun before it is a programme: his heart is full, he explains, playing on the name of a second tune, "My heart is full", so a tune called ease is the prescription. "Heart's Ease" was a genuine dance tune of the period, lively country-dance music that print preserved a generation later in Playford's Dancing Master (1651); Ross W. Duffin's Shakespeare's Songbook (2004) built a method on such survivals, recovering the actual melodies that the plays name, quote, or invoke, and on Duffin's evidence the first audiences could recognise Peter's request as a real tune with a real tempo, the merriest item on the band's book. The counter-tune has not been recovered: no melody answering to "My heart is full" has been securely identified, and the pair stand in the record as the scene in miniature, one tune recoverable, one lost, both refused. For the refusal is the point. Peter escalates to the request that gives the scene its emblem, "O play me some merry dump to comfort me", a dump being the period's slow instrumental lament, so that a merry one is a contradiction in terms, and the band answers with the tragedy's plainest sentence about music: "Not a dump we, 'tis no time to play now".

### Why does Peter end up singing the scene's only song himself?

Because the professionals refuse, and Peter's appetite for music outruns his standing to command it. Denied a dump by the band, he reaches for the one instrument nobody can stand down, his own voice, and performs the healing song as a quiz, turning consolation into a tradesman's riddle about pay.

The song Peter performs is the scene's buried treasure, and its textual history is as damaged as its performance is lonely. The lines are the opening of Richard Edwards's lyric in praise of music, printed in The Paradise of Dainty Devices (1576), the anthology Edwards largely wrote: "When griping grief the heart doth wound, / And doleful dumps the mind oppress, / Then music with her silver sound / With speedy help doth lend redress". The First Quarto preserves the quatrain entire. The Second Quarto and the Folio, between them, lose the second line, the dumps line, by the kind of eye-skip that jumps from one like ending to another, and modern texts silently restore the missing words from Q1. Consider what the damage means. The one song the play performs is a song about music's power to cure grief, sung by a servant, unaccompanied, over a body the singer believes dead, in a text that cannot even transmit the song whole. Peter then converts the performance into a seminar, demanding to know why the song calls music's sound silver, and the three answers the band gives are the play's compressed philosophy of its own score. Silver has a sweet sound, says the First Musician, the aesthete's answer. Musicians sound for silver, says the Second, the economist's answer, and the one Peter ratifies by asking the Third. The Third, the singer, has no answer, and Peter supplies it for him in the song's own coin: musicians have no gold for sounding. The healing art of Edwards's lyric turns out, in the mouths of its practitioners, to be a wage relation. Music gives redress, the scene concludes, where grief is an occasion with a budget; before a corpse, the band keeps its counsel and its cases shut.

Even the band's personnel will not sit still in the documents. Three musicians are certain; their famous names are an editorial assembly. The Folio's Peter addresses all three in the form modern readers know: "what say you, Simon Catling?", "what say you, Hugh Rebeck?", "what say you, James Soundpost?" The Second Quarto names only Rebeck and Soundpost in dialogue, putting the silver question to its First Musician without a name. The First Quarto names a different set again, Simon Soundpost and Matthew Minikin, and gives its third man no name at all. The names are the trade turned into dramatis personae: a catling is the gut of which fiddle strings are made, a rebec is the old three-stringed fiddle that Edward W. Naylor's Shakespeare and Music (1906) glosses as the ancient English fiddle, a soundpost is the small pillar inside a violin's body that carries the sound between belly and back, and a minikin is a fine small string. The joke requires a band of string players, which is the closest the text ever comes to specifying the consort's makeup: not a direction, not a summons, but a set of puns that only work if the men are fiddlers. Catling's string and Rebeck's three strings answer the ball's open question from the far side of the play. The band that played for the dancing was, on the only internal evidence available, a string band, and its leader's name exists only because the Folio's compositors worked from a text that had kept it.

Over the whole scene stands the speech in which the play writes its own sound design and then cancels it. Capulet, finding the wedding preparations converted, pronounces the conversion in the vocabulary of the score: "All things that we ordained festival / Turn from their office to black funeral: / Our instruments to melancholy bells, / Our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast, / Our solemn hymns to sullen dirges change" (4.5). No bell is directed, no dirge is sung; the funeral's music exists entirely inside this sentence, which is the play's method in its purest form. A few speeches earlier the same voice has married his daughter to the wrong bridegroom in the figure examined by [death as bridegroom, marriage and mortality fused](/2031/05/30/death-as-bridegroom-imagery/), and the two figures belong together: the bridegroom who replaces Paris brings no band with him, and the instruments that would have played the wedding are translated, in a father's rhetoric, into the bells of a burial that the stage never sounds. The musicians' scene, so often cut as comic relief, is the tragedy's thesis performed by tradesmen: feeling in this world is hired, occasion-bound, and terminable, and the one art that promises redress for grief declines, professionally and correctly, to practise at a deathbed.

## Directed, Spoken, Supplied: How the Centuries Built the Modern Score

The three evidence classes are not a pedant's filing system; they are the mechanism by which the play acquired the sound posterity attributes to it, and the mechanism started early. The Folio itself is the first supplier. Whoever prepared the copy behind the 1623 text, working from the later quartos, bequeathed the play three cues the Second Quarto lacks, the drum, the wedding-morning music, and the whistle, and the additions share a character: each is small, functional, and tied to a moment where the dialogue already implies a sound. Benvolio's drum makes audible the march Q2 stages in silence. "Play Musicke" cashes the cheque Capulet's promise writes. "Whistle Boy" simply notates the signal Paris has just described. The Folio's supplements behave like a playhouse annotating its own practice, writing in the margin the sounds a performance had found it needed. That is the strongest argument for treating them as evidence about early staging, and it is also the warning label: from 1623 onward, the text of this play's music is a sediment, and every reader reads the sediment as if it were the bed.

The eighteenth-century stage continued the deposition at industrial scale. Acting editions normalized the musicians' entrance, regularized the wedding music into a processional, and, most famously, gave Juliet a funeral the text never stages, complete with a composed dirge sung over her procession to the tomb, an interpolation associated with Garrick's Drury Lane version and examined in the record of [the music later centuries composed for the play in performance](/2028/02/25/music-in-performance-history/). The dirge is the supplied score's masterpiece and its clearest confession. Faced with a fourth act that ends in prose haggling and a fifth that admits no instrument but a whistle, the Georgian theatre composed the missing lament and inserted it bodily into the action. Audiences wept at music Shakespeare's company never played, for a procession Shakespeare never wrote, and the interpolation's success fixed the expectation that the tragedy ought to sound like that everywhere. The nineteenth century's resident composers and the twentieth century's film scorers inherited the expectation as a duty.

Against that history, the editorial bracket deserves respect as an honest instrument. When a modern text prints "[Music within]" or a bracketed entrance for the musicians, the brackets confess the supplement; the reader is told that an editor, not a document, is speaking. The dishonesty enters only when the brackets fall away, in reprints, school abridgements, and production scripts that strip the marks for cleanliness, at which point a Folio direction of 1623, itself already a supplement to the copy-text of 1599, circulates as the author's instruction. The catalogue's remedy is not purism. A company may play music at every joint of the evening and defend the choice as interpretation; Sternfeld's structural standard even invites the defence, since music placed by design is music doing dramatic work. The remedy is labelling. Directed, spoken, and supplied cues support different claims, and criticism that quotes a supplied direction as the play's own voice has mistaken the sediment for the bed.

René Weis's Arden Third Series edition (2012), the modern text whose copy-text is the Second Quarto, is instructive here precisely because its base document is the quiet one. A reader who meets the play in Weis's pages meets Q2's economy with the Folio's supplements admitted and marked through the apparatus, and the collation becomes a map of the accretion: one direction native to the chosen text, three immigrants naturalized by the Folio, and a penumbra of editorial stage business that the notes, honestly kept, distinguish from both. The lesson generalizes beyond any single edition. The play's score cannot be quoted; it can only be collated.

## Recovering the Tunes: The Songbook Method and Its Limits

If the directions are a sediment, the tunes are a quarry, and the most ambitious attempt to work it is Ross W. Duffin's Shakespeare's Songbook (2004), the product of eight years in the Folger Shakespeare Library's collections, which assembled 155 songs that the plays perform, quote, or allude to, recovering period lyrics and melodies and issuing the results with recorded performances. The Songbook's premise is that a named tune in a Shakespeare text is a citation, not a decoration: the audience of the 1590s knew these melodies the way a later audience knows hymn tunes, and the recognition did dramatic work that a silent page cannot reproduce. For this play the premise pays out at exactly one counter, the musicians' scene, where Peter's requests are citations. "Heart's Ease" survives, a country dance for two couples preserved in manuscript collections and printed by Playford in The English Dancing Master from 1651 onward, a tune with set figures and a set tempo. A band asked for "Heart's Ease" in a mourning chamber is being asked for choreography, for the set dance of the ballroom, and the obscenity of the request is measurable in beats per minute. No silent reading recovers that obscenity; the tune does.

The method's limits are written into the same scene. Peter's gloss tune, "My heart is full", has resisted recovery; Edwards's song survives as words in an anthology whose settings, where they existed, belonged to a court culture a generation older than the play; and the ball, the one place where the directed score certainly sounded, names nothing at all. Recovery can tell a company what the refused music would have been. It cannot tell the company what the played music was, because the played music is the one the text leaves anonymous, a function hired by the night. David Lindley's caution in Shakespeare and Music is the necessary counterweight: the meanings of these cues are carried less by their notes than by the period's codes of concord and discord, occasion and office, and a production that recovers every tune and misses the codes has restored the sound and lost the sense. The adjudication is a division of labour. Duffin is right about the citations, and the citations are in the refusal scene. Lindley is right about the system, and the system is the play. A score restored on both principles would play "Heart's Ease" in rehearsal until the company could hear exactly what the band declines to perform, and then decline with them.

There is a further asymmetry worth naming. The recoverable music in this play is all, without exception, music attached to words or dances in the spoken column: a named dance tune, a quoted song, a bridegroom's promised band. The directed column's single native cue, the ball, is the one event whose sound is unrecoverable in principle, since the direction specifies an activity and not a repertoire. The play's loudest moment is its least documented, and its best documented sounds are the ones that never happen. That inversion is not an accident of survival. It is the shape of a score designed around occasions: the occasions that proceed leave no trace but a direction, and the occasion that collapses leaves a transcript.

The dance's geometry sharpens the obscenity to a measurement. "Heart's Ease", in the form the dance collections preserve, is a dance for four, two couples working set figures in a small square of floor, a social machine with no spare part and no spectator's place inside it. Requested in a bedroom over a bier, the tune does not merely name the wrong mood; it specifies a choreography that would have to be danced around the corpse, in the room where the corpse's clothes are being laid out, by couples the room does not contain. Peter cannot know the figures, and the band, which does, hears the full specification in the title alone. That is what a named tune buys a reading: not colour, but dimensions. The refusal answers a request whose exact social shape the documents let a later age reconstruct, and the reconstruction convicts the request more precisely than any paraphrase of its inappropriateness could.

## The Band in the Playhouse: Hired Men, Sharers, and the Sound Between the Acts

Behind the fiction's band stands the playhouse's, and the documents let the two be compared without being confused. The tragedy was written for the public playhouses of the middle 1590s, big open-air rooms where a company of a dozen or so sharers, hired men, and apprentices played a changing repertory six afternoons a week. Music in such a house was labour before it was art. Trumpets gathered the audience and marked the great, drums served the wars of the history plays, and a small consort covered dances, songs, and the intervals of the afternoon. The men who made those sounds were a mixed economy. Some were hired specialists, the theatrical equivalent of the waits, engaged by the week or the season; some were the company's own, actor-musicians whose instruments appear in the period's wills and inventories because instruments were capital. Augustine Phillips's bass viol, bequeathed in 1605 to a former apprentice, is the famous residue of that economy: a sharer owned a valuable instrument, played it or lent it within the fellowship, and left it like a share, to a successor. The viol proves that serious instrumental capacity lived inside the Chamberlain's Men. It does not prove that a six-part broken consort played at the Capulet ball in 1595, and the distance between those two propositions is exactly the distance Lindley polices.

The children's companies throw the adult economy into relief. The choirboy troupes of St Paul's and the Chapel Royal, commercial rivals of the adult houses by the turn of the century, were musical institutions by constitution: their boys were trained singers and consort players before they were actors, and their repertory could assume instrumental scenes the adult stage had to hire in. Shakespeare's company wrote against that competition with a playwright's economy, concentrating its score where hired labour was already on the payroll. A wedding band is, in production terms, a group of walk-on specialists with one scene of prose and no lines of verse to learn; a ball consort can play from a gallery and never act at all. The tragedy's music is designed, whether by intention or by the discipline of the wage bill, to be performable by the cheapest configuration that satisfies it: a handful of hired string players, a drum when the Folio asks, a boy who can whistle.

The intervals deserve a sentence of their own, because they are where later institutional music most thoroughly rewrote the audience's memory of evenings like this one. At the indoor Blackfriars, which the King's Men occupied from 1608, a resident consort played between the acts, and act music became part of the commodity a fashionable audience bought. The public-stage original of the 1590s knew no such frame: acts ran on, and the only music inside the fiction was the fiction's. Productions that fill the tragedy's joints with consort music are therefore not restoring an original practice but importing a later house style, legitimate as interpretation, false as archaeology. Gurr's distinction between the amphitheatre and the Blackfriars consort survives the revisionist critique in this narrowed form: whatever the amphitheatre band's size, the play's own text is the score of the amphitheatre years, and it asks for almost nothing between its scenes.

The wage relation, finally, is not a metaphor the musicians' scene applies to music; it is the scene's literal subject, and it reaches back through the whole design. Verona's music is bought by the occasion, at the ball, at the wedding, and it stops when the occasion is void. The lovers, whose tragedy is precisely that no occasion will hold them, are the only principals in the play who never hire, command, or refuse music. Romeo hears a tongue as music and a lark as an enemy; Juliet weaponizes the treatise's terms and imagines shrieks in a vault. Neither of them ever has a band. The score's economy and the tragedy's economy are the same: music belongs to households, households belong to the feud, and the children of the feud die in the one place where the household's money cannot reach, a tomb kept by a friar, a page, and a whistle.

## An Organology in Speech: The Instruments Named and the Ones Refused

Catalogued by name rather than by moment, the play's instruments form a map with a politics. At the top of the period's hierarchy sits the lute, the gentleman-amateur's prestige instrument, the tool of courtiers and of the serenade convention, and the text will not touch it. The word never names the instrument anywhere in the play; its only appearance in the Folio's spelling is inside another word entirely, the Friar's "saluteth". The balcony scene is the refusal staged: the stock image of the period lover is a man with a lute beneath a window, singing upward, and Shakespeare's lover climbs instead, speaks in sonnets, and listens. Romeo courts with rhetoric, not with strings, and the woman's answering music is her voice unaccompanied. Beside the lute stands the trumpet, the instrument of princes, war, and ceremony, and the play names it once, in Juliet's mouth, as an apocalypse: "Then dreadful trumpet sound the general doom, / For who is living, if those two are gone?" (3.2). A trumpet exists in Verona only as the end of the world. No character in the tragedy is ever announced by one, no army marches to one, no prince is flourished in with one; the instrument of public honour is reserved for the Last Judgement, which is Juliet's way of saying that public honour has nothing left to announce.

The instruments the play does name live at the other end of the scale, in the hands of hirelings, servants, and boys. The string band of 4.5 is specified, as the musicians' scene showed, only by its puns: catling gut for the strings, the rebec's three-stringed body, the soundpost inside the violin's belly, the minikin's fine gut in the First Quarto's variant set. To these the duel adds the fiddlestick, a bow with no fiddle, and the First Musician adds the pipes, the text's single wind word, left generic between a family of instruments and a tradesman's whole kit. Percussion is rationed to the drum, and the drum to the margins of consciousness: dreamed by Mercutio's soldier, commanded only in the Folio as the masquers march. The play's loudest named family is the bells, and even they exist at two removes: the curfew bell is reported as rung, heard by Capulet as a clock, and the melancholy bells of the funeral exist only inside Capulet's metaphor for what the wedding instruments have become. Add the page's whistle, a signal with no instrument at all beyond breath and fingers, and the organology is complete: strings for hirelings, a drum for dreams, bells for metaphors, a trumpet for doomsday, and silence for the lute.

One trap in the record deserves a warning of its own. The Second Quarto's text of the potion scene gives Juliet the line "Come Violl, what if this mixture do not worke at all?" (4.3), and a careless catalogue could enter a viol among the play's instruments on the strength of the spelling. The word is the vial, the Friar's little bottle of distilled liquor, which modern editions spell as the vessel it is. The quarto's only viol is a poison bottle, which feels, in this play, less like an accident than like a summary: the instrument-shaped thing that actually governs the tragedy is a container of silence, carried by a friar, timed to a wedding, and set down beside a girl who is about to become the still centre of the loudest scene of mourning in the canon, a scene in which she makes no sound at all.

## The Clock of Sound: Cockcrow, Curfew Bell, and the Lark

Verona keeps time by ear, and the play's chronology can be read off its sounds. The wedding day, the tragedy's fatal Thursday that the calendar articles of this series have worried from every side, opens with two public clocks in two lines: "Come, stir, stir, stir, the second cock hath crowed, / The curfew bell hath rung, 'tis three o'clock" (4.4). A farmyard sound and a civic sound, the cock and the bell, agree that the day has begun, and the agreement is the last public harmony the play permits. The morning music Paris brings is the third term of the sequence, sound as schedule: the band's arrival is the appointment made audible, the hour at which a bride is fetched. Against these public clocks the lovers keep a private time that is also told in sound, and lost in sound. The dawn parting in 3.5 is an attempt to secede from the audible calendar by reclassifying its evidence: if the bird was the nightingale, the night continues; if the lark, the day has jurisdiction. Juliet loses the argument because sound in this play is public property, evidence any listener can audit, and a private renaming cannot stand against a bird the whole city hears. Her counter-claims are brilliant and doomed, the treatise terms deployed as a stay of execution, and the lark sings the sentence anyway.

The ball runs on the same economy. A measure is a unit of dance and a unit of time at once, and Romeo's approach to Juliet is timed by it to the line, "the measure done", as precisely as Capulet's morning is timed by the bell. Feast, wedding, dawn, curfew: the social world of the play is a timetable of sounds, each announcing an occasion and each enforceable because everyone within earshot hears the same signal. The tragedy's design can be stated as an attack on that timetable. The lovers marry between signals, in a cell, at an hour no bell announces. Juliet's death is counterfeited to a schedule the Friar computes in hours, forty-two of them, and the computation fails by the width of a quarantined messenger. The tomb scene, where the schedules collide, is staged in a soundscape of whispers, a whistle, and a watchman's questions, the public clock reduced to a boy's signal and a patrol. What the Prince finally restores is not music but narrative, an ordered account of events, spoken in the churchyard at dawn to a crowd that has been summoned by outcry. The city's last public sound is its ruler's voice, unaccompanied, doing the work the bells and bands were hired for and could not do: telling Verona what time it is in its own history.

## Peter's Seminar: Silver Sound and the Medicine of Music

The song Peter quotes was already old music when the play was new, and its age is part of its meaning. Richard Edwards, master of the children of the Chapel Royal under Elizabeth, had written the period's most quotable defence of his art, and the anthology that preserved it, The Paradise of Dainty Devices (1576), prints the claims Peter's scene puts on trial. Music, in Edwards's argument, is medicine: "Of troubled mind for every sore, sweet Music hath a salve therefore"; "in grief it cheers our heavy sprights"; the careful head finds release in its delights. The poem's refrain, the part Peter sings, promises redress with speedy help for the heart that griping grief has wounded. Peter's ailment is specified in the same vocabulary: his heart is full, playing on itself, a pressure of feeling that the period's medicine would treat with exactly the prescription the poem advertises. His request programme is self-medication by a servant who knows the pharmacopoeia and cannot afford the pharmacist.

What follows is a disputation in form, a little university exercise smuggled into a death chamber. A question is proposed in due form, "Why silver sound? Why music with her silver sound?", and put to three respondents, who answer in descending order of adequacy. The First Musician answers from essence: silver has a sweet sound, the metallurgy of tone. The Second answers from the market: musicians sound for silver, the metallurgy of wages. The Third, identified by Peter as the singer, the one whose instrument is the unmediated voice, answers nothing, and Peter, delighted, answers for him with the market's own corollary: musicians have no gold for sounding. The seminar's conclusion is economic, and the scene awards it the last word, but the disputation form cuts both ways. A quaestio does not refute the poem it examines; it prices the poem's claim. Edwards says music gives redress. The band replies, in effect, that redress is a service, services are rendered on occasions, and a corpse is the one client no occasion covers. Both propositions stand at the scene's end, and the tragedy of the next act is built in the space between them.

One listener in the theatre holds the fact that resolves the seminar, and it is not on the stage. The house knows that the corpse is a counterfeit, that the patient over whom the medicine is refused is a girl in a timed sleep whose cure is already scheduled, and that the redress Edwards promises is on its way by plot rather than by performance, in a friar's letter and a friar's timetable. The band's refusal is professionally correct and factually unnecessary; Peter's faith in the song is foolish and, in the event, justified, since help with speed is precisely what the plot is hurrying to deliver. The scene's laughter lives in that double knowledge, and so does its chill, because the letter fails, the timetable slips, and the medicine that finally reaches the tomb is administered by no musician: a cup, a dagger, and a silence in which the only sound left is a watchman asking which way to go. Edwards's poem survives the play that quoted it, drops its second line in transmission, and keeps its promise nowhere inside the fiction. The catalogue files the song where the evidence puts it, among the spoken cues, performed once, by an amateur, over a bier, as the play's whole philosophy of music in a single stanza: the art heals, the art is hired, and the hiring is the tragedy.



## The Musicians' Trade: Waits, Watchmen, and the Hired Band

The men Peter argues with have a real profession behind them, and its records explain the scene's economics better than any theory. England's town waits began as watchmen, posted on towers and walls to pipe the hours and raise the alarm, and by the sixteenth century they had become liveried civic musicians, maintained at municipal expense in towns across the kingdom, wearing their city's chains of office and playing a calendar of public duties: ceremonies, processions, the entertainment of distinguished visitors, morning music to wake the citizens, warnings of fire. The stipend bought the city's sound; the men sold the surplus. Waits supplemented their wages with private engagements, at weddings, feasts, and gentry entertainments, and the profile fits Capulet's band to the life: professionals of the civic establishment, hired by a great household for the morning of a daughter's wedding, equipped, punctual, and gone when the occasion closes. Morley's Consort Lessons, dedicated to the Lord Mayor and aldermen of London, addresses precisely this world, a civic band with pretensions, and Mary Chan's judgement that the broken consort suited dance music and popular song describes the waits' working repertoire as much as any court's.

The play's geography is Italian and its labour market is English, an anachronism the text makes no effort to hide, since a London audience measured Verona's band against the bands it hired for its own weddings. Italian cities kept their own civic wind players, the pifferi, and a historically fastidious Verona would sound its weddings with shawms; the play, characteristically, names no instrument at all for the wedding morning, leaving the band's constitution to whatever company was playing the tragedy that afternoon. What the text does specify is the contract's shape, and the shape is the waits' shape: arrival by appointment, performance as a function of the occasion, withdrawal when the occasion is cancelled, and a wage logic that Peter states as proverb. The musicians' refusal, read against the trade, is not a slur on the profession but a mark of its discipline. Civic music exists to notify: a wedding announced, a visitor greeted, a fire reported. The corpse in Juliet's chamber is an event the city has not yet been told about, misreported by the household itself as a death, and the band stands down because the notification it serves has been countermanded by the only authority it recognizes, the master of the house. Verona's sound fails at the exact point where Verona's information fails, and the play's information network, letters, friars, quarantine, is the tragedy's famous machinery of error. The band is one more messenger in that machinery, and its message is silence.

Against the waits' civic competence, the lovers' household amateur stands exposed, and the exposure is the scene's mercy. Peter is a servant with a quotation, a man whose musical culture is an anthology poem half-remembered and a country dance he cannot play, reduced to begging the professionals for the medicine his book recommends. The period's stages were full of such figures, the amateur at the counter of the trade, and the scene's comedy depends on the audience siding, against its sympathies, with the trade. The band is right. The occasion is dead. And the play, which has hired its music by the same contract all along, one direction for the ball, a promise for the wedding, lets the contract lapse in prose, in daylight, in a chamber above a silent street, which is to say it lets the audience hear what a city sounds like when its occasions stop.

One complication in the trade's record cuts against the article's string-band inference, and honesty requires it on the page. The waits began as pipers, watchmen whose office was a wind instrument on a tower, and the civic band's historic colour was wind-led long after its duties widened into consort playing; by the seventeenth century many waits were accomplished musicians across families, but the office's sound in the public ear remained the shawm band's outdoor blaze. A London audience hiring its mental waits for Capulet's feast may therefore have imagined more wind than the musicians' scene's string puns suggest. The discrepancy settles nothing and usefully unsettles everything: the fiction's band is imagined in the vocabulary of 4.5's jokes, the city's band was known by its pipes and shawms, and the playhouse's band was a third thing again. Three bands, three constitutions, one direction that names none of them, which is the consort question restated at the level of the trade.

## The Geography of Sound: Hall, Chamber, and Hollow Ground

The play's music has addresses, and the addresses descend. The ball's sound fills a hall, the largest interior the fiction owns, music as architecture's content, a room made festive by a band placed somewhere in it, gallery, screen's end, or floor, the documents declining to say, as the ball-staging article of this series has the record. The wedding morning's music is smaller and later: a sound "within", behind the chamber doors, announced as approaching and then, in the copy-text, never heard at all, music contracted to the threshold between the street and the bridal room. The musicians' scene relocates sound to a parlour's aftermath, where nothing plays and the loudest events are a case opening proposed and declined, pipes that stay in their bag. Act 5 completes the descent outdoors and underground. The tomb's soundscape is subterranean by design: Paris posts his page flat against the earth with an ear to the hollow ground, an acoustic sentry ordered to detect footsteps through the soil itself, the churchyard's earth used as a conductor of approach. The whistle that follows is the play's last music because it is the only kind the place admits, a signal through air from a boy lying in dirt, carrying one bit of information across a graveyard: something comes.

The street completes the map with the sound the play began with, public noise. The first scene opens on a brawl the citizens join with clubs; the last opens its revelation to a city roused by outcry, the Watch's questions, the bell of gathering that the text leaves to report rather than direction, and a prince woken before dawn by the "search" of his people's shouts. Between the hall and the churchyard, the fiction's sounds shrink from consort to whistle to voice, and the shrinkage is the tragedy's acoustic signature. Productions that swell the final scenes with strings are not wrong about how the ending feels; they are wrong about how the ending is built. It is built the way the whole score is built, by subtraction, until a boy's whistle and an old man's narrative are the only instruments left in Verona, and the play's most famous music is the sort a city makes when it talks.


## The Song Nobody Sings: The Withheld Voice at the Play's Centre

Measured against its author's own practice, the tragedy's strangest musical fact is a silence at its centre: nobody in Romeo and Juliet ever sings a song. The canon around the play is full of singing. Twelfth Night opens with a duke demanding music as the food of love and keeps its fool in song to the final exit. Othello gives its heroine a song at the edge of death, the Willow Song, sung while she undresses for the bed she will die in. Hamlet lets madness loose in snatches of ballad. The Tempest is scored with spirit music from its first act. Against that company, the Verona tragedy withholds the device entirely. Its heroine, the most voice-conscious figure in Shakespeare, a girl whose lover hears music in her tongue and whose own speeches run on birdsong, division, and discord, is given no song to sing. She argues about a lark; she does not imitate one. Her music is all metaphor, all analysis, all talk, and the talk is dazzling, but the distinction holds to the end: Juliet dies with a dagger and a sentence about a sheath, in a tomb where no one has sung.

Romeo fares the same. The period's stage lover serenades, in comedy after comedy and in the ballad tradition behind them, and this lover woos in sonnet form, overheard, unaccompanied, the music of his courtship a figure of speech he shares with the woman answering him. Their shared sonnet at the ball, the fourteen lines the lovers trade at their meeting, is the closest the play comes to a duet, and it is worth hearing it as one: two voices taking alternate lines on a set form, in public, inside a dance neither of them is dancing, while the consort plays on for other couples. The play's love music is that sonnet, dialogue fitted to a strict musical form and performed at conversational pitch. Everything else the tradition supplies, the serenade, the heroine's song, the dirge, belongs to other plays or to later centuries.

The one performed song in the tragedy is therefore load-bearing far beyond its two lines. Peter's stanza, sung by a servant over a counterfeit corpse, in a chamber the professionals have just vacated, is the play's sole act of singing, and the text arranges for it to be amateur, partial, and misattributed by its own singer to a purpose the band has refused. A canon-wide habit would call this a gap. The score's economy calls it the design. Song in Shakespeare is what characters do when feeling exceeds speech and the occasion permits the excess; this play's occasions permit nothing, its lovers feel everything in speech because speech is the only medium their world leaves uncontracted, and its single song falls to the one character with nothing to lose by singing, a servant whose mistress is dead, whose master is raving, and whose anthology is thirty years old. The tradition that later drenched the tragedy in song was not correcting an oversight. It was declining a refusal.

A corollary follows for the argument about evidence. Because the play performs so little, every performance tradition that adds music is making a large proportional intervention: one added serenade doubles the sung content of the text; one funeral dirge supplies the entire category of lament. The catalogue's classes keep the proportions visible. A production may add what it likes, and the greatest Romeo and Juliets of the stage and screen mostly have, but the proportions are the criticism. The text's own score is a hall of hired strings heard once, a band refused once, a servant's stanza, and a boy's whistle in a graveyard, and the fame of the play's music is the fame of everything that has been composed to fill the silence that score was designed to leave.


## Four Forks in the Score: A Director's Audit of the Directed Cues

Because the directed score is so small, each of its four cues is a fork at which a production declares its textual allegiance, and the four forks repay an audit in sequence. The ball cue is the only one every text-based company must face, since the Second Quarto and the Folio agree on it while differing on its grammar. Its placement is the problem and the gift. The direction fires inside Capulet's welcome speech, between the summons to the musicians and the cry for room, which means the dancing begins while the host is still talking, the floor filling behind his courtesies. A company that delays the music until the speech ends has tidy theatre and a modernized cue; a company that obeys the placement gets the scene's true dynamic, an old man's hospitality overtaken by the event it hosts, the young already moving as he calls for space. Winerock's note on the cue's position is the production's best friend here: the direction is not late or early but simultaneous, the documentary trace of a stage on which speech and dance shared the same seconds.

The drum fork is optional, and the option is diagnostic. Adopting the Folio's "Strike, drum" gives the masquers' exit from the street a martial pulse and plants the tragedy's war instrument inside its most festive movement, an irony the Folio's annotator may well have intended. Declining it keeps the First Quarto and Second Quarto's quieter threshold, torches and talk, the danger carried by Mercutio's dream speech instead of by percussion. Neither choice is neutral, and the catalogue's point is that the choice exists at all: a sound audiences register as Shakespearean in this play is a Folio supplement, available to be declined by any company that knows its documents.

The wedding-morning fork is the subtlest, because the Folio's "Play Musicke" sits inside Capulet's bustle, before the promise is even reported, so that obeying it to the letter puts Paris's band in the audience's ears while the household is still shouting for cords and logs, music arriving into chaos and imposing the schedule the scene is about. Modern editions that move the cue to "[Music within]" after "I hear him near" make the sound confirm the report, tidier and weaker: the Folio's placement has the music precede its announcement, the city's timetable asserting itself over the household's noise, while the copy-text's silence has the timetable fail to sound at all, a held breath before the discovery. Three documents, three mornings: one silent, one interrupted by music, one confirmed by it. A company chooses its Capulet, and its Friar's chances, at this fork.

The whistle fork closes the set. The Second Quarto leaves the signal to be inferred from Paris's acknowledgment, which permits a production to keep the tomb genuinely quiet and let the audience learn of the approach from Paris's startled line. The Folio notates the sound, and a whistle in a dark theatre is an event out of all proportion to its size: every head turns, the audience becomes the sentry, and Romeo's approach is announced to the house a full beat before it is announced to Paris. Companies that cut the Folio's cue are not simplifying; they are choosing the Quarto's epistemology, in which the audience knows only what the characters report. The four forks share a moral the catalogue was built to carry. Where the text directs almost nothing, each direction is a decision about what kind of knowledge the evening runs on, report or sensation, inference or event, and the play's famous sound begins, in every generation, as a set of choices among documents.


A fifth fork stands inside the scene the directions never reach, and it belongs in the audit because every company must decide it nightly: where are the musicians when the chamber door opens in 4.5? The early texts print no entrance for them, which leaves three honest stagings. The band may be discovered within, left over from the morning's duty and trapped by the discovery, an option the Second Quarto's "Exeunt manet" quietly supports, since a remaining implies a presence that preceded the exit of the principals. The band may enter with the Friar and Paris in the modern manner, freshly arrived for a wedding that has become a funeral, which sharpens the irony and spends the text's silence. Or the band may never be embodied at all in productions that cut the scene, a fork the catalogue records without honouring, since cutting deletes the play's philosophy of music along with its comedy. The three stagings distribute sympathy differently among the hired men, but they share the obligation the documents impose: whichever is chosen, it is chosen, and no edition's brackets may be allowed to pretend the play chose instead.

## The Boundary of the Catalogue: Knocks, Calls, and Sounds That Are Not Music

A catalogue of music owes its readers an account of its border, because the play is not a quiet text that occasionally sings; it is a noisy text that almost never does. Verona's acoustic system runs on signals. Doors knock, and the knocking is directed: the Folio prints "Enter Nurse, and knockes" at the Friar's cell in 3.3, where the sound, repeated in the dialogue until the Friar's composure cracks ("Hark how they knock"), forces Romeo from the floor and admits the message that carries Juliet's ring and the night's assignation. The Watch patrols the last act with calls and questions; the citizens answer a brawl in the first scene with clubs and shouting; Paris's page is posted by voice and replies by whistle. Alarms, knocks, cries, reports: the city communicates by unpitched sound at every joint of the plot, and the plot's hinges, an entrance at a cell door, a patrol at a tomb, a prince roused before dawn, turn on sounds that no one would call music.

The border matters because it explains the score's meaning by contrast. Signals in this play are free. Any servant can knock, any boy can whistle, any watchman can call, because a signal costs nothing but breath and belongs to no occasion; it interrupts, summons, warns. Music is the opposite in every property: pitched, organized, hired, and bound to the calendar of feasts and contracts. The tragedy distributes the two systems with total consistency. The lovers' plot advances by signals, knocks at cells, messages by servants, a whistle among graves, because the lovers live outside the occasion economy and must communicate in the free medium. The households' world advances by music, at the ball and the wedding, because households live inside that economy and announce themselves in the expensive one. When the systems cross, the crossing is the catastrophe in miniature: a hired band asked to play inside a signal situation, a chamber of emergency, refuses, because music cannot do a signal's work, and a servant sings, because a voice is the one instrument that belongs to both systems at once.

The catalogue therefore stops where it stops on principle, not from oversight. Knocks and calls belong to the play's sound design, and the fight articles of this series own their noisiest neighbourhood, but they are not cues for music, and counting them as such would dissolve the very scarcity that makes the music cues speak. A text in which everything sounded were music would have no refusal scene. This text has one, because its music is rationed, priced, and placed, and its signals are not. The whistle at the tomb sits exactly on the border, a breath of pitched sound made by no instrument, carrying a signal's whole message, which is why the catalogue admits it as the score's final, smallest member and why the Folio, alone among the early texts, thought it worth a direction.


## The Silence at the Tomb: What the Score Withholds at the End

The last act is the catalogue's emptiest page, and the emptiness is engineered. Act 5 admits exactly one musical event, if a whistle counts as music: Paris's page, posted among the graves with instructions to keep his ear to the hollow ground, gives the agreed signal as Romeo approaches. The Second Quarto carries the cue entirely in dialogue, Paris's order beforehand and Paris's acknowledgment, "The boy gives warning, something doth approach", after the fact. The Folio prints the sound itself as a direction, "Whistle Boy", the smallest item in the play's directed score and the only one addressed to a listener rather than a room. Everything else in the act is speech, steel, and report: a fight the text leaves unscored, a friar's long narrative of the plot, a prince's judgement delivered in couplets. The bridegroom who entered the fourth act behind a band keeps his vigil at the tomb alone, strewing flowers in a silence he fills with a vow of tears. Paris's obsequies for Juliet are nightly, manual, and unaccompanied, because the contract that brought musicians to her wedding morning has no clause for her grave.

Set the act against the canon and the withholding sharpens. Ceremonial sound is the period stage's small change: princes and generals elsewhere in Shakespeare enter to flourishes, sennets, and tuckets, the trumpet vocabulary the Birmingham survey of Shakespeare's music catalogues as the standard grammar of rank. Verona's Prince holds three entrances in this play and receives no flourish at any of them, in any text. He arrives as a magistrate arrives, announced by uproar and report, and his final entrance to the churchyard is preceded by the Watch's shouted inventory of the dead. A city whose great households keep bands for their feasts gives its ruler no band at all, because the Prince's authority in Verona is exactly as provisioned as his music: proclaimed, intermittent, and unsupported by any institution that plays on cue. The tragedy's last word, famously, is a couplet about the story's sadness. It is spoken into a silence the score has spent five acts constructing, the silence left when every paid sound in the city has been stood down, refused, or never ordered.

One further silence waits at the very end, made of gold. The reconciliation is sealed with statues, Montague's Juliet and Capulet's Romeo raised in pure gold to stand in Verona as the feud's permanent memorial, and the documents are exact about what the statues are: images, likenesses, a jointure paid in metal. A city that hired its feelings by the occasion ends by casting them. The statues are the final form of the play's music by inversion, sound's opposite in every property that mattered to the score: where the band was hired by the hour, the gold is permanent; where the music announced occasions, the statues conclude one; where the whistle carried a signal and vanished, the images carry a likeness and stay. Verona's last word on its children is a chord struck in metal that cannot ring, and the Prince's couplet closes over it like a case over pipes.

The verdict on the play's own music can therefore be stated without remainder. The text calls for music the way its world does: by hire, for occasions, under contract. It directs the band once in the copy-text, at the feast where the catastrophe is conceived; it speaks music constantly, as summons, promise, insult, metaphor, and riddle, because a culture that hires its joy talks about sound the way it talks about credit; it lets the band refuse at the funeral, in the scene where the play's philosophy of music is stated by the men who make it; and it withdraws every instrument from the last act except a boy's whistle, because the tomb is the one address in Verona where no occasion, no household, and no wage can follow. Later ages, unable to leave that design alone, supplied the dirges and love themes the documents withhold, and their additions are beautiful, and they are additions. Sternfeld's structural thesis is vindicated with a twist he would have relished: the music is integral to the dramatic structure chiefly at the moment it is withheld. Duffin recovers what the band would have played; Lindley explains what the refusal meant; the catalogue keeps the evidence in classes so that neither recovery nor explanation is mistaken for the text. The score of Romeo and Juliet is a contract, and the tragedy is what happens when it lapses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

The questions below collect the article's findings in the order the evidence presents them, from the single direction of the copy-text to the trade, the tunes, and the tomb. Each answer keeps the three classes separate, because the separation is the finding.

### Q: What is the only music cue the Second Quarto prints as a stage direction?

The Second Quarto prints exactly one music direction, at the Capulet ball in 1.5: "Musick playes, and they dance", set between Capulet's summons to the musicians and his cry for room in the hall. Every other sound in the 1599 text arrives by speech. The wedding-morning band is promised and reported, never directed; the drum is absent altogether; the page's whistle is ordered by Paris and acknowledged after the fact, with no direction at the moment of sounding. The Folio later supplements the record with three further directions, and the First Quarto prints none at all, which makes the Second Quarto's single entry the centre of the evidence: one directed dance in the authoritative text, framed by two texts that bracket it from either side, one emptier and one fuller.

### Q: Why does the Folio print three further music directions that the Second Quarto lacks?

Each Folio supplement notates a sound the dialogue already implies. Benvolio's "Strike, drum" gives the masquers' march the percussion the Second Quarto leaves silent; "Play Musicke" in 4.4 cashes the promise that Paris would arrive with music; "Whistle Boy" in 5.3 writes down the signal Paris has just described to his page. The pattern is conservative and functional, the profile of a playhouse completing its own practice in the margin of a copy rather than a reviser composing new effects. That reading remains an inference, graded as such in the article, but the supplements' smallness supports it: the Folio adds no serenade, no dirge, no flourish, only the notation of sounds the spoken text had been carrying unaided since 1599.

### Q: What does the First Quarto print in place of the ball's music direction?

Nothing. The First Quarto of 1597 prints neither Capulet's summons to the musicians nor any direction for music or dancing at the feast; the dance reaches a reader only through Romeo's aside that the measure is done and his plan to watch Juliet's place of standing. The event survives in the shortest of the early texts as pure dialogue residue, a danced measure inferred from a spectator's remark. The subtraction is instructive at both ends of the record. It shows the ball's music being treated, within two years of the play's first performances, as expendable notation, the first thing a compressed text could drop, and it leaves the Second Quarto's direction standing as the sole early witness that the playhouse marked the moment in print at all.

### Q: Which instrument is named for the masquers' approach, and in which text does the naming appear?

The drum, and the naming appears only in the Folio, where it takes the form of a spoken order rather than a stage direction: Benvolio's "Strike, drum", added at the close of 1.4 as the masquers prepare to move. The Second Quarto ends the scene with Romeo's foreboding speech and a silent march; the First Quarto likewise names no instrument. The cue is therefore a Folio supplement carried in the dialogue column, a hybrid the catalogue records with its class split, since the order exists as a character's words while the sounding exists only if a company obeys them. Productions that give the torchbearers a drum are staging 1623, not 1599, and the difference is audible in the scene's temperature: percussion turns a festive entry into a small military movement.

### Q: Do the early texts ever call for hautboys in this play?

No early text of the play calls for hautboys, in a direction or in dialogue. The word does not occur in the First Quarto, the Second Quarto, or the Folio text of Romeo and Juliet, and the ball direction that posterity hears as a consort of loud winds specifies no instrument whatsoever. Hautboys belong to the wider canon's directed sound, to Macbeth's sennet atmosphere and to the music under the stage in Antony and Cleopatra, and their migration into accounts of Capulet's feast is an import from that wider memory, the sound of Shakespearean ceremony in general assigned to the most famous feast in particular. The article's collation is blunt on the point because the point is checkable: the tragedy's band is a matter of inference from string players' jokes in 4.5, and no document gives the feast a single named wind.

### Q: How does Capulet's spoken summons differ from the printed direction that follows it?

The summons is a line of dialogue, "Come, musicians, play", spoken by a character inside the fiction and addressed to the band on the stage; the direction, "Musick playes, and they dance", is an instruction from the playhouse to its company, printed between the summons and Capulet's next sentence. The two utterances sit within a breath of each other and belong to different worlds of authority. A summons can be ignored inside a fiction, as the musicians of 4.5 will demonstrate when the same household begs for music and is refused. A direction cannot be ignored without changing the document. The ball cue is the one place where command and compliance are printed together, and the pairing is the catalogue's whole method in miniature: a spoken cue proves an intention, a directed cue proves an event, and criticism that quotes the first as the second has crossed the classes.

### Q: What does the title page of Morley's 1599 Consort Lessons list, instrument by instrument?

The title page of Thomas Morley's First Booke of Consort Lessons (1599) lists six instruments: the treble lute, the bandora, the cittern, the base viol, the flute, and the treble viol. The set is the period's broken or mixed consort, plucked strings of two families, wire-strung bandora and cittern against gut-strung lute, balanced by bowed viols at both ends of the range and a single flute. The book was printed by William Barley and dedicated to the civic authorities of London, which made its six parts the standing definition of a full consort for exactly the debate the ball scene provokes. Whether the Chamberlain's Men fielded all six behind Capulet's dancing is the graded question the article adjudicates; the title page at least fixes what the ceiling would have been, and what a full claim must name.

### Q: Why does David Lindley doubt a full mixed consort in the public theatres before the Blackfriars years?

Lindley's doubt, set out in Shakespeare and Music (2006), rests on evidence and economics rather than on taste. No document places a six-part broken consort of the Morley pattern in an adult public-stage company before the King's Men took the indoor Blackfriars in 1608, where a resident consort became famous. The amphitheatre's music, on the surviving traces, reads as smaller and functional, trumpets and drums for ceremony and war, a few strings for dances and songs. The doubt is carefully aimed: it concerns the playhouse's band, not the fiction's, since a great household hiring civic waits for a feast is documented practice on any reading. A bass viol in a sharer's will proves instrumental capital inside the company without proving the consort, and Lindley's line holds exactly there, between ownership and ensemble.

### Q: What does Augustine Phillips's will of 1605 prove, and what does it not prove, about the company's music?

The will proves that Augustine Phillips, a sharer in the Chamberlain's and then the King's Men, owned a bass viol valuable enough to bequeathe by name, leaving it to a former apprentice in 1605. Instrumental property of consort quality therefore lived inside the fellowship, held by the men who also acted the plays, and the company's musical resources included at least the personnel and hardware for serious string playing. The will does not prove that a mixed consort performed at the Capulet ball, that any particular afternoon heard six parts, or that the viol was played in this tragedy at all. A will records property at a death, a decade after the play's first performances, and the distance between a bequest and a band is the distance the article's graded method exists to mark.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch music-cue catalogue record for each cue in the play?

Each row of the InsightCrunch music-cue catalogue records one cue under five heads: the moment in the action, the evidence class (directed, spoken, or supplied), the early texts that carry it, the sound at stake, and the stake's meaning for the scene. Sixteen rows run from the dreamed drum of Queen Mab to the page's whistle at the tomb. The catalogue admits absences as entries, most pointedly the bridegroom's band that never enters in the copy-text, and it marks editorial material as supplied rather than silently absorbing it into the play's record, so a bracketed "[Music within]" stands in its row as an editor's act, not an author's. The catalogue's omissions are also deliberate: knocks, alarms, and street noise belong to the play's signal system and are excluded, because the argument depends on music's scarcity.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch music-cue catalogue to argue that the text, not the tradition, holds the play's first score?

An essay built on the catalogue should argue from the classes in order. The directed column comes first, with the Second Quarto's single ball direction quoted in old spelling and the Folio's three supplements named as supplements, so the textual score's smallness is established from documents rather than asserted. The spoken column follows, where the boasts, promises, and refusals show a culture conducting its music in talk, and the catalogue's rows for the refused band and the never-entering bridegroom's music carry the argument's weight. The supplied column closes the case, with one famous addition, the eighteenth-century funeral dirge, set against the directed record to measure the tradition's enlargement. Claims stay proportional throughout: what a direction orders may be stated as fact, what dialogue implies as inference, and what editors supplied as history.

### Q: Why does no instrument sound in the last act except a boy's whistle?

The last act is set in the one place the occasion economy cannot reach. Act 5 unfolds at night, in streets, a cell, an apothecary's shop, and a tomb, among friars, servants, watchmen, and the dead, and the households that hire music are absent or ruined by the time the churchyard fills. Paris, the act's one bridegroom, keeps his vigil alone and unaccompanied, his band a memory of the fourth act's contract. The plot meanwhile runs on the free medium of signals: a torch, a letter undelivered, a page posted with an ear to the ground. The whistle is a signal, admitted to the catalogue as the score's smallest member because the Folio directs it, and its smallness is the design's signature, the whole hired world of the play's music contracted to one boy's breath in a graveyard.

### Q: How does the Folio's "Whistle Boy" direction change what the Second Quarto leaves to inference?

The Second Quarto gives the whistle twice removed: Paris orders the page to whistle as a signal if anything approaches, and Paris's next speech reports that the boy gives warning. A reader of 1599 infers the sound from its description and its effect, and a performance on that text may keep the tomb silent and let the report do the work. The Folio prints the sound itself as a direction, "Whistle Boy", at the moment of sounding, which converts inference into event. The audience hears what Paris hears, at the same instant, and the approach of Romeo is announced to the house before any character speaks of it. The supplement is three words long and reverses the scene's epistemology, from a world known by reports to a world known by sensation, which is the Folio's habit across all three of its added cues.

### Q: Which of Peter's three musicians is named only in the Folio?

Simon Catling, the First Musician, carries a name only in the Folio, which adds "Simon Catling" to Peter's address in the silver-sound exchange. The Second Quarto names the other two in dialogue, Hugh Rebeck and James Soundpost, while its first player speaks under the bare prefix of his trade and receives no name in Peter's questions. The famous trio of Catling, Rebeck, and Soundpost, printed as a set in modern editions, is therefore a composite assembled across the texts, completed by the Folio seven years after the Second Quarto and more than two decades after the first performances. The First Quarto complicates the set further with names of its own, Simon Soundpost and Matthew Minikin, so that no early document contains the canonical three together.

### Q: Why do the musicians' instrument-names differ between the First Quarto and the Folio?

The names are jokes about the fiddle family's anatomy and materials, and jokes of that kind attach to parts rather than to persons. Catling is gut for strings, a soundpost is the wooden prop inside the body, a rebec is the small three-stringed fiddle itself, and a minikin is a fine small string. The First Quarto's set, Simon Soundpost and Matthew Minikin, and the later texts' set, Hugh Rebeck and James Soundpost with the Folio's added Simon Catling, recombine the same workshop vocabulary over the same three walk-on parts. Transmission through performance, report, and reprint shuffled the labels because the labels were the point: an audience heard a band named after its own components whichever combination a given text preserved. Editors who print one composite trio are tidying a record the documents left untidy on purpose.

### Q: What happens to the song Peter quotes between the First Quarto and the Folio?

The song loses its second line. The First Quarto preserves the quatrain entire, including "And dolefull dumps the minde oppresse", the line that names the merry dumps Peter has just been discussing. The Second Quarto and the Folio both print the stanza without that line, passing straight from grief's wound to music's silver sound, a copying loss in transmission that modern editions repair from the First Quarto. The accident is pointed enough to deserve its fame: the dropped line is the one that names the dumps, so the two most authoritative texts silently delete the very term the scene's comedy turns on, and generations of readers met a smoother quotation than any early audience heard. Peter's anthology piece survives the play's transmission the way the play's music survives its occasions, partially, and by servants' keeping.

### Q: Who wrote the song Peter quotes, and where was it printed in 1576?

Richard Edwards, master of the Children of the Chapel Royal and the leading court musician of the early Elizabethan years, wrote the lyric Peter quotes, and it was printed in The Paradise of Dainty Devices (1576) under the heading "In commendation of Music", signed with Edwards's name among the anthology's gentlemen poets. The poem argues that music is medicine, a salve for every sore of a troubled mind, and the stanza Peter sings is its refrain, promising redress with speedy help for the heart that griping grief has wounded. The quotation is therefore an authority cited in a labour dispute: Peter supports a request for dance music at a funeral with the period's most respectable defence of music's healing power, a generation old when he sings it and already a classic of the art he cannot practise.

### Q: What are the pipes the First Musician proposes to put up?

The pipes are the First Musician's own wind instruments, the tools of his trade, which he proposes to return to their case in the scene's first exchange: if the wedding has collapsed into a funeral, the band will put up its pipes and leave. The word "pipe" in the period names a family rather than a single instrument, covering recorders, flutes, and the louder reed pipes of civic bands, and the text specifies no member of it, consistent with a dialogue that names the string band's anatomy in jokes while leaving its constitution generic. The proposal matters as the scene's thesis in gesture. Instruments in cases are the score's negative image, music present as equipment and withheld as sound, and Capulet's great conversion speech will shortly make the same figure of the corpse herself, a case in which the wedding's music has been put away.

### Q: How does Capulet's funeral speech rewrite the wedding's sound without an instrument playing?

The speech converts the wedding's sound inventory item by item, in metaphor, at the moment the actual band stands silent: "Our instruments to melancholy bells, / Our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast; / Our solemn hymns to sullen dirges change". Instruments become bells, hymns become dirges, and the music Paris brought an hour earlier is re-scored by a father's rhetoric without a note being played. The dirge that the eighteenth-century stage would later compose and insert into the action exists in the early texts only inside this figure of speech, a sound imagined by the man who had ordered the wedding's. The speech is the tragedy's own supplied score, authored by a character rather than an editor, and the catalogue files it where it belongs, among the spoken cues, as the loudest music in the play that nobody performs.

### Q: What work does the curfew bell do on the wedding morning?

The curfew bell is the wedding morning's clock, reported by Capulet as rung at three o'clock alongside the second cockcrow, and the pairing opens the day in public, agreed sound. A bell of that kind belongs to the city rather than the household, its hour enforceable because every listener hears the same signal, and Capulet's bustle takes its tempo from it: cords, logs, and cooks are marshalled against an audible timetable that ends at the bridegroom's arrival with music. The bell is spoken of, never directed, consistent with the copy-text's economy, in which timekeeping is done by dialogue. Set against the dawn argument of 3.5, where Juliet tries to rename the lark and secede from the audible calendar, the bell marks the opposite pole of the play's sound world, public time that no private grief can overrule.
