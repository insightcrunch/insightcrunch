The Extradition Act 1962 is the statute that decides whether a person found in India may be handed over to a foreign State for trial or punishment, and it is equally the statute under which India asks a foreign State to hand a person back. Almost every public misunderstanding of extradition begins by collapsing those two directions into one, and by treating the diplomatic request as if it were the decision. Neither is accurate. The Act, cited as Act 34 of 1962, creates a government to government request, a judicial inquiry in the State where the person is found, and a final executive decision that may be taken only after the judicial stage has run its course and only within restrictions that the statute itself imposes. In both directions, the court in the requested State applies the law and the evidential standards of that State. That single structural fact explains more about the record of extradition involving India than any account centred on diplomatic goodwill or diplomatic friction, and it is the thread that runs through every section of this article.

![The Extradition Act 1962 and the law of surrender and return between India and foreign States - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-59.webp)

This article reads the Act whole. It sets out what the statute replaced, how its definitions construct the idea of an extradition offence, how Section 3 brings a foreign State within the Act by notified order, and how a multilateral convention may be treated as a treaty where no bilateral treaty exists. It then follows each direction step by step: the requisition to the Central Government, the order for a magisterial inquiry, the warrant, the hearing, the prima facie test, committal to prison, and the warrant of surrender, and, in the opposite direction, the requisition that India makes, the conveyance of a person surrendered or returned, and the protection in Section 21 against trial for other offences. It gives the restrictions on surrender the prominence the brief for a statute pillar requires, because those restrictions are where requests are resisted and where they most often fail. It distinguishes extradition from deportation and from an international police notice, and it separates both from the distinct mechanism for declaring a person a fugitive economic offender. Throughout, the article describes the framework alone. Extradition proceedings concern identifiable individuals, and no person and no individual proceeding is named or described here. Propositions drawn from case law are stated as propositions about the function of a magistrate, the scope of the political offence exception, and the place of assurances, without narrating the facts of any case.

A note on sources and on figures belongs at the outset. The treaty position changes as treaties are signed, brought into force, and notified, and arrangements are added to the official list maintained by the ministry concerned. This article therefore states the treaty and arrangement position in structural terms and directs the reader to that official list for the position on any given date, rather than repeating a count taken from secondary material that may already be out of date on the day it is read. The same discipline applies to the practical record. Where this article records that the number of persons extradited to India is small in relation to the number of requests made, and where it records reasons for delay and failure, those reasons are attributed to the official and academic material that identifies them, and they are presented as that material presents them, without adjudication between competing explanations.

## The statute and what it replaced

The Extradition Act 1962 received the assent of the President on 15 September 1962 and came into force on 5 January 1963, the date appointed by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette, G.S.R. 55 of 5 January 1963. Section 1 gives the short title, extends the Act to the whole of India, and leaves commencement to that appointed date. The long title, as amended, describes an Act to consolidate and amend the law relating to the extradition of fugitive criminals and to provide for matters connected with or incidental to extradition. The words of consolidation matter. Before 1962 the law applicable to India was scattered across instruments of different origin and different reach, and the Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded that scatter as the reason for a single code.

The earlier position rested on three bodies of law. The United Kingdom Extradition Acts of 1870 to 1932, in their application to India, authorised extradition between India and foreign States to which those Acts were made applicable by Order in Council. The Fugitive Offenders Act 1881 supplied a simplified procedure between British dominions and possessions, resting on endorsement of warrants rather than on a fresh inquiry of the kind the foreign State procedure required. The Indian Extradition Act 1903 modified and supplemented both, prescribing procedure for surrender to foreign States, supplying special machinery for States that were not foreign States in that sense, and identifying the officers in India who could exercise the powers conferred. That patchwork produced a further anomaly recorded in the Objects and Reasons: the Act of 1903 had not been extended to the former Part B States in 1951, so the position governing surrender from those territories to foreign States and Commonwealth countries was doubtful. A Bill introduced in the Lok Sabha in August 1961 sought to remove the anomalies and fill the gaps, and the Act of 1962 is the result: one statute for surrender and return involving all foreign States, organised in chapters that separate the general procedure from the chapter that applies to States with extradition arrangements.

Section 37 carries the repeals and savings. The repeal does not erase the treaty inheritance. The definition of an extradition treaty expressly includes a treaty, agreement or arrangement relating to extradition made before 15 August 1947 which extends to and is binding on India. That drafting choice preserves continuity with instruments concluded before independence while placing their operation inside the new statutory machinery. A reader who assumes that the Act began with an empty treaty book therefore misreads it. The Act began with a new procedure and an inherited set of obligations, and Section 3 is the bridge between the two, because no treaty operates under the Act for a given State until the Central Government directs by notified order that the Act shall apply to that State.

The place of the wider field should also be fixed at the outset. Extradition sits alongside citizenship, the law governing foreigners present in India, and the general criminal law, and it borrows concepts from each without merging into any of them. The field as a whole, including the acquisition and loss of citizenship and the status of persons who are not citizens, is mapped in the guide to [citizenship law in India](/2021/09/01/citizenship-law-india-complete-guide/), and this article returns to the boundary with the law on foreigners when it distinguishes extradition from deportation. Keeping those boundaries visible is not pedantry. A person may be removable as a foreigner under one statute, prosecutable in India under another, and extraditable under this Act, and the three questions are governed by different tests, different decision makers, and different protections.

### What does the Extradition Act 1962 actually govern?

The Extradition Act 1962, Act 34 of 1962, governs the surrender of a fugitive criminal from India to a foreign State and the return of an accused or convicted person from a foreign State to India. Both directions run through a request between governments, a judicial stage in the requested State, and statutory restrictions that may bar surrender.

## The definitions that build the offence

Section 2 supplies the definitions on which the whole Act turns, and four of them do most of the work. The first is the extradition offence. In relation to a foreign State that is a treaty State, an extradition offence means an offence provided for in the extradition treaty with that State. In relation to a foreign State other than a treaty State, it means an offence punishable with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than one year under the laws of India or of the foreign State, and it includes a composite offence. The structure of the definition deserves slow reading, because it contains the principle of dual criminality inside it rather than stating that principle in a separate section. Where a treaty exists, the treaty list or treaty description controls. Where no treaty exists, the threshold is seriousness measured by punishment, and the punishment test is expressed by reference to the law of India or the law of the foreign State, so that conduct which only one legal system treats as criminal, or which both treat as trivial, falls outside the Act.

Dual criminality is the requirement that the conduct alleged be criminal in both jurisdictions. Its function is protective as well as definitional. A requested State is not asked to lend its courts and its prisons to the enforcement of a prohibition that its own law rejects, and a requesting State cannot obtain surrender for conduct that the requested State regards as lawful activity. The requirement also disciplines the drafting of requests. The requesting State must characterise the conduct, not merely name a foreign offence, because the magistrate in the requested State must be able to see conduct that maps onto an offence known to the law that the magistrate administers. A request that recites a label without disclosing conduct that is criminal in both places fails at the definitional stage, before any question of evidence arises. That is why dual criminality defeats requests that appear, from the requesting side, to be straightforward: the offence names differ, the elements differ, and in some cases the underlying conduct, such as a purely fiscal or regulatory wrong, occupies a different position in the two systems.

The second definition is the composite offence, inserted by the amending Act of 1993. A composite offence means an act or conduct of a person which occurred wholly or in part in a foreign State or in India, but whose effects or intended effects, taken as a whole, would constitute an extradition offence in India or in a foreign State, as the case may be. The definition answers a problem created by conduct that crosses a border. Fraud, conspiracy, and offences committed through communications and financial systems rarely sit neatly inside one territory. Without a composite offence concept, a requesting State would have to fragment a single course of conduct into territorial pieces and show a complete offence in one place. With it, the totality of the conduct and its effects may be considered together, provided the whole, taken together, amounts to an extradition offence. The definition widens the Act without abandoning dual criminality, because the composite conduct must still constitute an offence of the required kind.

The third definition is the fugitive criminal. As substituted in 1993, a fugitive criminal means a person who is accused or convicted of an extradition offence within the jurisdiction of a foreign State, and includes a person who, while in India, conspires, attempts to commit, or incites or participates as an accomplice in the commission of an extradition offence in a foreign State. Two features matter. The definition covers both the accused person who has yet to face trial and the convicted person who is sought to serve or complete a sentence, and those two categories raise different questions later in the process, since a convicted person may raise limitation, the time already served, and the fairness of a conviction recorded in absence, while an accused person raises the prima facie question in its purest form. The definition also reaches inchoate and participatory conduct located in India that is directed at an offence abroad, which prevents the Act from being defeated by the simple fact that the person never left India.

The fourth definition is the extradition treaty itself. As amended in 1993, an extradition treaty means a treaty, agreement or arrangement made by India with a foreign State relating to the extradition of fugitive criminals, and includes any such instrument made before 15 August 1947 which extends to and is binding on India. The insertion of agreement and arrangement alongside treaty is the textual foundation for extradition without a formal treaty, and Section 3 gives it operational effect. A treaty State, in turn, means a foreign State with which an extradition treaty is in operation. The remaining definitions are machinery: a magistrate means a magistrate of the first class or a presidency magistrate, a notified order means an order notified in the Official Gazette, prescribed means prescribed by rules made under the Act, and the definitions of conviction and convicted exclude a conviction for contumacy under foreign law while treating a person so convicted as a person accused, a refinement that prevents a conviction recorded in absence from being treated as if it were a conviction after trial.

## Section 3 and the notified order

Section 3 is the gateway provision of the Act, and no account of extradition from India is accurate without it. Section 3(1), as substituted by Act 66 of 1993 with effect from 18 December 1993, provides that the Central Government may, by notified order, direct that the provisions of the Act, other than Chapter III, shall apply to such foreign State or part of a foreign State as may be specified in the order. The consequence is that the Act is not self applying across the world. It applies State by State, through an executive act published in the Gazette, and the first question in any matter is therefore a documentary one: whether a notified order exists for the State concerned, what it specifies, and whether it remains in force.

Section 3(2) permits the Central Government, by the same notified order or by a subsequent notified order, to restrict the application of the Act to fugitive criminals found, or suspected to be, in such part of India as may be specified. Section 3(3) governs the case where the notified order relates to a treaty State. The order must set out in full the extradition treaty with that State; it must not remain in force for any period longer than the treaty; and the Central Government may, by the same or a subsequent notified order, render the application of the Act subject to such modifications, exceptions, conditions and qualifications as may be thought expedient for implementing the treaty. The treaty therefore enters Indian law for extradition purposes through the notified order, and the order may qualify the general provisions of the Act so that the treaty bargain, rather than the default text, governs the particular relationship. A practitioner who reads only the bare Act and skips the notified order for the State in question will miss qualifications that may be decisive, including the list or description of offences, any threshold the treaty imposes, and any condition the treaty attaches to surrender.

Section 3(4), also inserted in 1993, supplies the convention route. Where there is no extradition treaty made by India with a foreign State, the Central Government may, by notified order, treat any convention to which India and that foreign State are parties as an extradition treaty made by India with that State, providing for extradition in respect of the offences specified in that convention. The provision matters because India and many States are parties to multilateral conventions on particular categories of crime that contain extradition obligations or permissions for the offences they define. Section 3(4) allows such a convention to do the work of a bilateral treaty for those specified offences, again through a notified order that a reader can inspect. Between the widened definition of a treaty to include an agreement or arrangement, and the convention route in Section 3(4), the answer to whether India can extradite without a treaty is therefore a statutory answer rather than a diplomatic one. Extradition without a bilateral treaty is possible where an arrangement exists and the Act has been applied by notified order, or where a shared convention is treated as a treaty by notified order. It is not possible merely because two governments wish it in a particular case, because the notified order is the legal bridge and without that bridge the magistrate has no Act to administer.

The earliest notified orders illustrate the machinery in operation. On the day the Act came into force, 5 January 1963, an order under Section 3, G.S.R. 56, directed that the provisions of the Act other than Chapter III should apply to Switzerland, Sweden, and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics with effect from that date. The example is worth recording for its form rather than for its list, which belongs to its time. It shows the Act beginning State by State, exactly as Section 3 contemplates, and it shows why any statement about the reach of the Act must be tied to the orders in force rather than to the statute alone.

### Can extradition proceed where no bilateral treaty exists?

Extradition may proceed without a bilateral treaty where the Central Government has applied the Act by notified order under an arrangement, or where Section 3(4) treats a convention to which India and that State are both parties as an extradition treaty for the offences the convention specifies. The notified order is the legal foundation in each case.

## The amending Act of 1993

The Extradition (Amendment) Act 1993, Act 66 of 1993, which took effect on 18 December 1993, is the principal amendment of the Act and it changed the statute in substance rather than in detail alone. Before turning to its individual changes, its general direction should be stated. The original Act of 1962 was drafted against a world in which the Commonwealth occupied a distinct procedural place, reflected in a separate chapter and in repeated paired references to a foreign State or a Commonwealth country. The amendment of 1993 removed that dual structure, unified the vocabulary around the foreign State, broadened the instruments that count as an extradition treaty, added the composite offence and the convention route, recast the rule of speciality, and inserted new provisions on prosecution where extradition is refused, on provisional arrest, and on the death penalty. A reader who works from an unamended text will therefore be misled on almost every point that matters in practice.

The definitional changes came first. The amendment inserted the definition of a composite offence, substituted the definition of an extradition offence so that the non treaty limb carries the one year imprisonment threshold and expressly includes a composite offence, expanded the definition of an extradition treaty to include an agreement or arrangement, omitted words that had confined the definition of a foreign State, and substituted the definition of a fugitive criminal so that it reaches conspiracy, attempt, incitement and accomplice participation committed while in India. Each change enlarges the class of cases the Act can reach, and each does so by definition rather than by procedural shortcut, which preserves the later safeguards.

The structural changes followed. The amendment substituted Section 3(1) in its present form and inserted Section 3(4). It omitted the references to Commonwealth countries across the Act, including in the requisition, surrender and simultaneous requisition provisions, and it deleted the separate Commonwealth framing that had run through Chapter III, leaving Chapter III as the chapter on return of fugitive criminals to foreign States with extradition arrangements. It renumbered Section 31 as Section 31(1) and inserted Section 31(2), and it substituted the speciality clause in Section 31(1)(c) so that the permitted exceptions are stated as the extradition offence itself, any lesser offence disclosed by the facts proved for the purpose of securing surrender or return other than an offence for which surrender could not lawfully be ordered, and any offence for which the Central Government has given consent. The amendment also inserted Sections 34A, 34B and 34C, which are considered in their own places below, and it made consequential changes to the Schedule and to the laying requirement in Section 35. The pattern across all of these changes is consistent: the 1993 Parliament widened the gateway while adding protections at the exit, which is the opposite of the assumption that amendment in this field moves in one direction only.

## Chapter II: the requisition and the order for inquiry

Chapter II governs the extradition of fugitive criminals to foreign States to which Chapter III does not apply, and Sections 4 to 10 supply the sequence that most readers mean when they speak of extradition from India. Section 4 deals with the requisition for surrender. A requisition for the surrender of a fugitive criminal may be made to the Central Government by a diplomatic representative of the foreign State at Delhi, or by the government of that State communicating through its diplomatic representative in India, or by such other mode as may be settled by arrangement. Where the person is sought in relation to a part of India for which a different channel has been settled, the requisition follows that channel. The point of the section is that the requisition is addressed to the Central Government, not to a court. Extradition begins as an act between governments, and the judiciary enters because the statute requires it to enter, not because the foreign State sues in an Indian court.

Section 5 then gives the Central Government its first decision. On receipt of a requisition, the Central Government may issue an order to a magistrate to inquire into the case, where it considers that such an order ought to be issued. The discretion is real. The Central Government may decline to set the machinery in motion, and Section 29, considered later, gives it a wider power to stay proceedings and discharge a fugitive criminal at any stage where surrender appears unjust or inexpedient. But where the Government does issue the order, the character of the proceeding changes. From that point the question is no longer whether a foreign government has asked, but whether the material placed before an Indian magistrate, tested in a hearing at which the fugitive criminal may be represented and may tender evidence, establishes a prima facie case for surrender under the Act and the applicable treaty or arrangement.

That division of labour between the executive and the magistrate is the constitutional centre of the Indian scheme, and it is worth contrasting with the assumption that extradition is an executive act with a judicial rubber stamp. The executive controls the gateway, the treaty relationship, and the final warrant. The magistrate controls the evidential sufficiency of the case for surrender. Neither can do the work of the other. An executive that wishes to surrender a person whom the magistrate has not committed has no warrant to act on under Chapter II, and a magistrate who is satisfied on the evidence cannot compel the Central Government to issue the warrant of surrender, because Section 8 leaves the final act to the Government. Requests fail, therefore, at three distinct points for three distinct kinds of reason: at the gateway, for reasons of policy, treaty scope or documentation; before the magistrate, for reasons of evidence, dual criminality or a statutory restriction; and at the final stage, for reasons that the Government weighs after receiving the magistrate's report. Public commentary that assigns every failure to one of those stages misdescribes the scheme.

## The warrant, arrest and bail

Section 6 governs the issue of a warrant for arrest. Where an order for inquiry has been issued, the magistrate shall issue a warrant for the arrest of the fugitive criminal, on the basis of the information and evidence furnished in support of the requisition or otherwise, where the magistrate is satisfied that the material justifies the step. Section 9 supplies a distinct and narrower power: a magistrate may issue a warrant even without an order from the Central Government in the urgent or exceptional circumstances the section contemplates, subject to the reporting and confirmation discipline that ties the step back to the Central Government, which may cancel the warrant and order discharge if it declines to proceed. Section 34B, inserted in 1993, provides separately for provisional arrest, considered below with the other inserted provisions. The three routes should not be conflated. The ordinary route runs requisition, order, warrant. The provisional route exists to prevent flight while a formal requisition is in preparation, and it is time bound by the discharge provision in Section 24.

Arrest under the Act carries the ordinary incidents of custody under Indian criminal procedure, adapted by the Act itself. Section 25 provides for the release of persons arrested on bail, so that custody pending inquiry is not automatic and a magistrate may admit a fugitive criminal to bail on conditions, weighing the risk of absconding that is inherent in a proceeding whose whole object is removal from the jurisdiction. Section 27 provides that custody under a warrant issued or endorsed under the Act is lawful, and that a person who escapes may be retaken in the same manner as a person accused of an offence against the law of India. Section 28 deals with property found on a fugitive criminal at apprehension, which may be delivered up with the person, subject to the rights of third parties. These provisions appear minor beside the inquiry itself, but they determine the lived reality of a proceeding that may run for a considerable period: whether the person waits in prison or on bail, whether custody is lawful against a habeas corpus challenge, and what happens to property seized at arrest. Section 24 supplies the outer limit for provisional custody. If a person apprehended under the Act is not surrendered or returned within two months of apprehension, the High Court may, on application and on notice to the Central Government, order discharge where sufficient cause is not shown, a provision that prevents provisional arrest from hardening into indefinite detention without a completed requisition.

The comparison with the general criminal law is instructive here, and it is the comparison the brief directs at the dual criminality sentence as well. The offences that count, the conduct that must be shown, and the procedure that follows arrest are all questions that require the extradition statute to be read beside the penal code and its successor codes, which is why the comparison in [the guide to the Indian Penal Code and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/) forms part of the background to this article. Extradition does not import the foreign offence into Indian law. It asks whether the conduct, if it had occurred in India, would amount to an offence of the required seriousness under Indian law, and it uses Indian procedure to answer the procedural questions that arise in India.

## The inquiry before the magistrate

Section 7 prescribes the procedure before the magistrate, and it is the provision on which the fairness and the duration of Indian extradition proceedings both depend. The magistrate shall inquire into the case in the same manner, and shall have the same jurisdiction and powers, as nearly as may be, as if the case were one triable by a magistrate under the code of criminal procedure. The magistrate shall receive any evidence that may be produced in support of the requisition, and shall also receive any evidence tendered on behalf of the fugitive criminal, including evidence to show that the offence of which the fugitive criminal is accused or convicted is an offence of a political character, or that the requisition has in fact been made with a view to try or punish the fugitive criminal for an offence of a political character. Section 10 governs the receipt in evidence of exhibits, depositions and other documents, and their authentication, which is the provision that allows a case assembled abroad to be proved in India without requiring every witness to travel, and which is therefore also the provision at which the quality of foreign documentation is tested.

The standard the magistrate applies is a prima facie standard. If, in the opinion of the magistrate, a prima facie case is made out in support of the requisition, the magistrate shall commit the fugitive criminal to prison to await the orders of the Central Government, and shall report the result of the inquiry to the Central Government, forwarding, where the magistrate thinks it proper, any written statement the fugitive criminal wishes to submit on the question of surrender. If a prima facie case is not made out, the magistrate shall discharge the fugitive criminal. The jurisprudence on this function is settled in its central proposition and should be stated exactly: the inquiry is not a trial. The magistrate does not determine guilt or innocence, does not weigh the evidence as a trial court weighs it at the end of a prosecution, and does not ask whether the evidence would secure a conviction. The magistrate asks whether the material, if accepted, discloses conduct amounting to an extradition offence and a case fit to be answered at a trial in the requesting State. Evidence tendered for the fugitive criminal is received and considered for that purpose and for the political character questions that Section 7 expressly preserves; it does not convert the inquiry into a dress rehearsal for the foreign trial.

That proposition cuts in both directions, and a balanced account must give each direction its weight. For the requesting State, the prima facie standard is lower than proof, so a request supported by coherent, authenticated and translated documentation that maps the conduct onto offences in both systems should ordinarily pass the inquiry stage. For the person sought, the inquiry is a genuine protection against surrender on assertion alone, because a request that cannot produce admissible and authenticated material to the prima facie standard fails in India regardless of the seriousness of the allegation abroad. Academic and official commentary on the practical record returns repeatedly to this point: the documentation question is not clerical. Affidavits sworn in a form the Indian court cannot receive, depositions that are not authenticated as Section 10 requires, translations that are incomplete or uncertified, and statements of foreign law that do not establish dual criminality each have the capacity to defeat or to delay a requisition at the inquiry, and each is within the requesting State's power to avoid by preparation. The magistrate's courtroom, in other words, is where the quality of a request is audited, which is the domestic instance of this article's central claim about where extradition is decided.

The wider architecture within which that magistrate acts, including the place of the courts of first instance, the supervisory jurisdiction of the High Courts on habeas corpus and judicial review, and the appellate structure above them, is set out in the guide to [the judicial system and its laws in India](/2021/04/01/judicial-system-laws-india-guide/), and this article assumes that architecture rather than repeating it. Two features of it matter here. A committal by a magistrate is not the end of judicial involvement, because the person committed may invoke the writ jurisdiction of the High Court, and the restrictions in Section 31 may be raised before the magistrate, before a court, or before the Central Government, as the text of that section itself provides. And the inquiry record travels: the magistrate reports to the Central Government and forwards the fugitive criminal's written statement where one is submitted, so the final executive decision is taken on a judicial record rather than on the requisition alone.

### What must a magistrate be satisfied of before committal?

A magistrate must be satisfied that a prima facie case is made out: that the material received, including authenticated exhibits and depositions, discloses conduct amounting to an extradition offence and a case fit for trial in the requesting State. The magistrate does not try the case or decide guilt, and discharges the fugitive criminal where that threshold is not reached.

## Surrender after committal and the executive decision

Section 8 governs surrender. Where a fugitive criminal is committed to prison under Section 7, the Central Government may, if it thinks fit, issue a warrant for the custody and removal of the fugitive criminal and for delivery at a place and to a person suitable for receiving the fugitive criminal. The warrant is the executive act that converts committal into surrender. Between committal and warrant, Section 31(1)(e) interposes a mandatory interval: a fugitive criminal shall not be surrendered until after the expiration of fifteen days from the date of being committed to prison by the magistrate. The interval is a protection with a practical purpose. It gives the person committed the opportunity to move the High Court, to make representations to the Central Government, and to raise any restriction on surrender that the inquiry did not finally resolve, before removal becomes irreversible in fact.

Section 29 qualifies the whole chapter and should be read with Section 8 rather than after it. If it appears to the Central Government that, by reason of the trivial nature of the case, or by reason of the application for surrender or return not being made in good faith or in the interests of justice or for political reasons, or otherwise, it is unjust or inexpedient to surrender or return the fugitive criminal, the Government may, by order at any time, stay the proceedings under the Act, direct that any warrant issued or endorsed be cancelled, and direct that the person be discharged. The power is deliberately broad and deliberately executive. It recognises that a case may satisfy every legal test and yet be one that India ought not to assist, because the allegation is trivial, the motive is improper, or the interests of justice point the other way. Its existence also completes the answer to a common criticism of the scheme, namely that the executive is a mere conduit after committal. Under Sections 5, 8 and 29 taken together, the executive decides whether to start the process, whether to complete it, and whether to halt it at any point, while the magistrate decides the distinct question the executive cannot decide for itself, whether the evidence sustains a prima facie case.

Simultaneous requisitions are governed by Section 30. If requisitions for the surrender of the same fugitive criminal are received from more than one foreign State, the Central Government may, having regard to the circumstances of the case, surrender the fugitive criminal to such State as it thinks fit. The provision states no hierarchy between competing requests, and any hierarchy a reader encounters in commentary, based on the date of the offence, the date of the request, the seriousness of the charge, or the nationality of the person sought, is a matter of the circumstances the Government weighs rather than a rule the section imposes. Section 22 completes the outbound picture by providing for the liability of fugitive criminals to be arrested and surrendered or returned in accordance with the Act, and Section 23 addresses jurisdiction over offences committed at sea or in the air, a provision whose importance has grown with offences committed on board ships and aircraft that cannot be assigned to a single territory without a jurisdictional rule.

## Chapter III: return under extradition arrangements

Chapter III occupies a distinct place in the Act and a distinct place in its history. As amended, the chapter is headed as the return of fugitive criminals to foreign States with extradition arrangements, and Sections 12 to 18 supply a procedure that differs from Chapter II in its starting point. Section 12 states the application of the chapter. Section 13 states the liability of fugitive criminals from the countries to which the chapter applies to be apprehended and returned. Sections 14 to 16 deal with endorsed warrants and provisional warrants: a warrant issued by the requesting State may be endorsed for execution in India, and a provisional warrant may be issued in anticipation, so that the procedure begins from a foreign warrant rather than from an Indian order for inquiry. Section 17 governs the dealing with a fugitive criminal when apprehended under the chapter, including production before a magistrate and the inquiry that follows, and Section 18 governs return by warrant.

The practical significance of the chapter division is twofold. First, the route that governs a given case depends on the notified position for the State concerned, since Section 3(1) applies the Act other than Chapter III by notified order, and Chapter III applies as its own section provides. A practitioner therefore begins by identifying the chapter, because the chapter determines whether the case proceeds by requisition and fresh inquiry or by endorsed warrant. Secondly, the protections converge even where the procedures diverge. Section 32 provides that Sections 29 and 31 shall apply without any modification, so the Central Government's power to discharge and the restrictions on surrender protect a person proceeded against under Chapter III exactly as they protect a person proceeded against under Chapter II. The Act thus permits procedural economy for States within the chapter without permitting any dilution of the substantive bars to surrender, a design that the 1993 amendment preserved while it removed the older Commonwealth vocabulary in which the chapter had originally been framed.

## Chapter IV: seeking return to India

Chapter IV, Sections 19 to 21, governs the surrender or return of accused or convicted persons from foreign States to India, and it is deliberately short, for a reason that goes to the heart of this article's claim. India cannot legislate the procedure of a foreign court. What Indian law can do, and what Chapter IV does, is regulate the Indian side of an inbound request: how the requisition is made, how a person surrendered or returned is conveyed, and what India may do with that person after arrival.

Section 19 states the mode of requisition or the form of warrant for the surrender or return to India of an accused or convicted person who is in a foreign State. The requisition is made by the Central Government through the diplomatic channel to the government of the State where the person is, supported by the material that the law of that State and any applicable treaty require. From that point, the decisive proceedings take place under foreign law. The foreign court or authority applies its own extradition statute, its own evidential threshold, its own treaty interpretation, and its own human rights obligations, which in many States include obligations under regional or international instruments that have no counterpart in the Indian proceeding. India supplies the request and the record; the requested State supplies the decision. Section 20 then provides for the conveyance of an accused or convicted person surrendered or returned, so that custody during transit and on arrival rests on statutory authority rather than on administrative improvisation.

Section 21 is the inbound counterpart of the rule of speciality, and it binds India. An accused or convicted person surrendered or returned by a foreign State shall not be tried in India for an offence other than the offence for which surrender or return was granted, subject to the exceptions the section states, until the person has been restored to, or has had an opportunity of returning to, the State that surrendered or returned the person. The provision is both a protection for the individual and a condition of the system's credibility. A requested State surrenders a person for a specified offence on the faith of that limitation. If India could prosecute the returned person for unrelated earlier conduct without consent, requested States would either refuse surrender or surround it with conditions that made it slower and rarer. Speciality, in both directions, is therefore best understood not as a technicality that obstructs prosecution but as the price at which surrender is obtainable at all, a point developed further in the discussion of Section 31 below.

The inbound direction also exposes an asymmetry that commentary often misses. When India seeks a person from a State whose law requires a prima facie case to be shown before its own court, India must prove that case abroad, with documentation admissible under foreign rules, frequently in translation, and frequently against challenges founded on the conditions in which the person would be detained or tried in India. When India seeks a person from a State whose law proceeds on a record of the case or on a certification model, the evidential burden differs in kind. The Indian statute cannot equalise those burdens, because they are imposed by the requested State's law. What preparation in India can do is anticipate them: a requisition that identifies the requested State's threshold at the outset, assembles the record to that threshold, and addresses the assurances that the requested State's courts are known to require stands a materially better prospect than a requisition assembled to an Indian standard and translated afterwards. Official and academic material on the practical record identifies precisely this gap, between the record as assembled and the record as the requested State requires it, as a recurring cause of delay.

### How does a person surrendered to India come to be tried?

A person surrendered to India is conveyed under Section 20 and may be tried for the offence for which surrender was granted, within the limit Section 21 imposes against trial for other offences until the person is restored to the surrendering State or could return there. Trial proceeds in India on that charge, plus any consented charge.

## The restrictions on surrender in Section 31

Section 31 states the restrictions on surrender, and the article gives them a full section because the brief identifies them, correctly, as the ground on which most requests are resisted. Section 31(1) provides that a fugitive criminal shall not be surrendered or returned to a foreign State in the cases it enumerates, and Section 31(2), inserted in 1993, qualifies the political offence restriction for a defined class of grave conduct, addressed below. Each restriction should be taken in turn, in the order of the section.

The first restriction, Section 31(1)(a), is the political offence exception, in two limbs. Surrender is barred if the offence in respect of which surrender is sought is of a political character. It is also barred if the fugitive criminal proves, to the satisfaction of the magistrate or court before whom the person is produced or of the Central Government, that the requisition or warrant has in fact been made with a view to try or punish the person for an offence of a political character. The two limbs are distinct. The first looks at the character of the offence charged. The second looks at the purpose of the request, and it protects a person charged with an ordinary offence where the true object of the request is punishment for political conduct. The burden under the second limb rests on the fugitive criminal, and the forum is disjunctive: the magistrate, a court, or the Central Government may be satisfied. Section 7, it will be recalled, expressly preserves the right to tender evidence on both questions at the inquiry.

The second restriction, Section 31(1)(b), is limitation. Surrender is barred if prosecution for the offence in respect of which surrender is sought is, according to the law of the requesting State, barred by time. The reference to the law of the requesting State is deliberate. India does not impose its own limitation period on a foreign prosecution; it refuses to surrender where the requesting State's own law would no longer permit the prosecution to proceed. The restriction prevents the extradition process from being used to revive a claim that the requesting system itself treats as spent, and it requires the magistrate and the Central Government to engage with foreign limitation law as evidenced before them.

The third restriction, Section 31(1)(c), is the rule of speciality in its outbound form. Surrender is barred unless provision is made, by the law of the foreign State or in the extradition treaty with that State, that the fugitive criminal shall not be detained or tried in that State for an offence other than the extradition offence in relation to which the person is to be surrendered or returned, any lesser offence disclosed by the facts proved for the purposes of securing surrender or return other than an offence for which an order for surrender or return could not lawfully be made, or the offence in respect of which the Central Government has given its consent. The clause as substituted in 1993 is more precise than the form it replaced, and its three exceptions define the exact space within which a requesting State may act after surrender. Anything outside that space requires either a provision in law or treaty satisfying the clause, or it bars surrender at the outset.

The fourth restriction, Section 31(1)(d), protects the priority of Indian proceedings. Surrender is barred where the fugitive criminal has been accused of some offence in India, not being the offence for which surrender or return is sought, or is undergoing sentence under a conviction in India, until after the person has been discharged, whether by acquittal, on expiration of the sentence, or otherwise. The bar is suspensive rather than absolute. It does not extinguish the request; it postpones surrender until the Indian process has run its course, which reflects a judgment that a State should complete its own criminal process against a person within its custody before delivering that person to another system.

The fifth restriction, Section 31(1)(e), is the fifteen day interval already noted: surrender is barred until after the expiration of fifteen days from the date of committal to prison by the magistrate. Read with Section 24, which addresses the two month limit on custody after apprehension without surrender or return, Section 31 constructs a timetable with protections at both ends: a minimum interval before surrender may be effected after committal, and a judicial remedy where custody after apprehension is prolonged without surrender following.

Beyond the five clauses, two connected provisions complete the protective scheme. Section 34C, inserted in 1993, addresses the death penalty by providing for the substitution of life imprisonment in the class of case it covers, so that a person surrendered in circumstances engaging that provision is not exposed to a penalty the provision excludes. The provision belongs to a wider pattern in extradition practice, in which a requested State whose law does not permit surrender for a capital offence without an assurance obtains an assurance, or relies on a statutory provision, that the death penalty will not be imposed or carried out. Section 34A, also inserted in 1993, supplies the converse protection for the administration of justice in India: where extradition is refused, it provides for prosecution of the person in India in the circumstances it states, so that refusal does not automatically produce impunity for conduct that Indian law can reach. Together, Sections 31, 34A and 34C show the Act balancing three interests that pull against one another: the interest of the requesting State in obtaining trial, the interest of the individual in not being surrendered into a process that is political, time barred, unlimited in scope, or capital without protection, and the interest of India in not becoming a place of refuge from prosecution that its own courts could conduct.

## The political offence exception and its limits

The political offence exception requires separate treatment because it is at once a long standing principle of extradition law, a protection with a defensible rationale, and the most contested boundary in the subject. Its rationale should be stated at full strength first. Extradition developed as cooperation between States in the suppression of ordinary crime. Political offences stand apart because they are offences against the political order of the requesting State itself, often committed in a contest about who should govern or how, and the requested State has historically declined to take sides in that contest by delivering the requesting government's opponents to it. The second limb of Section 31(1)(a) extends the rationale from the offence to the motive: a request framed as an ordinary prosecution may in substance pursue a political object, and a requested State that examined only the charge on its face would become an instrument of that pursuit. On this view, the exception is not an indulgence. It is the point at which extradition law refuses to treat every request from a recognised government as neutral.

The counter argument, also at full strength, is that the exception as classically framed protects conduct that no legal order should protect. Violence directed at civilians, hijacking, hostage taking, and attacks intended to coerce a government by terrorising a population are committed for political purposes in the plain sense, and a rule that classified them as political offences would place the gravest conduct beyond extradition precisely because of its motive. Modern treaty practice and modern legislation have answered that argument not by abolishing the exception but by narrowing it, through lists of offences that are deemed not to be of a political character, through multilateral conventions that remove defined offences from the exception for parties to them, and through provisions that direct attention to the character of the conduct rather than to the motive alone. Indian law contains such a narrowing provision in Section 31(2), inserted by the Act of 1993, which qualifies the operation of the political character bar for the class of conduct it specifies. The article states the existence and function of that qualification without reproducing a catalogue, because the operative question in any case is whether the conduct alleged falls within the qualification as the magistrate, court or Central Government construes it on the material produced.

The jurisprudence, stated as propositions and without narrating any proceeding, supports three conclusions. A magistrate inquiring under Section 7 does not treat the label attached by the requesting State as decisive; the character of the offence is a question for the requested forum on the material before it. Conduct involving indiscriminate violence sits at the outer edge of the exception and, in the reasoning courts have adopted, the presence of a political objective does not by itself convert violent conduct into a political offence where the connection between the conduct and the political object is remote or where the means are disproportionate to any political end. And the motive limb requires proof: the fugitive criminal must satisfy the relevant forum that the requisition has in fact been made with the prohibited view, which is an evidential burden that assertion alone does not discharge. Those propositions leave the boundary contested, as the brief anticipates, because the underlying disagreement is real. One side prioritises the requested State's neutrality in foreign political contests; the other prioritises the proposition that motive cannot launder grave violence. Section 31 as amended holds both, by retaining the exception in subsection (1) and qualifying it in subsection (2), and an accurate article should present that as a legislative resolution that remains open to argument in the individual case rather than as a settled answer that ends the debate.

## Provisional arrest, refusal and the inserted provisions

Sections 34A, 34B and 34C, each inserted by the amending Act of 1993, address three situations the original Act handled incompletely, and they are best read as a group. Section 34B provides for provisional arrest. On an urgent request from a foreign State, a person may be arrested provisionally in India pending the arrival of the formal requisition and the supporting material, so that the interval between the identification of a person's whereabouts and the completion of the diplomatic and documentary process does not become an opportunity for flight. Provisional arrest is, by design, custody on an incomplete record, and the Act therefore surrounds it with the discipline of Section 24: custody that is not followed by surrender or return within the period the Act allows exposes the person detained to discharge on application to the High Court. The two provisions together express a balance that runs through extradition law generally. Urgency justifies arrest before the full record exists; it does not justify detention while a requesting State assembles its record at leisure.

Section 34A provides for prosecution on refusal of extradition. Where surrender is refused in the circumstances the section covers, the person may be prosecuted in India for the conduct in question, under the jurisdictional provisions that support such a prosecution, including Section 34 on extraterritorial jurisdiction. The principle behind the provision is the maxim that a State should extradite or prosecute. A requested State that declines to surrender, whether because no treaty relationship exists, because a restriction applies, or because the Central Government exercises its power under Section 29, does not thereby declare the conduct lawful. Section 34A ensures that the file does not close with refusal where Indian law can try the conduct itself, and it changes the practical advice in such a case: refusal of extradition is the end of the surrender process, not necessarily the end of the criminal process.

Section 34C provides for life imprisonment in place of the death penalty in the case it specifies. Its significance extends beyond its own terms, because the death penalty is one of the subjects on which the human rights obligations of requested States most often condition surrender to India in the inbound direction, and on which India, in the outbound direction, must be able to give a legally grounded answer rather than a diplomatic assurance alone. Where a foreign court is prohibited by its own law from surrendering a person who faces capital punishment without protection, a statutory provision of this kind, and assurances furnished in a specific case and recorded by that court, are the instruments by which the obstacle is addressed. The article returns to assurances below; here it is enough to fix that the Act itself, after 1993, contains a provision directed at the capital objection, which an account written from the original text would miss entirely.

Chapter V collects the remaining miscellaneous provisions, several of which have already been woven into the account above: Section 22 on liability to arrest and surrender or return, Section 23 on offences at sea or in the air, Section 24 on discharge after two months, Section 25 on bail, Section 26 on abetment of extradition offences, Section 27 on lawful custody and retaking after escape, Section 28 on property found on the fugitive criminal, Section 29 on the Central Government's power to discharge, Section 30 on simultaneous requisitions, Section 31 on restrictions, Section 32 applying Sections 29 and 31 without modification to the arrangement chapter, Section 33 preserving the Foreigners Act 1946, Sections 34 to 34C as discussed, Section 35 requiring notified orders and notifications to be laid before Parliament, Section 36 conferring the rule making power, Section 37 on repeals and savings, and the Schedule. Section 35 deserves a sentence of its own. The laying requirement subjects the Section 3 machinery to parliamentary visibility, so that the extension of the Act to a new State, and any modification attached to a treaty relationship, enters the public and parliamentary record rather than resting as an unpublished executive decision.

## Two directions, one test

The findable artifact for this article sets the two directions side by side, because the comparison is the fastest route to the article's central proposition: in each direction a court in the requested State must be satisfied, and the standard applied is the standard of that State.

| Stage | Extradition from India to a foreign State | Return from a foreign State to India |
|---|---|---|
| Request | Requisition made to the Central Government under Section 4 | Requisition made by the Central Government under Section 19 |
| Authority receiving it | Central Government of India | Government of the foreign State where the person is found |
| Judicial stage | Inquiry before a magistrate under Section 7 | Hearing before the court or authority of the foreign State under its own law |
| Standard applied | Prima facie case on material received under Section 10 | The evidential threshold imposed by the law of the requested State |
| Government decision | Warrant for custody and removal under Section 8, subject to Section 29 | Decision of the foreign government under its own law after its judicial stage |
| Restrictions | Section 31 bars, fifteen day interval, discharge power in Section 29 | Restrictions and human rights obligations imposed by the law of the requested State |
| Timeline | Fifteen days after committal at the earliest, with High Court remedies available | Determined by the procedure and appeals of the requested State, extending over years in contested cases |

The table repays reading by column and then by row. By column, each direction has the same anatomy: a request, a receiving government, a judicial stage, a standard, an executive decision, restrictions, and a timeline. By row, the decisive rows are the judicial stage and the standard, and in both rows the governing law is the law of the place where the person is. That is the sense in which the artifact's title is literal. There are two directions and, structurally, one test, but the test is administered by different courts under different statutes, which is why two requests of equal moral strength may have unequal outcomes and why the outcome cannot be predicted from the request alone.

## Why requests take years and frequently fail

The practical record, presented with the attribution the subject requires, is that the number of persons extradited to India is small in relation to the number of requests made, and that contested requests in either direction commonly extend over years. Official statements and academic commentary on extradition involving India identify a set of recurring reasons, and this article records them as that material identifies them, without ranking them for any individual case and without attributing failure in any individual case to any of them, since no individual case is described here.

The first reason is the evidential threshold in the requested State. Where the requested State requires a prima facie case to be established before its own court, the requesting State must prove that case to a foreign standard, in a foreign procedure, frequently through documents rather than oral testimony. A record that satisfies a domestic court at the stage of charge may not satisfy a foreign court at the stage of committal, because the rules governing authentication, hearsay, and the form of sworn statements differ. The second reason is documentation and translation. Requests depend on complete documentation, and official and academic material identifies delay in transmitting complete and translated documentation as a recurring cause of adjournment and, in some cases, of discharge. Translation is not a clerical tail to the request; in a proceeding conducted in the language of the requested State, the translated record is the record.

The third reason is challenge founded on the human rights obligations of the requested State. A person sought may contend that surrender would expose the person to detention conditions, trial conditions, or a penalty incompatible with obligations binding on the requested State, whether under its constitution, its human rights legislation, or an international instrument by which it is bound. Prison conditions in the requesting State are a recurring subject of such challenges, and requested courts have required assurances regarding treatment and conditions, which the requesting State has furnished in specific proceedings and which the requested court then evaluates for specificity and enforceability. The fourth reason is appeal. Extradition decisions in the requested State are ordinarily subject to appeal or judicial review, and a contested request may pass through more than one tier of court, with habeas corpus or its equivalent available against detention in the interval. Each tier is a genuine proceeding with its own timetable, and the aggregate is measured in years.

Two explanations that circulate in public discussion should be handled with the care the brief directs. It is sometimes said that requested States are unwilling for political reasons. The material more commonly identifies the documentary, evidential and human rights reasons set out above, and an accurate account records the political unwillingness claim as a claim that is made, while noting that it is not the explanation the official and academic material most often substantiates. It is also sometimes assumed that a strong diplomatic relationship accelerates surrender. The structure of the process limits what diplomacy can do. A foreign minister cannot direct a foreign court, and a requested government that is favourably disposed must still wait for its judicial stage to conclude and must still act within the restrictions its own law imposes. Diplomacy may affect the gateway, the speed of transmission, and the willingness to furnish assurances. It does not decide the case. That is the practical meaning of the namable claim with which this article began, and it is the reason the series thesis, that the effective decision maker is often not the authority the statute foregrounds, has its clearest international instance here. The Extradition Act foregrounds the Central Government, which makes and receives requisitions and issues warrants. The effective decision, in the contested cases that define the record, is taken by a court in the requested State applying that State's law.

### Why does documentation decide so many requests?

Documentation decides requests because the requested court decides on the record placed before it, as its own law requires. Authenticated exhibits and depositions, a statement of conduct establishing dual criminality, proof of the requesting State law, and certified translations constitute the case before the court, and a defect in any of them is a defect in the case.

## Extradition, deportation and the international notice

Three mechanisms are conflated in public discussion with a regularity that makes a plain distinction necessary. Extradition is a judicial process under this Act or under the corresponding law of the foreign State. It requires a request between governments, a judicial stage in the requested State, satisfaction of an evidential threshold, compliance with restrictions, and an executive warrant. Its object is trial or punishment for a specified offence, and its product, where it succeeds, is surrender for that offence within the limits of speciality.

Deportation is different in kind. Deportation, and the related powers of expulsion and removal, are immigration decisions of the State where the person is present, taken under that State's law governing foreigners, on grounds that State's law specifies, through a process that does not require a request from another State and does not require a court to be satisfied of a prima facie criminal case. Section 33 of this Act expressly provides that the Act does not affect the Foreigners Act 1946, preserving that separate body of power, and the statute governing the presence, detention and removal of foreigners in India is treated in the article on [the Foreigners Act 1946](/2021/11/01/foreigners-act-1946-india-explained/) at the deportation sentence the brief directs. The distinction has practical consequences that commentary often blurs. A State that cannot obtain extradition, because no treaty relationship exists or because a restriction bars surrender, may in some circumstances remove a foreigner under immigration law, and the person may in fact travel to the State that sought extradition. That outcome is not extradition by another name. It carries none of extradition's protections: no prima facie inquiry, no speciality limit on later prosecution, and no statutory restrictions of the Section 31 kind, which is precisely why the two routes must not be described as interchangeable and why the use of removal in a case that is in substance a criminal surrender request is a subject of legal challenge in many systems.

An international police notice is different again. A notice circulated through the International Criminal Police Organization, of which the red notice is the best known form, is a request to member police forces to locate a person and, where the law of the place of arrest permits, to arrest that person provisionally with a view to extradition. A notice is not a warrant, it is not an extradition request, and it is not by itself a legal basis for surrender in India or elsewhere. Its legal effect is exhausted by the provisional arrest machinery: in India, provisional arrest under Section 34B and the related provisions, followed by the requirement that a formal requisition and a record sufficient for inquiry arrive within the period the Act allows, failing which discharge follows under Section 24. A report that a red notice has been issued therefore reports the beginning of a possible process, not its outcome, and a report that treats the notice as if surrender had been ordered misstates the law in a way that predictably misleads readers about what happens next.

A fourth mechanism must be separated from all three, because it is frequently confused with extradition in reporting on economic offences. The Fugitive Economic Offenders Act 2018 supplies a distinct statutory mechanism for declaring a person a fugitive economic offender and for confiscating property, operating independently of extradition and directed at property and status rather than at surrender for trial. Its relationship with the money laundering statute, including attachment and confiscation of proceeds of crime, is addressed in the guide to [the Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002](/2013/03/01/pmla-2002-complete-guide/), to which the reader is directed at this point rather than offered a summary here. The separation matters for a practical reason. A declaration and confiscation may proceed while an extradition request is pending, may proceed where no extradition request has been made, and may be complete without the person ever being surrendered. Treating confiscation as a stage of extradition, or extradition as a remedy for the failure of confiscation, confuses two statutes with different objects, different courts, and different protections.

## Assurances and the conditions of surrender

Assurances occupy a place in extradition practice that the bare text of the Act only partly reveals, and an accurate account must describe their function without narrating the proceedings in which particular assurances were given. An assurance is an undertaking by the requesting State, furnished to the requested State or recorded before its court, concerning the treatment the person will receive if surrendered: that a particular penalty will not be imposed or carried out, that the person will be held in specified conditions, that a trial will observe specified safeguards, or that the person will be returned if a contingency occurs. Requested courts have required assurances where, without them, surrender would conflict with obligations binding on the requested State, and India has furnished assurances in specific proceedings in both the capital context and the detention conditions context. The questions a requested court asks of an assurance are consistent across systems. Is the assurance specific enough to be capable of compliance and of verification, or is it a general statement of intent? Is it given by an authority that can bind the requesting State and control the institutions whose conduct it promises? And what follows if it is breached, given that the person, once surrendered, is outside the requested court's practical protection?

Those questions explain why assurances are contested even where they are accepted. Critics argue that an assurance substitutes a promise for a structural condition, and that monitoring after surrender is weak. Supporters answer that extradition between systems with different penal practices is impossible without such instruments, that the alternative is a blanket refusal that protects no one, and that an assurance recorded by a court and given at the level of government creates obligations whose breach carries diplomatic and legal consequences in future requests, which gives the requesting State a powerful incentive to comply. The Act accommodates the practice at two points already identified: Section 34C, which supplies a statutory answer on the death penalty in the case it covers, and Section 31(1)(c), which makes surrender conditional on provision, by law or treaty, for the speciality limit, a condition that in a given case may be satisfied or supplemented by the terms on which surrender is granted. What the Act does not do is prescribe the form of an assurance or the monitoring of one, and that silence is itself a finding worth recording: the assurance practice lives in treaty drafting, in diplomatic correspondence, and in the orders of requested courts, layered over a statute that creates the space for it without regulating its detail.

## The treaty position and how to read it

The treaty position should be described structurally, as the brief directs, because any enumeration is perishable. India has extradition treaties in operation with a number of foreign States, extradition arrangements with a further number, and convention based relationships available under Section 3(4) for the offences that shared conventions specify. The three categories are not interchangeable, and the differences between them are practical. A treaty relationship brings a negotiated text that defines offences, thresholds, mandatory and discretionary grounds of refusal, the treatment of nationals, the rule on capital punishment, and the documentary requirements for a request, and that text is set out in full in the notified order under Section 3(3)(a). An arrangement relationship brings the Act applied by notified order without a negotiated treaty text of that completeness, so the Act's own provisions carry more of the weight. A convention relationship is offence specific: it reaches only the offences the convention specifies, so a request that strays beyond those offences has no foundation in that relationship at all.

For a practitioner or a researcher, the working method follows from that structure. Identify the State. Find the notified order for that State and read it, including any modifications, exceptions, conditions and qualifications imposed under Section 3(3)(c). Read the treaty text set out in the order, where there is one, before reading commentary on it. Check whether the conduct alleged is an extradition offence under that text, or under the one year threshold where no treaty applies, and whether it is a composite offence. Check the restrictions, both in Section 31 and in the treaty, since a treaty may add grounds of refusal that the Act does not contain and may qualify grounds that it does. And check the documentary requirements the treaty prescribes, because treaties commonly specify the statements, warrants, texts of law and authentication that must accompany a request, and non compliance is a ground of failure that has nothing to do with the merits. A reader who follows that sequence will understand a given extradition question better than a reader who begins from a count of treaties, which is why this article declines to print such a count and directs the reader to the list published by the ministry concerned, where the position is maintained as orders are made and treaties enter into force.

One further structural point deserves emphasis, because it is a source of recurring error. The existence of a treaty does not oblige either State to surrender in a given case, and the absence of a treaty does not preclude surrender where an arrangement or convention route has been notified. Treaties in this field are characteristically frameworks of permission and condition rather than commands. They oblige the parties to consider requests through the agreed procedure and to refuse only on grounds the framework permits, and they leave the judicial stage intact. Commentary that treats the signing of a treaty as the creation of a pipeline, or its absence as a wall, misdescribes both.

## Reading the Act as a practitioner

For the practitioner reader, the payload of this article can be gathered into a sequence of questions that the Act itself dictates, stated here in prose as the house style requires. The first question is foundation: is there a notified order applying the Act to the State concerned, is there a treaty, arrangement or convention relationship behind it, and which chapter governs? The second is definition: is the conduct an extradition offence, tested through the treaty text or through the one year threshold, is dual criminality satisfied on the conduct rather than on the offence label, and is the conduct a composite offence whose effects complete it? The third is evidence: does the record contain authenticated exhibits, depositions and statements of law receivable under Section 10, in a form and language the inquiring court can act on, sufficient for a prima facie case? The fourth is restriction: does any clause of Section 31 bar surrender, does Section 31(2) qualify the political character argument on these facts, is an Indian accusation or sentence outstanding under Section 31(1)(d), and has the fifteen day interval been respected? The fifth is executive: has the Central Government ordered the inquiry, will it issue the warrant, and is there any ground on which it might instead act under Section 29? The sixth, in the inbound direction, is the mirror image: what does the requested State's law require, what threshold must the Indian record meet there, what assurances will its courts seek, and what appeals will follow a favourable decision?

That sequence also supplies the aspirant reader's examinable core, which the brief identifies as the statute, dual criminality, and speciality. An answer on extradition that states the Act's number and commencement, explains Section 3 and the notified order, defines the extradition offence in both limbs, distinguishes the inquiry from a trial, sets out Section 31 in full, and explains speciality in both its outbound form in Section 31(1)(c) and its inbound form in Section 21, has covered the ground on which questions in this area are set. An answer that narrates a celebrated request has covered none of it, which is a further reason for this article's discipline of describing the framework and naming no proceeding.

This article is legal information about a statute and its scheme; it is not legal advice on any person's position, and a person affected by a requisition, a warrant, or a provisional arrest requires advice on the facts of that matter from a practitioner instructed in it.

## Evidence, authentication and the statement of foreign law

Section 10 deserves a fuller treatment than its brevity suggests, because it is the provision through which a case investigated in one country becomes a case provable in another. The section provides for the receipt in evidence of exhibits, depositions and other documents duly authenticated, and for the manner of their authentication, including certification by the appropriate judicial or diplomatic authority and proof by oath or affirmation where the section requires it. Its effect is to displace, for extradition purposes, the ordinary rule that a witness must attend and be cross examined before the court may act on the witness account. Without such a provision, an inquiry in India into conduct abroad would require the attendance of foreign investigating officers, foreign complainants and foreign custodians of records, and the proceeding would collapse under its own logistics. With it, the foreign case travels as a certified record.

The displacement creates the central tension of the inquiry. The fugitive criminal may tender evidence, and the magistrate receives it, but the requesting State case is characteristically presented on paper, and the authors of that paper are not present to be tested. The magistrate answer to the tension is built into the standard itself. Because the question is prima facie sufficiency rather than proof, the magistrate asks whether the authenticated record, taken at the level the standard requires, discloses the conduct and the offence, whether it is internally coherent, whether the identity of the person sought is established by material the court can act on, and whether the documents are authenticated in the manner the Act and any applicable treaty prescribe. A record that fails on authentication fails entirely, however strong the underlying investigation, because an unauthenticated document is not evidence the magistrate may receive. A record that fails on identity fails for a different reason: surrender delivers a particular person, and a court that cannot be satisfied that the person before it is the person named in the requisition has nothing to commit.

Three documentary elements recur in practice and each merits attention. The warrant or conviction record from the requesting State establishes the legal foundation of the request, that a prosecution is pending or a sentence is outstanding, and it must correspond to the person and the offence described in the requisition. The statement of conduct, supported by depositions or statements received under Section 10, establishes what the person is alleged to have done, in enough particularity for the magistrate to test dual criminality and for the person sought to know the case to be met. The statement of law establishes that the conduct is an offence under the law of the requesting State, what punishment it carries, that prosecution is not barred by time, and, where the requesting State relies on it, what provision its law or treaty makes for speciality. Weakness in the third element is a recurring and under reported cause of failure. A magistrate cannot find dual criminality, cannot apply the one year threshold, and cannot test limitation under Section 31(1)(b) without evidence of the foreign law, and foreign law in an Indian court is a matter to be proved, not assumed. Requests assembled by investigators rather than by lawyers characteristically prove the facts with care and the law by assertion, and the inquiry exposes the imbalance.

Translation belongs in the same discussion. Where the record originates in a language other than the language of the inquiring court, the translated record is the evidence, and an incomplete or uncertified translation is not a presentational defect but an evidential one. In the inbound direction the position reverses with interest: an Indian record sent abroad must be translated into the language of the requested court, authenticated to that court standard, and organised to that court threshold, and official and academic accounts of delay identify the assembly of that translated and authenticated record, after the decision to request has been taken, as a stage at which months are consumed before the foreign proceeding has begun at all. The lesson the Act teaches, through Section 10 read with Section 7, is that extradition is a documentary jurisdiction. The investigation may be excellent and the diplomacy willing, and the request may still fail because the paper does not prove, in receivable form, what the investigation established.

## Habeas corpus, judicial review and the life of a committal

A committal under Section 7 is not the final judicial word in India, and the interval that Section 31(1)(e) guarantees exists precisely so that it need not be. A person committed to prison to await the orders of the Central Government is in custody under the Act, and custody in India is answerable to the writ of habeas corpus under Articles 32 and 226 of the Constitution. On such an application the High Court does not retry the inquiry and does not substitute its own assessment of the prima facie case for the magistrate assessment where the magistrate has applied the right test to receivable material. What the court examines is the legality of the detention and of the process that produced it: whether the Act applied to the State concerned by notified order, whether the offence was an extradition offence, whether the inquiry followed Section 7, whether a restriction in Section 31 was overlooked, whether the documents received were authenticated as Section 10 requires, and whether the Central Government acted within the powers Sections 5, 8 and 29 confer. A defect on any of those grounds is a defect in the authority to detain, and habeas corpus is the remedy shaped to it.

Judicial review reaches the executive stages as well. The decision to order an inquiry, the decision to issue a warrant of surrender after committal, and a refusal to exercise the discharge power in Section 29 are executive decisions taken under a statute, and they are reviewable on the ordinary grounds that govern such decisions, within the restraint that courts observe in matters touching foreign relations. That restraint is real and should be described accurately rather than either exaggerated or denied. A court will not conduct India foreign policy, will not assess the wisdom of a treaty relationship, and will not direct the Central Government to surrender or to refuse. A court will insist that the statutory conditions for the step taken existed, that the person affected had the hearing the Act provides, and that a restriction the Act imposes was not treated as if it were absent. The distinction explains outcomes that puzzle lay readers, in which a court intervenes decisively on a question of procedure or restriction while declining altogether to express a view on whether surrender is desirable. The court is policing the statute, not deciding the diplomacy.

The practical consequence is that an Indian extradition proceeding characteristically has two judicial lives. The first is the inquiry, in which the magistrate tests the record. The second is the supervisory life, in which the High Court tests the inquiry and the executive action around it, and in which the person committed may also place before the Central Government, through the written statement the magistrate forwards and through representations in the fifteen day interval, the considerations that bear on the final warrant. Time spent in the second life is not an aberration or a misuse of process; it is the working out of protections the Act and the Constitution both supply, and any estimate of how long extradition from India takes that counts only the inquiry understates the proceeding by omitting the stage in which its legality is finally settled.

### What can a High Court examine after a magistrate commits a fugitive?

A High Court on habeas corpus examines the legality of the detention: whether the Act applied to the requesting State, whether the offence was an extradition offence, whether the inquiry and authentication were proper, whether any Section 31 restriction was overlooked, and whether the Central Government acted within its powers. It does not retry the inquiry.

## Nationals, fiscal offences and military offences

Three subject matter questions recur in extradition law and each is handled by the Indian scheme in a way a reader should be able to state. The first is the surrender of a State own nationals. Legal systems divide on the question. Some refuse as a matter of constitutional principle to extradite their own citizens and prosecute them at home for conduct abroad instead. Others surrender nationals on the same terms as any other person. The Indian Act contains no general bar on the surrender of an Indian citizen who is a fugitive criminal within its definitions, and no clause of Section 31 rests on nationality. Where a treaty relationship exists, the treaty text governs the treatment of nationals for that relationship, and treaties differ, so the answer in a given case is found in the notified order and its treaty rather than in a supposed general rule. In the inbound direction the position of the requested State may be the opposite, and a requested State that does not extradite its nationals may offer prosecution at home in place of surrender, a substitution that changes what India can obtain from a request and that a request strategy must identify at the outset rather than discover after years of proceeding.

The second question is fiscal offences. Conduct concerned with taxation, customs and revenue has historically occupied an uneasy place in extradition, because the revenue of a foreign State was long treated as a matter no other State would enforce, and because fiscal offences are defined by statutes whose structure varies sharply between systems, which makes dual criminality harder to establish. The Indian Act contains no fiscal exclusion in Section 31, and the question is therefore governed by the definition of the extradition offence and by the applicable treaty, many of which address fiscal offences expressly in one direction or the other. The practical point is the dual criminality point in a sharpened form. A charge described in the requesting State fiscal vocabulary proves nothing in the requested forum until the conduct is stated: the false statement, the concealment, the fraudulent evasion, described as conduct, may map onto offences of dishonesty in both systems even where the revenue statutes that frame the charge have no counterpart. Requests in this territory succeed or fail on the translation of a fiscal charge into a statement of dishonest conduct, and they fail when the translation is not attempted.

The third question is the military offence. The brief for this article identifies, among the restrictions, the case of an offence under military law which is not also an offence under the general criminal law. Purely military offences, such as absence without leave, disobedience of a lawful command in its purely disciplinary form, or conduct punishable only because the actor is subject to service law, lack the dual criminality foundation on which extradition rests, because the general criminal law of the requested State contains no corresponding offence for a civilian and frequently none for a soldier either outside the service code. Extradition practice therefore treats a purely military offence as outside surrender, while treating conduct that is both a service offence and an ordinary crime, such as theft, assault or fraud committed by a service member, as extraditable in its ordinary criminal character. The distinction is the dual criminality principle applied to a special case, and it protects a person from being surrendered for a disciplinary wrong while leaving the requesting State free to seek surrender for the criminal conduct that accompanies it, charged as the crime it also is.

## Transit, expenses and the mechanics after surrender

A statute pillar article should not end at the warrant, because surrender is a physical act with legal incidents that the Act regulates and that occasionally generate litigation of their own. Section 20, already noted, governs the conveyance of an accused or convicted person surrendered or returned to India, and the corresponding outbound warrant under Section 8 specifies the place of delivery and the person to whom the fugitive criminal is to be delivered. Between warrant and delivery, custody must be lawful at every moment, which is why Section 27 provides that custody under warrants issued or endorsed under the Act is lawful and that a person who escapes from such custody may be retaken as a person accused of an offence under Indian law may be retaken. The provision forecloses a gap in which a person under a valid warrant might claim that the custodians authority expired in transit.

Property found on the fugitive criminal, governed by Section 28, raises a smaller question with a principled answer. Property seized at apprehension may be delivered up with the person, because it may constitute evidence in the foreign proceeding or the proceeds of the conduct alleged, but the delivery is subject to the rights of third parties, who may be owners, lien holders or victims with claims of their own. The Act does not confiscate by the back door of extradition. Confiscation, where it is sought, must rest on a confiscation statute and its own procedure, which is the same separation noted earlier between this Act and the fugitive economic offender mechanism.

The question of time does not end at surrender either. A person surrendered after committal has ordinarily spent a period in custody in the requested State, and the treatment of that period, whether it counts toward a sentence imposed after conviction in the requesting State, is governed by the law of the requesting State and frequently by the treaty, which commonly addresses credit for time spent in custody awaiting surrender. A person surrendered to India and later convicted may raise the period of custody in the surrendering State in the Indian sentencing process under the ordinary principles governing set off for pre trial custody, applied to the facts. These are not ornaments to the scheme. In a proceeding whose contested stages are measured in years, the treatment of time in custody is a substantial part of what the proceeding decides about a person life, and a treaty or request strategy that ignores it is incomplete.

Expenses and administrative responsibility complete the picture at the level of principle. Extradition generates costs in both States, for detention, for proceedings, for translation and authentication, and for escort and transport, and treaties characteristically allocate those costs by rule, ordinarily leaving the requested State to bear the costs incurred in its own territory up to surrender and the requesting State to bear the costs of transport and of proceedings after surrender, subject to the text of the particular instrument. The Act leaves the allocation to that treaty layer and to executive arrangement, which is consistent with its general method: the statute supplies the powers and the protections, and the bilateral instrument supplies the administrative detail that varies legitimately from one relationship to another.

## The debate about the record, stated fairly

The stance this series requires on a contested question is that each side be given in its strongest form, in the voice of those who make it, before any verdict is offered. The contested question here is why the extradition record involving India is what it is, and what, if anything, should change. Three positions are identifiable in official statements, parliamentary discussion and academic writing on the subject, and they should be distinguished because they prescribe different remedies.

The first position locates the difficulty in the law of the requested States. On this view, associated in parliamentary discussion with members who have pressed the government on pending requests, India assembles meritorious requests and sees them defeated or delayed by thresholds, human rights challenges and appellate structures abroad that are said to be more protective of the person sought than the seriousness of the alleged conduct warrants. The remedy this position prescribes is diplomatic and treaty based: negotiate clearer treaties, press for expedition, and seek assurances frameworks in advance rather than case by case. Its strength is that the thresholds abroad are real and are not within India control. Its weakness, as critics of the position observe, is that it treats the requested court as an obstacle rather than as the decision maker the system designates, and it cannot explain why some requests succeed in the same foreign systems that defeat others.

The second position locates the difficulty in the preparation of requests. On this view, developed most fully in academic commentary on Indian extradition practice, the recurring causes are domestic and remediable: delay in deciding to request, delay in assembling and translating the record, statements of law that do not establish dual criminality to the foreign standard, and assurances sought late rather than anticipated. The remedy prescribed is institutional: specialised preparation, early identification of the requested State threshold and of the objections its courts characteristically entertain, and a record built for the forum that will decide it. Its strength is that it identifies causes within the requesting State control, and it is consistent with the structure this article has described, in which the requested court decides on the record before it. Its weakness is that preparation cannot remove genuine legal obstacles, including a restriction in the requested State law, a capital punishment objection that assurances do not resolve to that court satisfaction, or a finding that detention conditions fall below the standard the requested State is bound to apply.

The third position questions the measure of success itself. On this view, found in academic writing on extradition and human rights, a low surrender rate is not by itself evidence of failure, because extradition is a process with protections whose operation necessarily produces refusals, and because alternative outcomes, including prosecution in the requested State, prosecution in India under Section 34A after refusal, deportation under immigration law in an appropriate case, and confiscation under a separate statute, may each serve part of the underlying purpose. The remedy prescribed is evaluative: judge the system by whether its protections operate and its alternatives are used, not by a single ratio of surrenders to requests. Its strength is that it resists a crude metric. Its weakness is that it can become a rationalisation of delay, since a process that takes many years imposes costs on victims, on witnesses whose evidence degrades, and on the person sought who waits in custody or under restriction, whether or not surrender follows.

The verdict the evidence in this article supports is the second position, qualified by the third. The structure of the Act and of every counterpart statute places the decision in the requested court on the record presented, so the variable most within a requesting State control, and the variable the official and academic material most often identifies, is the quality and completeness of that record and the anticipation of the objections the deciding court is bound to consider. That conclusion does not acquit requested State law of its effects, and it does not convert every refusal into an Indian failure. It states where effort is effective. A system that wants more surrenders should build better records earlier, and a system that wants to be judged fairly should report its outcomes across surrender, domestic prosecution after refusal, and the other lawful outcomes, rather than by surrender alone.

## A worked sequence in each direction

The scheme is clearer when it is followed from beginning to end in each direction, in general terms and without reference to any actual matter. Consider first a request received in India. A foreign State sends a requisition to the Central Government through its diplomatic representative, identifying the person, the offence, and the treaty or arrangement relied on, and enclosing the warrant, the statement of conduct, the text of its law, and the depositions on which it relies. The first work in Delhi is verification of foundation: the notified order for that State is located and read, the treaty text set out in it is checked against the offence charged, and the documents are examined for the authentication Section 10 will require. If the Central Government decides that an order ought to be issued, it directs a magistrate to inquire under Section 5. The magistrate issues a warrant under Section 6, the person is arrested or surrenders to the process, and the question of bail under Section 25 arises at once, argued on the risk of flight that the proceeding itself creates.

At the inquiry, the requesting State case is presented through the authenticated record, and counsel for the fugitive criminal tests it at the points the Act makes available: that the conduct does not amount to an offence in India and dual criminality fails; that the depositions are not authenticated or do not disclose the conduct alleged; that the offence is of a political character or the requisition is in fact made for a political purpose; that prosecution is barred by time in the requesting State; that no sufficient speciality provision exists. The magistrate receives evidence on those questions, including evidence tendered for the fugitive criminal on political character, and decides the prima facie question. A discharge ends the matter unless the requesting State can repair the defect in a fresh requisition that the Central Government is willing to entertain. A committal sends the report, and any written statement by the person committed, to the Central Government, and starts the fifteen day interval. In that interval the person may move the High Court and may make representations. Thereafter the Central Government decides whether to issue the warrant of surrender under Section 8, or whether a ground under Section 29 makes surrender unjust or inexpedient notwithstanding the committal. Delivery under the warrant ends the Indian proceeding and begins the foreign trial, bounded by speciality.

Consider then a request made by India. An investigation in India produces a charge, a warrant, and a decision that the person is in a named foreign State and that a request should be made. The first work is the mirror of the work described above and is the stage at which the outcome is most influenced: the law of the requested State is identified, its threshold is ascertained, its treaty with India is read for its documentary requirements and its grounds of refusal, and the record is built to that specification, with the statement of conduct framed to establish dual criminality in the requested State law, the statement of Indian law proved rather than asserted, and translations prepared and certified. Practitioners assembling such a record for presentation to officials and to the foreign authority alike make use of ordinary document tools in that preparation, including [a presentation viewer for checking how the assembled exhibits and translated record present as a single file](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), because a record that cannot be read as a coherent whole will not persuade as one. The requisition then travels through the diplomatic channel under Section 19. In the requested State, the proceeding follows that State law: arrest, a hearing before its court, challenges on the threshold, on dual criminality as its law frames it, on its own grounds of refusal, and on the human rights objections its law requires it to consider, including detention conditions and, where the charge carries it, the death penalty. Assurances may be sought and furnished. Appeals follow. Only when the judicial stage concludes in favour of surrender and the foreign executive issues its own warrant does conveyance under Section 20 occur, and on arrival Section 21 confines the Indian prosecution to the offence for which surrender was granted, unless the surrendering State consents to more.

Placing the two sequences side by side exposes three features that a section by section reading can obscure. The documentary burden falls on the requesting State in both directions, and it is discharged before a court that owes the requesting State nothing. The person sought has, in both directions, two distinct audiences: the court, on the evidence and the restrictions, and the executive, on discretion and on the considerations Section 29 collects. And the timetable in both directions is set by the requested State, because its court lists the hearing, its appellate structure fixes the intervals, and its executive issues the final warrant. A requesting State controls the quality of its request and nothing else. That is not a defect in the design. It is the design, and it is the reason extradition rewards preparation and punishes assumption in equal measure.

## Convicted persons, sentences and return for punishment

Much of this article has spoken of the accused person, because the prima facie inquiry is most naturally described in that setting, but the Act applies equally to the convicted person, and the differences deserve explicit statement. A requisition for a convicted person seeks surrender not for trial but for the imposition or completion of a sentence. The inquiry therefore changes its centre. There is no question of a case fit for trial in the same sense; the questions are whether the conviction is proved by an authenticated record, whether the person before the magistrate is the person convicted, whether the sentence or the unserved part of it is established, whether prosecution or punishment is barred by time under Section 31(1)(b) as applied to enforcement, and whether any restriction, including speciality as it bears on the sentence to be served, is satisfied. The definition in Section 2 that excludes a conviction for contumacy from the meaning of conviction, while treating the person so convicted as a person accused, does important work here. A conviction recorded in the person absence, without a trial at which the person could defend, is not treated as a conviction for the purpose of surrender to serve its sentence. The person is treated as accused, which routes the matter back to the prima facie inquiry and, in practice, to the question whether the requesting State will retry the person on surrender, a matter on which requested courts characteristically require clarity before they will surrender at all.

The convicted person case also sharpens the death penalty and conditions questions. A sentence already imposed is known, so the requested court is not speculating about exposure; it is deciding whether to deliver a person to a specified punishment and a specified prison system. Section 34C, in the outbound direction, and assurances, in the inbound direction, therefore operate with greater precision in convicted cases, and a requested court may distinguish between surrender for trial, where the outcome is open, and surrender for punishment, where it is fixed. Limitation likewise operates differently, since the relevant question may concern not the age of the offence but the age of the sentence, interruptions to its currency, and the effect of the person absence on its enforceability under the requesting State law, all matters to be proved as foreign law before the magistrate.

A related distinction, frequently missed, separates extradition of a convicted person from the transfer of sentenced persons under the separate arrangements that many States, including India through its own legislation on the repatriation of prisoners, maintain for a different purpose. Transfer of sentenced persons is ordinarily consensual, serves rehabilitation by allowing a sentence to be served in the person home State, and does not depend on a prima facie case or on the restrictions in Section 31. Extradition of a convicted person is compulsory in character, serves enforcement, and depends on both. A reader who encounters a report of a prisoner being moved between States should therefore ask which regime produced the movement before drawing any conclusion about extradition from it, just as the reader should ask whether a removal was deportation or surrender. The vocabulary of cross border movement of persons in the criminal process is shared across regimes whose legal content differs entirely, and precision about the regime is precision about the protections that applied.

## Conventions, terrorism offences and the widening of the treaty idea

Section 3(4) was described earlier as a gateway, and its full significance emerges only against the development of multilateral criminal law cooperation in the decades after the Act was passed. The conventions to which the provision refers are characteristically conventions directed at a category of conduct rather than at extradition in general: conventions on offences aboard aircraft, on hostage taking, on narcotic drugs, on corruption, on transnational organised crime, and on forms of terrorism, each of which defines offences, requires or permits their criminalisation in domestic law, and contains provisions under which the offences are deemed to be included as extradition offences in treaties between parties, or may be treated as a legal basis for extradition where the requested State makes extradition conditional on a treaty. Before 1993, an Indian court asked to act on such a convention faced a structural difficulty: the convention bound India internationally, but the Act required a treaty relationship and a notified order before its machinery engaged. Section 3(4) removes the difficulty by allowing the Central Government to treat the convention itself as the treaty for the offences specified in it, by notified order. The multilateral obligation is thereby converted into a bilateral operational relationship with each other party, for those offences and no others.

That conversion interacts with the political offence discussion in a way practitioners must hold together. The same body of conventions that widens the treaty idea also characteristically narrows the political offence exception for the offences the conventions define, by providing that those offences shall not be regarded as political offences for the purposes of extradition between parties. Section 31(2) performs the corresponding domestic function. Read together, the 1993 changes align the Act with the convention architecture: the gateway widens through Section 3(4), the exception narrows through Section 31(2), and the definitions expand through the composite offence, which is the definitional tool that cross border conduct of the kind the conventions address most often requires. A reader who treats the 1993 amendment as a miscellaneous tidying exercise therefore understates it. It was the amendment that connected a statute drafted in 1962, for a bilateral and Commonwealth centred world, to a multilateral system of offence specific cooperation that had grown up around it.

The limits of that connection should be stated with equal care. A convention relationship under Section 3(4) exists only where a notified order treats the convention as a treaty with the State concerned, so the international obligation does not bypass the Gazette. The offences reachable are only those the convention specifies, so conduct outside the convention categories gains nothing from the route. And every protection in the Act survives the route intact, because the convention becomes a treaty for the purposes of the Act, not a substitute for the Act: the inquiry, the prima facie standard, the restrictions in Section 31 as qualified by its own subsection (2), and the executive discretions in Sections 5, 8 and 29 all apply to a convention based request exactly as they apply to a request under a bilateral treaty. The widening is a widening of foundation, not a lowering of the test, which is the consistent pattern of the Act development from 1962 through 1993 and the strongest evidence for reading the statute as a protective code with an expanding gateway rather than as an instrument of removal with exceptions attached.

There is a final historical observation that a statute pillar should record. The Act has been amended in substance once, in 1993, and its architecture has otherwise carried the whole modern history of Indian extradition, including the growth of economic offences committed across borders, the rise of offences committed through electronic networks, and the intensification of human rights scrutiny in requested courts. That durability is partly a tribute to the generality of its drafting: definitions framed by conduct and punishment rather than by offence lists in the statute itself, a gateway framed by notification rather than by enumeration, and restrictions framed as principles rather than as responses to particular cases. It is partly also a warning. A statute that changes rarely places a heavier burden on the instruments beneath it, the notified orders, the treaty texts, and the rules made under Section 36, and on the practice of assurance and documentation that no statute in this field fully captures. The reader who wants the law of Indian extradition as it operates, rather than as it stood in 1962, must therefore read all three layers together: the Act, the order for the State concerned with the treaty it sets out, and the requirements of the forum that will decide. That three layer reading is the method this article has followed throughout, and it is the method the subject demands of anyone who writes about it with authority.

## How to read a report of an extradition request

The journalist and researcher reader, whom the brief names among the payloads of this article, is best served by a short discipline for reading reports in this field, since the predictable errors are known and each corresponds to a provision of the Act. When a report states that a request has been made, the reader should ask which direction the request travels, under which treaty, arrangement or convention relationship, and whether a notified order grounds it, because a request is the opening of a process and reports that present it as an outcome invert the scheme. When a report states that a notice has been issued by an international police organisation, the reader should record that a provisional arrest may follow where the local law permits, and that nothing else has been decided. When a report states that a court has found for the requesting State, the reader should ask at which stage, since a committal or its foreign equivalent is a finding that a prima facie case exists, subject to executive decision and to appeal, and not a finding of guilt or an order that has already moved the person. When a report states that a government has approved surrender, the reader should ask whether the judicial stage has concluded and whether the interval for challenge has expired, because executive approval given before the courts have finished is a statement of intent and not the final warrant. And when a report states that a person has arrived, the reader should ask under which regime the movement occurred, extradition, deportation, transfer of a sentenced person, or voluntary return, because the protections that applied, and the limits on what may be done with the person after arrival, differ entirely between them. Five questions, each drawn from the structure of the Act, convert a confusing report into a locatable stage of a known process, which is the practical literacy this statute rewards.

## Conclusion: the court in the requested State

One sentence gathers the whole statute for a reader who retains nothing else. The Extradition Act 1962, Act 34 of 1962, in force from 5 January 1963 and recast in material respects by Act 66 of 1993, permits the surrender and return of a fugitive criminal only where a notified order has applied the Act to the State concerned, only for conduct that is an extradition offence under the definition in Section 2, only after a magistrate in the outbound case has found a prima facie case at an inquiry that is not a trial, only within the restrictions in Section 31 and the speciality limits in Sections 21 and 31, and only on the warrant of a Central Government that may at any stage stay the proceedings and discharge the person under Section 29. Every clause of that sentence is a place where a request may lawfully fail, and the enumeration of them is the shortest accurate explanation of the record this article set out to explain.

The Extradition Act 1962 endures because its architecture is sound: a government to government request to open the matter, a judicial inquiry to test it, restrictions to limit it, and an executive warrant to complete it, with the whole scheme extended State by State through notified orders so that treaty bargains govern where treaty bargains exist. The amending Act of 1993 modernised that architecture by widening the instruments that count, adding the composite offence and the convention route, refining speciality, and supplying provisions on prosecution after refusal, provisional arrest, and the death penalty. What the Act cannot do, and what no requesting State's statute can do, is decide the case in the other State's court. Extradition is decided by the court of the State from which the person is sought, applying that State's standards and that State's obligations, on the record the requesting State places before it. Requests that appear straightforward take years and frequently fail for reasons that follow from that structure: the threshold must be met abroad, the documentation must be complete and translated, the human rights objections must be answered, and the appeals must be exhausted. A reader who holds that structure in mind will read reporting on extradition, in either direction, with the right question ready: not whether a request has been made, but whether a court in the requested State has been, or can be, satisfied.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Extradition Act 1962 provide?

The Extradition Act 1962, Act 34 of 1962, in force from 5 January 1963, provides the legal machinery for surrendering a fugitive criminal from India to a foreign State and for seeking the return of an accused or convicted person from a foreign State to India. It defines an extradition offence and a fugitive criminal, applies the Act State by State through notified orders under Section 3, prescribes a requisition, a magisterial inquiry applying a prima facie standard, and a warrant of surrender issued by the Central Government, and imposes restrictions on surrender in Section 31 that may bar surrender even where the evidential threshold is met.

### Q: What does dual criminality require?

Dual criminality requires that the conduct alleged against the person sought be criminal in both the requesting State and the requested State. The test is applied to the conduct, not to the name or classification of the offence, so a requesting State must describe what the person did in enough particularity for the court in the requested State to see conduct that amounts to an offence under its own law. Conduct that is lawful in the requested State, or that is criminal there only in a trivial form below the threshold the Act sets, cannot support surrender, however seriously the requesting State treats it under its own law.

### Q: Can India extradite without a treaty?

Extradition may proceed without a bilateral treaty in two situations the Act itself supplies. The definition of an extradition treaty includes a treaty, agreement or arrangement, so the Central Government may apply the Act by notified order under Section 3 on the basis of an arrangement with a foreign State. Separately, Section 3(4) permits the Central Government, where no treaty exists, to treat a convention to which India and the foreign State are both parties as an extradition treaty for the offences that convention specifies, again by notified order. In each case the notified order is the legal foundation, and extradition cannot rest on governmental willingness alone.

### Q: What is the procedure for extraditing a person from India?

A requisition is made to the Central Government under Section 4. The Central Government may order a magistrate to inquire under Section 5, the magistrate issues a warrant under Section 6, and the inquiry proceeds under Section 7 in the manner of a case triable by a magistrate, receiving authenticated evidence in support of the requisition under Section 10 and any evidence tendered for the fugitive criminal. If a prima facie case is made out, the magistrate commits the person to prison and reports to the Central Government. After fifteen days from committal, and subject to the restrictions in Section 31 and the discharge power in Section 29, the Central Government may issue a warrant for custody and removal under Section 8.

### Q: How does India seek the return of a fugitive from abroad?

India makes a requisition through the Central Government under Section 19, transmitted through the diplomatic channel to the government of the State where the person is found, supported by the record that the law of that State and any applicable treaty require. The decisive proceedings then take place under the law of the requested State, before its own court, applying its evidential threshold and its human rights obligations, with appeals under its own system. If surrender is granted, the person is conveyed to India under Section 20, and Section 21 then limits the offences for which the person may be tried in India to those for which surrender or return was granted, subject to the exceptions that section states and to any consent the surrendering State gives.

### Q: What are the restrictions on surrender?

Section 31 bars surrender where the offence is of a political character, or the requisition is in fact made with a view to try or punish the person for an offence of a political character; where prosecution is barred by time under the law of the requesting State; unless provision is made by that State law or by treaty for the rule of speciality, confining detention and trial to the extradition offence, a lesser offence disclosed by the facts proved, or an offence to which the Central Government consents; where the person faces an accusation or is serving a sentence in India, until discharged; and until fifteen days have expired from committal to prison by the magistrate.

### Q: Why do extradition requests fail?

Official and academic material identifies recurring reasons rather than a single cause. The requested State court must be satisfied to its own evidential threshold, which a record assembled to a different standard may not reach. Documentation may be incomplete, unauthenticated, or not fully translated, and in a documentary proceeding each defect is a defect in the case. Dual criminality may fail where the conduct is not criminal in both systems. A restriction may bar surrender, including the political character bar and the absence of a speciality provision. Challenges founded on the human rights obligations of the requested State, including detention conditions and capital punishment, may succeed, and appeals through that State courts extend and sometimes end the request.

### Q: How is extradition different from deportation and a red notice?

Extradition is a judicial process under the Extradition Act 1962 or the corresponding foreign law, requiring a request between governments, a judicial inquiry or hearing in the requested State, satisfaction of an evidential threshold, compliance with statutory restrictions, and an executive warrant, and it results in surrender for a specified offence within the limits of speciality. Deportation is an immigration decision of the State where the person is present, taken under that State law on foreigners, requiring no request and no prima facie criminal case, and carrying none of extradition protections. A red notice is a request circulated among police forces to locate a person and arrest provisionally where local law permits; it is not a warrant, not an extradition request, and not by itself a legal basis for surrender.

### Q: What is a treaty State under the Extradition Act 1962?

A treaty State means a foreign State with which an extradition treaty is in operation. The expression takes its content from Section 3. Where a notified order relates to a treaty State, the order must set out the extradition treaty in full, it must not remain in force longer than the treaty, and the Central Government may subject the application of the Act to modifications, exceptions, conditions and qualifications expedient for implementing the treaty. The status therefore depends on two documents read together, the treaty and the notified order that brings the Act into operation for that State, and a State may cease to be a treaty State for the purposes of the Act if the treaty or the order ceases to be in force.

### Q: What is a composite offence under the Extradition Act 1962?

A composite offence, a definition inserted by the amending Act of 1993, means an act or conduct of a person which occurred wholly or in part in a foreign State or in India, but whose effects or intended effects, taken as a whole, would constitute an extradition offence in India or in a foreign State, as the case may be. The definition addresses conduct that crosses a border, where no single territorial fragment amounts to the complete offence. It permits the totality of the conduct and its effects to be considered together, while preserving dual criminality, because the whole must still amount to an extradition offence of the kind the Act recognises.

### Q: What happens after a magistrate commits a fugitive criminal to prison?

Committal under Section 7 means that the magistrate, satisfied that a prima facie case is made out, commits the fugitive criminal to prison to await the orders of the Central Government and reports the result of the inquiry to that Government, forwarding any written statement the person wishes to submit on the question of surrender where the magistrate thinks it proper. Committal is not surrender. A mandatory interval of fifteen days from committal must expire before surrender may be effected under Section 31(1)(e), during which the person may move the High Court and make representations. The Central Government then decides whether to issue a warrant for custody and removal under Section 8, or to stay the proceedings and order discharge under Section 29.

### Q: What is provisional arrest under Section 34B of the Extradition Act?

Section 34B, inserted by the amending Act of 1993, provides for provisional arrest on an urgent request from a foreign State, so that a person may be arrested in India pending the arrival of the formal requisition and the supporting record, rather than being free to leave the jurisdiction during the interval that documentation requires. Because provisional arrest is custody on an incomplete record, it is disciplined by Section 24: a person apprehended who is not surrendered or returned within two months of apprehension may obtain discharge on application to the High Court, on notice to the Central Government, where sufficient cause is not shown. Urgency justifies the arrest; it does not justify indefinite detention while a record is assembled.

### Q: What does Section 21 of the Extradition Act provide after return to India?

Section 21 provides that an accused or convicted person surrendered or returned by a foreign State shall not be tried in India for an offence other than the offence for which surrender or return was granted, subject to the exceptions stated in the section, until the person has been restored to the State that surrendered or returned the person, or has had an opportunity of returning to that State. The provision is the inbound form of the rule of speciality and it binds India. Its function is both protective and systemic: it protects the returned person against the addition of unrelated charges after arrival, and it preserves the confidence on which surrendering States act when they grant future Indian requests.

### Q: What is the role of the Central Government in extradition from India?

The Central Government controls the gateway, the completion and the halt. It receives the requisition under Section 4 and decides whether to order a magisterial inquiry under Section 5. After committal it decides whether to issue the warrant for custody and removal under Section 8. Under Section 30 it chooses between simultaneous requisitions from more than one foreign State, having regard to the circumstances of the case. Under Section 29 it may at any time stay proceedings, cancel a warrant and direct discharge where surrender appears unjust or inexpedient, whether for the trivial nature of the case, want of good faith, the interests of justice, or political reasons. What it cannot do is supply the prima facie finding that Section 7 reserves to the magistrate.

### Q: How does Chapter III of the Extradition Act differ from Chapter II?

Chapter II supplies the general procedure for extradition to foreign States to which Chapter III does not apply: requisition, executive order for inquiry, warrant, inquiry, committal and warrant of surrender. Chapter III governs return to foreign States with extradition arrangements and proceeds from the warrant of the requesting State, providing for endorsed warrants and provisional warrants in Sections 14 to 16, the dealing with a person when apprehended in Section 17, and return by warrant in Section 18. The procedures differ in their starting point and economy. The protections converge, because Section 32 provides that Sections 29 and 31, the discharge power and the restrictions on surrender, apply without modification to cases under Chapter III as they apply under Chapter II.

### Q: What happens if a person is not surrendered within two months of apprehension?

Section 24 addresses prolonged custody after apprehension. If a person apprehended under the Act is not surrendered or returned within two months of apprehension, the High Court may, on application made by or on behalf of the person and on notice to the Central Government, order the person to be discharged unless cause is shown against discharge. The provision is the safeguard that corresponds to provisional arrest and to arrest in anticipation of a completed requisition: the Act permits custody ahead of a completed record, and in exchange it gives the person detained a judicial remedy where the process does not move to surrender or return within the period allowed. It prevents provisional custody from becoming indefinite detention by default.

### Q: For which offences may a person surrendered to India be tried?

A person surrendered or returned to India may be tried for the offence for which surrender or return was granted. Trial for another offence committed before surrender is confined by Section 21 until the person has been restored to the surrendering State or has had an opportunity of returning there, and by any consent the surrendering State gives to the additional charge. In the outbound direction the corresponding limit is Section 31(1)(c), which bars surrender from India unless provision is made that the person will not be detained or tried abroad for an offence other than the extradition offence, a lesser offence disclosed by the facts proved other than one for which surrender could not lawfully be made, or an offence to which the Central Government consents.

### Q: What does Section 34C of the Extradition Act address?

Section 34C, inserted by the amending Act of 1993, addresses the death penalty by providing for life imprisonment in place of the death penalty in the case it specifies. Its importance reaches beyond its own terms. Capital punishment is a ground on which the courts of requested States, bound by their own law, frequently condition or refuse surrender, and a statutory provision of this kind gives the answer a legal foundation rather than leaving the matter to diplomatic assurance alone. Read with Section 34A, which provides for prosecution in India where extradition is refused, and Section 34B, which provides for provisional arrest, Section 34C forms part of the group of provisions by which the amendment of 1993 completed the original scheme of the Act.

### Q: How is the fugitive economic offender mechanism different from extradition?

The fugitive economic offender mechanism, supplied by the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act 2018, is a separate statutory process for declaring a person a fugitive economic offender and confiscating property, operating independently of extradition. Its object is status and property; the object of extradition is the surrender of a person for trial or punishment for a specified offence. A declaration and confiscation may proceed while an extradition request is pending, may proceed where no request has been made, and may be completed without the person ever being surrendered. Extradition, conversely, may succeed or fail without any confiscation occurring. The two processes also differ in forum, in the protections that apply, and in the consequences that follow, and neither should be described as a stage of the other.

### Q: When may the Central Government discharge a fugitive criminal?

Section 29 empowers the Central Government, if it appears to that Government that by reason of the trivial nature of the case, or by reason of the application for surrender or return not being made in good faith or in the interests of justice or for political reasons, or otherwise, it is unjust or inexpedient to surrender or return the fugitive criminal, to order at any time that proceedings under the Act be stayed, that any warrant issued or endorsed be cancelled, and that the person be discharged. The power may be exercised at any stage, before inquiry, after committal, or after a warrant has been issued, and Section 32 applies it without modification to cases arising under the chapter on return under extradition arrangements as well.
