Most tax statutes tax a flow. A salary is taxed as it is earned, a profit as it is made, a gain as it is realised, and when the flow stops the charge stops with it. The Black Money Act 2015 broke with that design at its foundation. Enacted as the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015, it charges tax not only on undisclosed foreign income but on the value of an undisclosed foreign asset itself, on the stock of wealth rather than the stream it produces, at a flat rate of thirty percent, with a penalty of three times the tax on top and imprisonment behind the penalty. A bank account opened decades before the statute existed, fed by money earned in another century of a family's history, could be charged on its computed value in the year the tax department found it. That single design choice, taxing the asset rather than the income, is what makes the Act one of the most severe fiscal statutes on the Indian book, and it is also what makes the Act reach far beyond the small class of deliberate offshore concealers it was announced against.

![Blog Image](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The severity is not hidden in the fine print. It sits in the arithmetic. Tax at thirty percent of the value of the asset, and a penalty equal to three times the tax computed, produces a combined exaction of one hundred and twenty percent of the asset's value for a person assessed after the one-time compliance window closed, a sum that can exceed everything the asset is worth and can exceed, in the case of a bank account, every balance the account ever held at a single moment, because the rules value a bank account by adding up the deposits made into it since it was opened. For the asset classes the statute was aimed at, concealed fortunes parked in secrecy jurisdictions by residents who had never declared them, that arithmetic was the point. For the much wider population the statute also touches, the salaried employee whose employer granted shares in a foreign parent company, the returning emigrant who kept a pension account from years of work abroad, the heir who inherited a parent's account in another country and left it unreported because nobody in the family understood the return form, the same architecture applies with the same absence of gradation. The Act contains no de minimis threshold of its own beyond a narrow bank account carve-out, and its flat penalty for a disclosure failure in the return is ten lakh rupees whether the account held the equivalent of a fortune or of a modest savings balance.

This is the tension the whole statute has to be read through. On one side stands a genuine and long-standing national problem: income earned or routed out of India and concealed abroad escaped the ordinary tax system almost completely for decades, because the ordinary system depended on the taxpayer volunteering the information and had no independent way of learning that a foreign account existed. On the other side stands a statute whose trigger is not, in most cases that arise in practice, the discovery of a hidden fortune. The trigger is the tick-box. Liability under the Act's penalty and prosecution provisions begins, for most of the people who meet it, with a failure to complete the foreign asset schedule in an ordinary income tax return, a schedule that asks about accounts and holdings that many filers did not know they were required to report, or reported incompletely, or misunderstood. The distance between the announced target, the notorious concealer, and the practical trigger, an incomplete form, is where nearly every serious argument about this statute lives.

## The Problem the Act Was Built to Solve

The Act arrived on a tide of politics as much as on one of tax policy. Unaccounted Indian wealth held abroad had been a campaign theme for years before 2015, pressed in litigation by the senior advocate Ram Jethmalani and others, who petitioned the Supreme Court for directions to recover such wealth, and the Court's intervention produced the constitution of a Special Investigation Team on black money, which the incoming government of 2014 constituted in May of that year, chaired by Justice M. B. Shah, a former judge of the Supreme Court, with Justice Arijit Pasayat as vice-chairman. The team's remit covered the lists of account holders that had begun to reach India from foreign sources, most prominently the list of entities with accounts at the Geneva branch of HSBC, a list the Supreme Court handed to the team and which the finance ministry later described as covering 628 entities. The political promise attached to the subject was sweeping, and the government's own admissions were narrower: the finance minister told the Rajya Sabha in a written reply that there was no official estimate of the black money held abroad by Indians. A statute was therefore being designed against a quantity its own government declined to state, which is one reason the design privileged demonstration and deterrence over calibrated yield.

Beneath the politics sat a structural defect in the ordinary system, and the defect deserves to be stated precisely because the Act's defenders rest much of their case on it. The Income-tax Act, 1961 had always taxed residents on their worldwide income, so foreign income of a resident was within the charge in principle from the beginning. What the ordinary system lacked was not jurisdiction but information. A resident who earned income abroad and simply did not report it faced the ordinary machinery of assessment, reassessment and penalty, but that machinery started from what the department knew or suspected, and a foreign bank account in a secrecy jurisdiction generated no Indian paper trail for the department to start from. Detection depended on leaks, on treaty requests in specific cases already suspected, and on chance. The penalty structure of the ordinary law, calibrated for under-reporting discovered in the course of assessment, was not designed for a class of concealment that was, in the ordinary course, undiscoverable. The official argument for a separate and harsher regime ran in exactly these terms: where detection is close to impossible, the price of being caught has to carry the whole deterrent weight, because the expected cost of concealment is the penalty multiplied by a probability of detection that stood near zero.

The international environment was shifting at the same moment, and the timing of the Act cannot be separated from that shift. Through the years after the financial crisis of 2008, the automatic exchange of financial account information between tax administrations was being built into a global system, through the United States' Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act and through the Common Reporting Standard developed under the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. India committed early to that system. The significance for the statute is double. First, the promise of automatic information flows made a disclosure statute credible in a way it could not have been a decade earlier, because the threat behind it, that the department would learn of the account without any leak or tip-off, was becoming mechanically true. Second, and this is the honest counterweight that any account of the Act has to carry, it was the information system and not the statute that changed detectability. The Act supplied the consequences that would follow detection. The detection itself was supplied by treaty machinery, reporting rules and data standards built outside the Act, a point that returns in the assessment of what the statute achieved.

A further strand of the problem was the character of the earlier enforcement record. The Special Investigation Team's work on the HSBC list produced assessments and prosecutions in a limited set of cases; the finance ministry reported to Parliament that prosecutions had been launched in dozens of cases on that list by late 2015, with assessments completed in a larger number. Whatever the figures, the process was slow, list-bound and dependent on material that arrived by accident of a leak in a foreign bank. The legislative conclusion drawn in the Budget of 2015 was that a permanent regime, with its own charge, its own penalties, its own prosecution chapter and a one-time opportunity to regularise the past at a stated price, would convert an episodic enforcement story into a standing rule. The finance minister, Arun Jaitley, announced the measure in his Budget speech of 2015 and introduced the Bill in the Lok Sabha on 20 March 2015, under the name of the Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets (Imposition of Tax) Bill, 2015. The words Black Money entered the title during the Bill's passage through Parliament, a renaming that announced the statute's political identity as plainly as its long title announced its legal one.

## What Existed Before 2015, and Why It Was Judged Insufficient

To see what the Act changed, the prior position has to be assembled from four separate bodies of law, because no single one of them owned the subject. The first was the Income-tax Act itself. A person resident in India was chargeable on income accruing anywhere in the world, and foreign income that was concealed was taxable under the ordinary provisions when discovered, with interest and with penalties for concealment that ran, under the provisions then in force, between one hundred and three hundred percent of the tax sought to be evaded. The reassessment machinery allowed the department to reopen past years where income had escaped assessment, within limitation periods that depended on the amount involved and, for assets located outside India, had been extended by the Finance Act, 2012 to sixteen years. The second body of law was the disclosure obligation built into the return itself. The same Finance Act, 2012 had amended Section 139 of the Income-tax Act, with retrospective effect from 1 April 2012, to make the filing of a return compulsory for a resident person who held any asset, including a financial interest in any entity, located outside India, or who had signing authority in any account located outside India, even where the person had no taxable income at all. From assessment year 2012-13 the return forms carried Schedule FA, the schedule of foreign assets, and the obligation to complete it fell on residents other than those not ordinarily resident. The third body of law was the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, which regulated the holding of foreign assets as a matter of exchange control, treating contraventions as civil liabilities attracting monetary penalties rather than as crimes in the ordinary run of cases. The fourth was the general criminal law of tax evasion under the Income-tax Act, whose prosecution provisions existed but were sparingly used for offshore matters and carried sentences far below what the new statute would prescribe.

Measured against the problem as the government described it, each element had a gap. The Income-tax Act taxed income, and an asset acquired long ago out of income that could no longer be identified or proved presented a charging difficulty: the department might find the asset and still struggle to charge the historic income that created it, year by year, within limitation. The ordinary penalty regime was severe on paper but attached to tax evaded on income, and the same identification difficulty weakened it. Schedule FA created a disclosure duty but its breach, standing alone, was policed through the general penalty and prosecution provisions of the Income-tax Act, which were not built around foreign assets and which many practitioners regarded as uncertain in application to a pure failure to report an asset that had produced no taxable income in India. Foreign exchange law treated the holding itself through a regulatory lens, with compounding and civil penalty, and its object was the management of foreign exchange rather than the taxation of concealed wealth. Nothing in the prior law charged the asset. Nothing priced the act of non-disclosure itself at a confiscatory level. Nothing combined a disclosure window, a dedicated charge and a prosecution regime in one code.

The counter-argument, made at the time and developed since, is that the gaps were gaps of enforcement and information more than of legislative power, and that the Income-tax Act's combination of worldwide charge, extended reassessment for foreign assets, concealment penalties and prosecution was a sufficient legal armoury if the department had the data. On this reading the new statute's real additions were three: it removed the need to identify and charge historic income by charging the asset's value directly; it multiplied the financial consequences beyond the ordinary scale; and it created the window, a one-time priced exit that the ordinary law had no mechanism to offer with equivalent legal protection for the declarant. Those three additions are exactly where the argument about justification has to be joined, and the rest of this article takes them in turn after setting out how the statute is put together.

## How the Act Is Put Together

The Act, numbered 22 of 2015, received the assent of the President on 26 May 2015. It contains eighty-eight sections arranged in seven chapters, and its architecture is that of a complete fiscal code in miniature. It does not amend the Income-tax Act into a new shape. It stands beside it, with its own definitions, its own charge, its own assessment machinery, its own penalties, its own offences and its own appellate ladder, borrowing the administrative personnel of the income-tax department rather than creating a new service. Chapter One carries the title, the commencement provision and the definitions, among which the definitions of the assessee and of the undisclosed asset located outside India do the real work. Chapter Two, headed Tax Management, contains the charge itself and the rules of computation. Chapter Three deals with assessment, demand and recovery. Chapter Four contains the penalties. Chapter Five contains the offences and prosecutions. Chapter Six contains the one-time compliance window, the declaration scheme that operated for a single season in 2015 and then closed forever. Chapter Seven is miscellaneous: it holds, among other provisions, the power to enter agreements with foreign governments for the exchange of information, the removal of difficulties power, and the deeming provision that gives the asset charge its reach into the past.

One detail of the commencement deserves attention because it later reached the Supreme Court. Section 1(3) as enacted provided that the Act would come into force on 1 April 2016. The compliance window in Chapter Six, however, was designed to open at once, on dates to be notified, and the whole scheme of inviting declarations in 2015 made no sense if the parent Act slept until the following April. The government therefore used the removal of difficulties power in Section 86 and, by Notification No. 56 of 2015, issued on 1 July 2015 under the signature S.O. 1790(E), substituted 1 July 2015 as the date of commencement. The Act is accordingly treated as in force from 1 July 2015, while its charge, by the terms of Section 3, applies in relation to assessment years commencing on or after 1 April 2016. The legality of advancing the commencement date in this manner was challenged and was upheld by the Supreme Court in Union of India v. Gautam Khaitan, reported at [2020] 420 ITR 140, on the reasoning that the notified dates for the window would otherwise have lapsed before the Act began, defeating the scheme Parliament had enacted. The weight-bearing provisions, for any reader navigating the statute, are few: Section 2(2) and Section 2(11) define who is caught and what is caught; Section 3 charges; Section 4 fixes the scope of the charge; Section 5 denies deductions and grants a limited credit for income already taxed; Section 10 starts assessment on information; Section 41 sets the great penalty; Sections 42 and 43 police the return; Sections 49 to 51 carry the imprisonment terms; Sections 59 to 63 ran the window; and Section 72(c) deems an undeclared asset of earlier years to have been acquired in the year a notice is issued.

The personnel point matters more than it might appear. Section 6 designates the income-tax authorities as the tax authorities under the Act, and Section 8 gives them the powers of a civil court for the purposes of the statute. There is no separate black money directorate created by the Act, no new cadre, no dedicated tribunal. The same assessing officer who handles a resident's ordinary assessment can become the assessing officer under this Act on receiving information, and the same appellate forums hear the appeals. The statute's distinctness is legal rather than institutional: a separate code administered by a familiar machine. That design kept the administrative cost of the regime low and its integration with the ordinary system high, because information gathered in an ordinary assessment, a search, or a treaty exchange could flow into a proceeding under this Act without crossing an institutional boundary.

## Who the Act Binds: Residence as the Gate

Every obligation and every exposure under the Act passes through a single gate: residence. As enacted, Section 2(2) defined an assessee as a person resident in India, other than a person not ordinarily resident, within the meaning of the Income-tax Act. The cross-reference imports the tests of Section 6 of that Act, under which an individual is resident on the strength of physical presence in India for the prescribed periods, and is not ordinarily resident where the person's history of presence and residence in earlier years falls within the exceptions Section 6(6) sets out. The consequence is that the Act, in its original form, bound the class known in tax practice as resident and ordinarily resident: persons whose lives and presence centred in India. A non-resident stood outside the charge altogether, and a resident but not ordinarily resident person, the returning emigrant in the first years after coming back or the Indian citizen working abroad who crosses the residence threshold only lightly, stood outside it as well, even though such a person might hold substantial assets abroad acquired during years of foreign employment.

The definitions then fix what counts as the taxable subject matter. Section 2(11) defines an undisclosed asset located outside India as an asset, expressly including a financial interest in any entity, located outside India, held by the assessee in the assessee's name or in respect of which the assessee is a beneficial owner, and on which the assessee has no explanation about the source of investment in it, or offers an explanation that the assessing officer considers unsatisfactory. Two features of that definition repay attention. The first is the reach of beneficial ownership: an asset held through another name, a company, a trust or a nominee is within the definition where the assessee is the beneficial owner, so the interposition of a structure does not by itself take an asset out of the charge. The second is the evidential cast of the definition: an asset is undisclosed not merely because it was never reported, but because its source of investment stands unexplained or unsatisfactorily explained. The definition builds the inquiry into the charge itself. Section 2(12) completes the pair by defining undisclosed foreign income and asset compendiously, covering income from a source located outside India that was not disclosed in a return furnished within the time allowed, income for which no return was furnished though one was required, and the value of an undisclosed asset.

The residence gate was redrawn by the Finance Act, 2019, and the redraw is one of the most consequential amendments in the Act's history. With retrospective effect from 1 July 2015, the substituted Section 2(2) covers two classes. The first limb covers a person resident in India in the previous year. The second limb reaches a person who is non-resident, or not ordinarily resident, in the previous year, but who was resident in India in the previous year to which the undisclosed income relates, or in the previous year in which the undisclosed asset was acquired, with a proviso directing that the year of acquisition be determined without giving effect to the deeming fiction in Section 72(c). In plain terms, a person who acquired a foreign asset while resident, then left India and gave up residence, does not walk out of the Act by leaving: the charge can follow the person into later non-residence, because the asset was acquired in a year of residence. The same amendment did not, however, rewrite the return-related provisions. Sections 42 and 43, the ten lakh rupee penalties, and Sections 49, 50 and 51(1), the principal offences, continue to be keyed in their own text to a person resident other than not ordinarily resident. The result is an asymmetry that practitioners debate: the charge, after 2019, is wider than some of the penalty and prosecution provisions that police it, and the width of each provision has to be read in its own words rather than assumed from the definition section.

The amendment also created the sharpest of the Act's fairness problems, which later sections of this article return to. The compliance window of 2015 had been framed for the world of the original Section 2(2); a person who was non-resident in 2015, and outside the charge as then understood, was under no obligation to declare, and the Central Board of Direct Taxes' Circular No. 13 of 2015, answering a question on the point, treated such a person as eligible to use the window voluntarily rather than required to. When the 2019 amendment retrospectively widened the class of assessees, no fresh window accompanied the widening. A person retrospectively brought within the charge had never been offered the priced exit that residents had received in 2015, and the full post-window consequences, tax plus threefold penalty plus prosecution exposure, were the only terms on offer. Published commentary by the law firm Lakshmikumaran and Sridharan, examining the interaction of the deeming provision with the amendment, has argued that the deeming fiction cannot fairly be applied to persons who were outside the Act in the window year and had neither a disclosure obligation nor a window of their own. That argument is stated here as the position of its authors; the wider question of the amendment's reach remains contested ground rather than settled law, and it is treated at length in the discussion of temporal reach below.

## The Returning Resident and the Not Ordinarily Resident

The residence gate has a human geography, and its most travelled path is the return. Under the tests the Act borrows from Section 6 of the Income-tax Act, an individual who has lived and worked abroad for many years and comes back to India will ordinarily pass through a transitional status: resident but not ordinarily resident. Section 6(6) of the Income-tax Act treats a resident individual as not ordinarily resident where the person has been non-resident in nine of the ten previous years preceding the relevant year, or has been present in India for seven hundred and twenty-nine days or fewer during the seven previous years. The status is a bridge, typically lasting two to three years after a long absence, and during it the ordinary charge itself is narrower, since a person not ordinarily resident is taxed in India on foreign income only where it is derived from a business controlled in, or a profession set up in, India. The Black Money Act as enacted respected that bridge completely: the assessee definition stopped at the ordinarily resident line, so the returning emigrant's retained foreign accounts, pension entitlements and investments stood outside the Act during the bridge years, and the Schedule FA obligation in the Income-tax Act is likewise addressed to residents other than those not ordinarily resident.

The practical danger arrives at the end of the bridge. In the first year of ordinary residence, every retained foreign holding becomes disclosable in Schedule FA, and the holder who has filed Indian returns during the bridge years without the schedule, correctly, must begin completing it in the transition year, often without any adviser flagging the change, because nothing about the assets has changed: only the holder's status has. An omission in that year is a Section 43 default. The Act thus places one of its sharpest traps at a point of administrative invisibility, a status transition that occurs by operation of day counts rather than by any act of the taxpayer. The 2019 amendment to the assessee definition left the return-stage sections keyed to the ordinarily resident class, as noted earlier, but widened the charge in a way that bears on the same population in reverse: the emigrant who leaves India, rather than the immigrant who returns, may remain within the charge for assets acquired during the years of residence, so that departure no longer closes the file. Between the two movements, arrival and departure, the Act's message to the mobile population is consistent and demanding: years of Indian residence create a disclosure relationship with the Indian state that travel does not dissolve, and the records of the foreign years must be kept in a form that can be produced in India long after the foreign life has ended.

## The Charge: Tax on the Income and on the Asset Itself

Section 3(1) is the heart of the statute, and it is short. In respect of every assessment year commencing on or after 1 April 2016, tax is charged at the rate of thirty percent on the total undisclosed foreign income and asset of the previous year of every person who is an assessee under the Act. There are no slabs, no surcharge structure of the ordinary kind worked into the charging words, no basic exemption, and no distinction between the salary earner and the industrialist. The rate is flat because the subject matter is defined as undisclosed: the statute's premise is that everything within its charge has already had its opportunity to be taxed gently under the ordinary law and declined it. Section 4 then fixes the scope of the total. It comprises income from a source located outside India which the assessee failed to disclose in a return furnished within the time limits of Section 139 of the Income-tax Act; income from such a source in respect of which no return was furnished at all, where the assessee was required to furnish one; and the value of an undisclosed asset located outside India. Section 4(2) strips away the ordinary computational machinery, directing that the variations and adjustments of the Income-tax Act's computation provisions do not apply, and Section 4(3) draws the boundary against double taxation of the same income by providing that income charged under this Act does not form part of the total income under the Income-tax Act.

### Why does the Act tax the asset itself rather than the income it produced?

The asset charge exists because historic income is hard to tax. Income earned abroad decades earlier may be impossible to identify year by year and assess within limitation, while the asset it created sits visible in a bank record or a property register. Charging the asset's value in the year of discovery converts an unprovable history into a taxable event.

The mechanism by which an old asset enters a charge that operates from assessment year 2016-17 is the proviso to Section 3(1), and it is the most litigated sentence in the Act. The proviso directs that, in the case of an undisclosed asset, tax is charged on the value of the asset in the previous year in which the asset comes to the notice of the assessing officer. The charge therefore does not pretend that the asset was acquired in the notice year. It does something quieter and, for the assessee, more consequential: it selects the value in the notice year as the tax base, whatever the year of acquisition and whatever the value then. An asset bought cheaply long ago and worth a great deal when discovered is charged on the discovered value, subject to the single relief in Section 5. Section 3(2) completes the charging scheme by providing that the value of an undisclosed asset is its fair market value as prescribed, which hands the operative detail to the rules examined in the next section.

Section 5 states what may be called the Act's economy of denials and its one concession. In computing the total undisclosed foreign income and asset, no deduction in respect of any expenditure or allowance is allowed, and no set-off of any loss carried forward from any earlier year under the Income-tax Act, or of any loss under this Act itself, is permitted. The charge is on the gross. The concession is Section 5(1)(ii): where an asset was acquired out of income that has been assessed, or was assessable, under the Income-tax Act or under this Act, the value charged is reduced to the extent of that taxed source, so that money already through the tax net is not charged a second time merely because the asset it bought was not reported. For immovable property, Section 5(2) applies the concession proportionately where the consideration was paid partly from taxed income and partly from untaxed sources. The Act illustrates its own rule: a house acquired for fifty lakh rupees, of which twenty lakh had been offered to tax, and worth one crore rupees in the year it comes to notice, is charged on sixty lakh rupees, being the untaxed three-fifths of the noticed value. The illustration is a fair emblem of the regime: the relief is real, but it relieves only the taxed fraction, and the appreciation on the untaxed fraction is charged in full.

Two structural features of the charge deserve emphasis before the detail of valuation. The first is the absence of any credit for foreign tax. The Income-tax Act, through its relief provisions and India's tax treaties, ordinarily relieves double taxation of foreign income by crediting the foreign tax paid. The Black Money Act's charge is self-contained: it allows the Section 5 reduction for income already taxed in India, but its text grants no credit for tax paid abroad on the same income or asset, and Section 4(2)'s exclusion of the ordinary computation provisions underlines the separation. A person whose foreign income bore tax in the country where it arose, but who failed to disclose it in India, faces the Indian charge on the gross without a statutory credit for the foreign levy. The second feature is the relationship with the ordinary charge in the other direction. Income from foreign sources that is disclosed in the Indian return stays where it always was, inside the Income-tax Act, taxed at the ordinary rates with treaty relief; it is only non-disclosure that moves value into this Act. The boundary between the two statutes is therefore not the location of the income but the act of disclosure, which is why the return, and the schedule within the return, carry the weight they do in this regime.

## Valuation: What an Undisclosed Asset Is Worth Under the Act

Because the charge falls on value, the valuation rules determine the real size of the liability, and Rule 3 of the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Rules, 2015, notified on 3 July 2015, is where the regime's severity becomes concrete. For immovable property, the value is the higher of the cost of acquisition and the price the property would ordinarily fetch in the open market on the valuation date, supported by a report of a recognised valuer. For quoted shares and securities, the value is the higher of the cost of acquisition and the average of the highest and lowest prices quoted on the valuation date. For unquoted shares and other assets, the higher-of principle applies again, cost against open market price determined through a recognised valuer. The pattern is consistent: the statute takes the most favourable measure available to the revenue between what was paid and what the asset is worth when found.

The rule for a bank account is different in kind, and it is the single most startling computation in the regime. The value of a bank account is the sum of all the deposits made in the account since the date of its opening, excluding deposits made from withdrawals out of the same account. Work through what that means. An account used actively over twenty years, receiving salary credits, transfers and rents and paying them out again, accumulates deposits that may be a large multiple of any balance the account ever held at one time. The redeposit exclusion protects only the narrow case of money withdrawn and put back; money that arrived, left for good, and was replaced by fresh receipts counts in full. The charge of thirty percent, and the penalty of ninety percent computed on the tax, are then applied to that cumulative figure. It is entirely possible, on the face of the rules, for the combined demand to exceed the highest balance the account ever reached, and to exceed the value of everything the account holder owns abroad. Defenders of the rule answer that an account's deposits are the best available proxy for the volume of undisclosed flows it carried, and that a balance-based measure would reward the holder who emptied the account before discovery. Critics answer that a proxy which can exceed the wealth itself has stopped measuring anything and has become a punishment computed on a fiction. Both descriptions are accurate about different aspects of the same rule, and the rule's defenders and critics are really arguing about whether the asset charge is a tax at all in such a case, or a fine wearing the dress of a tax.

Valuation interacts with the Section 5 concession in ways that reward documentation and punish its absence. The reduction for a taxed source depends on showing that the investment came out of income assessed or assessable in India, and for an old asset that showing has to be built from old returns, old bank records and old remittance papers that families rarely keep in complete form. The practical burden of the regime therefore falls not only on the fact of non-disclosure but on the quality of a family's paper across decades. Two holders of identical accounts, one with a preserved trail of taxed remittances and one without, face materially different charges on identical facts of concealment, because the second cannot prove the taxed fraction. That evidentiary tilt is a deliberate feature of a statute whose definition of an undisclosed asset is itself framed around the absence of a satisfactory explanation of the source of investment.

## Three Computations: The Act at Work

Abstract severity is easy to misjudge, so the regime is best tested against concrete cases, computed strictly on the provisions set out above. Take first the case the statute was designed for. A resident holds an account in a secrecy jurisdiction into which business receipts were diverted over many years, never disclosed in any return, and the account comes to the Assessing Officer's notice through an exchange of information. The deposits into the account since it was opened total two crore rupees, excluding redeposits of its own withdrawals. Under Rule 3 that sum is the value of the asset. Tax at thirty percent is sixty lakh rupees. The Section 41 penalty at three times the tax is one crore and eighty lakh rupees. The combined demand is two crore and forty lakh rupees against an account whose balance on any given day may have been a fraction of the deposits that passed through it, and behind the demand stands Section 51(1), a wilful attempt to evade tax, carrying three to ten years. Whatever view is taken of the policy, the case shows the regime working as designed: the concealed flow is treated as forfeit, with a margin, and the historic income that fed the account never has to be reconstructed year by year.

Take next the Act's own illustration, which Parliament placed in the Explanation to Section 5(2), worked here in full because it is the statute's chosen self-portrait. A house abroad was acquired in an earlier year for fifty lakh rupees. Twenty lakh of the price came from income that had been offered to tax in India; thirty lakh did not. In the year the house comes to the notice of the Assessing Officer its value is one crore rupees. The reduction under Section 5 follows the taxed proportion: twenty lakh of fifty lakh is two-fifths, and two-fifths of the noticed value, forty lakh rupees, is relieved. The charge falls on sixty lakh rupees. Tax at thirty percent is eighteen lakh rupees, and the Section 41 penalty, if directed at the full multiple, is fifty-four lakh rupees, a combined seventy-two lakh rupees on an asset worth one crore, of which two-fifths was bought with money that had borne Indian tax. The illustration repays a second look. The relief is genuine but proportionate only to the taxed fraction of the original price; the appreciation attributable to the untaxed fraction is charged in full, and the penalty multiple applies to the whole tax. Even the statute's own worked example, designed to display its fairness mechanism, ends with an exaction of seventy-two percent of the asset's value.

The same account, taken through the window in 2015, prices the design from the other side. Had the holder declared the account during the compliance window, the charge would have been tax at thirty percent of the value together with penalty equal to that tax, sixty lakh rupees of tax and sixty lakh rupees of penalty on the two crore rupees of deposits, a combined one crore and twenty lakh rupees, and the matter would have closed with the protection of Section 67 behind the declaration. Declining the window doubled the price and added the prosecution chapter to it. The pair of figures, sixty percent inside the window and one hundred and twenty percent outside it, was the whole of the government's salesmanship in the summer of 2015, and the small number of holders who paid the lower figure is the measure of how few believed the higher one would ever be exacted.

Take finally the case at the other pole, the one the proportionality debate is about. An employee of an Indian subsidiary of a foreign company is allotted shares in the parent under a stock plan, held in an overseas account, worth in aggregate thirty lakh rupees. The dividends are small, the tax on them smaller, and through an adviser's error or the employee's own misunderstanding the holding is not entered in Schedule FA for several years, though the salary from which nothing was hidden is fully taxed. No charge under Section 3 need arise if the source of investment, salary savings and the employer's allotment, is explained to the Assessing Officer's satisfaction; the asset is not undisclosed in the definitional sense once its source is satisfactorily explained. But the return default is complete on its own terms: Section 43 describes it exactly, and the penalty the section prescribes is ten lakh rupees, one third of the value of the holding, for an omission that cost the revenue nothing or next to nothing. The 2024 amendment relieves holdings of this size for the future, since the carve-out extends to assets other than immovable property up to twenty lakh rupees in aggregate, though thirty lakh rupees of shares still stands outside it. The three cases together map the Act's terrain: a regime of forfeiture for the deliberate concealer, a charge tempered by proportionate relief for the partly documented asset, and a reporting penalty of real weight for the ordinary holder whose only failure was a form. Any judgment of the statute that does not hold all three cases in view at once is a judgment of a part presented as the whole.

## The Trigger: A Schedule in an Ordinary Return

For most people who ever meet this statute, the encounter does not begin with a search, a summons or a foreign bank. It begins with a return of income. The Finance Act 2012 had already amended Section 139 of the Income-tax Act, with retrospective effect from 1 April 2012, to oblige a resident, other than a person not ordinarily resident, who held any asset outside India, including a financial interest in any entity, or who had signing authority in an account located outside India, to furnish a return whether or not that person had taxable income at all. From assessment year 2012-13 the return forms carried a dedicated schedule, Schedule FA, in which the resident declared foreign bank accounts, financial interests in entities, immovable property, custodial accounts, equity and debt interests, and accounts over which the resident merely held signing authority. The Black Money Act did not create this disclosure duty; it armed it. What had been a reporting obligation inside the ordinary income tax system, described in full in the pillar account of [the Income-tax Act 1961 and the framework it built](/2012/07/01/income-tax-act-1961-complete-guide/), acquired in 2015 a dedicated penal machinery of its own, and the schedule became the hinge on which the entire foreign asset regime swings.

The point deserves emphasis because it explains both the reach and the grievance of the regime. The population obliged to complete Schedule FA is not a population of suspected evaders. It is the ordinary filing population of residents who hold anything abroad: the software professional whose employer in the United States allotted shares under an employee stock plan, the consultant who kept a bank account from a posting in Singapore, the parent who is a joint holder on a child's account overseas, the returning resident who retained a retirement account from years of work in the Gulf or in Britain, the spouse named as nominee, the director with signing authority over a company's foreign account who owns nothing in it. Each of these holdings is lawful. Each is taxable in India only in the ordinary way, on income arising from it, and each becomes a problem under the Black Money Act only through the disclosure step: fail to enter it in the schedule, or enter it inaccurately, and the statute's penalty and prosecution provisions are engaged by the omission itself, irrespective of whether any tax was evaded on the income the asset produced.

This is the sense in which the trigger is the tick-box. The Act's charge on undisclosed assets does require an absence of explanation about the source of investment, and an Assessing Officer who proceeds on the charge must engage with whatever explanation the assessee offers. But Sections 42 and 43, the provisions that operate at the return stage, do not wait for evasion to be proved. Section 42 imposes a penalty of ten lakh rupees on a person, resident other than not ordinarily resident, who holds a foreign asset, a beneficial interest in one, or foreign income, and fails to furnish a return within the time allowed. Section 43 imposes the same penalty where a return was furnished but the person failed to furnish the information required about the foreign asset, or furnished inaccurate particulars. The failure is the offence against the section. A taxpayer who paid every rupee of tax due on dividends from foreign shares, but left the shareholding out of Schedule FA, has committed the precise default Section 43 describes. A taxpayer who filed a return for salary income and omitted a dormant account holding a small balance has done the same. The statute thus operates on two planes at once: a charge plane, aimed at wealth whose source cannot be explained, and a reporting plane, aimed at the completeness of the return itself, and it is the reporting plane that touches the far larger population.

Parliament did write one relief into the reporting plane, and its limits matter. As enacted, the provisos to Sections 42 and 43 disapplied those sections where the only foreign holding was one or more bank accounts whose aggregate balance did not exceed five lakh rupees at any time during the previous year, valued at the State Bank of India telegraphic transfer buying rate. The carve-out recognised that a trivial account should not carry a ten lakh rupee penalty. But it was confined to bank accounts. It did not extend to a small shareholding, a small interest in a fund, or a small property interest; it did not help the employee whose stock plan shares were worth four lakh rupees; and five lakh rupees is itself a modest line against the cost of foreign education accounts and expatriate savings that ordinary families hold. The widening of this carve-out nearly a decade later, by the Finance (No. 2) Act of 2024, is taken up in the section on proportionality below. As enacted in 2015, the architecture was uncompromising: a reporting duty of great breadth, a flat penalty for its breach, and a relief drawn narrowly around small bank balances alone.

Above the penalties sits the prosecution plane. Section 49 makes wilful failure to furnish a return in due time, by a person within the disclosure class, punishable with rigorous imprisonment for a term between six months and seven years with fine; Section 50 does the same for wilful failure to furnish the information about a foreign asset in a return that was filed. The word wilful does real work in these sections, since it imports a mental element the department must establish, and Section 49 carries the further safeguard that no prosecution lies where the return is furnished before the expiry of the assessment year. But the headline remains arresting: the failure to complete a schedule, if wilful, is an imprisonable offence carrying up to seven years, a severity that has no parallel elsewhere in the return machinery of Indian tax law, where late or defective filing is ordinarily a matter of fees, interest and monetary penalty. The disproportion between the gravity of the typical default and the gravity of the threatened consequence is the ground on which the proportionality objection, examined below, is built.

## Assessment: A Proceeding That Begins With Information

The assessment machinery of the Act is deliberately unlike the ordinary assessment machinery of the Income-tax Act, and the differences are structural. There is no concept of a routine annual assessment of foreign assets, no scrutiny selection of returns, no reassessment window of the kind Section 147 of the Income-tax Act creates. Section 6 of the Act constitutes the income-tax authorities as the authorities for its purposes, and Section 10 empowers the Assessing Officer to proceed where information is in his possession: information received from an income-tax authority, from any other authority, or coming to his notice in any other manner, suggesting that undisclosed foreign income or an undisclosed asset is chargeable under the Act. On such information he may serve a notice calling on the person to produce accounts, documents and other evidence, and, after considering what is produced and giving the person an opportunity of being heard, assess the total undisclosed foreign income and asset and the tax payable. Where the person fails to comply with the notice, the officer may proceed to a best judgment assessment on the material available, again after an opportunity of being heard.

### Is there a time limit for starting a Black Money Act assessment?

No. The Act prescribes no limitation period for issuing the notice under Section 10, so a proceeding may begin whenever information reaches the Assessing Officer, however old the asset. The time discipline operates at the other end: Section 11 requires the order within two years from the end of the financial year of the notice, excluding exchange-of-information reference periods.

The absence of a limitation period for commencing proceedings is one of the most consequential procedural choices in the statute, and it pairs with the charge's design. Under the Income-tax Act, reassessment is fenced by limitation periods that lengthen for foreign assets but still expire; the passage of enough time eventually closes a year. Under the Black Money Act, time alone closes nothing. An account opened in the nineteen-nineties can be taken up whenever information about it surfaces, because the charge, as the valuation discussion below explains, is not laid on the year of acquisition at all but is computed on the asset's value in the previous year in which it comes to the officer's notice. The two features together, an information-triggered start with no limitation clock and a charge keyed to the year of notice, mean that the age of an asset is no protection under this Act. What the statute offers in place of a limitation period is the two-year completion clock in Section 11, which disciplines the proceeding once begun, and the ordinary appellate discipline that follows the order.

The remaining assessment provisions are workmanlike. Section 12 permits rectification of a mistake apparent from the record within four years. Section 13 governs the notice of demand that follows an assessment, and Sections 30 to 32 carry the recovery scheme: tax is payable within thirty days of the demand notice, a defaulter is an assessee in default, and the Tax Recovery Officer may proceed by certificate, with garnishee-type notices available against persons holding money for the assessee. Sections 13 and 14 also make clear that assessing the person in whose name an asset stands does not bar assessment of the person for whose benefit the income or asset is held, a provision that works with the beneficial ownership limb of the definition of an undisclosed asset. Interest, where advance tax obligations were not met, is charged by reference to the machinery of Sections 234B and 234C of the Income-tax Act, computed in accordance with those provisions, so the Act borrows the ordinary interest code rather than writing a new one. Practitioners assembling the papers for such a proceeding, often years of foreign statements, valuations and source documents gathered into a single presentation for the client and the record, will find that a browser-based [PowerPoint viewer for reviewing a disclosure file](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) is a practical way to open the deck without moving the underlying documents between machines.

The best judgment power in Section 10 deserves a sentence of its own, because it disciplines the entire conduct of a proceeding. A person who ignores the notice, or who answers it with silence on the source of an investment, does not stall the assessment: the officer proceeds on the material in his possession, which in an exchange-of-information case includes the foreign reporting itself, and computes the charge on that footing after giving an opportunity of being heard. Non-cooperation therefore carries no tactical reward under this Act. It merely surrenders the explanation stage, the one stage at which the holder's own documents can reduce the charge under Section 5 or take the asset outside the definition altogether, and it leaves the valuation rules to operate on the department's figures unopposed.

## The Penalty Architecture

Chapter IV of the Act distributes its penalties across five provisions, and their calibration tells the story of the statute's priorities. The central provision is Section 41. Where the Assessing Officer has computed tax under Section 10 on undisclosed foreign income or an undisclosed asset, he may direct the assessee to pay, by way of penalty, a sum equal to three times the tax so computed. The language is permissive in form, may direct, and penalty proceedings under the Act carry the usual procedural clothing: Section 46 requires that no penalty order be made without a show-cause notice and a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and Section 47 requires the penalty order to be made within one year from the end of the financial year in which the show-cause proceedings were initiated. But the scale is fixed by the multiple. Tax at thirty percent plus penalty at three times the tax produces the figure by which the Act is known: a total exaction of one hundred and twenty percent of the chargeable value. For an asset valued at one crore rupees, the tax is thirty lakh rupees, the penalty ninety lakh rupees, and the combined demand of one crore and twenty lakh rupees exceeds the value of the asset itself. The statute does not treat this as an accident of arithmetic. It is the arithmetic.

Set beside Section 41, the return-stage penalties of Sections 42 and 43, each a flat ten lakh rupees, look almost modest, and for a substantial evader they are. Their bite falls at the other end of the scale. Ten lakh rupees is a rounding error against a concealed fortune of fifty crore; it is a crushing sum against a bank account holding the equivalent of six lakh rupees, and the enacted carve-out stopped at five lakh rupees of bank balance. The remaining penalty provisions complete the chapter in more conventional terms. Section 44 imposes, on an assessee in default in payment of arrears, a penalty equal to the amount of the arrears themselves, and Section 45 provides penalties between fifty thousand rupees and two lakh rupees for specified procedural defaults committed without reasonable cause, the one place in the chapter where Parliament wrote a band rather than a fixed figure.

### Can the penalty under this Act exceed the value of the asset itself?

Yes, in two ways. Tax at thirty percent combined with the Section 41 penalty of three times the tax reaches one hundred and twenty percent of the value charged, so the demand exceeds the asset by design. The ten lakh rupee return penalty, meanwhile, bears no proportion to the holding and can exceed a small account's balance many times over.

The design question the chapter poses is whether a penalty structure of this shape can distinguish between the cases it must distinguish between. At the top end, against the deliberate concealer of crores, a one hundred and twenty percent exaction is a considered legislative judgment: the concealed asset is treated as forfeit with a margin, on the reasoning that a lesser price would simply be a cost of concealment discounted by the low probability of detection. At the bottom end, the same structure meets defaults of a wholly different character through the return-stage sections, and there the absence of graduation is hardest to defend. The Act contains no threshold of materiality for the Section 41 penalty beyond the tax computed, no discretion spelled out to scale the penalty to the gravity of the default beyond the permissive may, and no statutory distinction between the asset concealed for thirty years and the asset omitted from one year's schedule in error and disclosed in the next. The defence of the structure, and the criticism of it, are both developed at full strength in the sections on the counter-reading below. What matters for understanding the Act as enacted is that the severity is not a drafting excess. It was the announced policy: the finance minister told Parliament that a person who did not use the compliance window would face tax and penalty of one hundred and twenty percent and prosecution in addition, and the enacted multiples deliver exactly that figure.

## Offences: Imprisonment and a Reversed Burden

Chapter V converts the fiscal regime into a criminal one, and two of its features place it among the most demanding prosecution chapters in Indian tax law. The first is the spread and weight of the offences. Section 49 punishes wilful failure to furnish a return in due time with rigorous imprisonment of between six months and seven years; Section 50 punishes wilful failure to furnish the information about a foreign asset in a return that was furnished, or the furnishing of information known to be false, with the same range. Section 51(1) addresses the substantive case, a wilful attempt to evade tax, penalty or interest chargeable under the Act, by a person within the resident class, and raises the range to rigorous imprisonment of between three years and ten years with fine; Section 51(2) separately punishes a wilful attempt to evade payment, with imprisonment of three months to three years. Section 52 punishes a false statement in a verification made under the Act with six months to seven years, Section 53 punishes abetment, including abetment by a bank or financial institution that aids concealment, with the same range, and Section 58 provides that a second or subsequent offence carries rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years and a fine of between five lakh rupees and one crore rupees. Section 48 prefaces the chapter by providing that its provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law, and that prosecution does not depend on the outcome of any assessment or other proceeding under the Act: the criminal track runs on its own rails.

The second feature is Section 54. In a prosecution under the Act, the culpable mental state of the accused is presumed, and Section 54(2) provides that the accused may rebut the presumption only by proving the absence of that mental state beyond reasonable doubt. Reverse burdens are not unknown in Indian economic legislation, and presumptions of mental state appear elsewhere in fiscal statutes. What distinguishes Section 54 is the standard it imposes on the defence: the accused must disprove wilfulness not on a preponderance of probabilities, the standard ordinarily applied to a defence burden, but beyond reasonable doubt, the standard the prosecution itself usually bears. For offences whose core is a failure to fill in a schedule correctly, a presumption of wilfulness rebuttable only to the criminal standard is a formidable provision, and it has been a standing target of professional criticism since the Act was passed.

The chapter does carry internal safeguards, and fairness requires them to be stated. Prosecution may be instituted only with the prior sanction of the Principal Commissioner, the Commissioner or the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 55, which interposes a senior administrative decision between the assessing level and the criminal court. Section 49 withholds prosecution where the return is furnished before the expiry of the assessment year. The offences built on failure all carry the word wilful in their ingredients, so an inadvertent omission, honestly come by, is not in terms the offence described, even though Section 54 makes the practical burden of establishing inadvertence heavy. Section 56 deals with offences by companies, defined broadly to include unincorporated bodies and Hindu undivided families, attributing liability to the persons in charge of the conduct of the business in the usual manner of such provisions. Section 57 addresses proof of entries in records and documents. The chapter's centre of gravity is nonetheless unmistakable. Parliament legislated for the foreign asset field the kind of criminal apparatus (imprisonment measured in years, a presumption against the accused, prosecution independent of assessment) that the ordinary income tax system reserves, in practice if not in text, for its gravest cases, and it applied that apparatus to defaults that include the non-completion of a return schedule.

The criminal chapter also fixes the Act's position among its neighbouring regimes, and one overlap is deliberate. The same concealed offshore wealth that attracts this Act may, where it represents the proceeds of a scheduled offence, attract the attachment and confiscation machinery of the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002, whose treatment of [the proceeds of crime and the enforcement directorate's powers](/2013/03/01/pmla-2002-complete-guide/) runs on a civil standard against the property itself; Section 48 of the Black Money Act, by keeping its prosecutions additional to every other law, ensures that the two regimes can proceed in parallel against the same fortune, one against the person and one against the property. A declarant under the compliance window received, as explained below, a statutory protection against the use of the declaration itself as evidence in proceedings under several named statutes, but that protection attached to the declaration and the window. Outside it, the regimes accumulate.

## Appeals and Recovery

The appellate ladder of the Act follows the familiar income tax pattern with its own periods. An assessee aggrieved by an order of the Assessing Officer may appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 15 within thirty days. A further appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 18 within sixty days of the order appealed against. From the Tribunal, Section 19 provides an appeal to the High Court within one hundred and twenty days where the case involves a substantial question of law, and Section 21 provides an appeal to the Supreme Court where the High Court certifies the case as fit for such appeal. Sections 23 and 24 confer revision powers on the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner, the first for orders prejudicial to the interests of the revenue and the second for orders otherwise amenable to revision. The ladder is examined in its institutional setting in the account of [taxpayer appeals and the tribunal system in India](/2012/11/01/taxpayer-appeals-tribunals-india/), and its salient feature here is that Parliament built no special forum for this Act: the same Commissioner (Appeals), the same Tribunal and the same High Courts carry Black Money Act appeals alongside the ordinary tax list, with the result that the jurisprudence of the Act has developed inside the general body of tax appellate practice rather than in a specialised corner.

Recovery follows assessment through the machinery already noted: payment within thirty days of the notice of demand, default rendering the assessee liable as an assessee in default, and coercive recovery through the Tax Recovery Officer by certificate proceedings and by notices to persons holding money on the assessee's account. The enforcement figures discussed later in this article show a striking gap between demand raised under the Act and amounts recovered, a gap that the appellate structure partly explains, since demands under appeal or in dispute are demands raised but not collected, and a charge computed at one hundred and twenty percent of an asset's value invites contest at every rung of the ladder. The gap is a datum about the regime's operation, not by itself a verdict on it: a deterrent statute may raise demands it does not expect to collect in full. But a recovery figure in the low hundreds of crores against demands in the tens of thousands of crores, on the finance ministry's own answers to Parliament, is a fact any honest account of the Act has to carry.

## How Proceedings Under the Act Are Contested

Because the Act's outcomes are so heavy, its proceedings are fought hard, and the recurring grounds of contest follow the statute's own pressure points rather than the habits of ordinary tax litigation. The first contest is over the threshold: whether the material before the Assessing Officer is information within Section 10 at all, material that suggests undisclosed foreign income or an undisclosed asset chargeable under the Act, or whether the proceeding has been launched on a bare mismatch, a foreign report of an account whose disclosure in Schedule FA the officer has not first checked. Given that the entire regime is premised on non-disclosure, a proceeding founded on an asset that was in fact disclosed strikes at the root of the charge, and the explanation stage of a Section 10 proceeding is characteristically devoted to exactly that record. The second contest is over the explanation of source, for the reasons developed in the section on explanations above: the sufficiency of old records, the drawing of inferences from gaps, and the proportion of an asset relieved under Section 5 are fact questions on which appeals to the Commissioner (Appeals) and the Tribunal do their most consequential work under this Act. The third contest is over valuation, and in particular over the bank account rule, where the difference between deposits made from the account's own withdrawals and deposits from outside is a matter of statement-by-statement proof across years. The fourth is over sanction and procedure in the penalty and prosecution chapters: whether the show-cause requirement of Section 46 was honoured, whether the penalty order met the one-year discipline of Section 47, and whether a prosecution carries the prior sanction Section 55 requires from the specified senior authority.

Behind these particular contests stands the structural one, the temporal reach argument, which is reserved for the section devoted to it below. The pattern as a whole is instructive about the kind of statute this is. In ordinary tax litigation the characteristic fight is about characterisation of a receipt or the allowability of a deduction; the law's centre of gravity sits in the computation. Under the Black Money Act the centre of gravity sits at the edges of the proceeding, in the trigger, the explanation, the valuation rule and the sanction, because the computation itself, thirty percent of a prescribed value with nothing deducted, leaves almost nothing to argue about. Parliament designed a charge with no interior disputes, and the litigation migrated to the perimeter. That migration is also why the appellate record of the Act matters beyond the parties: rulings on what counts as information, on the treatment of explanations and on the reach of the deeming provision are, in substance, the judiciary's continuing calibration of a statute whose text offers the citizen few internal protections and relies on procedural discipline at the boundary to do the work of fairness.

## The One-Time Compliance Window of 2015

Chapter VI of the Act ran, once, a transaction between the state and the concealer: disclose the asset, pay a stated price, and close the past. Section 59 permitted any person to make a declaration of an undisclosed asset located outside India, where the asset had been acquired out of income chargeable under the Income-tax Act for an assessment year before assessment year 2016-17. By Notification No. 57 of 2015, issued on 1 July 2015, the window for declarations opened on that day and closed on 30 September 2015. Section 60 charged tax on the declaration at thirty percent of the value of the asset as on the date of commencement of the Act, and Section 61 charged penalty at one hundred percent of that tax, producing a combined price of sixty percent of the asset's value. Section 63 required the tax and penalty to be paid by 31 December 2015, failing which the declaration was deemed never to have been made. Section 64 kept the declared amount out of the declarant's total income under the Income-tax Act, Section 65 protected completed assessments from reopening on the strength of the declaration, and Section 66 barred any refund of amounts paid under the chapter.

The protection at the heart of the scheme was Section 67, and its mechanism is worth stating precisely because it is often described loosely as an immunity clause. Section 67 provides that a declaration made under the chapter is not admissible in evidence against the declarant in any proceeding relating to imposition of penalty, other than the penalty under Section 61 itself, or in a prosecution, under the Income-tax Act, the Wealth-tax Act of 1957, the Foreign Exchange Management Act of 1999, the Companies Act of 2013 or the Customs Act of 1962. The declarant's safety thus rested on an evidence bar: the state disabled itself from using the confession against the confessor across the principal fiscal and exchange control statutes. Section 69 exempted the declared asset from wealth tax, and Section 68 made a declaration founded on misrepresentation or suppression of facts void, with the amounts paid treated accordingly. The window was fenced by Section 71, which closed it to defined classes: persons detained under the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act, persons facing prosecution under specified chapters of the Indian Penal Code, the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act and the Prevention of Corruption Act, persons notified under the Special Court statute for securities offences, and, critically, persons whose assets were already the subject of a notice, search or survey under the Income-tax Act, or about which India had already received information under an agreement of the kind Section 90 or Section 90A of that Act contemplates. The window was for the undiscovered. A person the system had already reached could not buy the priced exit.

The yield of the window is a matter of record, and the record has to be reported with its reconciliations visible, because the figures were revised as counting proceeded and careless accounts merge numbers that belong to different stages of the count. The Central Board of Direct Taxes announced on 1 October 2015, the day after declarations closed, a preliminary figure of 638 declarations involving undisclosed foreign assets of 3,770 crore rupees, expressly subject to reconciliation. The Revenue Secretary, Hasmukh Adhia, thereafter placed the reconciled position on record: 638 declarants and assets of 4,147 crore rupees, with a total tax receipt of 2,488.20 crore rupees. The finance ministry's answers to Parliament settled on a third formulation, first given in an answer of 7 February 2017 and repeated in later answers: 648 disclosures involving 4,164 crore rupees, with about 2,476 crore rupees collected in tax and penalty. These are one episode counted at three moments, not three episodes, and this article uses the Parliament-answer set as the settled figure, since it is the number the ministry has consistently defended on the floor of the House.

By any of the three counts, the yield was modest against the rhetoric that had preceded the statute, and the modesty is itself evidence about the problem the Act addressed. Six hundred and forty-eight disclosures is a small number of declarants for a country of India's size and diaspora; 4,164 crore rupees of disclosed assets is a small sum against the popular imagination of the offshore stock, an imagination the government itself, it will be recalled, declined to quantify officially. Several explanations were offered at the time and since. The price, sixty percent, was high against the alternative of continued concealment at a moment when automatic exchange of information had been announced but had not begun to flow, so the discounted probability of detection still looked low to a holder in 2015. The exclusions shut out the holders the state knew most about. The definition of a declarant's protection, an evidence bar rather than a general absolution, left residual anxieties, particularly about foreign exchange consequences, that advisers recorded at the time. And a class of holders simply doubted that the data revolution would arrive. The window's real function, on the most persuasive reading, was never principally revenue. It was a fairness device and a line-drawing device: it gave every holder a priced opportunity to regularise before the harsher regime began, so that the one hundred and twenty percent exaction afterwards could be defended as a price chosen by those who declined the sixty percent one. That defence is examined in the counter-reading below; its force depends on the window having been a genuine opportunity, which is why the position of persons who were outside the Act's original residence definition, and received no such opportunity when the definition was later widened against them, is the weakest point in the whole structure.

## The Window in Practice

The mechanics of using the window were simple in form and demanding in fact. A declaration was made to the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner having jurisdiction, in the prescribed form and manner under Section 62, in respect of the asset and its value as on the commencement date. The declarant then had to convert the declaration into money by 31 December 2015, on pain of the declaration being deemed never to have been made, a sanction that made the payment date, rather than the declaration date, the true point of no return. The Central Board of Direct Taxes issued Circular No. 13 of 2015 on 6 July 2015, within the window's first week, answering the questions that immediately arose, and its answers map the anxieties of the summer. Declarants asked whether assets held through entities and trusts could be declared, how valuation was to be supported, what happened where part of an asset's source had been taxed, and whether a person who had been non-resident when an asset was acquired, and was therefore outside the charge as the Act then stood, could use the window at all. The Board's answer to the last question, its Question 23, was that such a person was eligible to declare voluntarily, and a number did, purchasing certainty against a change of law at the sixty percent price. The poignancy of that answer became visible only in 2019, when the law did change, retrospectively, and those who had bought the certainty were protected by their declarations while those who had relied on the Board's implicit premise, that they were outside the regime, found the regime extended to meet them with no window attached.

Valuation under the window followed the same Rule 3 measures as the charge, so the declarant faced the same computations described in the valuation section of this article, with the difference that the exercise was voluntary and its output known in advance: thirty percent tax on the value and a penalty equal to that tax. The exclusions in Section 71 did the gatekeeping, and two of them shaped behaviour in ways visible in the final numbers. The exclusion of assets about which India had already received information under its treaty network meant that the holders at greatest immediate risk, those on the lists already in the department's hands, could not use the window for those assets at all, and the exclusion of assets under notice, search or survey closed it to everyone already in proceedings. The window was addressed, by design, to the unknown holder, and it was the unknown holder who had the least reason in 2015 to believe that discovery was near, since automatic exchange was a promise dated in the future and the leaks of the past had touched only a few banks. The modest yield is overdetermined: price, exclusions, scepticism about detection and anxiety about the sufficiency of the Section 67 evidence bar each predict a small take-up, and all four operated together.

## The Detection Revolution: Information, Not the Statute

No account of this Act is honest unless it assigns the detection revolution to its actual authors. The Black Money Act contains, in Section 73, a power for the central government to enter agreements with foreign governments and specified territories, including agreements for the exchange of information. But the system that made foreign accounts visible to the Indian revenue was built through instruments outside the Act, and the chronology shows the statute riding a wave rather than making one. India signed the Multilateral Competent Authority Agreement for the automatic exchange of financial account information under the Common Reporting Standard on 3 June 2015, weeks after the Act received assent. The intergovernmental agreement with the United States under the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act was signed on 9 July 2015, the week the compliance window opened, with automatic exchange under it scheduled to begin on 30 September 2015. Within the domestic machinery, Rules 114F to 114H of the Income-tax Rules obliged Indian financial institutions to identify reportable accounts and report them, the mirror image of the reporting Indian residents' accounts abroad would undergo in foreign institutions. India, as an early adopter of the Common Reporting Standard, made its first exchanges in September 2017, covering new accounts and pre-existing high-value individual accounts, with the remaining pre-existing individual accounts following in September 2018.

The consequence is a precise division of labour. Before automatic exchange, the probability that an unremarkable foreign account would come to the department's notice was low and accident-driven: a leak, a stolen data set, a disgruntled employee, a treaty request in an investigation that already knew its target. After automatic exchange, information about accounts held by Indian residents in participating jurisdictions arrives as a matter of routine, in bulk, in a standard format, whether or not anyone has suspected anything. Section 10 of the Act is drafted exactly for that world: information in the Assessing Officer's possession, from any source, is the trigger, and the statute asks no questions about how the information age arrived. The combination is what changed behaviour. The Act supplied consequences severe enough to concentrate the mind; the exchange system supplied the probability that made those consequences a real expectation rather than a remote threat; and Schedule FA supplied the domestic record against which the incoming data could be matched, so that a mismatch between a foreign report and an Indian return identifies itself. Remove any leg of the tripod and the regime weakens. It is therefore a mistake, though a common one, to credit the statute alone with whatever deterrence the regime has achieved, and equally a mistake to dismiss the statute as decorative: without the one hundred and twenty percent exaction and the prosecution chapter behind it, the incoming information would have fed the ordinary assessment machinery, with its limitation periods, its income-based charge and its graduated penalties, and the price of a discovered account would have been far lower.

The machinery has a domestic half that is easy to overlook. Automatic exchange is reciprocal: foreign institutions report accounts of Indian residents to their own authorities, which transmit the data to India, and Indian institutions perform the corresponding labour for accounts held in India by residents of partner jurisdictions. Rules 114F to 114H of the Income-tax Rules build that labour into Indian law, defining the reporting financial institutions, the reportable accounts, the due diligence by which account holders' tax residences are identified, including self-certification by account holders, and the statements the institutions must file. The individual consequence is quiet but complete: opening or holding an account abroad as an Indian resident generates, at the foreign institution, a residence enquiry whose answer travels. The account holder who certifies Indian residence abroad and omits the account from Schedule FA at home has created two records that contradict each other, and the exchange system exists to bring them together. It is this closing of the loop, rather than any single assessment, that practitioners identify as the regime's deepest behavioural effect: the foreign account ceased to be a private fact between the holder and a foreign bank and became a reported fact in two tax systems at once. The Black Money Act's Section 73, authorising agreements for the exchange of information for the purposes of the Act, plugs the statute into that flow, so that information received under the exchange network can found a Section 10 proceeding without further ceremony. The statute and the network are legally distinct and operationally one system, and the limitation-free, information-triggered assessment the Act prescribes is intelligible only as the domestic endpoint of the network's design.

## Ordinary Tax Law Versus the Foreign Asset Regime

The comparison the whole article has been building towards can be set out directly. The table below places the ordinary income tax treatment of foreign income and assets beside the treatment under the Black Money Act across the dimensions on which the two regimes differ, and it is the named companion artifact of this article: the single page on which a reader can see what changed in 2015.

| Aspect | Ordinary income tax law | Black Money Act 2015 |
|---|---|---|
| The charge | Tax on income, including worldwide income of a resident, year by year | Tax on total undisclosed foreign income and on the value of the undisclosed asset itself |
| Rate | Slab rates for individuals, with surcharge and cess as applicable | Flat thirty percent, irrespective of the size of the income or asset |
| Deductions and set-off | Expenses, allowances and set-off of losses available under the ordinary computation provisions | No deduction for expenditure or allowance, and no set-off of any loss, under Section 5 |
| Assessment year of charge | The year in which the income accrued or was received | For an asset, the previous year in which the asset comes to the notice of the Assessing Officer |
| Limitation period | Reassessment fenced by limitation periods, extended for foreign assets but finite | No limitation period prescribed for issuing the notice under Section 10 |
| Penalty | Concealment penalties computed by reference to the tax sought to be evaded | Three times the tax computed under Section 41, producing a combined exaction of one hundred and twenty percent; flat ten lakh rupee penalties for return defaults under Sections 42 and 43 |
| Prosecution exposure | Prosecution under the Income-tax Act for wilful evasion and false statements, sparingly used for offshore matters | Rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years for wilful evasion under Section 51, six months to seven years for return and information defaults, with a presumption of culpable mental state under Section 54 |
| Disclosure obligation | Schedule FA in the return, compulsory for residents holding foreign assets or signing authority since assessment year 2012-13 | The same schedule, armed with dedicated penalties and offences for its omission or inaccuracy |

The table understates one difference that prose must restore, because it does not fit a cell: the two regimes allocate the burden of explanation differently. Ordinary assessment proceeds on the taxpayer's return, and the department that alleges concealment must build its case within the discipline of limitation and evidence that the ordinary code prescribes. The Black Money Act begins from information in the department's possession, defines its subject matter partly by the absence of a satisfactory explanation of the source of investment, and, in a prosecution, presumes the culpable mental state and requires the accused to disprove it beyond reasonable doubt. The foreign asset regime is not merely the ordinary regime with higher numbers. It is the ordinary regime with its procedural polarity reversed at several points, and that reversal, as much as the rates, is what practitioners mean when they describe the Act as a different species of statute.

## The Temporal Reach Question

No feature of the Act has been argued about more than its reach into the past, and the argument must be reconstructed mechanically before it can be judged, because the Act does not say, in terms, that it taxes the past. It achieves its reach through two provisions working together. The first is the proviso to Section 3(1): an undisclosed asset is charged on its value in the previous year in which it comes to the notice of the Assessing Officer. The second is Section 72(c), which provides that where an undisclosed asset was acquired before the commencement of the Act and no declaration was made under the compliance window, the asset is deemed to have been acquired in the year in which a notice under Section 10 is issued. Between them, these provisions mean that an asset acquired in 1998, held quietly for two decades, and discovered in 2019 is charged in the assessment that follows discovery, at its value in the year of notice, as though its acquisition belonged to that year. The acquisition date, the political and legal fact on which a charge of retrospectivity would ordinarily turn, is removed from the computation altogether.

The constitutional objection is framed most sharply where prosecution follows. Article 20(1) of the Constitution forbids a conviction for an act that was not an offence when committed, and forbids a penalty greater than the one then in force. The objector's case runs thus: the defaults for which Sections 49 and 50 punish an assessee include failures to disclose in returns of years before the Act existed as a penal statute, and the charge itself, though dressed as a tax on value in the notice year, is in substance a levy on the historic act of acquisition out of untaxed income, so that both the tax and the prosecution reach conduct completed before commencement. The government's answer, given in Parliament and in the structure of the statute, is that the charge fastens not on the acquisition but on the continuing state of non-disclosure subsisting at and after commencement: the asset remained undisclosed when the Act began, the holder was invited to disclose it in the window on stated terms, and the tax falls on value in a year when the Act unquestionably operates. On that answer the statute is not retrospective at all; it is a present charge on a present and continuing default, and the deeming provision merely fixes the year of charge for administrative certainty.

### Has the Supreme Court settled the temporal reach question?

Only narrowly. In Union of India v. Gautam Khaitan, the Supreme Court upheld the 2015 Removal of Difficulties Order that advanced the Act's commencement to 1 July 2015, reasoning that the window dates would otherwise have lapsed before the Act began. The challenge to charging assets acquired before commencement remains contested and is not treated as finally settled.

The debate belongs to a longer Indian argument about the taxation of past transactions, and it is usefully read alongside [the retrospective taxation controversy that followed the Vodafone judgment](/2012/10/15/retrospective-taxation-vodafone-india/), where Parliament amended the Income-tax Act to overturn a Supreme Court interpretation with effect reaching back decades, and where the same two vocabularies (the legislature's claim to tax a continuing economic reality against the taxpayer's claim to settled expectations under the law as it stood) were deployed in full. The Black Money Act's technique is subtler than the Vodafone amendment's: rather than amending a charge retrospectively, it drafted the charge from the outset to fasten on value in the notice year and deemed old acquisitions into that year. Whether that subtlety is a principled answer to the retrospectivity objection or merely a drafting route around it is the genuine open question in the Act's life, and it is compounded by the Finance Act 2019 amendment discussed earlier, which widened the class of assessees retrospectively to persons who, when the window ran, were outside it. A person brought within the charge in 2019 with effect from 2015, in respect of an asset acquired while resident and held while non-resident, confronts the full post-window exaction without ever having declined the window's terms, because in 2015 the window was not addressed to that person. Whatever the fate of the wider constitutional argument, that asymmetry is the point on which the statute's fairness defence is thinnest, and published professional commentary has pressed it in exactly those terms.

## Assets Held Through Structures: Companies, Trusts and Nominees

Concealment rarely travels in its own name, and the Act is drafted on that assumption. The definition of an undisclosed asset in Section 2(11) reaches an asset held in the assessee's own name and an asset in respect of which the assessee is a beneficial owner, and it expressly includes a financial interest in any entity. The three limbs together cover the standard architecture of offshore holding: the personal account, the account or property standing in the name of a nominee or a controlled company, and the interest, shareholding, partnership share, trust interest or contractual stake, in a foreign entity that itself holds the underlying wealth. The return-stage sections speak the same language, addressing the person who holds an asset as beneficial owner or otherwise, or who is a beneficiary of an asset, so that a settlor, trustee or beneficiary of a foreign trust cannot assume that the structure's legal personality interposes a shield between the individual and the disclosure duty. Section 14 reinforces the point from the assessment side, preserving the power to assess the person for whose benefit income or an asset is held notwithstanding proceedings against the holder of record.

The drafting choice has a consequence for the many entirely lawful structures through which Indians hold foreign assets. A family trust settled abroad for estate reasons, a holding company interposed for a genuine foreign business, an investment held through a nominee for administrative convenience: each is within the disclosure language, because the language is built on beneficial interest rather than on wrongful purpose, and each must appear in Schedule FA with the individual identified in the capacity the schedule prescribes. The Act does not prohibit the structure; it prohibits the silence about it. Where the structure is used to fragment a holding among family members or entities so that each fragment falls below a reporting or carve-out line, the aggregation language of the 2024 amendment, addressed to an asset or assets in aggregate, and the beneficial ownership limbs of the definitions are the provisions that meet the attempt. For the student of the statute, the structural sections are the clearest evidence of the legislature's information about its subject: the Act was written by drafters who knew that the unit of concealment is seldom an account in a false name and is usually a chain, and who therefore defined their subject matter as a position in the chain, the beneficial interest, rather than as the chain's visible end.

## How an Explanation Is Judged: The Source of Investment

Because the definition of an undisclosed asset turns on the absence of a satisfactory explanation about the source of investment, the practical law of the Act is largely a law about explanations: what counts as one, who must produce it, and what happens when the papers run out. The structure of the inquiry under Section 10 is that the Assessing Officer, acting on information, calls for accounts, documents and evidence; the assessee's answer on the source of the investment is the centre of the proceeding. Where the investment is shown to have come out of income that was assessed or assessable in India, Section 5(1)(ii) grants the reduction and the asset, to that extent, leaves the charge. Where the investment came out of foreign income of years when the assessee was non-resident, the income was never within the Indian charge at all, and a satisfactory showing of that history answers the definition, since the asset is not unexplained wealth but the savings of a life lived outside the Indian tax net. Where the investment came by inheritance or gift, the explanation is the chain of title: the will, the donor's own history, the remittance trail. In each case the Act's question is not, in the first instance, was tax evaded, but can the money's biography be told in documents.

This evidentiary design has a consequence that deserves to be named, because it distinguishes the Act from a simple penalty statute. The regime places the practical burden of reconstructing that biography on the holder, across periods that may run to decades, in a documentary world that was not built for the purpose. Bank statements are retained by foreign banks for limited periods. Employers from the nineteen-nineties no longer exist. Parents whose accounts are inherited kept papers in houses that have been sold. The Act does not grade its demand by the realistic availability of records: an explanation the officer considers unsatisfactory leaves the asset within the definition, and the charge follows. The safeguard against arbitrariness is the appellate ladder, where the Tribunal can re-examine whether an explanation was rightly rejected, and the requirement that the officer engage with the material produced rather than proceed in its absence. But the starting polarity of the proceeding, information with the department and biography with the citizen, is set by the Act itself, and it explains why professional advice in this field consists so largely of archive-building: the assembly, years before any notice, of the returns, remittance certificates and acquisition papers that constitute an explanation in waiting.

There is a related boundary that holders and advisers must keep in view, between the undisclosed asset of this Act and the unexplained investment of the ordinary income tax law. The Income-tax Act has long contained its own deeming provisions under which unexplained money, investments and expenditure are taxed as income of the year of discovery, and those provisions continue to govern assets and investments within their field, principally domestic ones and those not drawn into the foreign asset regime. The Black Money Act did not repeal that machinery; it built a parallel and harsher track for the foreign field, with its own charge on value, its own penalty multiple and its own prosecution chapter, and Section 4(3) polices one direction of the boundary by keeping income charged under this Act out of the total income under the Income-tax Act. The same fortune should not be charged twice, but which track a given discovery travels is a question of the asset's location, its disclosure history and the source of the information, and the tracks differ enough in consequence that the characterisation is itself often the first contest in a proceeding.

## The Parallel Domestic Regime

The Act is the offshore half of a policy whose onshore half is older and was being rebuilt at the same time. Wealth concealed inside India has historically been held not abroad but in property standing in another's name, and the statute addressed to that technique is the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, whose prohibitions, attachment machinery and 2016 reconstruction are the subject of [the companion account of the benami regime](/2012/09/15/benami-transactions-act-explained/). The pairing is instructive because the two statutes attack concealment from opposite directions. The benami law asks whose property a thing really is, and strikes at the arrangement by which the real owner hides behind a name-lender. The Black Money Act asks whether a thing held abroad has been disclosed and its source explained, and strikes at the silence. A fortune can be vulnerable to both: funds moved out of India into a foreign account held by a nominee entity present the benami question of beneficial ownership in one forum and the disclosure question in another, and the definition of an undisclosed asset, built as it is on beneficial ownership, is drafted to catch the foreign limb of exactly such arrangements. Read together, the two statutes state a single policy in two dialects: the state will treat the unexplained gap between legal title and real ownership, and between real ownership and declared ownership, as itself the taxable and punishable event.

## Proportionality at the Low End, and the Softening That Followed

The counter-reading of the Act, given at full strength, concedes the design and defends it. Concealment of foreign assets, the defence runs, was a crime of calculation: the expected cost of hiding money abroad was the penalty multiplied by a probability of detection close to zero, and no penalty within conventional limits could have outweighed the gains of concealment while that probability held. A legislature confronting that arithmetic has three choices: raise the penalty to confiscatory levels, raise the probability of detection, or accept the concealment. India, in the event, did the first two together, and the severity of the first is defensible precisely because the second was not yet in place when the statute was enacted: the one hundred and twenty percent exaction was the price that made non-disclosure irrational even on the old detection odds, and the compliance window was the fairness mechanism that accompanied it, offering every holder sixty percent terms before the severe regime began. On this reading the Act is not a revenue measure that failed, but a deterrent that was always meant to collect little, because a deterrent that works is one under which the threatened exaction is rarely incurred.

The objections that survive that defence are, in the end, two, and both have been foreshadowed. The first is temporal reach, treated in the preceding section: a genuine and open constitutional argument, strongest where prosecution follows for defaults of pre-Act years and where the 2019 amendment swept in persons the window never addressed. The second is proportionality at the low end. The architecture of the Act draws no line of substance between the deliberate concealer of a fortune and the holder of a small inherited account or a modest block of employer shares, beyond a narrow bank account carve-out and the administrative discretion of sanctioning and assessing authorities. The same flat penalty, the same prosecution ranges, the same presumption, and a valuation rule that can carry the combined demand past the value of the asset meet both cases. A statute may rationally choose severity against concealment as a class; the proportionality objection is that the class as drafted includes thousands of people whose default is a form left incomplete, and that a legal system committed to graduated responses should grade the consequence by the size and character of the default rather than by the accident of the section under which it is charged.

One refinement of the proportionality objection should be recorded before the softening is described, because it is the refinement the statute's defenders most often press. Section 41, they point out, says the officer may direct the penalty of three times the tax; it does not command it, and a discretion conferred in those terms must be exercised on the facts of the case, so that a total and unexplained failure and a marginal and promptly corrected one need not receive the same direction. Section 45, for its band of procedural penalties, expressly requires the default to be without reasonable cause. The reply of the critics is structural: a discretion without statutory guidance, exercised in the first instance by the assessing authority itself, is a thin safeguard against a penalty the legislature chose to fix at a confiscatory multiple, and the history of such discretions in tax administration is that they are exercised at or near the maximum unless appellate authority disciplines them. Both points are fairly made, and the practical law of the Act is found in their tension: the text permits graduation, the architecture discourages it, and the appellate forums are left to supply, case by case, the proportion the statute declined to prescribe.

Events since enactment amount to a partial official concession to the second objection, delivered through administration first and legislation later. By an instruction of 15 March 2022, the Central Board of Direct Taxes directed that penalty under Sections 42 and 43 not be imposed, and prosecution under Sections 49 and 50 not be initiated, in respect of small bank accounts within the original five lakh rupee line, regularising the restraint the carve-out had contemplated. The Finance (No. 2) Act of 2024 then amended the provisos to Sections 42 and 43 with effect from 1 October 2024, providing that those sections do not apply to an asset or assets, other than immovable property, whose aggregate value does not exceed twenty lakh rupees: the carve-out was quadrupled in amount and widened in scope from bank accounts to assets generally, immovable property alone excepted. In August 2025 the Board revised its instruction to align prosecution policy with the same twenty lakh rupee threshold, as reported in the financial press. The direction of travel is unmistakable and it is legislative as well as administrative: the low end of the regime has been progressively relieved, first by forbearance and then by amendment, while the top end, Section 41, Section 51, the one hundred and twenty percent, stands untouched. The Act that operates is therefore somewhat less indiscriminate than the Act that was passed, and the credit for the difference belongs to the critics who pressed the proportionality case for a decade.

## Passage, and the Voices Around It

The Bill's passage was brisk and deliberately so. Introduced in the Lok Sabha on 20 March 2015, it was pressed forward without the reference to a Standing Committee that the opposition sought; the finance minister resisted referral on the ground that delay would give holders of concealed assets time to move them beyond reach, an argument that itself states the enforcement theory of the statute. The Lok Sabha passed the Bill on 11 May 2015, the Rajya Sabha passed it within the same week, and assent followed on 26 May. On the floor, the government's framing was arithmetic and admonitory: tax at thirty percent; the window's declarants paying an equal penalty and closing the matter at sixty percent; those who declined facing tax and penalty of one hundred and twenty percent and rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years. The framing was not universally received as severity. Ram Jethmalani, the statute's most prominent outside critic and the litigant whose petitions had done much to force the subject onto the state's agenda, dismissed the Bill as worthless and a kind of amnesty scheme, an attack from the direction opposite to the proportionality critique: not that the Act was too harsh, but that its window was too generous and its machinery unlikely to touch the largest fortunes. Practitioner opinion occupied the remaining ground. Mukesh Butani of BMR Legal, quoted in contemporary reporting, described the law as tough-nosed and as signalling a zero tolerance approach to undisclosed foreign assets and income. The three voices together (the minister's deterrent, the campaigner's disappointment, the practitioner's recognition of intent) frame the evaluative question the next section takes up: what, measured against its own purposes, the Act has done.

## The Record Since 2015

The enforcement record, stated on the finance ministry's answers to Parliament and attributed as such, is the hardest evidence available about the regime's operation, and it cuts in two directions. In an answer to the Lok Sabha given on 1 December 2025, the Minister of State for Finance, Pankaj Chaudhary, informed the House that, for the period from 1 July 2015 to 30 June 2025, 1,087 assessments under the Act had been completed, that tax and penalty demand of over 40,564 crore rupees had been raised, and that recovery against those demands stood at 339 crore rupees. A Rajya Sabha answer reported in August 2025 gave the position to 31 March 2025 as 1,021 completed assessments, demand of over 35,105 crore rupees, and 163 prosecution complaints filed. Set beside the compliance window's settled figures, 648 disclosures, assets of 4,164 crore rupees and about 2,476 crore rupees collected, the assessment record presents a paradox in two numbers: demands an order of magnitude larger than the window's yield, and recoveries an order of magnitude smaller than the demands. The gap is partly the ordinary gap of tax litigation, since demands under appeal are demands uncollected, and partly a consequence of the charge's own design, since a demand of one hundred and twenty percent of an asset's computed value, where the computed value of a bank account is the sum of a lifetime's deposits, is a demand its recipient will contest at every stage the appellate ladder offers.

What the record does not directly measure is the regime's quiet effect, the change in ordinary compliance behaviour, and honest assessment has to mark that as unmeasured rather than claim it either way. The volume of Schedule FA reporting, the professional advice industry that grew around foreign asset disclosure, the care with which returning residents and employees with foreign share plans are counselled to file, are consistent with a regime whose principal product is disclosure discipline rather than collections. The statute's defenders are entitled to that point, and its critics are entitled to the reply that a discipline purchased by threatening small holders with seven years' imprisonment for a schedule's omission is discipline purchased at a price a legal system should count carefully. Both are entitled, finally, to the structural observation with which this article began its analysis of detection: the transformation that made the regime real was the arrival of automatic information exchange, which would have strengthened any statute behind it, and the Act's own contribution was to set the price of being found. Prices can be set too high as well as too low, and the decade-long softening at the low end is the system's own running commentary on where the original price was mis-set.

A note on method applies to everything said here about individual cases: this article states the general law of the statute and the public record around it, and nothing in it is advice on the position of any particular holder of a foreign asset, for whom the interaction of residence years, acquisition years and disclosure history is decisive and must be worked out on its own facts.

## The Rules, the Circulars and the Amendment Trail

A statute of this kind lives in its subordinate instruments to an unusual degree, and a complete account has to follow the trail of them. The first layer is the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Rules, 2015, notified on 3 July 2015, two days after commencement, which supply among other things the valuation code in Rule 3 on which the charge's size depends. The second layer is the pair of notifications of 1 July 2015 that set the Act in motion, No. 56 of 2015 advancing the commencement itself and No. 57 of 2015 fixing the window's dates, the first of them an exercise of the removal of difficulties power whose validity the Supreme Court later upheld. The third layer is the administrative gloss: the Central Board of Direct Taxes' Circular No. 13 of 2015, issued on 6 July 2015 in question-and-answer form, which told holders and advisers how the department would treat the window's hard cases, and which acquired an importance beyond its station because people ordered their affairs on its answers, including the answer that a then non-resident was outside the charge and merely eligible to declare. The fourth layer is the amendment trail in Parliament. The Finance Act of 2018 made procedural amendments touching the penalty and prosecution machinery, including the authorities whose approvals and sanctions the Act requires. The Finance Act of 2019 made the amendment of substance, recasting the assessee definition with retrospective effect from 1 July 2015 in the manner described in the residence section of this article, and it is the single most important event in the statute's life after its enactment. The Finance (No. 2) Act of 2024 made the amendment of mercy, raising the small-holding carve-out in Sections 42 and 43 to twenty lakh rupees of assets other than immovable property with effect from 1 October 2024. Between the statutes stand the Board's instructions of 15 March 2022 and August 2025, restraining penalty and prosecution at the small end first at the five lakh line and then at the twenty lakh line.

Read as a sequence, the trail shows a statute being governed in two directions at once. The charge, the multiple and the prosecution ranges, the features aimed at the deliberate concealer, have been left exactly as enacted, and one amendment, that of 2019, made the charge wider with retrospective effect. The reporting edge, the features that meet the ordinary holder, has been progressively softened by every instrument that has touched it since 2022. A reader who consults only the Act as passed in 2015 will therefore misstate the law in both directions: too narrowly, by missing the 2019 widening of the charge, and too harshly, by missing the reliefs that have come to surround the return-stage penalties. The Act's text is the beginning of its law, not the whole of it, and the discipline of reading the trail is part of what this series means by treating a statute as a working institution rather than a framed document.

## The Lawful Holder: Exchange Control, Remittances and Assets That Were Never Hidden

A persistent confusion around the Act, worth dispelling in its own section, is the belief that holding a foreign asset is itself suspect under Indian law. It is not. The holding of foreign assets by residents is regulated by the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 and the rules and directions under it, and within that framework residents lawfully hold a great deal abroad: accounts funded through the Liberalised Remittance Scheme within its annual limits, accounts retained from periods of employment overseas under the permissions the Reserve Bank extends to returning residents, shares allotted by foreign employers, property acquired under the permissions applicable at the time. Exchange control asks whether the acquisition and holding were permitted. The Income-tax Act asks whether the income from the asset was returned. The Black Money Act asks a third question, whether the asset was disclosed and its source explained, and it is entirely possible for a holding to pass the first two tests and fail the third, just as it is possible for a holding to fail the first and be compounded under exchange control while never entering this Act's field at all, because the holder disclosed it faithfully in Schedule FA throughout. The statutes are cumulative, not alternatives, and Section 67's list of enactments in which a window declaration could not be used in evidence, the Income-tax Act, the Wealth-tax Act, the Foreign Exchange Management Act, the Companies Act and the Customs Act, is a compact map of the legal neighbourhoods a foreign asset inhabits.

The distinction matters practically because it separates two populations that public discussion habitually merges. The first population holds assets that were unlawful from birth: income that should have been taxed in India, moved out in breach of exchange control, and concealed. For that population the Act's full apparatus, charge, multiple penalty and prosecution, is the designed response, and the compliance window was its offered exit. The second population holds assets lawful in origin and in exchange control terms, whose exposure arises, if at all, at the disclosure step alone: the retained account of the returning worker, the inherited account whose Indian heir did not know the schedule existed, the stock plan holding of the employee in the worked example above. For that population the rational legal response is the one the system has slowly moved towards: a disclosure obligation enforced by penalties scaled to the default, relief for the small, and prosecution reserved for the wilful. The original Act made almost no distinction between the populations, because its reporting sections keyed liability to the default rather than to the character of the asset or the holder. The amendments and instructions of 2022, 2024 and 2025 are, in substance, the system learning to draw the distinction its first draft declined to draw, and a reader who wants to know what the Act means in practice must read the 2015 text and the softening instruments together, as one developing settlement between deterrence and proportion.

## Verdict

The Black Money Act 2015 is best understood as a statute that solved a real charging problem by radical means and then lived with the consequences of its radicalism. The charging problem was genuine: income concealed abroad defeated a tax system that could neither see the assets nor charge their value, and the device of charging the asset itself, at its value in the year of discovery, answered that problem in a single move. The price was a set of tensions the Act has never resolved: a temporal reach built on deeming that keeps a constitutional question permanently open; a severity calibrated for the largest concealers applied by the same sections to the smallest defaulters; a prosecution chapter whose presumption standard fits uneasily on offences of omission; and a widening amendment in 2019 that taxed yesterday's outsiders on terms they were never offered a window against. Against those tensions stands the compliance transformation the regime, statute and information exchange together, has plainly worked on the disclosure culture of Indian residents with foreign holdings, and the considered judgment of the legislature, never revisited at the top end, that concealment of this kind is to be priced as forfeiture. The series thesis finds here one of its clearest instances: a statute aimed at a narrow and notorious category, the offshore concealer of legend, which in operation governs a wide and ordinary population, because its trigger was drafted not as the discovery of hidden wealth but as the incompleteness of a schedule in a return. The Act's neighbours in this series take up each strand of the story: the ordinary income tax framework from which the regime broke away, the benami law that polices concealment at home, the money-laundering statute that proceeds against the property, the appellate system through which its demands are contested, and the retrospective taxation controversy with which its temporal argument is intertwined.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What income and assets does the Black Money Act 2015 reach?

The Act reaches two things. The first is undisclosed foreign income: income from a source located outside India that was not disclosed in a return furnished within the time allowed by the Income-tax Act, and income of that kind for which no return was furnished at all although one was required. The second is the value of an undisclosed asset located outside India, which Section 2(11) defines as an asset, including a financial interest in any entity, held in the assessee's name or of which the assessee is the beneficial owner, where there is no explanation about the source of investment in it, or the explanation offered is one the Assessing Officer considers unsatisfactory. The charge is addressed to persons within the residence definition of the Act, so a foreign asset of a person outside that definition is not reached.

### Q: What is the rate of tax and penalty under the Black Money Act?

The tax under Section 3 is charged at a flat rate of thirty percent on the total undisclosed foreign income and asset. There are no slabs and no basic exemption within the charge. On an assessment, Section 41 empowers the Assessing Officer to direct a penalty equal to three times the tax computed, that is, a further ninety percent of the chargeable value, so that tax and penalty together reach one hundred and twenty percent of the amount charged. Separately, the return-stage defaults carry fixed penalties: ten lakh rupees under Section 42 for failure to furnish a return and ten lakh rupees under Section 43 for failure to furnish, or inaccuracy in, the information about a foreign asset in a return that was furnished, subject in each case to the small-holding carve-out in the provisos.

### Q: What was the one-time compliance window of 2015?

Chapter VI of the Act opened a single opportunity for any person to declare an undisclosed asset located outside India that had been acquired out of income chargeable under the Income-tax Act for an assessment year before assessment year 2016-17. Declarations could be made between 1 July 2015 and 30 September 2015. The declarant paid tax under Section 60 at thirty percent of the value of the asset as on the commencement of the Act and penalty under Section 61 equal to one hundred percent of that tax, a combined sixty percent, and both had to be paid by 31 December 2015, failing which the declaration was treated as never made. Section 67 protected the declarant by making the declaration inadmissible in evidence against the declarant in penalty or prosecution proceedings under the principal fiscal statutes. The finance ministry's answers to Parliament record 648 disclosures involving 4,164 crore rupees, with about 2,476 crore rupees collected.

### Q: Does the Black Money Act reach assets acquired before it commenced?

Yes, by the design of its charging provisions. The proviso to Section 3(1) charges an undisclosed asset on its value in the previous year in which it comes to the notice of the Assessing Officer, and Section 72(c) deems an asset acquired before commencement, for which no declaration was made under the compliance window, to have been acquired in the year in which a notice under Section 10 is issued. The year of acquisition therefore does not limit the charge, and an asset acquired decades before 2015 may be charged at its value in the year of discovery, subject to the reduction in Section 5 for any part of the investment that came out of income already taxed. Whether this mechanism is a permissible charge on a continuing default or an impermissible retrospective levy has been contested, and the wider constitutional challenge is not treated as finally settled; the Supreme Court decision in the Gautam Khaitan case decided the narrower question of the order that advanced the Act's commencement date.

### Q: What happens if you fail to disclose a foreign asset in your return?

The immediate consequence is at the return stage. Section 43 imposes a penalty of ten lakh rupees on a person within the disclosure class who furnished a return but failed to furnish the information about a foreign asset, or furnished inaccurate particulars, subject to the carve-out for small holdings in the proviso. If the failure was wilful, Section 50 makes it an offence punishable with rigorous imprisonment of six months to seven years and a fine, and a prosecution requires the prior sanction of the specified senior authority under Section 55. If the asset itself is undisclosed within the meaning of Section 2(11), because its source of investment cannot be satisfactorily explained, the failure may also lead to assessment under Section 10, with tax at thirty percent and the Section 41 penalty of three times the tax. Disclosure failures and concealment are treated by different provisions, and both may be engaged by the same facts.

### Q: Who is a resident for the purposes of the Black Money Act?

As enacted, Section 2(2) defined an assessee as a person resident in India other than a person not ordinarily resident, applying the tests of Section 6 of the Income-tax Act, including the not ordinarily resident conditions in Section 6(6). Residence therefore turned on physical presence and residential history, and non-residents and persons not ordinarily resident stood outside the Act. The Finance Act of 2019 substituted the definition with retrospective effect from 1 July 2015. The definition covers a person resident in India in the previous year, and also a person who is non-resident or not ordinarily resident in the previous year but was resident in India in the previous year to which the undisclosed income relates, or in the previous year in which the undisclosed asset was acquired. The penalty and offence provisions keyed to return defaults continue to speak of a person resident other than not ordinarily resident.

### Q: Can you be prosecuted under the Black Money Act?

Yes. Chapter V creates a set of offences with substantial imprisonment terms. Wilful failure to furnish a return in due time, under Section 49, and wilful failure to furnish the information about a foreign asset in a return, under Section 50, each carry rigorous imprisonment of six months to seven years with fine. A wilful attempt to evade tax, penalty or interest carries, under Section 51(1), rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years with fine; a false statement in a verification and abetment of an offence carry six months to seven years; and a second or subsequent offence carries three to ten years with a fine between five lakh rupees and one crore rupees. A prosecution may be instituted only with the prior sanction of the Principal Commissioner, the Commissioner or the Commissioner (Appeals). Section 54 presumes the culpable mental state of the accused, who must prove its absence beyond reasonable doubt, and Section 48 makes the prosecution provisions independent of any assessment proceeding.

### Q: How is the Black Money Act different from ordinary income tax law?

The differences are structural. Ordinary income tax law charges income year by year at graduated rates, allows deductions and set-off of losses, relieves foreign tax through credit and treaty provisions, and fences reassessment with limitation periods. The Black Money Act charges undisclosed foreign income and, distinctively, the value of the undisclosed asset itself, at a flat thirty percent; it allows no deduction, allowance or set-off under Section 5; it prescribes no limitation period for the notice that starts an assessment under Section 10; it imposes a penalty of three times the tax under Section 41; and it backs return disclosure with offences punishable by years of imprisonment and a presumption of culpable mental state. The boundary between the two regimes is disclosure. Foreign income disclosed in the Indian return remains within the Income-tax Act; it is non-disclosure that moves income and assets into the Black Money Act.

### Q: How is the value of an undisclosed foreign asset calculated?

The value is the fair market value as prescribed, and Rule 3 of the Black Money Rules of 2015 supplies the measures. For a bank account, the value is the sum of all deposits made in the account since it was opened, excluding deposits made from withdrawals from the same account, so the figure can exceed any balance the account held at one time. For immovable property, the value is the higher of the cost of acquisition and the price the property would ordinarily fetch in the open market on the valuation date, supported by a recognised valuer's report. For quoted shares and securities, the value is the higher of the cost of acquisition and the average of the highest and lowest quoted prices on the valuation date. For unquoted interests and other assets, the higher of cost and open market price applies. Section 5 then reduces the charge where the investment came out of income already taxed.

### Q: Does the Black Money Act apply if the asset was acquired while the person was living abroad?

It depends on the person's residence in the year of acquisition and on the source of the funds. An asset acquired by a person who was non-resident at the time, out of foreign income that was never chargeable in India, is not an undisclosed asset of that person, because its source is explained by a history outside the Indian charge. The position changed for another class by the Finance Act of 2019, which, with effect from 1 July 2015, brought within the assessee definition a person who is non-resident or not ordinarily resident in the assessment year but was resident in India in the year the asset was acquired or the income arose. A person who acquired a foreign asset while resident and later emigrated may therefore remain within the charge after leaving. The Central Board of Direct Taxes had confirmed in Circular No. 13 of 2015 that a person who was non-resident when the asset was acquired could use the 2015 window voluntarily.

### Q: What happens to a small foreign bank account that was never reported?

As enacted, the provisos to Sections 42 and 43 disapplied the ten lakh rupee return penalties where the foreign holding consisted of one or more bank accounts whose aggregate balance did not exceed five lakh rupees at any time during the previous year, valued at the State Bank of India telegraphic transfer buying rate. A small account outside that line, or accompanied by other foreign holdings, was exposed to the flat penalty despite its size. Administration softened the position first: an instruction of the Central Board of Direct Taxes dated 15 March 2022 directed that penalty under Sections 42 and 43, and prosecution under Sections 49 and 50, not be pursued for small bank accounts within the five lakh line. Legislation followed in the Finance (No. 2) Act of 2024, which from 1 October 2024 raised the carve-out to assets, other than immovable property, not exceeding twenty lakh rupees in aggregate.

### Q: How does the tax department come to know about foreign assets?

Principally through automatic exchange of information. India signed the Multilateral Competent Authority Agreement for the Common Reporting Standard on 3 June 2015 and the intergovernmental agreement with the United States under the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act on 9 July 2015, and it began receiving information under the Common Reporting Standard in September 2017 as an early adopter. Financial institutions abroad identify accounts held by Indian residents and report them to their own authorities, which transmit the information to India; Rules 114F to 114H of the Income-tax Rules impose the mirror obligations on Indian institutions. Information also reaches the department through treaty requests, other authorities and the sources Section 73 agreements cover. Section 10 of the Act is drafted for this environment: the Assessing Officer acts on information in his possession from any source, and a mismatch between an exchanged report and the resident's Schedule FA is the characteristic starting point of a proceeding.

### Q: Can the same amount be taxed under both the Income-tax Act and the Black Money Act?

Not for the same income. Section 4(3) provides that income charged to tax under the Black Money Act does not form part of the total income of the assessee under the Income-tax Act, so the Act displaces the ordinary charge for the income it takes up. The boundary works in both directions. Foreign income that is disclosed in the Indian return is taxed only under the Income-tax Act, at ordinary rates and with treaty relief where applicable; it is non-disclosure that moves the income into the Black Money Act. Section 5(1)(ii) protects a related case: where an undisclosed asset was acquired out of income that had already been assessed, or was assessable, under either statute, the value charged is reduced to that extent, with a proportionate reduction for immovable property bought partly from taxed income. What the Act gives no general credit for is tax paid abroad on the same income, a point of contrast with the ordinary law's foreign tax credit.

### Q: What is the appeal route against an assessment under the Black Money Act?

The ladder follows the income tax pattern. An appeal against the Assessing Officer's order lies to the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 15, within thirty days. A further appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 18, within sixty days. From the Tribunal, an appeal lies to the High Court under Section 19 within one hundred and twenty days, where the case involves a substantial question of law, and Section 21 provides an appeal to the Supreme Court where the High Court certifies the case as fit for appeal. Sections 23 and 24 confer revision powers on the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner, for orders prejudicial to the revenue and for other orders respectively. Recovery during the contest runs through the demand machinery of the Act: tax is payable within thirty days of the notice of demand, subject to the remedies the appellate forums can grant, and coercive recovery proceeds through the Tax Recovery Officer.

### Q: Is an inherited foreign bank account covered by the Black Money Act?

The account is within the Act's field if its holder is within the residence definition, because an undisclosed asset includes an asset held by the assessee as beneficial owner, and an heir who owns an inherited account holds it in that capacity. Inheritance is, however, an explanation of source: an account shown to have devolved under a will or on intestacy, built from the deceased's own funds, is an asset whose source of investment can be explained, and the Section 5 reduction protects any part of it that came out of income already taxed. The heir's practical exposure is usually at the disclosure step. From the year the heir is resident and ordinarily resident, the account belongs in Schedule FA of the heir's return, and an omission engages Section 43 even where no tax was ever evaded on the account. Families frequently discover the obligation late, when an exchange of information report reaches the department, which is why inherited accounts feature prominently in the proportionality debate about the Act.

### Q: Are assets held through a foreign company or trust covered?

Yes. Section 2(11) defines an undisclosed asset to include a financial interest in any entity, and reaches an asset held in the assessee's name or in respect of which the assessee is a beneficial owner. An interest in a foreign company, partnership or trust is therefore within the definition in its own right, and the underlying holding cannot be kept outside the Act by interposing a structure. The return-stage provisions use matching language, addressing a person who holds an asset as beneficial owner or otherwise, or who is a beneficiary of an asset, and Schedule FA requires the interest itself to be reported. Section 14 also preserves the power to assess the person for whose benefit income or an asset is held, notwithstanding proceedings against the holder of record. A structure is not prohibited by the Act; what the Act requires is that the Indian resident's interest in it be disclosed and its source be capable of explanation.

### Q: Why did Parliament enact a separate statute instead of amending the Income-tax Act?

Three features of the design could not have been achieved by ordinary amendment with the same effect. The first is the charge on the asset itself: the Income-tax Act charges income, and a tax on the value of an asset whose originating income could not be identified or proved required a charging section outside that architecture. The second is the compliance window, which needed a self-contained code of declaration, price, evidence protection and exclusions that would operate for one season and close. The third is the enforcement package, a penalty of three times the tax and a prosecution chapter with a presumption of culpable mental state, calibrated for this subject alone rather than for the generality of tax defaults. A separate statute also carried a signalling purpose that the finance minister expressed in Parliament: a named law on black money, with its own commencement and its own window, announced a regime change in a way an amending schedule could not.

### Q: How much was declared under the compliance window, and how does that compare with what the Act has raised since?

The finance ministry's answers to Parliament record 648 disclosures under the window involving undisclosed foreign assets of 4,164 crore rupees, with about 2,476 crore rupees collected in tax and penalty; the count had been announced preliminarily by the Central Board of Direct Taxes as 638 declarations of 3,770 crore rupees and was revised as late declarations were reconciled. The assessment record since is of a different shape. In an answer to the Lok Sabha on 1 December 2025, the Minister of State for Finance stated that 1,087 assessments had been completed to 30 June 2025, with tax and penalty demand of over 40,564 crore rupees raised and recovery of 339 crore rupees made. A Rajya Sabha answer reported in August 2025 recorded 163 prosecution complaints filed to 31 March 2025. The demands therefore dwarf the window's yield, while recoveries remain small against the demands, a gap attributable in large part to demands under appeal and dispute.

### Q: What does Schedule FA in the income tax return require a resident to disclose?

Schedule FA is the foreign asset schedule of the Indian return, in the forms since assessment year 2012-13, and it is required of a resident other than a person not ordinarily resident. It calls for particulars of foreign depository and custodial accounts, foreign equity and debt interests, financial interests in entities located outside India, immovable property situated abroad, other foreign assets, and accounts located outside India over which the resident has signing authority without ownership. The duty to file the return itself, under Section 139 of the Income-tax Act as amended by the Finance Act of 2012, applies to a resident holding such an asset or signing authority even where the person has no taxable income. The Black Money Act attaches its return-stage penalties and offences to this schedule: Section 43 penalises a failure to furnish the information, or inaccurate particulars, in the return as furnished, which is why the schedule, rather than any search or summons, is where most encounters with the Act begin.

### Q: Does a prosecution under the Black Money Act depend on an assessment being completed first?

No. Section 48 provides that the prosecution chapter applies in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law, and that a prosecution is not dependent on the making of an assessment or the outcome of any other proceeding under the Act. The criminal track and the assessment track run independently, so a complaint may be filed on the strength of the default itself, for example a wilful failure to furnish return information, without awaiting the computation of tax. Two disciplines qualify that independence. A prosecution may be instituted only with the prior sanction of the Principal Commissioner, the Commissioner or the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 55, and in the trial the culpable mental state of the accused is presumed under Section 54, with the accused required to prove its absence beyond reasonable doubt. The independence of the tracks is one of the features that distinguishes the Act's enforcement design from ordinary income tax practice.
