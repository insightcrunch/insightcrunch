The story told by the UAPA amendments is a story of movement in one clear direction, away from a narrow statute concerned with associations and towards a far broader instrument. That movement can only be measured properly if the starting point is held firmly in view. A reader who begins with the later shape of the legislation risks treating that shape as natural, as if the text had always carried the weight it carries after successive changes. A reader who begins with the enactment of 1967 sees something quite different. The original measure was limited in its object, restrained in its machinery and silent on subjects that later came to dominate debate about it. The distance between those two positions is the real subject of an amendment trail, and it is a considerable distance.

![The UAPA power ledger: four amendments traced from the 1967 associations statute to the designation of individuals - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Direction matters as much as distance. The drift was not random, and it was not a matter of tidying language or correcting drafting slips across the decades. Each stage of change pulled the statute away from a single preoccupation, the protection of the sovereignty and integrity of India against organised advocacy of separation, and pushed it towards territory that the framers of the original text had left untouched. The measure that received assent at the end of 1967 was not a general security code. It was a preventive measure aimed at particular kinds of activity, carried out through a particular device, the declaration that an association was unlawful, and surrounded by a particular safeguard, confirmation by a Tribunal presided over by a judge of a High Court.

That restraint is easily missed if the title alone is allowed to do the work. The words unlawful activities sound open ended, and they can tempt a reader into assuming a sweeping reach from the very beginning. The operative provisions tell a tighter story. Power was attached to a defined concern with territorial integrity. Whole fields of criminal law were left to other legislation, or left alone altogether. The omissions were not gaps waiting to be noticed by an inattentive draftsman. They were part of the design, and they marked the boundary between what Parliament had chosen to do in 1967 and what it had chosen not to do.

Tracing the UAPA amendments therefore begins with an act of recovery. The original boundaries have to be restored to view before any later expansion can be judged, because expansion is a relative idea. A provision can only be described as wider than what went before it if what went before it is known in detail. What follows in this segment is that groundwork. First, the Act as enacted is examined in full, its constitutional roots, its objects, its machinery and its deliberate silences. Then the three early amending measures of 1969, 1972 and 1986 are considered together. For nearly four decades the statute barely moved, while other laws carried burdens that many readers later came to associate with it.

## The 1967 Act as enacted: a statute about associations, not terrorism

The constitutional root of the Act lay in the Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act, 1963. That amendment added the words sovereignty and integrity of India to the permitted restrictions on the freedoms secured by Article 19(1)(a), Article 19(1)(b) and Article 19(1)(c). The change did not arrive in isolation. It was made on the recommendation of the Committee on National Integration and Regionalism, a body appointed by the National Integration Council. The problem identified was advocacy that threatened the unity of the country by pressing for parts of its territory to be detached, or by treating its territorial settlement as open to question. The constitutional language chosen to meet that problem was specific, and the statute enacted four years afterwards kept faith with that specificity.

The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967, numbered Act 37 of 1967, received the assent of the President on 30 December 1967. Its stated purpose was to give effect to the constitutional position created in 1963. In that sense it was an implementing measure, a piece of legislation whose authority and whose limits both traced back to a deliberate alteration of the fundamental rights chapter. Parliament was not legislating at large against disorder. It was using a newly expressed ground of restriction, framed around sovereignty and integrity, to restrain forms of expression, assembly and association that were said to endanger those values.

Describing the Act as preventive captures its method. It did not wait for a completed act of separation, nor did it confine itself to punishing harm after harm had occurred. It intervened earlier, at the stage of advocacy, organisation and incitement. The activities at which it aimed were activities threatening the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India. Within that field, the text was concerned with advocating the cession of a part of the territory of India, advocating secession, questioning territorial integrity and inciting disaffection. Each of those phrases points in the same direction. Cession and secession are about the map of the country and whether it may be redrawn. Together those phrases describe a statute preoccupied with the survival of the Union as a territorial whole.

That preoccupation explains why the association stood at the centre of the scheme. Political advocacy of separation rarely presents itself as a purely solitary act. It gathers support, it forms bodies, it gives itself a name, a programme and a continuing presence. A legislature worried about such advocacy therefore has reason to address the vehicle through which it travels. The 1967 text did exactly that. Its central device was not a list of violent acts. Its central device was a status, the status of an association declared to be unlawful. Once that status attached, consequences followed for the body itself and for the individuals connected with it. The rest of the machinery existed either to bring that status into being, to test whether it should stand, or to punish conduct that defied it.

Section 3 supplied the first step. It allowed the Central Government to declare an association unlawful by notification. The choice of the Central Government as the acting authority fitted the subject matter. Sovereignty and territorial integrity are concerns of the Union as a whole rather than of any single region within it, and the power was placed at the Centre accordingly. A notification is an executive instrument. It is swift, it is formal and it does not by itself involve a court. That swiftness was plainly part of the point of a preventive scheme, since a power that could only be exercised after lengthy proceedings would intervene far later against a body whose activities the Government regarded as a threat to integrity. Yet the very features that made the device effective also made a check upon it necessary, and the Act provided one in the next provision.

Section 4 provided for a Tribunal to confirm a declaration made under Section 3. The Tribunal consisted of one person, and that person was a judge of a High Court. Both details carry weight. A single member body could act without the delay that a larger bench might involve, so the safeguard did not destroy the speed that the preventive design required. At the same time, the member was not an official drawn from the administration that had issued the notification. The member was a serving judge of a High Court, a figure placed by office outside the executive chain that had made the declaration. The structure therefore separated the act of declaring from the act of confirming. The Government could proclaim an association unlawful, but the declaration depended for its continued life on confirmation by an independent judicial figure sitting as a Tribunal.

That separation is the most instructive feature of the early Act. It shows a legislature balancing two impulses that pull against each other in all preventive law. One impulse is towards executive speed, because prevention that arrives after the event has failed in its own terms. The other impulse is towards restraint, because a power to brand an association unlawful reaches deep into the freedoms of expression, assembly and association that the Constitution protects, subject to the permitted restrictions. The Tribunal was the point at which those impulses were reconciled. It did not transfer the initiative away from the Government. It did subject the exercise of that initiative to scrutiny by a judge before the declaration could settle into its full term. A confirmed declaration stayed in force for two years, a fixed span rather than a permanent character, so the status created under Section 3 was conceived as a measured response with a defined end, not as an irreversible condemnation.

Punishment under the original scheme followed the same associative logic. Section 13 punished unlawful activities with imprisonment that could extend to seven years. Section 10 punished membership of an unlawful association with imprisonment that could extend to two years. The contrast between those two figures reflects a distinction the Act kept in view. Participating in the activities at which the statute aimed was treated as the graver matter. Belonging to a body that had been declared unlawful was itself made an offence, and the fact that mere membership could attract imprisonment shows how seriously the legislature treated the organisational vehicle of separatist advocacy. Yet the penalty for membership was set well below the penalty for the activities themselves. Grades of involvement were marked by grades of punishment, and the scale topped out at seven years.

Membership as an offence also reveals what kind of wrong the Act had in mind. The wrong was relational. A person became liable under Section 10 because of a connection with a body that the State had declared unlawful and a Tribunal had confirmed as such. The provision did not depend on proof that the individual member had personally carried out any particular act of violence. Liability flowed from the link between person and proscribed body, once that body had passed through the declaration and confirmation process. The Tribunal stood between executive assertion and criminal consequence, and its presence was not decorative. It was the load bearing element in a scheme that punished association as such.

Equally revealing is what the statute left alone. The Act as enacted contained no chapter on terrorism. It contained no definition of a terrorist act. It contained no special rules on bail, and it contained no special rules on custody. Each absence can be stated plainly, and each deserves to be stated plainly, because the later history of the title has made those subjects seem inseparable from it. In 1967 they were absent not by oversight but because the Act had a different job. A definition of a terrorist act would have served no purpose in a text that was not directed at terrorism. Special bail rules would have served no purpose in a scheme that relied on ordinary criminal process apart from the declaration machinery. Extended custody provisions belonged to a model of legislation built around investigation of violent conspiracies, a model that this Act did not adopt.

Read in that light, the original occupies a precise place in the constitutional story that produced it. The Sixteenth Amendment had identified sovereignty and integrity as values that could justify restricting the freedoms in Article 19, and the legislation of 1967 gave that identification a working form, translating a constitutional phrase into a notification, a Tribunal, a two year term and two offences with defined maxima. Little in the original text ranged beyond the ground that the amendment had marked out. That closeness is the baseline for everything that the amendment trail later records.

## The housekeeping amendments: 1969, 1972 and 1986

The early amending history of the Act can be told briefly, and the brevity is itself informative. The Act was amended by the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 1969. It was amended again by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1972. It was amended again by the Delegated Legislation Provisions (Amendment) Act, 1986. Those three measures, spread across a period of nearly two decades, are the whole of the amending record for the first long stretch of the life of the statute. Their names give an early clue to their character. One was an amendment Act directed at the statute itself. One arrived as part of a wider criminal law amendment. One arrived through a measure concerned with delegated legislation. None of them presented itself as a new charter for the Act, and none of them behaved like one.

These were adjustments to the unlawful association machinery, not expansions of it. The distinction between adjustment and expansion is fundamental to reading an amendment trail honestly. An adjustment keeps a scheme working. It refines, it clarifies, it alters an ancillary arrangement, it brings some part of the apparatus into line with a wider change in legislative practice. The core of the scheme, its object, its central device and its sanctions, remains recognisably the same after the adjustment as it was before. An expansion does something else. It adds a new object, reaches a new class of conduct, creates a new offence or arms the State with a new procedure. Judged by that test, the measures of 1969, 1972 and 1986 belong firmly in the first category. The Act that emerged from them was still the Act of 1967 in substance, a proscription statute aimed at associations threatening sovereignty and integrity.

The clearest proof lies in what none of them did. None introduced terrorism provisions. That negative fact can be put even more directly, because the statute contained no terrorism provisions for an amending Act to enlarge or revise. There was no terrorism chapter to extend, no definition of a terrorist act to widen, no special bail regime to stiffen and no custody regime to lengthen. A Parliament that wished to turn the Act towards terrorism during those years would have had to build that apparatus from nothing, inserting an entirely new subject into a text that had been written for a different one. No such building took place in 1969, in 1972 or in 1986. The silence of those amending Acts on the subject is therefore complete, and it confirms that the direction of travel associated with the title in later debate had not begun during this period.

Plain speaking serves the record best at this point. For nearly four decades the Act sat almost still. Its text was touched in 1969, in 1972 and in 1986, and each touch left its essential character undisturbed. During that long stillness, the anti terror work of the country was carried by other legislation, by TADA and then by POTA. Those laws, not the Act of 1967, were the instruments through which the State addressed terrorism while the older statute continued in its narrower field.

Stillness of that kind is a fact about legislative choice as much as about legislative neglect. Parliament returned to the Act three times in its first two decades and each time declined to enlarge it. Those returns matter because they remove any suggestion that the original limits survived merely because nobody looked at the statute again. The statute was looked at. It was amended. Its machinery was adjusted. Its purpose was left where the enactment of 1967 had placed it. The result is a clean baseline for the changes that belong to a later stage of the trail. Whatever was added afterwards was added to a text that had remained, through 1969, 1972 and 1986, faithful to its founding design, association centred, proscription based, Tribunal guarded and untouched by terrorism law.

### What did the 1969, 1972 and 1986 amendments actually change?

They changed the machinery for declaring and dealing with unlawful associations, through the Amendment Act of 1969, the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 1972, and the Delegated Legislation Provisions (Amendment) Act of 1986, without expanding the statute into terrorism or adding offences, definitions, bail rules or custody rules.

## 2004: terrorism enters the statute

The history of the UAPA amendments turns on a paradox that 2004 captures more completely than any other year in the life of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act. A government elected with a promise to repeal a much criticised anti-terrorism law carried out that repeal, and yet the statute book did not become empty of anti-terrorism law as a result. Instead, terrorism entered a statute that had not previously carried it as a central concern, and it entered through an amendment so extensive that the resulting Act looked, in structure and in reach, unlike the Act that had stood before it. To understand any later stage in the trail of UAPA amendments, a reader has to grasp this double movement first, because every part of it was deliberate. The repeal was deliberate, the replacement was deliberate, and the space between repealing a named terrorism statute and amending a general unlawful activities statute is where the politics, the drafting and the argument all meet.

On coming to power in 2004, the United Progressive Alliance government [repealed the Prevention of Terrorism Act, 2002](/2011/05/01/pota-2002-repeal-explained/) by the Prevention of Terrorism (Repeal) Act, 2004, which was Act 26 of 2004. In the same package, Parliament passed the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2004, which was Act 29 of 2004. The pairing matters more than either measure read in isolation. A repeal standing alone would have signalled a decision to manage terrorism through the ordinary criminal law and whatever remained of the older framework. An amendment standing alone would have signalled a decision to strengthen that framework while leaving the existing terrorism statute in place beside it. Together, the measures said something different and more precise. One particular statute, with its own name, history and reputation, would go, and the powers considered necessary would be housed instead inside another statute, reshaped for the purpose. The UAPA amendments of 2004 are therefore not an afterthought to a repeal, and the repeal is not a preface to the amendments. Each is half of a single settlement.

### The repeal and the amendment as a single package

The mechanics of the settlement show how tightly the two halves were joined. The 2004 Amendment Act received presidential assent on 29 December 2004 and was deemed to have come into force on 21 September 2004, the same day the Prevention of Terrorism Act ceased to operate. That alignment closed a potential interval in which one regime had ended and its replacement had not yet begun. Whether a reader approves of the settlement or disapproves of it, the drafting leaves little room to describe it as improvisation. A deemed commencement tied to the very day of cessation reflects a legislature conscious of continuity, anxious that no gap should open between the departure of one framework and the arrival of the other. Continuity of that kind also sharpens a question that runs through this article.

Continuity, however, should not be confused with identity. The Act that emerged was not the Prevention of Terrorism Act pasted into a different cover, even though a prominent critic would describe it in terms close to that, with one large exception. The location of a power can matter as much as the power itself. A freestanding terrorism statute announces its subject in its title and invites every case under it to be understood as a terrorism case. A chapter inside a broader unlawful activities statute works differently, at least in design. It places terrorist activities alongside a wider set of unlawful activities, subjects them to a shared definitional foundation, and presents the terrorist offence as one grave species within a larger genus. Defenders of the settlement could point to that relocation as discipline, a refusal to let counter-terrorism float free of the general law of unlawful associations. Opponents could answer that relocation changed the label more than the substance.

### A changed name and a changed foundation

The first textual sign of transformation appears before any offence is reached, in the long title of the principal Act. The 2004 amendment substituted that long title, adding the words "and for dealing with terrorist activities". A long title creates no offence, imposes no penalty and confers no power by itself, and for that reason it is easy to pass over. Yet a long title declares what a statute is for, and operative provisions are read in the light of a declared purpose. By writing terrorist activities into the statement of purpose, Parliament signalled that the amendment was no minor adjustment at the edges of an association law. The statute was being reoriented, its centre of gravity moved, and those few added words announced the movement to anyone who opened the Act.

Beneath the title, the amendment substituted sections 1, 2 and 2A, rebuilding the definitional foundation on which the rest of the Act would stand. Definitions in a statute of this character do far more than settle vocabulary for convenience. A definition decides what must be proved, which conduct falls inside a prohibition and which conduct falls outside it, and how far a chapter reaches when its operative provisions deploy a defined term. The substituted definitions included "terrorist act", "terrorist gang", "terrorist organisation", "proceeds of terrorism" and "property". Read as a group, those terms map the territory the new chapters would occupy. One term identifies the prohibited deed, another identifies a collective actor below the level of a formal organisation, another identifies the organisation itself, another identifies the money and value generated by offending, and another supplies the general language of assets through which the money provisions could bite.

### Four chapters where one closing chapter had stood

The scale of reconstruction becomes unmistakable at the end of the Act. The amendment replaced the closing chapter of the principal Act with four new chapters, numbered as Chapter IV, Chapter V, Chapter VI and Chapter VII, together with a Schedule listing terrorist organisations. Replacing a single closing chapter with four chapters is not tidying. It is expansion by design, and it gave the Act a second half devoted in substance to terrorism, its proceeds, its organisations and a body of supporting provisions. Chapter IV, comprising sections 15 to 23, dealt with punishment for terrorist activities. Chapter V, comprising sections 24 to 34, dealt with forfeiture of proceeds of terrorism. Chapter VI, comprising sections 35 to 40, dealt with terrorist organisations. Chapter VII, comprising sections 41 to 53, contained miscellaneous provisions.

Chapter IV is the punitive heart of the new structure, and its placement ahead of the property and organisation chapters reflects a familiar legislative instinct, to define and punish the gravest conduct before turning to assets and associations connected with it. A punishment chapter does work beyond the courtroom in which a sentence is eventually passed. It marks the seriousness with which Parliament regards a class of conduct, frames the choices of investigators and prosecutors when a case is characterised, and influences how officials and the public understand an allegation long before any trial concludes. An allegation under the amended Act was therefore no longer only about an unlawful association in the older sense. It could be an allegation of terrorist activity carrying the punitive weight Chapter IV assembled. Critics of expansive security law focus on exactly this characterising power, because the label attached at the start of a case can shape the whole course that follows. Supporters answer that grave conduct deserves a grave label.

Chapter V moved the focus from the person to the proceeds. Its subject, forfeiture of proceeds of terrorism, rests on a rationale legislators have found persuasive in many settings, namely that organised violence depends on money, premises, equipment and other material support, and that a law confined to punishing individuals leaves sustaining resources intact for others to use. Stripping proceeds aims at the infrastructure of offending rather than at the offender alone, and that rationale has genuine force. Money gathered for terrorism does not become harmless because the person who gathered it has been arrested, and a legal order that ignored the material base of violent organisations would be incomplete. At the same time, forfeiture raises characteristic risks, because property interests can involve third parties, family members, landlords, lenders and others whose connection with an offence may be remote, disputed or innocent. The strength of such a chapter depends heavily on procedure, on who must prove what, and on remedies open to a person whose property is caught.

Chapter VI addressed the organisation as an organisation. Its provisions, sections 35 to 40, dealt with terrorist organisations, and the Schedule attached to the Act listed the organisations to which that chapter applied. Listing is a distinctive legal technique. Rather than requiring the character of a group to be established afresh in every proceeding, a schedule fixes a status that other provisions can then reference, and membership or support acquires a different complexion once an organisation carries a listed status. The efficiency of the technique is plain, since a schedule brings consistency across cases and spares repeated proof of the same organisational character. Its dangers are equally plain, because a status fixed in a schedule can harden, can lag behind changes in a group, and can carry severe consequences for individuals judged through the lens of the listing.

Chapter VII, the miscellaneous chapter, supplied the supporting rules that make grander chapters function, the connective tissue between a declared power and its everyday exercise. That it ran from section 41 to section 53 underlines the scale of what was built, since thirteen sections of supporting machinery are not the footprint of a token amendment.

### The safeguards that arrived with the powers

An honest account of the 2004 package has to hold two truths in view at once. The powers were extensive, and safeguards arrived with them in the same legislative moment. Sections 36 and 37 gave a listed organisation a remedy, in the form of an application to the Central Government for removal from the Schedule and review by a Review Committee. The remedy speaks directly to the anxiety that listing creates. An organisation that contended it should not be listed was not left to rely solely on political pressure or collateral litigation. It had a statutory route, addressed to the Central Government that maintained the Schedule, with review by a Review Committee built into the scheme. The existence of that route does not prove that the route was generous, swift or effective in every instance, and a careful assessment would need the record of applications and reviews to judge its practical worth. It does, nevertheless, distinguish a considered listing regime from a bare power to list.

The repeal package added a second safeguard of a different kind, aimed backwards at the caseload of the departing statute rather than forwards at the listings of the new one. The package separately provided for a one year review of pending cases under the Prevention of Terrorism Act. That provision acknowledged a problem that repeals often create and sometimes ignore. When a statute falls, the people charged under it do not vanish with it, and a legislature that repeals a law because of concern about its misuse has a particular reason to look again at cases still travelling through the courts under the repealed provisions. A review confined to one year was a bounded commitment, neither an amnesty nor an abandonment of every pending prosecution, but a structured second look within a defined period. Supporters of the repeal could present the review as evidence that the settlement was about correcting misuse rather than weakening the response to genuine violence. Critics could reply that a review of old cases did little to answer their objection to the loss of the old powers.

### The political argument around the package

No account of 2004 is complete without the political contest that surrounded the settlement, because that contest explains why the settlement took the paradoxical shape it did. The Bharatiya Janata Party opposed the repeal of the Prevention of Terrorism Act as weakening the fight against terrorism. That objection, stated at its strongest, rests on a coherent view of security law. Terrorism presents a distinctive threat, ordinary criminal procedure was not designed for conspiratorial violence planned in secrecy, and a dedicated statute signals resolve to investigators, to courts and to armed groups themselves. To repeal such a statute is, on that view, to send the opposite signal when clarity matters, and to gamble that a general statute amended in haste can carry the same weight.

The United Progressive Alliance defended the repeal as a response to the misuse of the Prevention of Terrorism Act and presented the UAPA amendment as the replacement. That defence, also stated at its strongest, rests on an equally coherent view. A security law that is misused loses the public confidence on which effective security depends, exposes people to grave process harms, and discredits genuine prosecutions by association. Repeal of a misused statute, accompanied by a replacement that preserves the powers a state legitimately needs, is on that view not softness but repair. The replacement matters enormously to the strength of the argument. A government that had simply repealed and stopped would have been vulnerable to the charge of leaving a void. A government that repealed and rebuilt inside another Act could claim to have removed the discredited instrument while retaining necessary capacity. Whether the rebuilt capacity was in truth equivalent, greater in some respects or lesser in one crucial respect, is the question the confession discussion below answers.

Beneath the disagreement, the two positions shared ground. Neither side proposed that terrorist organisations should face no proscription, that proceeds should face no forfeiture, or that terrorist acts should attract no dedicated punishment. The argument concerned the vehicle, the history and the safeguards, and it was resolved by moving the substance of a predecessor statute, with one significant exception, into an older Act transformed to receive it.

## The one real subtraction: the confession power that was left behind

Every account of a legislative replacement contains a temptation to list what the new law copied from the old, because copying is visible, section by section, in the text. Subtraction is harder to see. A power that disappears leaves no section to quote, no chapter to summarise and no heading to announce its absence. Yet in the story of the UAPA amendments, the most instructive single event of 2004 is an absence. The amendment that rebuilt so much of the terrorism framework inside the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act carried no provision equivalent to the confession power that its two predecessor statutes had contained. That omission is the one real subtraction in the trail, and it deserves treatment out of proportion to the nothing that appears in the text, because the nothing was chosen, and chosen against a background in which the alternative had been enacted twice before.

Section 32 of the Prevention of Terrorism Act made a confession recorded by a police officer admissible in evidence, and section 15 of the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act [had done the same before it](/2011/08/01/tada-pota-uapa-comparison/). Both provisions were a direct departure from the ordinary rule. Under the Indian Evidence Act, a confession made to a police officer is inadmissible. That rule embodies a long standing distrust, rooted in the realities of custody, of statements obtained by the very authority that detains, questions and investigates the person making the statement. A suspect in a police station is not in a position of equality with the officer recording the words, and the law of evidence answered that inequality, in the ordinary case, by exclusion. The prosecution had to build its case from evidence gathered around the accused rather than from a confession gathered by the police themselves. Whatever view a reader takes of that rule, as a vital protection or as an obstacle to truth, its clarity is not in doubt.

The break mattered because a confession is unlike almost every other kind of evidence. A confession recorded and admitted can carry a prosecution a very long way by itself, since it comes from the accused and appears to settle the central question of involvement without the laborious construction of a circumstantial chain. That potency is why the departure was a signature feature of the special statutes, and why its critics regarded it with such alarm. Their objection was not a technicality about admissibility but an objection about incentives. If a statement made to a police officer can be used in evidence, obtaining such a statement becomes one of the most rewarding objectives of an investigation, and the pressures custody can exert acquire a legal prize at the end of them. Supporters of the provisions made the opposite case at its strongest. Terrorist conspiracies are secretive by design, witnesses are frightened, forensic trails can be thin, and an accused willing to speak should not have that account discarded by a rule framed for ordinary crime.

Against that background, the silence of the 2004 amendment is eloquent. Parliament rebuilt the punishment chapter, the forfeiture chapter, the organisation chapter and the miscellaneous chapter. Parliament recast the title and the definitions, supplied a Schedule, and provided a removal application and a Review Committee. Across that extensive reconstruction, Parliament demonstrated that it knew how to carry a power across from the world of the special statutes into the amended Act when it wished to do so. The confession power was not carried across. The 2004 amendment contained no equivalent provision, and the consequence is that the ordinary rule of the Indian Evidence Act continued to govern confessions in prosecutions under the amended Act. A statement made to a police officer remained inadmissible, as in an ordinary prosecution, even though so many other features of the special regime had been preserved. An oversight might explain a missing detail in a schedule or a gap in a procedural rule. An oversight cannot plausibly explain the absence of the most debated evidentiary power in the predecessor statutes, in a package whose purpose was to replace one of those statutes.

Human rights lawyer Colin Gonsalves captured the resulting position in a sentence with the compression of a verdict. He described the amended UAPA as bringing back the Prevention of Terrorism Act "except for admitting confessions made to a police officer as legal evidence". The sentence works on two levels at once. As criticism, it denies the government any large claim to have liberalised the law, since on his account the substance of the repealed statute returned in a new home. As description, it isolates the exception with surgical precision, and the exception he named was the confession power. A reader who disagrees with the critical thrust can still learn from the descriptive accuracy, because the exception is real, verifiable and singular. Of all the powers associated with the predecessor regime, this was the one that did not come back. Critics of the amended Act who invoke Gonsalves tend to stress the first level, the return of substance. Defenders of the settlement who invoke the same sentence tend to stress the second, the reality of the exception.

Why does a missing provision matter so much? The first reason is practical, and it concerns the shape of investigations and trials under the amended Act. Without an admissible police confession, investigators gain no evidentiary reward for extracting an admission in custody, and prosecutors must construct cases from witnesses, documents, material objects, communications and other evidence capable of being tested in court. Cases may become harder to prove, and some who might have been convicted under a predecessor regime on the strength of a recorded confession may instead be acquitted or never charged. That cost is the price supporters of the omission were willing to acknowledge, because the benefit is a protection that operates before any trial begins. A rule of exclusion shapes conduct in the police station, not merely rulings in the courtroom. It tells every officer that a confession obtained there is worthless as evidence, and in doing so it removes the legal incentive that made custodial pressure potentially profitable.

The second reason is interpretive, and it reaches beyond terrorism law to the way repeals should be read in general. Repeals have a poor reputation in the study of security legislation, and the reputation is partly earned. A repealed statute can return under a new name, its powers scattered across other Acts and its substance intact beneath a change of label, so that the citizen who welcomed the repeal discovers that little has changed. The 2004 experience supplies the corrective to cynicism of that kind, because it shows a repeal doing one piece of real subtractive work amid a great deal of transfer. The lesson is not that repeals always subtract, which the chapters described above contradict. The lesson is that subtraction is possible, that it can be identified with precision, and that it should be measured power by power rather than asserted or denied in general terms. The confession omission is the strongest evidence in this trail that repeal can subtract something, not only add, because the subtracted item was central rather than peripheral, debated rather than obscure, and present in both predecessor statutes rather than in one alone.

The third reason is comparative, and it is deliberately kept tight here, because the full head to head comparison of the three statutes belongs to the companion comparison article in this series rather than to this history of the UAPA amendments. In essentials, the pattern is stark. One predecessor placed its confession power in section 15, the other placed its confession power in section 32, and the amended Act placed a confession power nowhere. Two statutes spoke, and one, after their example was fully available to its drafters, stayed silent. That pattern converts the omission from a gap into a decision, and the decision distinguishes the amended Act from its predecessors in the daily experience of an accused person more sharply than any chapter enacted in 2004. Chapters can be amended, definitions can be widened and schedules can grow, as later stages of this trail would show. A subtraction of this clarity is rarer, and it marks the outer limit of what the 2004 settlement was willing to preserve. Everything else crossed over. This did not.

### Why is there no confession-to-police provision in the UAPA?

Parliament, replacing POTA in 2004, deliberately transferred its punishment, forfeiture and organisation chapters into the amended UAPA but enacted no equivalent to POTA section 32 or TADA section 15, leaving the Indian Evidence Act rule intact, under which a confession made to a police officer remains inadmissible evidence.

## 2008: the amendment that passed in days

The story of the UAPA amendments cannot be separated from the violence that produced the 2008 round, because the sequence of events explains both the content of the changes and the manner of their enactment. The Mumbai attacks of 26 November 2008 killed more than 160 people, and a death toll on that scale placed extraordinary pressure on every institution that claimed responsibility for internal security. Grief, anger and fear moved through public life together, and each emotion pointed in a slightly different direction. Grief demanded acknowledgement. Anger demanded accountability from those who had failed to prevent the killing. Fear demanded assurance that a comparable assault would be met with stronger preparation. Parliament was asked to answer all three demands at once, and it answered them largely through legislation. That choice deserves attention in itself. A legislature can respond to catastrophe with inquiry, with appropriations, with administrative reform, or with new criminal law, and each response carries a different relationship to time. Inquiry looks backward. Appropriation enables future capacity. Administrative reform works slowly through institutions. Criminal law, by contrast, can be altered on the page within days, and its alteration feels immediate even when its practical effects take far longer to judge. The UAPA amendments of 2008 belonged to that last category. They were presented and understood as action, and the speed of their passage became part of their meaning. To read them well, a reader has to hold two thoughts together. The attacks were real, the loss was real, and the demand for a capable state was legitimate. The legal changes were also real, they endured long after the emergency atmosphere faded, and they rearranged the balance between the investigating agency and the individual accused in ways that later litigation would test again and again.

Political change at the Home Ministry formed part of the same sequence. Home Minister Shivraj Patil resigned after the attacks, and P. Chidambaram took charge of the Home Ministry. A resignation in such circumstances performs several functions at once. It marks acceptance of responsibility at the political level, it creates space for a successor to speak with a different voice, and it signals to the public that the government regards the failure as serious rather than routine. The arrival of a new minister also changes the legislative atmosphere, because proposals carried by a fresh office holder arrive with the authority of a response rather than the burden of a defence. The UAPA amendments were therefore not debated in a neutral setting. They came forward under new leadership, in a capital still absorbing the scale of what had happened in Mumbai, and with an opposition and a public primed to measure every proposal against a single question, namely whether the proposal would make another such attack harder to mount and easier to defeat. That question is understandable, and it is also incomplete as a test for criminal legislation. A provision may look strong in the week it is enacted and yet operate unfairly across hundreds of ordinary investigations over many years. A provision may also look severe in the abstract and yet prove necessary where an organised conspiracy crosses borders, uses camps and recruiters, and depends on secrecy. The tension between those two perspectives runs through every clause discussed in this segment.

The legislative vehicle was a pair of bills introduced together. The government introduced the UAPA Amendment Bill together with the National Investigation Agency Bill in December 2008, and the two measures were understood from the start as companion parts of a single response. One bill created a new investigative institution. The other reshaped the substantive and procedural law that the institution, and other agencies besides, would apply. Considered separately, each bill raised distinct questions. Considered jointly, they raised a larger question about the direction of Indian counter terrorism law as a whole, because institutional design and offence definition influence one another. A new agency armed with a narrow statute behaves differently from a new agency armed with a wide one. The pairing therefore mattered, and Parliament treated the NIA Bill and the UAPA Amendment Bill [as a package that passed together within days](/2011/06/15/nia-act-2008-explained/). The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2008 (Act 35 of 2008) received assent and came into force on 31 December 2008. Between the introduction of the bills in December and the commencement of the amending Act on the final day of the year, there was no interval for the slower habits of legislative life to assert themselves. Both bills passed within days without reference to a standing committee. That omission is central to any honest account of the UAPA amendments, because committee reference is the stage at which a bill is ordinarily taken apart clause by clause, at which officials are questioned in detail, and at which drafting weaknesses are most likely to be exposed before they harden into law.

Standing committee scrutiny is easy to underestimate when attention is fixed on floor debate. A floor debate is public and dramatic, but its structure favours broad argument over technical examination. Members speak to the nation as much as to one another, and party positions harden. In committee, by contrast, a single phrase can occupy an entire sitting, and a phrase is often where the real power of a statute lives. Whether custody may extend to a stated number of days, whether a presumption arises on proof of specified facts, whether bail turns on a prima facie assessment, these are questions of wording before they are questions of principle, and wording benefits from time. The 2008 process denied the UAPA amendments that time. Supporters of the amendment pointed to the attacks as proof that the state needed sharper instruments, and that argument carried genuine force in a chamber legislating weeks after more than 160 people had been killed. Civil liberties critics argued from the opposite direction. Human Rights Watch, in its 2010 report titled Back to the Future, argued that the speed prevented scrutiny of permanent changes to bail and custody. The two positions were not really answers to the same question. One side asked what the state lacked when the attacks occurred. The other side asked what the state would retain, and use, in cases far removed from Mumbai. A mature reading keeps both questions open, because the statute itself kept both alive.

The first and most fundamental change concerned the definition of the central offence. The 2008 amendment substituted Section 15 wholesale, recasting and widening the definition of a terrorist act. Wholesale substitution is a significant drafting choice. A legislature that merely adds words to an existing provision signals continuity with what went before. A legislature that replaces a provision entire invites the reader to begin again from the new text, and to resolve doubts by reference to its terms rather than by loyalty to a superseded formulation. For the UAPA amendments, that choice mattered because Section 15 sits at the gateway of the Act. The definition determines which conduct enters the special regime at all, and everything downstream follows from that entry point. Investigation periods, custody rules, bail restrictions and presumptions acquire their practical importance only once conduct has been characterised under the substituted definition. Widen the gateway and the special regime reaches further, even if every later provision remains untouched. Critics of wide definitions make a point here that deserves to be put at its strongest. A counter terrorism statute, on this view, should mark out the gravest conduct with precision, because the label attached to an offence shapes public judgment and the accused person's prospects long before any verdict. Supporters answer at their strongest as well. Organised violence, on this view, mutates in method and target, and a definition drawn too tightly invites evasion by groups that adapt faster than Parliament can legislate. Both arguments are really arguments about where error should fall. One side prefers the error of exclusion, leaving some dangerous conduct to ordinary criminal law. The other prefers the error of inclusion, bringing doubtful conduct inside the special regime and trusting later stages to sort the cases. The substitution of Section 15 placed the statute closer to the second preference.

Definitions elsewhere in the Act were adjusted alongside the central substitution. Definitions in Section 2 were amended, and although definitional clauses rarely attract public attention, they do quiet and decisive work. A definition fixes the reach of every operative provision that uses the defined term. Change the meaning of a single expression and the change travels through chapters that were never themselves debated, altering offences, powers and penalties by reference. For that reason, definitional amendments reward slow reading, and slow reading is precisely what a compressed timetable makes hardest. The UAPA amendments were, in that sense, a system rather than a collection of isolated tightenings. The system had a logic. Its logic began with a wider account of the prohibited act, moved outward to the support structures that make such acts possible, namely camps and recruitment, and then turned inward to the process by which suspects would be held, investigated and kept in custody pending trial. Cumulative effect is the proper unit of analysis for special legislation. Each individual provision, defended in isolation, can sound modest. A few more days for investigation. A stricter test for bail. A presumption triggered by specified facts. Added together across a single case, those modest steps can transform the experience of being accused, and transformation of that kind is what the critics of speed were asking Parliament to pause and examine.

The outward move, toward support structures, took statutory form in two new provisions. The amendment inserted new Section 18A, providing punishment for organising terrorist camps, and new Section 18B, providing punishment for recruiting any person for a terrorist act. The pairing reflects a judgment about how organised violence sustains itself. An attack is an event, but the capacity to attack is an infrastructure. Camps train, house and discipline participants. Recruiters identify, persuade and draw in new participants, often long before any particular target has been chosen. A law confined to the moment of violence would reach the final act and miss the preparation that made the act possible, leaving investigators to rely on general doctrines for conduct that the legislature regarded as distinctive in character. Sections 18A and 18B answered that concern directly, by naming the camp and the act of recruitment as objects of punishment in their own right. At its strongest, the case for such provisions is straightforward. Prevention, if the word is to mean anything in this field, must attach before the explosion or the shooting, and preparation on the scale of a camp or a recruiting effort is neither private thought nor harmless association. At its strongest, the opposing case is also straightforward, and it concerns proof and proximity. Recruitment can be a conversation. Organising can shade into arranging, funding or merely attending. The further an offence moves from the completed act, the more the prosecution case depends on inference about intention, and the more the defence needs room to test that inference. The statute, by creating these offences, placed that contest inside the special regime rather than the ordinary one, with all the procedural consequences that followed from placement.

Procedure occupied the largest part of the new architecture. The amendment inserted new Sections 43A to 43F, a block of provisions dealing with powers of arrest, search and seizure, together with a modified application of Section 167 of the Code of Criminal Procedure through Section 43D. Arrest, search and seizure are the points at which a statute touches the citizen most directly, because they precede charge, precede trial and often precede any judicial assessment of the evidence. A power of arrest determines who may be taken into custody and on what threshold of suspicion. Powers of search and seizure determine how far the state may enter property and take possession of material that may later anchor a prosecution. Placing such powers inside a special statute matters because special statutes develop their own habits of interpretation. Courts asked to construe them are reminded, through the very existence of a separate chapter, that the ordinary balance was reconsidered and reset for this class of case. Supporters of the inserted chapter could point to the character of organised conspiracies, where evidence is fragile, material is moved quickly, and delay between suspicion and search can destroy the object of the investigation. Critics could answer that fragility of evidence is an argument for skilled investigation rather than for lowered thresholds, and that the home and the person deserve protection precisely when public feeling runs against the suspect. What can be said, without taking sides beyond the evidence, is that Sections 43A to 43F ensured that the UAPA amendments were not merely declaratory. A wider offence definition without enforcement powers would have changed labels. Enforcement powers without a wider definition would have changed process for a fixed class of case. Together, they changed both the class and the process.

Within that procedural block, Section 43D carried the provisions that would shape cases most intimately, because it governed time. The period for completing investigation and filing the charge sheet was extended from 90 days to 180 days, with police custody permitted up to 30 days. Time limits in criminal procedure are never mere administrative conveniences. A limit on investigation is also a limit on detention without charge, and detention without charge is one of the most serious things a legal system can authorise, because the detained person has not been convicted of anything and may never be. Extending the period from 90 days to 180 days doubled the outer boundary within which an accused person could remain in custody while the agency assembled its case. The case for the extension, put at its strongest, rests on complexity. A conspiracy investigation may involve multiple accused, material held in different places, records requiring analysis, and links that only become visible after earlier leads have been followed. An agency forced to file prematurely may file badly, and a badly prepared charge sheet serves nobody. The case against the extension, put at its strongest, rests on incentive and on liberty. A longer period reduces the pressure to prioritise, rewards delay, and keeps an unconvicted person behind bars through months that cannot be returned if the case later collapses. Police custody up to 30 days sharpens the same contest. Investigators regard such custody as indispensable where a network must be mapped before its members disperse. Defence lawyers regard extended police custody as the phase of a case in which the accused is most vulnerable and least able to make a contemporaneous record of what occurs. The UAPA amendments chose the investigative side of that contest, in a statute that would apply generally, across every case brought under the Act while the provisions remained in force.

Bail was the second great subject of Section 43D, and in time it became the most litigated. Section 43D(4) excludes anticipatory bail, providing that Section 438 of the Code of Criminal Procedure does not apply to offences under the Act. Anticipatory bail occupies an unusual place in criminal law. It protects a person before arrest, on the understanding that arrest itself inflicts harm to reputation, livelihood and family life that later release cannot fully undo. Excluding it means that a person who apprehends arrest under the Act cannot seek that advance protection and must face the ordinary sequence of arrest, production and a subsequent application for release. Supporters of exclusion argue that a person genuinely involved in organised violence may use advance proceedings to frustrate arrest, alert associates or destroy material, and that the gravity of the offences justifies requiring the accused to submit to custody before seeking liberty. Opponents answer that gravity is an assertion the prosecution makes, not a fact a court has found, and that the exclusion removes a safeguard at exactly the stage where an unfounded allegation can do the most damage with the least scrutiny. Section 43D(5) then bars bail where the court finds reasonable grounds for believing that the accusation is [prima facie true](/2011/07/01/uapa-bail-judicial-interpretation/). The structure of that bar deserves careful attention. In ordinary discourse, bail is described as the rule and custody as the exception, a description rooted in the presumption of innocence. Under the bar, the court is directed toward the accusation itself, and if the accusation clears the stated threshold, release is barred. The hearing therefore turns on an early assessment of prosecution material, made before cross examination and before the testing that a trial provides. That early assessment is not a finding of guilt. Its consequence, however, is detention that may extend for a long period while the trial itself remains distant.

The bar in Section 43D(5) produced the bail case law that later defined the practical meaning of the UAPA amendments, and one decision above all organised the field. In NIA v Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali (2019), the Supreme Court held that at the bail stage a court must accept the prosecution material and cannot conduct a mini trial. The reasoning of that holding flows directly from the statutory threshold. If release turns on whether there are reasonable grounds for believing the accusation is prima facie true, then a court that dismantles the prosecution material piece by piece at the bail stage, weighing credibility as a trial court would, risks collapsing two distinct stages of the process into one. Watali instructed courts to resist that collapse, to take the material at the level the statute prescribed, and to leave the deeper testing for trial. The consequences of that instruction have been argued over intensely, with supporters presenting it as fidelity to the text Parliament enacted and critics presenting it as a formula under which bail becomes almost unattainable once the agency has assembled a coherent narrative. The detailed jurisprudence belongs to the companion treatment of bail under the Act, and it is compressed here deliberately. What matters for an account of the amendments themselves is the line of causation. Parliament enacted a threshold expressed in terms of prima facie truth. Litigation tested what a court may do with that threshold. The Supreme Court answered in Watali by narrowing the inquiry a bail court may undertake. The words chosen under pressure did not remain in the chamber. They migrated into courtrooms, acquired layers of interpretation, and settled into doctrines that govern cases whose facts bear no resemblance to the event that prompted the drafting.

Presumptions formed the third procedural element of the 2008 package. Section 43E creates a presumption against the accused where specified facts are proved. A presumption is a rule about what follows from proof of something else, and its presence in a criminal statute always requires justification, because the ordinary rule places the burden on the prosecution throughout. Where a presumption operates, proof of the specified facts allows the court to treat a further fact as established unless the accused displaces it, and the practical burden of contesting that further fact shifts accordingly. The case for such a device, at its strongest, is evidential realism. Certain facts in organised crime are peculiarly within the knowledge of participants, and a legislature may judge that once the prosecution has proved a defined foundation, fairness does not require it to prove every connected element by the same route. The case against, at its strongest, is that presumptions erode the discipline that proof beyond reasonable doubt imposes. Section 43E did not stand alone, and it should not be assessed alone. Read alongside an extended investigation period, a stricter bail regime and a wider offence definition, a presumption contributes to a cumulative rebalancing that each side characterised differently. One side saw a coherent scheme for conspiracies that are difficult to penetrate. The other saw a scheme in which every doubtful point had been resolved against the individual at the drafting stage.

The political dynamic of enactment completes the picture, and it is worth describing in durable terms rather than through the heat of any single speech. The amendment passed with broad cross party support days after a mass casualty attack. That breadth of support resists any simple attempt to assign the statute to one party or one ideology. In the aftermath of Mumbai, the demand for a stronger legal response crossed the aisle, and legislators who might in calmer conditions have pressed hard on custody periods or bail bars voted for a package that contained both. Such moments reveal something about democratic legislatures under shock. Opposition parties fear the electoral cost of appearing indifferent to security. Government parties fear the administrative cost of appearing unprepared. Both fears push toward enactment, and neither fear is well designed to produce fine drafting. Measures adopted with the widest agreement often receive the least examination, because agreement removes the friction that forces examination. Civil liberties critics, including Human Rights Watch in Back to the Future, made exactly that structural point when arguing that speed prevented scrutiny of permanent changes to bail and custody. The permanence point is the heart of it. Emergency rhetoric surrounded the passage, but nothing in the resulting Act confined its new powers to the emergency. The substituted Section 15, the offences in Sections 18A and 18B, the powers in Sections 43A to 43F, the custody and bail rules in Section 43D and the presumption in Section 43E all entered the general law, available for general use. Supporters responded that permanence was the point, because the vulnerability exposed in Mumbai was itself permanent, and a temporary statute would merely have deferred the reckoning. Between those positions there is no synthesis that honest writing should manufacture. There is only a choice that Parliament made, in days, under grief and pressure, with consequences that unfolded over years of investigation and litigation.


### What does the Section 43D(5) bail bar actually say?

Section 43D(5) provides that a person accused of an offence under the Act must not be released on bail if the court finds reasonable grounds for believing that the accusation against that person is prima facie true, and the provision therefore places a heavy statutory threshold before release.

### How fast did Parliament move after the 2008 attacks?

Parliament moved within weeks. The attacks occurred on 26 November 2008, the government introduced the bills in December, both passed within days without reference to a standing committee, and the amendment received assent and came into force on 31 December 2008, about five weeks after the attacks ended.

## 2012: the amendment that answered the FATF

Among the UAPA amendments, the legislation passed in 2012 stands apart because its organising concern was money. Its declared object was terror financing and economic security, and that object shaped almost every clause that followed. Definitions were widened at the front of the Act, the description of a terrorist act was enlarged, a financing offence was recast, liability was extended to collective bodies, and the provisions on attachment and forfeiture were broadened. Read as a whole, the package was a chain rather than a set of disconnected alterations. Each link supported the next, so that property, persons and organisations connected with terrorism could be reached at the stage of preparation, funding and holding, rather than only after violence had occurred.

The legislative path began with the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Bill, 2011, introduced in the Lok Sabha. Its Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded two foundations. The first was internal: an Inter-Ministerial Group had reviewed the Act, so the proposals were framed as the product of examination across government rather than as a reaction drafted after a single incident. The second foundation was external and specific. India, having joined the Financial Action Task Force as its 34th member, had committed to legislative measures by 31 March 2012. That date converted a general intention to strengthen the law into a stated commitment with a deadline. The Bill therefore carried a dual character from its introduction. It was a domestic amendment to an existing security statute, and it was also the means by which a commitment made on joining an international body was to be discharged in Indian law. The UAPA amendments that followed cannot be understood apart from that pairing of internal review and external obligation.

Parliament passed the Bill in 2012. The resulting statute was the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2012, cited as Act 3 of 2013. It received presidential assent on 3 January 2013 and came into force on 1 February 2013. The sequence tells a story of its own. A Bill introduced in 2011, passed in 2012, assented to at the start of 2013 and brought into force a month later is not emergency legislation in form. Unlike 2008, this amendment moved through the ordinary legislative process over more than a year from the 2011 Bill, and its declared object was terror financing and economic security rather than custody or bail. The longer passage did not by itself settle any question about the merits of the changes, since length of consideration is no guarantee of wisdom in drafting. It did change the character of the exercise. The measure arrived as a considered revision with its object stated in advance, while the substantive reach of what that process produced was considerable. Both points have to be held together in any fair assessment of these UAPA amendments.

The architecture of the amendment repays attention because Parliament began with definitions. In a statute of this kind, a definition is not a dictionary entry placed at the front for convenience. It is operative law. Every later section that uses a defined term inherits the width or narrowness of the definition, so a small change in Section 2 can alter the reach of offences, liability and forfeiture across the entire Act without any later section being touched. The 2012 legislation used that technique repeatedly. It inserted a definition of economic security, inserted an expansive definition of person, and redefined proceeds of terrorism. Taken together, those three moves widened the subject matter the Act could address, the range of actors it could address, and the range of property it could address. Subject, actor and property are the three coordinates of a financing offence, and the amendment adjusted all three before turning to the operative provisions. The later changes to Sections 15, 17, 22A to 22C, 24 and 33 all drew strength from that groundwork, which is why Section 2 is the right place to begin.

The new definition of economic security, inserted as clause (ea) in Section 2, described financial, monetary and fiscal stability, the security of means of production and distribution, and food, livelihood, energy, ecological and environmental security. The list deserves slow reading. Financial stability, monetary stability and fiscal stability are related but distinct ideas, concerning respectively the health of the financial system, the currency and the conditions under which it holds value, and the revenue and expenditure position of the state. It then moved beyond the institutions of finance to the means of production and distribution, the material machinery by which goods are made and moved. From there it moved again to food, livelihood, energy, ecological and environmental security, the conditions under which people live and work. The progression runs from the abstract to the concrete, from stability of systems to security of daily life. Whether that breadth is regarded as necessary recognition that harm to economic life can wound a country, or as an expansion placing much ordinary activity within the vocabulary of a terrorism statute, the textual fact is plain. Economic security arrived as a defined term with a wide and layered content.

The breadth of clause (ea) raises a question that runs through the whole amendment. A definition drawn in wide terms can ensure that a statute is not defeated by narrow reading, so that harm belonging within its mischief does not escape because the drafter failed to foresee its exact form. It can also shift power towards those who apply the law, because the wider the defining words, the more situations can be argued to fall within them, and the greater the burden on a court to supply limits that the text does not state. Both readings are available on the language, and neither cancels the other. At the same time, a clause joining fiscal stability to ecological security in one definition asks a great deal of interpretation later. Coverage and limit pull against each other throughout the UAPA amendments of 2012, and clause (ea) is where that tension first appears.

The second definitional change was the new definition of person in clause (ec) of Section 2. A person was defined to mean an individual, a company, a firm, an organisation or association of persons or a body of individuals whether incorporated or not, artificial juridical persons, and agencies or branches owned or controlled by any of these. The drafting moves outward in stages. It begins with the individual, the ordinary subject of criminal law. It then gathers in the collective forms through which economic life is conducted, the company and the firm. It widens further to an organisation or association of persons and to a body of individuals, expressly covering such bodies whether incorporated or not, so that absence of formal incorporation does not place a collective outside the term. It adds artificial juridical persons, entities which the law treats as persons although they are not human beings. Finally, it reaches agencies or branches owned or controlled by any of the preceding categories, extending the definition along lines of ownership and control rather than stopping at the named entity itself. For a statute concerned with financing, that final extension has obvious logic, since money and instructions move through branches and agencies, and a definition confined to a parent entity would invite evasion through structure.

Clause (ec) also explains why the later provisions on companies, societies and trusts were not ornamental. Once person had been defined to include collective and juridical forms, the Act needed operative provisions that could attach consequences to such persons in a coherent way. A definition can bring an entity within the language of a statute, but offences, liability and forfeiture still require sections that do the work. The relationship between clause (ec) and Sections 22A to 22C is therefore one of definition and machinery. The definition answered the question of who could count as a person. The new sections answered the question of what followed when the person in question was a company, a society or a trust. The 2012 legislation moved on the two fronts together, and that coordination is one reason the UAPA amendments of this round form a recognisable scheme rather than a miscellany.

The third definitional change concerned proceeds of terrorism, redefined to include any property intended to be used for terrorism. The operative word is intended. Property derived from an act already committed belongs to a familiar category of proceeds, because a completed act stands behind it. Property intended to be used looks forward rather than backward. It captures value assembled, held or directed towards terrorism before the act it was meant to serve has taken place, and in some situations before any such act takes place at all. Yet the same character creates the hardest evidential questions in the scheme. Intention must be proved, and property not yet used will often look entirely ordinary in itself, because money, premises and equipment acquire their character from the purpose for which they are held rather than from any mark upon them. The redefinition placed intention at the centre of the property provisions, and with it the quality of the evidence by which intention is established.

Away from Section 2, one change of duration is short to state and significant in effect. Under Section 6, the period for which a confirmed declaration of an unlawful association stays in force was extended from two years to five years. Such a declaration is not a finding about a single act. It is a determination about an association, carrying consequences for the association and for those connected with it for as long as it remains in force. Extending the period more than doubled the span during which a confirmed declaration would operate. Support for a longer period can be stated in terms of stability. An association found unlawful after a confirmatory process does not necessarily change its character quickly, and repeated proceedings at short intervals consume institutional effort while producing uncertainty. The contrary argument can also be stated strongly. The longer a declaration endures, the longer its consequences endure on the strength of the material that supported it when it was confirmed, and conditions, membership and conduct may alter considerably across five years. The amendment chose the longer period, tilting Section 6 towards continuity, in keeping with the general direction of the UAPA amendments in 2012.

Section 15, which defines a terrorist act, received the most concentrated attention. Its intent limb gained the expression economic security, so that the term inserted by clause (ea) was carried directly into the operative heart of the Act. This is where the definitional work in Section 2 acquired its sharpest consequence. A term placed in a definition section may influence many provisions, but its insertion into Section 15 placed economic security alongside the established objects of the intent limb in the very section that decides what counts as a terrorist act. The move enlarged the range of intended harm that could bring conduct within Section 15, by recognising that an intention directed at the economic security of the country, as clause (ea) defines that expression, could stand in the same position as the intentions already named in the limb. Argument about Section 15 after the amendment could therefore no longer be confined to physical targets or immediate danger in the conventional sense. It had to engage with financial, monetary and fiscal stability, with production and distribution, and with food, livelihood and energy, because Parliament had written those subjects into the section through the definition.

Within Section 15, a new sub-clause (iii-a) made damage to the monetary stability of India, by production, smuggling or circulation of high quality counterfeit Indian paper currency, coin or any other material, a terrorist act. The provision is remarkable for the precision of its target amid the breadth elsewhere. It identified a particular harm, damage to monetary stability, and particular means, namely production, smuggling or circulation, confined to high quality counterfeit material. Each means does distinct work. Production addresses the making of the material, smuggling its movement, and circulation its passage into the hands of people who will spend or transfer it, which is the stage at which false currency performs its damaging function. Parliament thereby characterised an assault on monetary stability through such material as terrorism rather than as an economic offence standing alone.

The Explanation substituted by the amendment defined public functionary and defined high quality counterfeit Indian currency by reference to the key security features specified in the Third Schedule. Referring the definition to specified features gave the expression a technical anchor, instead of leaving high quality to impression or assertion in an individual case. Two further changes completed the enlargement of Section 15. Clause (c) was extended to compelling an international or inter-governmental organisation, so that the compulsion limb reached such organisations alongside the bodies already within its contemplation, removing any argument that such an organisation stood outside the limb merely because it was not a government in the ordinary sense. A new Section 15(2) brought in offences under the treaties specified in the Second Schedule, linking the domestic definition of a terrorist act to a schedule of treaties. Both changes looked outward, one towards international organisations as possible objects of compulsion, the other towards treaties as a source of offences, in a statute whose immediate occasion had itself been an international commitment.

Consolidation accompanied enlargement. Section 16A, inserted in 2008 to punish demanding bombs, explosives, radioactive substances or nuclear devices to aid terrorism, was omitted, and its substance was folded into the amended Section 15. The omission was not a relaxation, since the conduct was relocated rather than released. Placing that substance within Section 15 gave the definition of a terrorist act a single centre of gravity, instead of leaving closely related conduct in a neighbouring provision and inviting argument about the relationship between the two. At this point the technique of the UAPA amendments was tidying in service of expansion.

Section 17 was substituted, and the substitution went to the heart of the financing object. Raising or collecting funds for a terrorist act, from legitimate or illegitimate sources, became punishable with imprisonment from five years to life imprisonment, whether or not the funds were actually used. Three features require emphasis. The pairing of raising and collecting covers both the person who generates funds and the person who gathers them from others, roles that may be performed by different hands at different stages. The express reference to legitimate or illegitimate sources directed attention away from the origin of the money and towards the purpose for which it was raised or collected, since lawfully earned or openly given money does not lose its character as financing because its source was innocent. The declaration that liability does not depend on actual use detached the offence from the fate of a later act, so that intervention or abandonment preventing expenditure would not defeat it. In favour of that design, money is the enabling condition of organised violence, and a law that waits for expenditure surrenders the preventive advantage justifying a financing offence. Against it, purpose based liability of this width rests entirely on evidence of intention, in a field where donations and transfers are everyday acts performed constantly for innocent reasons. The section chose preventive reach and accepted that evidential burden, locating the wrong in raising or collecting for the prohibited purpose.

New Sections 22A, 22B and 22C dealt with offences by companies, societies and trusts. Their insertion answered a practical problem anticipated by clause (ec). Funds are held by companies, collected through societies, settled in trusts and moved through structures whose legal personality is distinct from the men and women who direct them. A statute proceeding only against natural persons would leave the entity holding the funds or providing organisational cover outside the offence provisions, even while individuals within it were exposed. The three sections closed that gap at the level of the entity, through provisions addressed to each form rather than a single formula forced upon unlike structures.

Sections 24 and 33 were amended to widen attachment and confiscation, Section 24 being concerned with proceeds of terrorism and Section 33 with forfeiture. Those amendments belonged with the redefinition of proceeds of terrorism, since an expanded definition would have limited force if the provisions securing such property, and finally taking it under the authority of the Act, remained narrow. Attachment operates while proceedings take their course and forfeiture operates at the final stage, so widening both strengthened the property dimension of the statute at the provisional and the final moment alike. Set against the whole, the scheme is coherent in a single movement. Section 2 widened the field, Section 6 lengthened a confirmed declaration from two years to five years, Section 15 absorbed the new concepts and the substance of Section 16A, Section 17 punished the funding, Sections 22A to 22C reached the entities, and Sections 24 and 33 reached the assets. Neither the orderly process nor the breadth of substance cancels the other, and holding both findings together is what distinguishes analysis of the UAPA amendments from advocacy about them.

### Why did the 2012 amendment touch Sections 17, 22 and 33 as well?

Because the Bill's declared object was terror financing, the expanded definition in Section 15 needed a matching financing offence in Section 17, corporate liability in the new Sections 22A to 22C, and wider forfeiture in Sections 24 and 33 to make the scheme coherent and enforceable in practice.

## 2019: the individual becomes the target

For most of its life, the principal Act dealt with unlawful associations and with terrorist organisations as collective entities. A group could be listed, its name placed in a schedule, and its activities pursued through the offences and procedures that followed from that listing. The person behind the group, directing its affairs, recruiting for it, financing it or sheltering inside its changing names, occupied a different position. Prosecution had to fasten upon acts proved against that person in the ordinary criminal process. The amendment of 2019 altered that architecture at its most sensitive point. It allowed the state to attach the description of terrorist to a named human being by executive notification, separately from any listing of an organisation and separately from any conviction recorded by a court. That movement, from organisation to person, is why the 2019 exercise stands apart among the UAPA amendments. 

### Passage through Parliament

The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Bill, 2019 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 8 July 2019 by Home Minister Amit Shah. The Lok Sabha passed it on 24 July 2019 and the Rajya Sabha passed it on 2 August 2019. During that passage, opposition members pressed for the Bill to be sent to a select committee, a course that would have subjected its clauses to closer committee examination before enactment, and the House did not oblige. Presidential assent came on 8 August 2019, and the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2019, Act 28 of 2019, came into force on 14 August 2019. The measure was a substantive enlargement of executive power, contested in both Houses on the question of further scrutiny. A government moving at that pace can say that a gap identified by its agencies should not remain open, while those who sought committee referral can answer that a power touching reputation and liberty deserved the slower discipline of evidence and clause by clause consideration.

### What Section 35 changed

Section 35 was amended so that the Central Government may designate an individual as a terrorist by notification and add the name of that individual to the Fourth Schedule, a schedule created for this new class of entry. Before the amendment, the listing mechanism in this part of the Act was directed at organisations. After it, the Centre could proceed against the organisation and against the person in parallel, or against the person without waiting for the organisational form through which that person was said to operate. The statutory grounds set out in Section 35(3) describe the individual who commits or participates in acts of terrorism, who prepares for terrorism, who promotes or encourages terrorism, or who is otherwise involved in terrorism. The width of that language became part of the controversy from the outset, because its later limbs, promoting, encouraging and being otherwise involved, reach forms of connection whose boundaries are less easily stated in advance than committing, participating and preparing.

That breadth can be defended as a response to the way terrorist networks divide labour. One person recruits, another raises money, another supplies shelter, another lends public encouragement intended to draw recruits towards violence, and few participants need to place a device or fire a weapon for the enterprise to function. A definition confined to the final physical act would miss much of the machinery that makes the act possible. The opposing argument draws the opposite inference from the same division of labour. Precisely because encouragement and involvement can describe speech, association and assistance at several removes from violence, a power to designate on those grounds calls for tight procedure before the label is applied, not merely a remedy after the label has done its work. Section 35 does not supply a pre-designation hearing, and that absence became the hinge of the criticism.

### The first four names

The first four designations were notified on 4 September 2019. They were Maulana Masood Azhar, Hafiz Muhammad Saeed, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi and Dawood Ibrahim Kaskar. These were not marginal figures drawn from a contested domestic prosecution, and their alleged roles were familiar in Indian public discussion of cross border terrorism. A government introducing a controversial instrument gains an advantage when its first use is directed at figures against whom public objection is least likely to be organised. The legal question, however, is never settled by the identity of the first persons affected. A power framed in general language remains available for later use against persons whose cases may be harder, whose alleged involvement may rest upon material that has not been tested, and whose capacity to challenge a notification may be far weaker. Assessing the amendment therefore requires attention to the ordinary operation of that continuing power, not only to the notoriety of the men named on 4 September 2019.

### Seizure, attachment and the rank of the investigator

Two further changes concerned the machinery of investigation rather than the definition of the designated person. Section 25 was amended so that, in cases investigated by the National Investigation Agency, the approval of the Director General of the NIA suffices for seizure or attachment of property. Previously, the approval of the state police chief was required in that setting. Its detractors could present the same alteration as a thinning of external check, because an approval sought outside the investigating agency at least compels a second institution to examine the proposal before property is seized or attached. Section 43 was amended in a related direction. Officers of the NIA of the rank of Inspector or above may investigate offences under Chapters IV and VI. Previously, the minimum rank for investigation was Deputy Superintendent of Police or Assistant Commissioner of Police.

Lowering that floor widened the pool of officers available to the agency and reduced the bottleneck that a seniority requirement can create when cases multiply. The gain in flexibility is plain, and so is the corresponding concern, because rank requirements in special legislation operate as a rough proxy for experience and judgment in the exercise of coercive powers. When Parliament lowers such a threshold for offences carrying the gravity attached to Chapters IV and VI, it trades a measure of that proxy protection for speed and staffing depth. Read together, the changes to Sections 25 and 43 reveal the secondary purpose of the exercise: the headline amendment concerned designation, while the quieter amendments concerned friction inside investigation, whose signature was needed before property could be reached and how junior an officer could lawfully conduct the inquiry.

### The case for the change

The government rationale rested first upon evasion. Organisations banned by name reconstitute under new names, so individuals must be designable to prevent evasion of the prohibition by the simple expedient of renaming the body. A front can dissolve on paper while its leadership, funds and networks pass into a successor entity with a fresh title and the same purpose, leaving a listing regime that must begin again with each successor name permanently one step behind. Designation of the individual was presented as the answer, attaching consequences to the continuity of the person rather than to the discontinuity of organisational names. The second strand of the rationale concerned the National Investigation Agency and the procedural difficulties it faced in investigation and prosecution, difficulties that the changes were meant to remove. On this account, the approval rule for seizure and the rank rule for investigation corrected a process in which an agency created for the gravest cases depended upon approvals from outside its own chain, or upon a restricted cadre of senior officers, for work that had to be done promptly. Put at its strongest, the case for the amendment is a case about continuity and capacity.

### The case against

The criticism, put at its strongest, begins where the government case ends. Designation carries reputational and practical consequences without a prior judicial finding and without a pre-designation hearing. A person is named by the executive as a terrorist in a schedule to an Act of Parliament. The description is among the gravest that a state can publish about any individual, and it affects how employers, banks, neighbours, officials and strangers will treat that person from the moment of notification, long before any review body has examined whether the statutory grounds were properly made out. A remedy operating only after publication cannot restore a reputation to the position it occupied before publication, and a process that hears the affected person only after the decision has been taken reverses the order that natural justice is ordinarily expected to follow in matters of such severity. Critics added that the review provided after designation is departmental, since the application goes first to the Central Government that made the designation and the Review Committee is constituted within a framework established by that government.

The objection is not that no remedy exists. Sections 36 and 37 do provide remedies, and any fair account must say so. The objection is that the remedies are weighted towards confirmation of an executive act already performed, with the designated person carrying the burden of displacing a finding reached without hearing that person at all. Supporters answer that a prior hearing would frustrate designation by giving warning to persons whose assets and movements the state seeks to restrain, and that listing regimes in public law commonly proceed by executive act followed by review. The contest is genuine, and it cannot be resolved by repeating either slogan, security or liberty, as though the other carried no weight.

### Remedies after designation

Section 36, extended to individuals, allows the designated person to apply to the Central Government for removal of the name from the Fourth Schedule. Section 37 provides a Review Committee for the situation in which the government refuses that application, so that the refusal does not rest without further institutional examination. The committee is chaired by a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, and it may have up to three other members. Its limits are as visible as its strengths. An application under Section 36 asks the designating authority to undo its own notification. A committee under Section 37 reviews a refusal within the scheme created by the Act, rather than conducting the original inquiry into designation as a court of first instance would conduct it.

A designated person therefore faces a sequence designed by the same statute that authorised the label: apply to the government, and, upon refusal, seek the committee. Anyone reading that sequence for practical purposes should treat the description here as a map of the general statutory position only, since that general position does not substitute for advice on an individual case and limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving. The distance between a remedy on paper and its effective use can be considerable, turning upon the grounds stated in the notification, the material disclosed to the applicant and the time taken at each stage. Material supporting the grounds may include intelligence assessments that cannot necessarily be placed before the applicant in full, so a review body must decide how much deference to give to executive claims about sources and methods. Too much deference empties the review of content, while too little risks compromising the material on which counter terrorism work depends.

### The challenge in the Supreme Court

Petitions challenging the designation power were filed in the Supreme Court in 2019 by Sajal Awasthi and by the Association for Protection of Civil Rights. A bench led by Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi issued notice to the Union of India on 6 September 2019. Issuing notice signifies that the court considered the challenge fit for a response from the Union. It is not a ruling upon validity, and no accurate account should present it as one. The petitions placed before the court, in constitutional form, the questions that the parliamentary debate had already exposed in political form. The procedural history then took a turn that left those questions open. In February 2025, a bench led by Chief Justice Sanjiv Khanna declined to hear the challenges as the court of first instance and directed them to the High Courts. The constitutional validity of the designation power therefore stands undecided on the merits.

That posture deserves emphasis. Delay is not an implied verdict, the issue of notice is not an implied endorsement, and a direction to approach another forum is not an implied rejection. It would not be accurate to describe the power as upheld, and it would not be accurate to describe it as condemned. The statute occupies the harder position of an enacted power under unresolved challenge, enforced by agencies, resisted by those it touches and reserved for a judicial answer that arrives on a timetable no participant controls.

### Who sits on the Review Committee that hears designation challenges?

Under Section 37, the Review Committee is appointed by the Central Government and chaired by a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, with up to three other members sitting alongside the chairperson when it is properly constituted for the hearing of applications.

## The UAPA power ledger

Four exercises in amendment, spread across a decade and a half, can be misread if each is treated as a self contained response to the politics of its own year. Placed in a single ledger, they show a pattern of accumulation, in which powers granted for one occasion remain in the statute for every later occasion, while the protections accompanying them vary sharply from one exercise to the next. The ledger names for each amendment [the safeguard that was or was not added alongside the new power](/2011/05/15/uapa-1967-complete-guide/). Reading across the rows, rather than down any single column, is what allows the balance of each bargain to be seen, and it is that balance, repeated or abandoned across the four rows, that the closing section of this article examines.

## The arc: what four UAPA amendments reveal about security legislation

The pattern that emerges from the four exercises can be stated plainly. Powers arrive with occasions, and safeguards arrive with litigation. Each significant enlargement of the Act followed a triggering event or a pressing institutional demand that supplied Parliament with a reason to act at that moment and in that form. The repeal of the Prevention of Terrorism Act and the electoral commitment given against that statute produced the settlement of 2004, in which terrorism provisions were placed inside the older Act. The Mumbai attacks of 26 November 2008 produced the enlargement of 2008, with its extended investigation window, its custody provisions and its restriction upon bail. Membership of the Financial Action Task Force and the review conducted by the Inter-Ministerial Group produced the exercise of 2012, enacted as Act 3 of 2013, which widened the definition of a terrorist act and the offences surrounding finance. The enforcement needs of the National Investigation Agency, together with the evasion of organisational bans by renaming, produced the designation power of 2019. 

Constraint followed a different timetable and travelled by a different road. Parliament sometimes placed a safeguard beside the power, as the ledger records for the Review Committee remedy in 2004, the forensic gate in the exercise of 2012 and the committee check extended to individuals in 2019. Yet the deeper disciplining of such powers, the kind that determines how broadly a definition will be read, how strictly a bail restriction will be applied, how much material an accused person may see and how far executive satisfaction may be questioned, has characteristically been worked out later, in courtrooms, in cases brought by persons who had felt the power in its concrete form. Litigation is slow because it waits for a live grievance, for a record, for counsel, for a bench and for a judgment pronounced after argument on both sides. The power is drafted in the heat of the occasion, at the breadth the occasion seems to demand. The limit is settled afterwards, in the cooler and narrower language of a judgment confined to the case before the court.

Occasion driven legislation is not automatically bad legislation. The Mumbai attacks exposed real questions about investigation time, custody and coordination, and a Parliament that had refused to address them would have faced a serious charge of neglect. A financial system brought within an international framework against terrorist finance required statutory language capable of meeting obligations that India had accepted, and an investigating agency that reports procedural obstruction is entitled to be heard. Equally, litigation driven constraint is not automatically wise constraint. Courts decide the case argued before them, and their rulings can leave adjacent questions unanswered for years, as the unresolved challenge to the designation power illustrates. The harder criticism is therefore structural. When powers accumulate occasion by occasion, each addition is defended by reference to the event that prompted it, but the addition does not retire when the event recedes. The investigation window granted after Mumbai governs cases unconnected with that attack, and the designation power justified by named men of exceptional notoriety governs any later notification issued upon the statutory grounds. Permanence converts an argument drawn from a particular emergency into an ordinary rule.

Defenders of the statute have a substantial answer, and it should be given without abbreviation. Security threats, in their submission, do not present themselves as a series of sealed episodes after which the legal system can safely return to an earlier position. Networks persist, financing methods adapt, recruitment changes its channels, and an agency deprived of a power after a fixed interval may face a threat that has outlasted the interval. On this view, the permanence of the amendments is a recognition that the underlying phenomenon is durable, and that each amendment corrected a weakness that experience had shown to be real rather than conjectural. The same defenders point out that Parliament retains its ordinary legislative power at all times, so a provision that has outlived its justification can be repealed or narrowed whenever a majority is persuaded, and courts remain available to strike down or read down what exceeds the Constitution. Review, on this account, exists continuously in the institutions of the Republic, even if no calendar compels it.

### The sunset clause debate

Against that background, critics of anti-terror law have proposed that the Act, or at least its most coercive provisions, should carry a sunset clause or a requirement of mandatory periodic review. The proposal draws its force from the history of the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, a statute enacted as temporary legislation and renewed across a decade. The lesson its critics draw is double edged. A temporary statute at least obliged its supporters to return to Parliament and seek renewal, so continuation was a fresh legislative act rather than a silence. Yet renewal across a decade also showed that a sunset date does not guarantee expiry, because a Parliament persuaded of continuing danger can extend a temporary law again and again until temporariness becomes a formality observed at intervals. 

Supporters of the proposal argue that such a discipline would change the quality of debate in ways that ordinary legislative power does not. A government that knows a power will lapse unless renewed must prepare an account of its use: how often it was invoked, against whom, with what result in prosecution, and at what cost to persons against whom proceedings failed. Parliament, for its part, would vote upon a record rather than upon the memory of the occasion that first produced the power. Periodic review would also create a natural vehicle for amendments that experience had shown to be necessary, allowing correction and continuation to be considered together. In that setting, a safeguard would not need to wait upon the accidents of litigation. It could be proposed, debated and enacted as part of the renewal bargain, by the institution that created the power.

The reply made by defenders of the statute meets that argument at its centre. Security threats do not expire on a legislative timetable, and a lapse caused by parliamentary congestion, political deadlock or the accidents of a session could strip investigators of powers in the middle of operations that depend upon them. A sunset clause, on this view, manufactures a cliff edge where none needs to exist, and it invites adversaries to wait out the calendar. Periodic review, the defenders continue, happens anyway through ordinary means: questions, debates, committee examination, amendment whenever a majority wills it and judicial scrutiny whenever a case raises it. To add a formal expiry is to mistake a procedural device for a substantive protection, and the history relied upon by the critics proves the point, since a temporary statute renewed across a decade protected no one merely by being temporary. If a provision is unjust, it should be repealed or struck down for that reason.

Each side exposes a weakness in the other. The critics are right that ordinary legislative power, though always available in theory, is seldom exercised to revisit a security statute without a forcing event, and that forcing events in this field have historically expanded powers rather than contracted them. The defenders are right that a sunset clause is a blunt instrument, capable of producing either perfunctory renewal or dangerous lapse. What the debate actually concerns is the placement of inertia. Without a sunset clause, inertia favours the power: it continues unless opponents assemble a majority against it. With one, inertia favours expiry. A legal order that wishes its security powers to be reconsidered in calmer conditions must find a mechanism that reverses that alignment, whether by sunset, by structured review, by reporting duties with real consequences, or by courts willing to decide challenges on their merits.

That is the arc the cluster of articles on this statute has followed, and a reader who wishes to pursue any strand of it in depth will find the fuller treatment in the specialist pieces that accompany this one: the statute pillar, which sets out the Act as a whole in its amended form; the article on the National Investigation Agency, which examines the institution whose procedural needs shaped so much of the later amendment; the article on bail jurisprudence, which traces how the courts have handled the restriction that the 2008 exercise placed upon release; and the comparison of TADA, POTA and the UAPA, which places three generations of anti-terror legislation side by side. Readers who present the trail to others can [step through the UAPA power ledger as a presentation](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), row by row, before the discussion opens. Taken together, those pieces support the single observation with which this account began. In Indian security legislation, the occasion supplies the power, promptly and in general terms, and the argument about limits begins only afterwards, in forums that move more slowly and speak more narrowly than Parliament on the day of the vote.

### Has any UAPA amendment ever carried a sunset clause?

No. No UAPA amendment has ever carried a sunset clause, and every amendment discussed in this ledger inserted permanent provisions into the principal Act. That permanence is precisely why the contrast with TADA matters, because TADA began as temporary legislation and was renewed repeatedly across a decade overall.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many times has the UAPA been amended?

Seven amending exercises are usually counted. The Act was amended by the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 1969, by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1972 and by the Delegated Legislation Provisions (Amendment) Act, 1986, all of which adjusted the machinery for declaring associations unlawful. Four later amendments transformed the statute: the Amendment Act of 2004 (Act 29 of 2004), which inserted the terrorism chapters; the Amendment Act of 2008 (Act 35 of 2008), passed after the Mumbai attacks; the Amendment Act of 2012 (Act 3 of 2013), which widened the definition of a terrorist act; and the Amendment Act of 2019 (Act 28 of 2019), which permitted the designation of individuals. The first three amendments are footnotes to the associations regime; the last four built the anti-terror law.

### Q: What did the 2004 UAPA amendment add?

The Amendment Act of 2004 (Act 29 of 2004), deemed in force from 21 September 2004, the day POTA ceased to operate, rebuilt the Act. It substituted the long title to add the words "and for dealing with terrorist activities", replaced Sections 1, 2 and 2A with definitions including "terrorist act", "terrorist gang" and "terrorist organisation", and replaced the closing chapter with four new chapters: Chapter IV (sections 15 to 23) on punishment for terrorist activities, Chapter V (sections 24 to 34) on forfeiture of proceeds of terrorism, Chapter VI (sections 35 to 40) on terrorist organisations, and Chapter VII (sections 41 to 53), with a Schedule listing terrorist organisations. Sections 36 and 37 added a removal application and a Review Committee for listed organisations.

### Q: What changed in the UAPA after the 2008 Mumbai attacks?

Within weeks of the attacks of 26 November 2008, Parliament passed the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2008 (Act 35 of 2008), in force on 31 December 2008, together with the National Investigation Agency Act. The amendment substituted Section 15, recasting the definition of a terrorist act; inserted Sections 18A and 18B, punishing the organisation of terrorist camps and recruitment for a terrorist act; and inserted Sections 43A to 43F. Through Section 43D, the period for completing investigation and filing a charge sheet rose from 90 days to 180 days, with police custody permitted up to 30 days. Section 43D(4) excluded anticipatory bail, Section 43D(5) barred bail where the accusation appeared prima facie true, and Section 43E created a presumption against the accused.

### Q: What did the 2012 UAPA amendment do about economic offences?

The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2012 (Act 3 of 2013), in force from 1 February 2013, wrote economic security into the offence itself. It inserted a definition of "economic security" in Section 2, covering financial, monetary and fiscal stability and the security of production, distribution, food, livelihood, energy and the environment, and it added "economic security" to the intent limb of Section 15. A new sub-clause (iii-a) made damage to the monetary stability of India, by production, smuggling or circulation of high quality counterfeit Indian paper currency, coin or any other material, a terrorist act. The Explanation defined high quality counterfeit currency by the key security features in the Third Schedule, with forensic examination as the gate.

### Q: Why was the 2019 UAPA amendment controversial?

The controversy centred on designation. Before 2019, only organisations could be listed as terrorist entities; the Amendment Act of 2019 (Act 28 of 2019) let the Central Government designate an individual as a terrorist by notification, on the grounds in Section 35(3), and place the name in the new Fourth Schedule. The government defended the change on the ground that banned organisations reconstitute under new names, so the law had to reach the persons behind them. Critics, including the petitioners who challenged the amendment in the Supreme Court, answered that designation carries reputational and practical consequences without any prior judicial finding and without a hearing before designation, leaving the designated person to a departmental remedy under Sections 36 and 37.

### Q: Can a designated individual challenge the designation?

Yes, through a staged remedy. Section 36, as extended by the 2019 amendment, lets a designated individual apply to the Central Government for removal of the name from the Fourth Schedule. If the government refuses, Section 37 provides review by a Review Committee whose chairperson is a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, sitting with up to three other members; the committee can order removal where it finds the decision flawed. The remedy is administrative rather than a trial, and it begins only after designation. Separately, the constitutional validity of the designation power itself has been challenged in court; the Supreme Court declined to hear that challenge as the court of first instance in February 2025 and directed it to the High Courts, so the question stands undecided on the merits.

### Q: Did the UAPA amendments import provisions from repealed laws?

In substance, yes, with one famous exception. The 2004 amendment was enacted in the same package as the Prevention of Terrorism (Repeal) Act, 2004, and it transplanted much of POTA's architecture into the UAPA: a definition of a terrorist act, offences of membership, fundraising and harbouring, a listing regime for terrorist organisations and forfeiture of proceeds of terrorism. The exception was POTA's Section 32, which had made a confession recorded by a police officer admissible in evidence, as TADA's Section 15 had done before it. The 2004 amendment carried no equivalent, leaving the ordinary Evidence Act rule in place. Human rights lawyer Colin Gonsalves described the result as POTA brought back except for the police-confession provision.

### Q: Has the UAPA become stricter with every amendment?

In direction, broadly yes; in every detail, no. The 2008 amendment lengthened investigation and custody and barred bail on a prima facie test; the 2012 amendment widened the central definition to economic security and counterfeit currency; the 2019 amendment extended designation from organisations to individuals. Each of those steps enlarged the powers of the state. But the trail is not a uniform ratchet. The 2004 amendment declined to import POTA's rule admitting confessions recorded by police officers, a real subtraction compared with the statute it replaced, and the 2012 amendment omitted Section 16A even while folding its substance into Section 15. The accurate statement is that powers accumulated amendment by amendment, while safeguards generally arrived later and from the courts.

### Q: Did the original 1967 UAPA mention terrorism at all?

No. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 (Act 37 of 1967) was enacted to implement the Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act, 1963, which had placed the sovereignty and integrity of India among the grounds for restricting the freedoms of speech, assembly and association in Article 19. Its subject was unlawful activity: action intended to bring about the cession or secession of Indian territory, to question territorial integrity, or to incite disaffection against India. Its machinery was the declaration of an association as unlawful under Section 3, confirmation by a Tribunal under Section 4, and the offences in Sections 10 and 13. The words "terrorist act" entered the statute only with the amendment of 2004, nearly four decades later.

### Q: What changed in the UAPA's long title in 2004?

The long title of a statute states its object, and the 2004 amendment changed the object. The original long title described an Act to provide for the more effective prevention of certain unlawful activities of individuals and associations. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2004 inserted, after the word "associations", the words "and for dealing with terrorist activities". The small verbal change announced the large structural one that followed in the same amending Act: four new chapters on terrorist activities, forfeiture of proceeds of terrorism and terrorist organisations. Courts read a statute in light of its stated object, so the altered long title is the first textual sign that the Act of 1967 had acquired a second and larger purpose.

### Q: Why was the 2004 amendment given effect from 21 September 2004?

Because repeal and replacement were designed to meet on a single day. The Prevention of Terrorism (Repeal) Act, 2004 and the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2004 were a package: one statute ended, and the other acquired its terrorism chapters, with no interval in which India lacked a central anti-terror law. Parliament chose 21 September 2004 as the hinge date. The Amendment Act, passed later in the year and assented to on 29 December 2004, was therefore deemed to have come into force on 21 September 2004, the day POTA ceased to operate. Retrospective commencement of that kind is a recognised drafting device, and here it is what converted two separate Acts into one continuous settlement.

### Q: What is Section 43E of the UAPA and when was it inserted?

Section 43E creates a presumption in a prosecution for a terrorist act under Section 15. If it is proved that arms, explosives or other substances specified in Section 15 were recovered from the possession of the accused and there is reason to believe that such material was used in the offence, or that expert evidence, such as fingerprints, links the accused to the site of the offence or to the arms and vehicles used in it, the court shall presume, unless the contrary is shown, that the accused committed the offence. It was inserted by the Amendment Act of 2008, in the block of Sections 43A to 43F. The presumption reverses the ordinary starting point of a criminal trial for the facts it covers.

### Q: Why did the 2012 UAPA amendment take effect only in February 2013?

The short title misleads if read carelessly. The Bill was introduced in 2011 and passed by Parliament in 2012, so the amending Act carries the short title of 2012; but an Act is numbered by the year of assent, and presidential assent came on 3 January 2013, making it Act 3 of 2013. Its commencement clause left the starting date to notification by the Central Government, and the appointed day was 1 February 2013. Courts have treated that date as decisive: the Kerala High Court held that the amended Section 15 could not be applied to counterfeit currency offences committed before 1 February 2013, consistently with the bar in Article 20(1) of the Constitution on retrospective criminal liability.

### Q: How did the 2012 amendment expand the definition of a person in the UAPA?

The Amendment Act of 2012 inserted clause (ec) in Section 2, defining "person" expansively for the whole Act. A person includes an individual; a company; a firm; an organisation or an association of persons or a body of individuals, whether incorporated or not; every artificial juridical person not falling within the earlier heads; and any agency, office or branch owned or controlled by any of them. The definition mattered because the same amending Act substituted Section 17 on raising funds, inserted Sections 22A, 22B and 22C on offences by companies, societies and trusts, and widened forfeiture. A narrow definition of person would have left the financing and corporate liability provisions unsure of their subjects; clause (ec) closed that gap in advance.

### Q: What did the 2012 amendment change about how long a declaration of an unlawful association lasts?

Section 6 of the Act fixes the life of a declaration. A notification under Section 3 declaring an association unlawful takes effect when the Tribunal confirms it, and the confirmed declaration then remains in force for the period Section 6 prescribes. Before the amendment of 2012 that period was two years. Section 3 of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act, 2012 substituted "five years" for "two years", so a confirmed declaration runs for five years from the Tribunal's order. The change belongs to the older associations regime of the Act rather than to its terrorism chapters, and it is easy to miss because the 2012 amendment is remembered for economic security. Its practical effect is that the executive's characterisation of an association, once judicially confirmed, endures two and a half times as long.

### Q: What new offences did the 2012 amendment create for companies, societies and trusts?

Sections 22A, 22B and 22C, inserted by the Amendment Act of 2012 (Act 3 of 2013). Section 22A deals with offences by companies, a term its Explanation extends to firms and other associations of individuals: the company and every person in charge of and responsible for its business are deemed guilty, subject to a defence of absence of knowledge or the exercise of reasonable care. Section 22B applies the same structure to societies and trusts, reaching promoters, trustees and officers whose consent, connivance or neglect is proved. Section 22C prescribes the punishment: imprisonment of not less than seven years, extending to life, and a fine of not less than five crore rupees, extending to ten crore rupees. The provisions gave the financing offences of the same amendment a corporate subject.

### Q: Why does the 2019 amendment let the Director General of the NIA approve property seizure?

Section 25 governs the seizure and attachment of property representing proceeds of terrorism, and an investigating officer needs prior approval before acting. Before 2019, the approving authority was the Director General of Police of the state, even where the case was being investigated by the National Investigation Agency. That produced a mismatch: a central agency answerable for an investigation depended on a state officer's approval at a step where delay could defeat the seizure. The Amendment Act of 2019 therefore provided that where the case is investigated by an officer of the NIA, the approval of the Director General of the NIA suffices. The amending Bill's stated objects framed the change as part of removing the procedural difficulties the agency faced in investigation and prosecution.

### Q: Who were the first individuals designated as terrorists under the UAPA?

The first four names entered in the Fourth Schedule, by notifications of 4 September 2019, weeks after the Amendment Act of 2019 came into force on 14 August 2019, were Maulana Masood Azhar, Hafiz Muhammad Saeed, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi and Dawood Ibrahim Kaskar. All four were already the subjects of international designations and of Indian allegations arising from attacks including the assault on Mumbai in 2008. The choice of the first cohort was itself part of the debate about the power: supporters of the amendment pointed to the exceptional notoriety of the four men, while critics answered that a power justified by its first targets would govern every later case, including accused persons whose cases had nothing in common with the first four.

### Q: Has the constitutional challenge to the 2019 UAPA amendment been decided?

No. Petitions challenging the 2019 amendment, including the designation of individuals, were filed in the Supreme Court in 2019 by Sajal Awasthi and by the Association for Protection of Civil Rights. A bench led by Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi issued notice to the Union of India on 6 September 2019, and the petitions remained pending in that Court for years. In February 2025, a bench led by Chief Justice Sanjiv Khanna declined to entertain the challenges as the court of first instance and directed that they be pursued before the High Courts instead. The designation power therefore operates with its constitutional validity undecided on the merits, which fits the amendment trail's pattern: the power arrived in 2019, and its limits await a court.

### Q: What did the 2012 amendment change about the punishment for raising funds for a terrorist act?

It substituted Section 17 wholesale. The substituted section punishes whoever, in India or in a foreign country, directly or indirectly raises, provides or collects funds, whether from a legitimate or an illegitimate source, knowing that the funds are likely to be used, in full or in part, by an individual terrorist, a terrorist gang or a terrorist organisation to commit a terrorist act, whether or not the funds were actually used for such an act. The punishment is imprisonment for a term of not less than five years, extending to imprisonment for life, with liability to fine. An Explanation adds that raising funds includes raising or collecting them through the production, smuggling or circulation of high quality counterfeit Indian currency, tying the financing offence to the counterfeit currency limb the same Act wrote into Section 15.
