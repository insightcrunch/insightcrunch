The First Amendment to the Indian Constitution is usually introduced as a footnote to the founding: a small repair job carried out in June 1951, seventeen months after the Constitution came into force, which adjusted a handful of articles and added a schedule. That introduction is accurate in every detail and misleading in its whole effect. The Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 was the moment the Indian executive first reached for the amending power to escape a courtroom defeat, and the reaching became a habit. Between 1951 and 1976 the same move recurs, each time larger, until the Forty-Second Amendment of the Emergency years rebuilt great stretches of the constitutional text in a single statute. The claim defended here is stated plainly at the outset. The First Amendment was not the Constitution's correction; it was the precedent for executive-driven constitutional change that culminated in the Emergency.

## The Procedural Account and What It Hides

The standard summary can be written in one breath. The Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 received the assent of President Rajendra Prasad on 18 June 1951. It inserted a new clause into the equality code, permitting special provision for socially and educationally backward classes. It rewrote the permissible restrictions on speech and added protection for state trading monopolies. It created two new articles, 31A and 31B, shielding land reform legislation from challenge under the fundamental rights, and it placed thirteen zamindari abolition statutes beyond judicial reach in a new Ninth Schedule. It tidied a set of smaller provisions on parliamentary sessions, on the lists of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and on the tenure arrangements inherited from the interim period. Coaching manuals reproduce the summary faithfully, and examination candidates memorise it. Nothing in the summary is wrong. Everything about its framing is evasive.

The evasion lies in the order of explanation. A procedural account presents the amendment as a response to drafting defects, as though the Constituent Assembly had left loose ends that a conscientious government gathered up. The record of 1950 and 1951 shows a different sequence. In that record, the government of Jawaharlal Nehru lost cases. The Supreme Court struck down a Madras ban on a left-wing journal on 26 May 1950. The same bench struck down a Delhi pre-censorship order against the Organiser on the same day. The Patna High Court held the Bihar land reform statute unconstitutional in March 1951. The Supreme Court then held, on 9 April 1951, that the Madras communal order allocating medical and engineering college seats by caste and religion violated the equality guarantee. Within eleven weeks of the last of those defeats, the constitutional text itself had been rewritten so that the defeats could not repeat. Sequence is the argument. The amendment followed the judgments the way a reply follows an argument, and it was drafted to win the argument by changing the rules under which the argument was judged.

Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), gave the procedural account its most respectable scholarly form: an institutional explanation in which a young state, facing the administrative friction of a new rights regime, adjusted the machinery. That reading is engaged at length below, and it explains the substance of several provisions well. What it does not explain is the choice of instrument. A government that loses a case can legislate within the judgment, can appeal, can wait for a larger bench, or can accept the constraint as the price of a rights constitution it had itself just written. The Nehru government, in 1951, chose the most drastic instrument on the list, and chose it within weeks. Later governments inherited both the instrument and the memory of its first successful use. To understand why the Forty-Second Amendment was thinkable in 1976, the place to begin is not 1975 but the spring of 1951, when amendment first proved itself as the executive's reliable answer to an inconvenient court.

## Seventeen Months: How Young the Constitution Still Was

The Constitution of India came into force on 26 January 1950. The republic it created had held no general election. The legislature that amended it in 1951 was the Provisional Parliament, the Constituent Assembly continuing in its legislative capacity, a body whose members had been chosen in 1946 by provincial assemblies elected on a restricted franchise. [The founding settlement that the First Amendment began modifying barely seventeen months later](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) had been designed by the same politicians who then sat in judgment on its first failures, wearing a different hat. Nehru was at once a principal author of the settlement, the head of the government constrained by it, and the mover of the bill that revised it. That concentration of roles is not a scandal; the founding generation staffed every institution of the new state. It does mean that the amendment carried fewer of the brakes that a settled constitutional culture supplies. No convention of restraint had yet formed around the amending power, no line of commentary warned that amendment within seventeen months was a grave step, and no opposition in the chamber commanded the numbers to slow the bill. The step was taken at the speed of ordinary legislation, and speed, in constitutional matters, is itself a precedent.

### Why was the Indian Constitution amended so soon after it was adopted?

The amendment came within seventeen months because three defeats converged in a single season. The Supreme Court freed the journal Crossroads and the newspaper Organiser from state bans on 26 May 1950, the Patna High Court condemned the Bihar zamindari statute in March 1951, and the Supreme Court struck down Madras reservations on 9 April 1951.

The Constituent Assembly had anticipated amendment, of course. Article 368 supplied a procedure, and nobody in the Assembly generation believed the text was perfect. B. R. Ambedkar, defending the draft in November 1948, had described a constitution as a mechanism to be worked, and the Assembly had deliberately made the Indian text easier to amend than the American. Flexibility, however, was defended in the Assembly as a property of the document, a provision for corrections that time would reveal. What happened in 1951 was different in kind from the correction of a revealed drafting error. The text had not malfunctioned. It had functioned exactly as a rights text is meant to function: it had told the government that certain things could not be done, in the cases of a banned journal, a censored newspaper, a reservation order and a land statute. The government's complaint was not that the machine was broken. The complaint was that the machine's output, on the first occasions it produced output, obstructed policies the government held dear. Amending a constitution because its constraints bind is a coherent political act, but it is a different act from amending a constitution because its clauses misfire, and the difference set the pattern for the decades that followed.

The founding had also left the underlying questions open, as Madhav Khosla argues in India's Founding Moment (2020). The Assembly deferred the hierarchy between the Directive Principles and the fundamental rights, deferring with it the question whether social reform could claim constitutional priority over individual rights when the two collided. The collisions of 1950 and 1951 were that deferred question arriving in court, wearing the dress of a Madras admissions order and a Bihar land statute. The judges who decided Champakam Dorairajan and the Bihar case were not inventing a conflict; they were answering, with the tools the text gave them, a question the founders had declined to answer in the Assembly. The amendment answered it at last, in the executive's favour, and the manner of the answering mattered as much as the answer. A deferred question had been settled not by deliberation in calm weather but by the interested party, mid-litigation, rewriting the rule that had produced an unwelcome judgment.

## Champakam Dorairajan and the Reservation Trigger

The first trigger was an admissions order in Madras. Since 1927 the Madras Presidency, and after it Madras State, had distributed seats in government medical and engineering colleges under a communal order that fixed proportions for communities: out of every fourteen seats, six went to non-Brahmin Hindus, two to Brahmins, two to backward Hindus, two to Muslims, one to Anglo-Indians and Indian Christians, and one to the Scheduled Castes. Champakam Dorairajan, a Brahmin applicant, challenged the order after being denied admission, and Srinivasan, a second applicant, joined the challenge. The Madras High Court held the order unconstitutional, and on 9 April 1951 a bench of the Supreme Court, in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) AIR 226, affirmed. The reasoning was textual and, on the text as written, hard to escape. Article 29(2) forbade denial of admission to a state-aided institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language. The communal order classified by exactly those grounds. The Directive Principle in Article 46, urging the state to promote the educational interests of weaker sections, could not rescue the order, because the Directive Principles were expressly unenforceable and subordinate to the fundamental rights. The Court stated the hierarchy in terms that admitted no ambiguity: the chapter on fundamental rights was sacrosanct, and the Directive Principles had to conform to it and run subsidiary to it.

Politically, the judgment arrived as a detonation. Reservation in Madras was not a marginal welfare scheme; it was the institutional inheritance of the non-Brahmin movement, decades older than the republic, and it commanded the allegiance of the governing class of the state. The judgment told that class that the Constitution it had just celebrated had abolished its central instrument at a stroke, and told it in the name of a clause, Article 29(2), whose drafting the Assembly had completed with the communal order's existence known to every Madras member. The Nehru government's Statement of Objects and Reasons for the amending bill took up the judgment directly. In spite of Article 46, the Statement observed, the Supreme Court had held that caste-based reservation in admissions could not be sustained, and it had become necessary to rewrite the equality provisions so that the state's policy of special provision for backward classes would stand. The remedy chosen was a new clause, Article 15(4), stating that nothing in Article 15 or in Article 29(2) prevented the state from making special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes.

The clause deserves a fair reading before the pattern argument resumes, because on its own terms it has worn well. India in 1951 was a society of steep, hereditary educational disadvantage, and a colour-blind equality clause administered without qualification would have frozen that disadvantage in place while calling the freezing neutrality. Article 15(4) made substantive equality constitutionally legible, and the architecture of Indian affirmative action, from the Mandal Commission's implementation in the 1990s to the present structure of admissions, rests on the clause the First Amendment inserted. A reader can defend every word of Article 15(4) and still notice what the episode taught the executive about method. The lesson was not that reservation was just. The lesson was that a judgment obstructing a settled policy of the governing party could be reversed by amending the text the judges were interpreting, and that the reversal could be carried through the Provisional Parliament in a matter of weeks, with the judges left to apply the new text to the next case. Shankari Prasad, decided later in 1951, would confirm that the courts themselves accepted the lesson's legality. The substance of the Madras trigger was equality. The precedent it produced was about power.

## Romesh Thapar, Brij Bhushan and the Speech Trigger

The second trigger was already a year old when the bill was drafted, and it concerned the printed word. In February 1950 the journal Crossroads, edited in Bombay by Romesh Thapar and aligned with the communist movement, published reporting on a firing inside Salem Central Jail in which twenty-two prisoners died. On 1 March 1950 the Madras government banned the journal's entry and circulation in the state under the Madras Maintenance of Public Order Act, 1949. In Delhi, on 2 March 1950, the Chief Commissioner imposed pre-censorship on the Organiser, the weekly associated with the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, requiring the printer and publisher, Brij Bhushan, to submit all matter on communal questions and on Pakistan for official approval before publication. Both targets were critics of the government from opposite ends of the political field, and both petitioned the Supreme Court under Article 32. On 26 May 1950 the Court decided the two cases together in effect if not in form. In Romesh Thapar v. State of Madras (1950) SCR 594, Justice Patanjali Sastri held that the Madras ban fell outside the restrictions the Constitution then permitted: Article 19(2) as adopted saved laws relating to libel, slander, defamation, contempt of court and matters offending decency or morality, and laws undermining the security of the state or tending to overthrow it, but it contained no general public order ground. A ban justified by public order therefore could not stand. In Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi (1950) SCR 605, the same majority struck down the pre-censorship order on the same ground.

### What did the Supreme Court actually forbid in the Romesh Thapar case?

The Court forbade a state from banning a journal in the name of public order, because Article 19(2), as adopted in 1949, contained no public order ground. Political discussion, Justice Patanjali Sastri wrote, was the foundation of democratic government, and only restrictions tied to the security of the state could survive.

The government read the two judgments as a constitutional emergency of a peculiar kind. The early 1950s were violent years: a communist insurgency in Telangana, communal tension after Partition, refugee movements in Bengal, and a press that included party organs willing to print material the government regarded as incitement. Nehru wrote privately, and said publicly, that the freedom guaranteed by Article 19(1)(a) had been construed so widely by some courts that even advocacy of murder had been placed beyond legislative reach; the Patna and Madras high courts had divided on whether speech inciting violence could be punished, and the Statement of Objects and Reasons complained of the divergence. A narrower government might have sought a ruling from a larger bench, or framed a statute aimed precisely at incitement and tested it. The bill instead rewrote Article 19(2) wholesale. The substituted clause permitted reasonable restrictions on speech in the interests of the security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, and restrictions in relation to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence. Two phrases did the heavy work. The word reasonable subjected restrictions to judicial review, a concession to the courts. The ground public order handed back to legislatures, in general terms, the very power the Court had denied Madras in May 1950.

The opposition in the Provisional Parliament saw the exchange clearly and said so on the record. Syama Prasad Mookerjee, who conceded the chamber's formal power to amend, warned that a public order ground in the hands of provincial governments would be turned against legitimate criticism, and that a constitution amended against a judgment within a year of the judgment taught every future ministry the same trick. The amendment's defenders answered with the circumstances of 1951, and the circumstances were real. The dispute was never between liberty and tyranny in the abstract. It was between two accounts of who corrects whom. In the Court's account, the text of 1949 had drawn a line, and the government's remedy was to work within it or persuade a constituent body in calmer conditions. In the government's account, the line had been drawn in ignorance of governing conditions, the conditions were dangerous, and the amending power existed for precisely such discoveries. The pattern reading adds the observation that decides between the accounts as a matter of institutional history: whatever the merits in 1951, the government's account, once vindicated, became the default setting of the Indian state. Speech lost a case in 1950 and the clause lost its shape in 1951. That order of events, repeated across fields, is the pattern.

## Zamindari Abolition in the Courts: The Third Trigger

The third trigger was land. The Congress had promised zamindari abolition for a generation, and the promise was the centrepiece of its claim to be a reforming government rather than a caretaker of the colonial settlement. Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh enacted abolition statutes in 1950 and 1951, and the former zamindars sued at once, resting on the property guarantee of Article 31 and the equality guarantee of Article 14. The litigation moved faster than the reform. On 12 March 1951, in State of Bihar v. Maharajadhiraja Sir Kameshwar Singh, the Patna High Court struck down the Bihar Land Reforms Act, 1950, holding provisions of it discriminatory under Article 14, while the Allahabad and Nagpur high courts upheld the corresponding Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh statutes. The agrarian programme of the governing party thus stood valid in two states and void in a third, on a compensation and classification logic that threatened every abolition law in the country, with appeals certain and years of delay assured. For a government whose provincial ministries had staked their authority on abolition, the Bihar judgment was not an abstraction. It was the flagship social reform of the republic being dismantled in the courtroom of its largest province.

Nehru's letters of the period treat the zamindari litigation and the speech cases as one problem, and that bundling is the analytical key to the amendment. The same instrument was applied to three distinct fields, equality in admissions, freedom of the press and property in land, because from the Prime Minister's desk the fields were one field: the capacity of the elected executive to govern against the resistance of a rights text interpreted by judges. The Statement of Objects and Reasons stated the land difficulty with unusual frankness, observing that the validity of agrarian reform measures had formed the subject matter of dilatory litigation and that implementation of measures affecting large numbers of people had been held up. The remedy was the most radical device in the bill. Article 31A immunised a class of acquisition laws, covering estates and rights in estates, from Articles 14, 19 and 31 altogether. Article 31B went further: it declared that the statutes listed in the new Ninth Schedule, thirteen enactments at the start, were not to be deemed void on the ground of inconsistency with any fundamental right, notwithstanding any judgment to the contrary, and it operated retrospectively. A schedule of statutes placed outside the rights chapter by name, immune even from the courts' power to say what the rights meant, had no precedent in the founding text. The Ninth Schedule was invented in 1951, in a hurry, for land. Its later career, as a warehouse for hundreds of statutes far removed from agrarian reform, began in this clause.

Consider what the device reveals about the amendment's logic. Article 15(4) corrected a clause; Article 19(2) widened a clause; Articles 31A and 31B did something categorically different, removing a subject from constitutional adjudication itself. The escalation within a single bill, from editing the text to exempting whole statutes from the text, shows an executive learning in real time how far the amending power could be pushed. Nothing in the chamber's reception discouraged the learning. The bill passed with the government's majority intact, the land provisions attracted less debate than the speech provisions, and the Ninth Schedule entered the Constitution with a fraction of the scrutiny its later importance warranted. When later governments reached for the same schedule to protect reservation statutes, industrial nationalisations and eventually, in the Emergency, a swathe of the regime's legislative programme, they were not abusing an innocent device in a manner its inventors could not have foreseen. They were using the device as designed: a list, amendable by the same majority that passed ordinary amendments, of laws the courts were forbidden to test.

## Nehru Defends the Bill: The Provisional Parliament, May and June 1951

The bill entered the Provisional Parliament in May 1951, moved by Nehru on 10 May, and the debate that followed, running through the middle of May and into the first days of June, is the founding document of the amendment pattern. Nehru's speech of 16 May 1951, the under-cited primary source at the centre of the brief for this article, repays attention because the Prime Minister declined to hide the bill's logic inside procedural language. He told the chamber that high courts had given a construction to the fundamental rights that paralysed the government's programme, that the judiciary could not act as a third house of the legislature, and that where judges and the elected government differed on the direction of social policy, the Constitution had to be brought into line with the policy. The land question and the speech question, he insisted, admitted no delay: zamindari abolition was a commitment on which ministries would stand or fall, and the state facing communal propaganda and insurgent violence could not accept a reading of Article 19 that placed public order beyond its reach. The speech is remarkable less for its candour than for its theory. Nehru presented amendment not as an emergency exception but as the normal relation between a sovereign parliament and a fallible text, the legislature correcting the judiciary as one organ corrects another within a going concern.

### Why did Nehru choose amendment instead of working within the judgments?

Amendment was chosen because the defeats were structural rather than incidental. The judgments rested on the constitutional text, on the absence of a public order ground in Article 19(2), on the wording of Article 29(2), on the property guarantee of Article 31, so no statute drafted within the text could restore the policies. Only rewriting the text could.

[The Nehru position in the founding triangulation that the First Amendment operationalised in legislative form](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) helps explain the confidence of the performance. Within the founding trio, Nehru had carried the reforming programme: land abolition, a managed economy, a secular state actively intervening in social practice. Patel, who had supplied the administrative spine and much of the Assembly's conservative ballast, had died on 15 December 1950, five months before the bill was moved. Ambedkar, the text's principal draftsman, sat in the same cabinet as Law Minister, in worsening disagreement with Nehru over the Hindu Code Bill, and the chamber debating the amendment contained no figure of equivalent constitutional authority to argue the text against its interpreters. The amendment was thus carried by the strongest political figure of the age, at the head of a dominant party, in an unelected house, against an opposition of fragments. Every institutional condition favoured speed, and speed was achieved: the bill was passed by the Provisional Parliament at the start of June 1951 and received presidential assent on 18 June 1951 as the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951.

The debate was not a formality, and the record preserves the counter-argument in a form strong enough to state fairly. Members including Syama Prasad Mookerjee, H. V. Kamath and Naziruddin Ahmad attacked the speech provisions as a confession that the government feared criticism more than disorder, and the property provisions as retrospective confiscation wearing constitutional dress. K. T. Shah, who had argued in the Assembly for a more thoroughly social constitution, objected from the other flank to a method that strengthened the executive while leaving the structure of property substantially intact. The strongest form of the opposition case was constitutional rather than partisan: a text amended by the very majority whose actions the text restrains, in direct response to restraints imposed, converts amendment from a people's reserved power into an incumbent's tool. Nehru's majority answered the case by outvoting it, and the answer, too, became part of the pattern. In 1971 and again in 1976, governments defending far larger amendments would reach for the rhetoric of 1951, the mandate of the elected house against the obstruction of courts, and would find the rhetoric ready-made in the record of the founding Prime Minister. Precedent in politics is rarely a citation. It is a remembered permission, and 1951 is where the permission was first granted.

## What the Amendment Changed, Clause by Clause

Taken clause by clause, the Act was compact, and its compactness is part of its interest: a small number of textual moves, each answering a named defeat, whose combined effect exceeded any of them. Article 15 gained clause (4), permitting special provision for socially and educationally backward classes and for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, notwithstanding Article 15 itself and Article 29(2). Article 19(2) was substituted wholesale, adding the word reasonable, adding the grounds of public order and friendly relations with foreign states, and adding incitement to an offence, so that the restrictions legislatures had assumed they possessed became restrictions the text expressly allowed. Article 19(6) was clarified to place state monopolies in trade and business beyond challenge under the freedom of occupation, a sentence aimed at the nationalisation programme. Articles 31A and 31B, with the Ninth Schedule, built the land reform shelter described above. Around these headline changes, the Act adjusted the machinery of the interim republic: the provisions on sessions of Parliament and of the state legislatures in Articles 85, 87, 174 and 176, the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes lists in Articles 341 and 342, and transitional arrangements in Articles 372 and 376. The minor clauses matter to the pattern argument in one respect. A government amending a constitution within seventeen months for reasons of high policy chose to use the same statute as a vehicle for housekeeping, treating the constitutional text, from its first amendment onward, as a working document of the ministry rather than a monument approached only in solemn circumstances.

The documentary record of the change is unusually complete, and it rewards the kind of close reading the pattern argument requires. The Act's text and its Statement of Objects and Reasons state the government's reasons in its own words; the parliamentary debates preserve the opposition's warnings verbatim; Nehru's correspondence of the season, including his letters to chief ministers, records the private reasoning. Researchers who reconstruct the episode work from papers that governments and families have preserved under pressure, and the practical disciplines of that work are the ones archivists and investigators share with journalists who must [keep sensitive source material protected while a story is still vulnerable](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/). The completeness of the 1951 record is itself evidence in the adjudication below. A government conscious of transgression hides its reasoning; the Nehru government published its reasoning, because it believed the reasoning was right and expected history to agree. Granville Austin's institutional reading feeds on exactly this openness, and any honest pattern reading must absorb the fact: the precedent was set in daylight, defended in principle, and recorded in full. Daylight, however, does not dissolve a precedent. It dignifies it, and a dignified precedent is the kind later governments find easiest to cite.

One further clause-by-clause observation belongs here, because later sections lean on it. The amendment's drafters were careful lawyers, and the care shows in the calibration of the devices. Article 15(4) was drawn as an enabling provision inside the equality code, leaving courts to police its use, as they did for decades afterward. Article 19(2) added the discipline of reasonableness, an express invitation to judicial review of restrictions. Articles 31A and 31B, by contrast, excluded review entirely for their protected classes. The bill thus contains, in miniature, the whole subsequent argument about Indian amendment practice: calibrated adjustment within the rights framework on one page, outright exclusion of the framework on the next. The pattern that culminated in 1976 was not a slow slide from the first page to the second. Both pages were present in the founding amendment, signed on the same day, and the history of the next three decades is in part the history of which page governments chose to imitate.

## Shankari Prasad: The Judiciary Ratifies the Pattern

The amendment's legal completion came from the institution it had been aimed at. Zamindari abolition in Uttar Pradesh, protected by Articles 31A and 31B, was challenged by Shankari Prasad Singh Deo and other proprietors, who argued that a constitutional amendment was itself law within the meaning of Article 13, which forbade the state from making any law taking away or abridging the fundamental rights, and that the First Amendment, abridging rights as it did, was void. A five-judge bench of the Supreme Court, in Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India, decided on 5 October 1951, rejected the argument unanimously. Justice Patanjali Sastri, who had written the Romesh Thapar judgment the amendment had partly reversed, held for the Court that the power to amend the Constitution, conferred by Article 368, was a constituent power distinct from ordinary legislative power, that the word law in Article 13 meant law made in the exercise of legislative power, and that a constitutional amendment made in the exercise of constituent power stood outside the prohibition. Parliament could therefore amend Part Three, could restrict or remove fundamental rights by amendment, and the First Amendment was valid in every part.

The judgment deserves the same fair statement as the judgments it displaced. The text supported it: Article 368 spoke in general terms of amending the Constitution, Article 13 did not mention amendments, and the Assembly had plainly contemplated a flexible text. A contrary holding in October 1951 would have voided the reservation clause and the land settlement four months after their enactment, on the motion of the zamindars whose cause had already lost in Parliament, and would have placed the young Court in a confrontation with the founding generation from which the Court, itself a founding institution of four years' standing in its federal predecessor's tradition and barely twenty months in its own, might not have recovered. The judges chose institutional peace and textual plausibility. The pattern reading does not need to call the choice cowardice, and does not. It needs only to record the choice's consequence. After Shankari Prasad, the executive's new method carried a judicial certificate. The amending power had been tested by the very interests the amendment injured, and the courts had announced, in advance of every future controversy, that the certificate covered fundamental rights themselves. When the question returned in Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan in 1965, a five-judge bench followed Shankari Prasad and upheld the Seventeenth Amendment, and although Justices Hidayatullah and Mudholkar recorded doubts that would flower in Golak Nath, the majority's reasoning treated the 1951 settlement as closed. Sixteen years passed between the founding amendment and the first judicial attempt to limit what it had licensed. In those sixteen years the Fourth, Seventh and Seventeenth Amendments ran the pattern three more times, and the Ninth Schedule grew from thirteen statutes to a hundred and twenty-four. Practice hardens while doctrine sleeps, and the hardening, in this story, is the point.

There is a subtler consequence, visible in the rhetoric of the later confrontation. Because the courts had validated the method in 1951 in the language of constituent power, the governments of 1971 could present the Twenty-Fourth Amendment not as an innovation but as a clarification, a restatement of Shankari Prasad against the aberration of Golak Nath, and could present the judges who resisted as the innovators, breaking a twenty-year settlement. The rhetorical advantage in constitutional argument belongs to the side that can claim continuity, and the First Amendment's defenders had secured that advantage for every successor. Austin's institutional reading, at its strongest, is a reading of exactly this continuity: a working constitution, amended within its own procedure, validated by its own court, developing by consent of the organs. The pattern reading answers that continuity is precisely what a precedent is, and that the question is never whether a precedent is continuous but what it is continuous with. The line from Shankari Prasad runs through the Twenty-Fourth Amendment's text, which borrows the judgment's constituent-power language almost verbatim, to the Forty-Second Amendment's claim that the power so described knew no limits at all. The Court in Minerva Mills would eventually hold that the power was limited by the structure of the document it amended, a holding that required overruling the spirit, if not the letter, of its own certificate of 1951. Certificates, like amendments, are easier to issue than to withdraw.

## The Amendment and the First General Election

One circumstance of timing completes the founding picture, and it is rarely given its weight. The amendment was enacted in June 1951, in the last months before the republic's first general election, held across the winter of 1951 to 1952, in which some 173 million voters were eligible to choose a Parliament for the first time. The sequence cuts in two directions, and both belong in the record. Against the amendment, the sequence is an indictment of its democratic pedigree: the Provisional Parliament revised the rights chapter in the full knowledge that an elected Parliament was months away, and declined to wait for it. A government confident of its mandate, the opposition argued in substance, could have carried the same bill before the new Lok Sabha with an authority no one could question, and the refusal to wait suggested that the litigation calendar, the need to have the land statutes and the reservation order safe before the campaign, mattered more than the constitutional proprieties. The Madras order and the Bihar Act were not abstractions in an election season; they were the governing party's record in two great provinces, and the amendment secured that record against the courts before a single vote was cast.

For the amendment, the sequence supplies the only popular ratification the episode ever received, and it was emphatic. The Congress fought the election of 1951 to 1952 on the programme the amendment had protected: zamindari abolition, social reform, planned development under a strong centre. The opposition to the amendment inside the chamber had been led by figures, Mookerjee above all, who carried the argument to the country at the head of a new party, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded in October 1951 partly in the amendment debate's aftermath. The country returned the Congress with 364 of 489 seats in the Lok Sabha and with provincial majorities across the states whose statutes the amendment had sheltered. No serious reading of that verdict can treat it as a referendum on Article 31B, and this article does not. Voters chose a government, a leader and a programme in the first exercise of a franchise most had never held. Yet the verdict matters to the adjudication because it closes the simplest version of the usurpation charge. The amendment's makers submitted themselves, within months, to the largest electorate ever assembled, on the policies the amendment defended, and were returned in triumph. Whatever the First Amendment was, it was not a coup against a resisting public. It was the act of a dominant party in a dominant moment, ratified after the fact by the democracy it had declined to wait for, and that combination, legality, popularity and institutional impatience in a single act, is exactly what made the precedent so portable. A usurpation can be disowned by successors. A ratified success is inherited.

The election also fixed the amendment's place in the Congress system's self-understanding, the framework Rajni Kothari would later name. The party that had made the Constitution had amended it, defended the amendment as governance, and won the founding election on the defence. In the party's memory the episode proved that constitutional flexibility and electoral dominance were partners, that the text could be adjusted to the programme and the electorate would reward the adjustment. That memory governed the party's reflexes for a generation, through the amendments of the 1950s and 1960s and into the confrontations of the 1970s, when a different Congress leadership, facing courts rather than riding a founding wave, reached for the same reflex and discovered that the country's answer had changed. The pattern's life was never only legal. It was carried in the habits of a party that had learned, in the season of its greatest authority, that amending the Constitution was something winners did.

## The Amendment-Pattern Timeline, 1951 to 1980

The findable artifact for this article is the Thirty-Year Amendment-Pattern Timeline, set out in the table below, and it is labelled here so that a reader can cite it by that name. The timeline lists each major executive-driven amendment between 1951 and 1980, the judicial ruling or line of rulings that triggered it, and the judicial response that followed, because the pattern is visible only when trigger, amendment and response are read together in sequence. Read down the trigger column and a rhythm emerges: the courts speak, the executive amends, the courts regroup around a new line, and the executive amends again at a higher level of generality, until the contest reaches the amending power itself in Golak Nath, is answered by the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, and is settled, provisionally and on terms neither side had proposed in 1951, by the basic structure doctrine of Kesavananda Bharati and its defence in Minerva Mills. Three lessons fall out of the timeline before the narrative resumes. The first is escalation by generality. The First Amendment answered three specific defeats with three specific devices. The Fourth answered one compensation ruling by removing adequacy from review altogether. The Twenty-Fourth answered a ruling about the amending power by redefining the amending power. Each round, the executive's response operated one level above the ruling that provoked it, because a response at the same level could be litigated again, while a response one level up changed the ground. The second lesson is the judiciary's counter-escalation, which was slower and doctrinal rather than textual: Shankari Prasad conceded the field in 1951, Golak Nath attempted a total prohibition in 1967, and Kesavananda Bharati in 1973 found the durable line, conceding amendment of every provision while reserving the basic structure from destruction. The third lesson is the one the pattern thesis exists to state. None of the later actors, on either side, behaved as though the First Amendment had been a routine repair. Governments cited its logic when they amended; judges, when they finally limited the power, wrote against the memory of its unlimited use. The timeline is the biography of a precedent, and precedents are judged by their descendants.

## The Pattern Extends: 1955 to 1971

The Fourth Amendment of 1955 is the cleanest early proof that 1951 had set a template rather than closed a file. In State of West Bengal v. Bela Banerjee, decided in December 1953, the Supreme Court read the word compensation in Article 31(2) to require a just equivalent of the property acquired, a market-value standard that threatened the economics of every acquisition programme of the state. The government's reply followed the 1951 choreography exactly: no acceptance of the standard, no legislative redesign within it, but an amendment of the clause itself, declaring that no acquisition law could be called in question on the ground that the compensation provided was inadequate. The same statute widened Article 31A, extending its shelter from estates to a list of public purposes including the taking over of property and the amalgamation of corporations. Four years after the First Amendment, the sequence judgment, obstruction, amendment, had run a second full cycle in the property field alone, and had begun to acquire the quality that defines a pattern: it no longer needed arguing for. Ministers and draftsmen reached for amendment as the ordinary sequel to an adverse ruling, the way an administration reaches for a press release.

The Seventh Amendment of 1956 belongs in the timeline with a distinguishing mark, because its trigger was administrative rather than judicial, and the distinction clarifies the thesis rather than weakening it. The reorganisation of states on linguistic lines, set in motion by the creation of Andhra in 1953 and systematised by the States Reorganisation Commission under Fazl Ali, whose report came in 1955, required constitutional surgery: the threefold classification of states abolished, boundaries redrawn, provisions for High Courts, legislatures and the services recast. The surgery was performed by amendment, in a single statute, without serious suggestion that so deep a redesign exceeded the proper use of the amending power. The founding generation had normalised amendment so thoroughly, in the five years since 1951, that it had become the republic's all-purpose instrument of constitutional adaptation, used against judgments and used for geography with the same unhesitating hand. A separate study in this series traces the linguistic settlement on its own terms; here the Seventh Amendment matters as evidence that the instrument's prestige, established in conflict with the courts, had generalised into a habit of governance.

### How did Golak Nath change the amendment contest in 1967?

Golak Nath changed the contest by attempting to end it. On 27 February 1967 an eleven-judge bench held, six to five, that Parliament possessed no power to amend the fundamental rights at all. The ruling protected the Punjab family's land from ceiling legislation, and it converted sixteen years of skirmishing into a direct confrontation over the amending power.

The confrontation matured quickly. The Supreme Court's decision in R. C. Cooper v. Union of India in February 1970, striking down the nationalisation of fourteen banks on compensation and equality grounds, showed the Indira Gandhi government that the judicial front remained active on every flank of its programme. The reply was the double amendment of 1971. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment rewrote Article 368 to declare that Parliament, in the exercise of its constituent power, could amend any provision of the Constitution, and amended Article 13 to place constitutional amendments outside its definition of law, a point-by-point reversal of Golak Nath. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment recast the compensation clause once more, substituting the word amount for compensation, and inserted Article 31C, giving laws implementing the Directive Principles in Articles 39(b) and 39(c), on the distribution of material resources and the prevention of concentrated wealth, primacy over Articles 14, 19 and 31. Read against 1951, the escalation is exact. Nehru's amendment had subordinated particular rights to particular policies in particular clauses. The 1971 amendments, moved by his daughter's government, claimed the general principle: the amending power was constituent, unlimited by the rights chapter, and within it the social programme named in the Directive Principles enjoyed express priority. The founding amendment's logic had been stated as a system.

## The Ninth Schedule Grows Up

The Ninth Schedule's biography, between its invention in 1951 and the Emergency, is the pattern in its purest form, because the schedule required no fresh argument each time it was used. The original list held thirteen enactments, the zamindari abolition and land reform statutes of Bihar, Bombay, Madras, Uttar Pradesh and the other reforming states, each named, each validated retrospectively against the litigation described above. The Fourth Amendment added nothing to the schedule. The Seventeenth Amendment of 1964 added forty-four statutes at a stroke, extending the shelter to land ceiling and tenancy laws across the country, and in the same statute widened Article 31A's definition of estate to reach ryotwari land and other holdings the courts had read outside the original clause, after rulings in the Kerala agrarian litigation had threatened the state's reform Acts. By the middle of the 1960s the schedule held over a hundred statutes, and the additions had begun to drift from the founding cargo: the list was acquiring insurance, sugar, cement and industrial statutes whose connection to agrarian reform was, at best, genealogical. Nobody designed the drift. The device invited it, because a schedule that immunises by listing asks of a government only one question, whether the statute matters enough to protect, and governments reliably answer that question in the affirmative for their own legislation.

The Twenty-Fifth and later amendments of the 1970s completed the transformation, and the Emergency amendments of 1976 used the schedule as a general warehouse, adding statutes on elections, industries and essential commodities to a list that by the decade's end approached two hundred entries. The Constituent Assembly's text had contained eight schedules, each a piece of constitutional machinery: the forms of oaths, the allocation of seats, the lists of subjects, the tribal areas. The ninth, added in haste for land, was the first schedule whose function was negation, a list of the Constitution's own non-application, and its growth measures the distance the republic travelled from the founding assumption that the rights chapter was the fixed point. When the nine judges in Coelho finally fenced the schedule in 2007, they fenced its post-1973 additions only, and the restraint of that holding is the surest evidence of the device's legitimacy in its original form. Even a bench determined to end the abuse would not disturb the founding list. The First Amendment's shelter for zamindari abolition had become, by universal consent of the organs, part of the settlement itself. What the pattern thesis adds is the observation that a device can be both: legitimate in its founding use, indefensible in its unlimited extension, and dangerous in the interval precisely because the founding use supplies the extension with its alibi. Every addition to the schedule after 1951 was defended, in the chamber, by the precedent of the additions before it, back to the thirteen statutes nobody wished to disturb. That is how precedents compound.

## Culmination: The Forty-Second Amendment and the Judicial Reply

The Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 is where the pattern arrived at its destination, and the brief for any honest account of it must begin by recording the conditions. Proclaimed during the Emergency of June 1975 to December 1976's constitutional working, enacted by a Parliament whose opposition benches had been emptied by detention, and steered by a committee under Swaran Singh, the amendment ran to fifty-nine clauses. It rewrote the Preamble, inserting the words socialist and secular and adding integrity to the nation's unity. It extended Article 31C from two Directive Principles to all of them, subordinating Articles 14 and 19 across the entire field of social legislation. It curtailed the High Courts' writ jurisdiction, restricted judicial review of constitutional amendments by inserting clauses into Article 368 declaring that no amendment could be called in question in any court and that the constituent power had no limits, extended the life of the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies from five years to six, froze the allocation of parliamentary seats, and armed the President with a duty to act on ministerial advice in terms. Each device had an ancestor in the pattern. The unlimited amendment clause was the Twenty-Fourth Amendment's claim stated without embarrassment. The expanded Article 31C was the First Amendment's subordination of rights to directive policy, generalised. The review exclusions were Articles 31A and 31B rewritten at the scale of the whole text. Indira Gandhi's government did not invent a method in 1976. It inherited a method, proved across twenty-five years by the founding generation and by its own earlier amendments, and applied the method at full strength under conditions that removed the usual friction.

The judicial reply, when politics permitted it, came in two instalments and on foundations the pattern itself had laid. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973 by thirteen judges, had already held, by the narrowest of majorities, that the amending power reached every provision of the Constitution but could not damage or destroy its basic structure. The doctrine was the judiciary's considered answer to the question first posed in 1951: if the executive may always amend, what, if anything, may it not do? The answer, that it may amend everything except the identity of the thing being amended, was formulated against the Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Amendments, before the Forty-Second tested it in earnest. After the Emergency, the Janata government's Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978 reversed much of the Forty-Second's machinery, deleting the fundamental right to property from Part Three altogether and converting it into the legal right of Article 300A. Then, in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India, decided on 31 July 1980, the Supreme Court struck down the Forty-Second Amendment's clauses that had declared the amending power unlimited and amendments unreviewable, holding that a limited amending power was itself part of the basic structure, and struck the expanded Article 31C with it. The pattern's first full cycle thus closed in 1980, twenty-nine years after it opened: executive amendment without internal limit, asserted in 1951 as a practical permission, claimed in 1971 as a principle, enacted in 1976 as a system, and refused in 1980 as a doctrine. What survived the refusal is equally part of the record. The calibrated devices of 1951, Articles 15(4), the rewritten 19(2), and a Ninth Schedule still on the books, were untouched by Minerva Mills. The courts had not repudiated the First Amendment. They had repudiated its unlimited descendant, and the distinction is the hinge of the adjudication that follows.

## The Directive-Rights Tension Beneath the Pattern

Beneath the institutional contest ran the ideological one, and the First Amendment is unintelligible without it. The Constitution the Assembly delivered in 1949 contained two programmes in two registers. Part Three guaranteed justiciable rights, equality before the law, freedoms of speech and occupation, property with compensation, protection against discrimination. Part Four stated Directive Principles, unenforceable by design, committing the state to a social order of welfare: equitable distribution of material resources, prevention of concentrated wealth, village panchayats, a uniform civil code in due course, free and compulsory education within a decade. The judges of 1950 and 1951, reading the two parts together, applied the hierarchy the text announced: the principles were fundamental in governance, Article 37 said, but the rights were enforceable, and where a policy collided with a right, the right prevailed, as Champakam held in terms. [The directive-rights tension that the First Amendment first attempted to resolve in the executive's favour](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/) was therefore not a contest between a reforming executive and a reactionary bench, in the simple form government rhetoric suggested. It was the founders' own deferred question, the priority between the Constitution's promises of liberty and its promises of welfare, returning to the political arena through the only door open to it, litigation.

The amendment resolved the tension selectively, and the selectivity maps the governing party's priorities with precision. Where the collision concerned reservation in education, the amendment sided with the directive policy, inserting Article 15(4) as a bridge between Article 46's aspiration and Article 29(2)'s prohibition. Where the collision concerned land, the amendment sided with the directive policy absolutely, walling abolition statutes off from the rights chapter entirely. Where the collision concerned speech, no directive principle was even in play: the government's interest was order, and the amendment widened the state's hand against the citizen for reasons of administration rather than social transformation. The pattern reading draws the conclusion the map invites. The directive-rights language supplied the amendment's public justification, its claim to be completing the founding rather than revising it, and the justification was sincere in the fields of reservation and land. The same statute, however, used the occasion to settle a speech question that the justification did not cover, on grounds the justification could not reach. From its first exercise, the amending power in executive hands behaved as executives behave: it solved the problem it had, using the authority it could claim, and banked the surplus. Later amendments repeated the structure. Article 31C in 1971 clothed the subordination of rights in the language of Articles 39(b) and 39(c); the Forty-Second Amendment extended the clothing to the whole of Part Four. The garment was woven in 1951.

## Austin Against the Pattern Reading: The Adjudication

The scholarly disagreement the evidence must settle can be stated in its strongest competing forms. Granville Austin, whose Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) remains the standard institutional history of the Constitution's middle decades, reads the First Amendment as a necessary procedural correction within a working system: the Assembly's text, applied to the conditions of 1950, generated frictions that obstructed policies, land reform above all, which the text itself endorsed in its Directive Principles, and the amendment removed the frictions so the Constitution could function as its makers intended. On Austin's reading, the amendment is continuous with the founding, an act of the same constituent spirit by substantially the same men, and its proper company is the other early adjustments of a new state learning to operate its machine. The pattern reading, developed in Pratap Bhanu Mehta's essays on constitutional politics from the 2000s onward and given narrative form in accounts of the amendment's stormy passage, reads the same events as the founding of a precedent with a direction: the executive discovered that amendment could reverse judgments, the discovery generalised across fields and decades, and the line from 1951 through 1971 to 1976 is a line of descent, not a series of coincidences. Madhav Khosla's founding-deferral reading in India's Founding Moment (2020) supplies the premise both sides need: the Assembly had left the deepest questions open, so the early amendments were never going to be mere housekeeping; somebody's answer to a deferred question was going to harden into the working constitution, and the only issue was whose answer, given by what method.

The primary evidence adjudicates between Austin and the pattern reading more decisively than either side's partisans usually allow, and the verdict is the synthesis the record supports. On the substance of the individual provisions, Austin is largely right, and the pattern reading is obliged to concede the point in its strongest form. Article 15(4) repaired a genuine defect: an equality code that forbade the state to remedy hereditary educational disadvantage, in a society constituted by that disadvantage, would have discredited the Constitution faster than any amendment could. The land devices answered litigation that had made a promised reform ungovernable, and the alternative reading of Article 31 offered by the zamindars' counsel would have entrenched the largest concentration of landed property in the republic behind the property clause of a constitution whose Directive Principles commanded its dispersal. Even the speech clause, the weakest of the three on the merits, answered a period of insurgency and communal violence with a restriction power that every comparable democracy possessed in some form. Judged provision by provision, as corrections, the First Amendment's contents were defensible, and several were admirable.

On the method, and therefore on the precedent, the pattern reading is right, and Austin's institutional account, precisely because it treats each amendment as a discrete response to friction, cannot see what the timeline in this article makes visible. The method chosen in 1951 had three properties that no account of the provisions' merits can launder. The amendment was moved by the losing litigant, the executive, against judgments in cases to which the executive had been a party. It was carried by an unelected chamber, at legislative speed, within weeks of the last defeat. And its most innovative device, the Ninth Schedule, did not adjust the balance between rights and policy but abolished the forum in which the balance could be argued for the listed statutes. A constitution can survive the correction of its clauses by such a method; what it cannot do is fail to learn the method. The governments of 1955, 1964, 1971 and 1976 learned it, cited its products, and extended its devices in the direction the devices pointed, toward wider exclusions and higher claims, until the judiciary in 1973 and 1980 drew the line that the political process had shown no intention of drawing for itself. The adjudication therefore lands where the brief for this article required it to be tested: the immediate provisions were defensible, and several were necessary; the pattern they established became less defensible with each exercise, because each exercise was freer of the excuses, the insurgency, the litigation emergency, the founding personnel, that had made the first exercise understandable. Nehru amended a seventeen-month-old constitution to save land reform and reservation. His successors amended a mature one to save bank nationalisation, then a ministry, then a regime. The slope was built in 1951, and it was built, as slopes in institutions usually are, by men solving the problem in front of them.

## The Pattern After 1980: Survival, Review and the Post-2014 Cycles

Minerva Mills closed the pattern's first cycle without dismantling its founding instruments, and the decades since 1980 show the instruments at work inside the limits the Court had drawn. The Ninth Schedule, the First Amendment's most radical invention, continued to grow by amendment long after land reform had ceased to be its cargo: statutes on nationalisation, reservation and regulation accumulated in the list, which passed two hundred and eighty entries, each placed there by the same simple device of 1951, a line in an amending act. The judiciary's eventual answer came in I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu, decided by nine judges on 11 January 2007, which held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of Kesavananda Bharati, remained open to challenge where they damaged the basic structure through Articles 14, 19 and 21. Coelho did not disturb the original thirteen statutes or the schedule's founding logic. It fenced the device at the point where the basic structure doctrine began, leaving the First Amendment's own handiwork intact and its later imitations exposed. The judgment is the pattern's mature equilibrium in doctrinal form: the executive keeps the instruments of 1951, and the courts keep a review power over their extension, and each side's title to its position runs back to the founding amendment and to the Emergency amendment that had abused its logic.

The amendment cycles of the years after 2014 operate inside the same equilibrium, and the brief for this article directs that the connection be drawn without flattening the differences. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, which built the Goods and Services Tax, was the pattern's cooperative face: a redesign of the fiscal federation negotiated with the states, ratified by more than half of them as Article 368 requires for federal provisions, and carried with opposition support. The Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, 2019, which reserved ten per cent of seats and posts for economically weaker sections outside the existing caste-based quotas, was the pattern's classic face in one precise respect: it amended Articles 15 and 16 by inserting clauses (6) into each, extending the enabling architecture that Article 15(4) had founded in 1951, and it was upheld, by a three-to-two majority in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India in November 2022, expressly on the ground that economic criteria could supplement the founding amendment's social and educational backwardness. The changes of 2019 touching Article 370, by contrast, were executed not by a freestanding amendment of the article's text but by presidential orders and a parliamentary resolution under the article's own clauses, a route whose constitutional character the Supreme Court examined in its judgment of December 2023. The differences among these episodes are real: consent against imposition, ratification against simple majority, text against interpretation. The continuity is also real, and it is the continuity of 1951: a governing executive, facing a constitutional arrangement that obstructs a settled policy, reaches for the instruments of constitutional change, in the order of convenience, and the argument begins from the premise that the elected government's programme is the fixed point to which the text must be adjusted. That premise was first enacted, respectably and in daylight, by the founders themselves.

### Did the First Amendment make the Indian Constitution unusually easy to change?

It confirmed a design that was already flexible. Article 368 let Parliament amend most provisions by a special majority of both houses, without the state ratification the American text demands. The First Amendment's contribution was cultural: it demonstrated, within seventeen months, that the flexible procedure would be used at full strength, against judgments, without apology.

The comparison with the United States, raised by the opposition in the 1951 debate itself, sharpens the point. The American First Amendment, a restraint on Congress, shares nothing with its Indian namesake beyond the ordinal, and the ordinal misleads generations of readers. India's First Amendment was an assertion of Parliament, closer in spirit to a parliamentary answer to a supreme court than to the American Bill of Rights it is confused with. Syama Prasad Mookerjee made the comparison in the chamber in May 1951, holding up the American settlement, in which the speech guarantee had stood unamended against the government for a century and a half, against a republic amending its speech clause in its second year. The government's answer, that India faced disorders the American founders had not, was a fair answer about 1951. It was not an answer about the precedent, and the precedent is what the decades collected. A constitution amended a hundred times in seventy-five years, against the American twenty-seven in two and a half centuries, is not thereby a worse constitution; the Indian text carries a social programme the American text never attempted, and programmes require maintenance. The pattern argument insists on the narrower claim the evidence carries. Frequency is innocent. Direction is not. The amendments that descend from 1951 in the direct line are the ones that run from the judiciary toward the executive, reversing judgments, excluding review, subordinating rights, and the line's existence, visible in the timeline above, is the First Amendment's true bequest to the republic that its authors were trying, honourably and in part successfully, to reform.

## The Amendment at Work in the States: Madras and Bihar After June 1951

The amendment's first effects were felt where its triggers had arisen, and the state-level sequel tests the competing readings against outcomes. In Madras, the restoration of reservation was immediate and complete. The communal order's policy, voided by the Supreme Court in April 1951, returned under the authority of Article 15(4) in a revised form, and admissions to the state's professional colleges resumed on the principle that the Constitution, as amended, permitted what the Constitution, as adopted, had forbidden. The sequel in Madras politics ran far beyond admissions. The judgment and its reversal together became a founding episode of the Dravidian movement's constitutional memory: proof, in the telling of Periyar E. V. Ramasamy's movement and of the parties that descended from it, that Delhi's text had to be bent before social justice could move, and a standing argument for the state-level vigilance over reservation that has marked Tamil Nadu's politics into the era of the sixty-nine per cent quota and its own Ninth Schedule battles of the 1990s. An admissions clause inserted to answer one judgment in 1951 thus acquired a political afterlife of seventy years, outliving every other element of the amendment except the schedule itself. Austin's reading scores heavily here. A correction that founds a durable and broadly legitimate settlement of a real social question looks, from the vantage of its consequences, like statecraft.

In Bihar, the sequel was slower and uglier, and it scores for the other side. The Bihar Land Reforms Act returned to the Supreme Court on appeal, and in State of Bihar v. Maharajadhiraja Sir Kameshwar Singh, decided in May 1952, the Court upheld the Act in its main structure while striking a compensation provision, a result the amendment had made possible by removing the equality and property objections from the field. Vesting of the great estates proceeded, the Darbhanga Raj and the other historic zamindaris passed into the state, and the legal architecture of abolition was complete within five years of the amendment. The social architecture was another matter. Abolition abolished the intermediaries named in the statutes; it did not redistribute the land to the tenants who worked it, and the decades of Bihar's agrarian conflict, the struggles over bataidari rights, the rise of armed movements in the central districts, unfolded on ground the amendment had cleared of law but not of power. The pattern reading draws its lesson precisely. The amendment had been justified, in the chamber and in the Statement of Objects and Reasons, by the urgency of a reform whose beneficiaries were waiting. What the instrument delivered was the removal of the legal obstacle, and the removal was real; what it could not deliver was the reform itself, which required an administrative and political follow-through that the constitutional victory, once won, made easier to postpone. The habit the amendment taught, of treating the constitutional obstacle as the obstacle, recurred wherever later governments amended first and implemented later, and it is among the subtler counts in the pattern's ledger. A state that can always change the text will always be tempted to count the changing as the achievement.

The two sequels, read together, state the adjudication of this article in miniature, and they explain why neither the textbook story nor the polemic survives contact with the record. In Madras, amendment served a policy that commanded democratic allegiance in the state, delivered it, and founded a settlement that has renewed its mandate at election after election. In Bihar, amendment served a policy that commanded allegiance in the abstract, delivered its legal form, and left its substance to a countryside whose conflicts the republic is still digesting. The instrument was identical. The difference lay in the politics underneath it, in whether a mobilised public stood behind the policy ready to operate the settlement the amendment created. That difference is the strongest available qualification of the pattern thesis, and the thesis is better for absorbing it: the First Amendment's precedent was dangerous not because amendment is always an executive trick, but because it put into every future executive's hands an instrument whose effects depended entirely on the virtue and energy of its user, while removing, device by device, the external checks that might have compensated for a user without either. Nehru had both virtue and energy, by the standards of his office and era, and the amendment in his hands built more than it broke. The instrument did not require a Nehru. By 1976 it had found other hands.

## The Speech Clause After 1951: Reasonableness at Work

The rewritten Article 19(2) had a second life that any balanced verdict on the First Amendment must weigh, because the clause the executive widened contained, in the single word reasonable, the seed of its own discipline. The amendment's drafters had taken the word from the other restriction clauses of Article 19, and its consequence was that every restriction on speech imposed by statute after 1951 had to survive judicial assessment of its reasonableness, its proportionality to a listed ground, in a way the original clause, with its narrower saving for laws relating to specified subjects, had structured differently. The early returns favoured the state: in State of Bihar v. Shaila Bala Devi, decided in 1952, the Supreme Court upheld a press statute against a printer whose material the Bihar government had condemned, reading the new clause with the breadth its movers had intended. The longer record is more mixed, and more interesting. The public order ground the amendment inserted was used for a generation to sustain preventive and penal provisions, yet the same amended clause became the text under which the Court, in case after case from the 1960s onward, demanded a proximate connection between speech and disorder, read down the sedition law in Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar in 1962 to reach only speech inciting violence or public disorder, and built the modern doctrine that restrictions must be narrowly tied to the harms the clause enumerates. The executive of 1951 had bought a wider gate and accepted a gatekeeper as the price. Whether the price was worth paying, for the governments that followed, is a question the pattern answers in a backhanded way: no later amendment attempted to remove the word reasonable from Article 19(2), even in 1976, at the pattern's high-water mark, when so much else was rewritten. The clause stands as the First Amendment's internal counter-example, proof that the founding amendment contained, beside the exclusionary devices of Articles 31A and 31B, a calibrated device whose calibration held.

Set beside the reservation clause, the speech clause completes the amendment's double character. Article 15(4) enabled a policy and left the courts to supervise its exercise, which they did, striking excesses and sustaining the architecture across seventy years of litigation from Champakam's own sequels to Indra Sawhney in 1992 and beyond. Article 19(2) widened a restriction and left the courts to test its applications, which they did, with results that varied by era and bench but never collapsed into abdication. Articles 31A and 31B alone excluded supervision, and it is no accident that the schedule they created is the one product of 1951 that required, in the end, a nine-judge bench and a doctrine of basic structure to bring it back within the Constitution's ordinary disciplines. The lesson generalises beyond India, and the article states it as the founders' inadvertent gift to constitutional theory: an amendment that adjusts a clause and trusts the courts with the adjustment can be absorbed by a constitutional order indefinitely, while an amendment that excludes the courts creates a debt that the order must eventually collect, with interest, in a crisis. The First Amendment wrote both kinds of instrument on a single day in June 1951. The republic has been living off the first kind and paying for the second ever since.

## The Road Not Taken in 1951

A disciplined counterfactual clarifies what the amendment actually decided, because the government of 1951 had alternatives, and the alternatives were neither fanciful nor untried in comparable systems. On the reservation question, the government could have defended the Madras order before a larger bench, arguing that Article 29(2)'s prohibition of denial on grounds only of caste left room for classification by educational backwardness proved on evidence rather than presumed by community; the word only in the clause was a genuine textual handhold, and a Court that had decided Champakam on the order's communal arithmetic might have sustained a redesigned scheme. On land, the government could have accepted the Patna High Court's equality objection and recast the Bihar statute's classification and compensation terms, as legislatures routinely recast statutes after adverse rulings, at the cost of a season's delay. On speech, the government could have framed a narrow incitement statute and tested the boundary of the security of the state ground before a Constitution bench, building doctrine case by case. Each alternative was slower than amendment, each carried litigation risk, and each left the policy exposed to judicial revision. Each also shared one property amendment lacked: it would have developed the Constitution by interpretation, depositing doctrine that bound future governments as well as future courts, and it would have left the text of 1949 standing as the common possession of all parties rather than the instrument of the strongest.

The counterfactual must be run honestly in both directions, and its second direction is the one the pattern's critics are entitled to press. Interpretation is not neutral ground. A judiciary that had voided reservation, land abolition and press regulation within sixteen months of the founding, in the name of clauses the founding generation had written in full knowledge of the policies at stake, was constructing its own kind of precedent, a veto over the social programme of the only elected-in-waiting government the republic possessed, and it is not obvious that a constitution can survive the entrenchment of that precedent either. The zamindars and the communal order's opponents had lost the political argument of the 1940s; litigation offered them a second arena, better furnished for property and for individual complaint than for programme and majority, and the judges of 1951, applying the text conscientiously, were delivering the arena's verdicts. Nehru's answer, for all its consequences, addressed a real pathology: a settlement in which the least democratic organ decides the largest questions by default, because the democratic organ has been denied the means of reply. The amendment supplied the means of reply in its most absolute form. The tragedy of the pattern, if the word is allowed once, is that the reply's absoluteness was the flaw. A narrower instrument, an amendment confined to Articles 15 and 31, leaving speech to doctrine, or a statute of constitutional convention restraining amendment against pending judgments, might have answered the pathology without teaching the lesson. No such narrower instrument was seriously entertained, in a chamber where the government commanded the votes for the wider one and the season rewarded speed. Institutions choose from the menu their incentives print. The incentives of the spring of 1951 printed a short menu, and the longest shadow on it was the one the diners could not see.

## What the First Amendment Teaches About Constitutional Change

The First Amendment rewards study because it refuses the two comfortable stories told about it. The first comfortable story is the textbook's: a young constitution, a few defects, a tidy repair. The record dissolves it. The repair was drafted against named judgments, debated as a contest between Parliament and the courts, and equipped with a device, the Ninth Schedule, whose purpose was to place statutes beyond the reach of the rights the same document guaranteed. The second comfortable story is the polemic's mirror image: a founding betrayal, liberty strangled in its cradle by a domineering Prime Minister. The record dissolves that one too. The provisions answered genuine emergencies of governance, the reservation clause founded an architecture of substantive equality that has outlasted every government since, the land clauses executed a reform any plausible Indian government of 1951 would have attempted, and the whole was debated in public, on reasons stated in a published Statement of Objects and Reasons, by a Prime Minister who believed, and said, that the elected house was entitled to the last word on social policy. Between the textbook and the polemic stands the pattern, and the pattern is the harder and more useful story: institutions learn from their first successes, and the first successful use of the amending power against the courts taught the Indian state a move it has never unlearned.

Three durable lessons follow for any reader of the Constitution as a working document. The first concerns the difference between a power and a practice. Article 368 conferred the power in 1949; the practice, amendment as the executive's answer to adjudication, was created by the use of June 1951, and practices outlive the arguments that justify their first exercise. The second concerns the judiciary's side of the education. The courts conceded in Shankari Prasad, overreached in Golak Nath, and found in Kesavananda Bharati the settlement that has held for half a century, a settlement whose shape, unlimited amendment short of basic structure, is unintelligible except as an answer to the pattern's escalation from 1951 to 1971. The third concerns the citizen's side. Every protection the pattern threatened was restored, where it was restored, by politics as much as by doctrine: the election of 1977, the Forty-Fourth Amendment, the litigation of Minerva Mills and Coelho, each an act of a public that had learned the pattern's cost by watching its culmination. The First Amendment was not the Constitution's correction; it was the precedent for executive-driven constitutional change that culminated in the Emergency. Holding both halves of the episode in view, the defensible provisions and the dangerous precedent, the sincere reformer and the remembered permission, is what it means to read 1951 as history rather than as ammunition.

For the reader who arrived with the examination question, what was the First Amendment and what did it do, the full answer can be carried away in the shape this article has given it. It was an Act of June 1951 that answered three judgments by rewriting three fields of the rights chapter and inventing a fourth device, the schedule of immunised statutes, to place the fourth field beyond argument. It was moved by a Prime Minister of unmatched authority, through a legislature that had never faced the voters, in the last season before those voters spoke, and the voters, when they spoke, sustained the men and the programme. It was defended by its makers as the completion of the founding and attacked by its opponents as the founding's betrayal, and the scholarly argument between Granville Austin's institutional reading and the executive-driven-pattern reading is, at bottom, an argument about which of those two chamber speeches to believe. The evidence assembled here supports the harder verdict: believe both, in sequence. The provisions were the completion. The method was the precedent. The Emergency was the precedent's maturity, and the doctrines of Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills were the republic's reply, delivered by the institution the method had been designed to bypass. Seventeen months was all the time the founding settlement was given to be old. The amendment that ended its youth is where the study of Indian constitutional politics properly begins.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the First Constitutional Amendment of India?

The First Constitutional Amendment was the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951, the first exercise of the amending power under Article 368. Moved in the Provisional Parliament by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru in May 1951 and assented to on 18 June 1951, it answered three courtroom defeats of the government: the freeing of the journals Crossroads and Organiser in 1950, the voiding of the Bihar land reform statute, and the striking down of Madras seat reservations in the Champakam Dorairajan judgment. It added Article 15(4) for backward class provision, rewrote the speech restrictions of Article 19(2), protected state trading monopolies, and created Articles 31A and 31B with the Ninth Schedule to shelter land reform. Its deeper significance is precedential: it established amendment as the executive's answer to adverse judgments.

### Q: When was the First Amendment passed?

The amending bill was introduced in the Provisional Parliament on 10 May 1951, debated through the middle of May, with Nehru's principal defence delivered on 16 May 1951, and passed by the chamber at the start of June 1951. President Rajendra Prasad gave his assent on 18 June 1951, and the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 took effect from that date. The whole sequence, from introduction to assent, occupied under six weeks, and it began barely seventeen months after the Constitution itself had come into force on 26 January 1950. The speed was part of the point disputed in the chamber: supporters treated the bill as urgent governance business, while opponents objected that a constitution was being rewritten against specific judgments, at legislative pace, by a legislature that had never faced a general election.

### Q: What did the First Amendment change in the Constitution?

The Act made five principal changes. It inserted Article 15(4), permitting special provision for socially and educationally backward classes and for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. It substituted a new Article 19(2), making speech restrictions permissible where reasonable and adding the grounds of public order, friendly relations with foreign states and incitement to an offence. It clarified Article 19(6) so that state monopolies in trade could not be challenged under the freedom of occupation. It inserted Articles 31A and 31B, shielding land reform and acquisition laws from the fundamental rights, and it created the Ninth Schedule, listing thirteen statutes placed beyond judicial challenge. Around these, the Act adjusted provisions on sessions of Parliament and state legislatures, the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes lists in Articles 341 and 342, and transitional arrangements in Articles 372 and 376.

### Q: What is the Ninth Schedule?

The Ninth Schedule is a list of statutes appended to the Constitution by the First Amendment in 1951 and protected by Article 31B, which declares that none of the listed enactments shall be deemed void on the ground of inconsistency with any fundamental right, notwithstanding any judgment to the contrary. The original list contained thirteen enactments, the zamindari abolition and land reform laws of the reforming states, and its purpose was to end the litigation that had stalled abolition. Later amendments kept adding statutes, on land ceilings, nationalisation, reservation and regulation, until the list passed two hundred and eighty entries. In I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu in 2007, a nine-judge bench held that laws added after 24 April 1973 could still be struck down if they damaged the basic structure of the Constitution, fencing the device while leaving the founding list intact.

### Q: Why did Nehru push the First Amendment?

Nehru pushed the amendment because his government's central policies had been stopped in court within the Constitution's first seventeen months, and the stoppages rested on the constitutional text itself. The Supreme Court had freed two publications his provincial governments had banned, the Patna High Court had voided Bihar's zamindari abolition statute, and the Supreme Court had struck down the Madras reservation order his party's government in the state regarded as settled policy. Because the obstacles were textual, no ordinary statute could remove them. Nehru also held a theory of the matter, stated to the Provisional Parliament on 16 May 1951: the elected legislature, not the judiciary, was entitled to settle the direction of social policy, and the courts could not be permitted to function as a third chamber. Land reform, reservation and public order were, in his presentation, one question of governmental capacity, and amendment was its answer.

### Q: What is the Romesh Thapar judgment?

Romesh Thapar v. State of Madras (1950) SCR 594 is the Supreme Court's judgment of 26 May 1950 freeing the journal Crossroads. The Madras government had banned the journal's circulation under a public order statute after it reported a firing in Salem Central Jail. Justice Patanjali Sastri, for the majority, held that freedom of political discussion was the foundation of democratic government, and that Article 19(2) as adopted permitted restrictions only on grounds tied to the security of the state and a short list of subjects such as defamation and contempt; public order was not among the permitted grounds, so the ban could not stand. Decided the same day, Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi struck down pre-censorship of the Organiser on the same reasoning. The First Amendment answered both judgments by inserting public order into Article 19(2).

### Q: How does the First Amendment relate to the Forty-Second Amendment?

The Forty-Second Amendment of 1976, the Emergency-era restructuring of the Constitution, is the First Amendment's pattern carried to its destination. The founding amendment had shown that an executive facing judicial obstruction could rewrite the text, subordinate rights to directive policy in selected clauses, and place statutes beyond review in a schedule. The amendments of 1971 generalised the claims, declaring the amending power constituent and giving two Directive Principles primacy over equality and freedom. The Forty-Second then applied the method at full scale: Article 31C extended to all Directive Principles, judicial review curtailed, the Preamble rewritten, and clauses inserted into Article 368 declaring the amending power unlimited and amendments unreviewable. Indira Gandhi's government was extending a precedent, not inventing one. The Supreme Court's reply in Minerva Mills in 1980 struck the unlimited clauses while leaving the calibrated devices of 1951 untouched.

### Q: What new clause did the First Amendment add to Article 15, and what did it permit?

The amendment inserted clause (4) into Article 15, providing that nothing in Article 15, and nothing in Article 29(2), the clause forbidding denial of admission to state-aided institutions on grounds of religion, race, caste or language, prevented the state from making special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. The clause was drafted to reverse the effect of the Champakam Dorairajan judgment, which had voided the Madras communal order allocating college seats by community. Its reach proved much wider than the judgment. Article 15(4) became the constitutional foundation of reservation in education for backward classes, the text on which the implementation of the Mandal Commission's recommendations in the 1990s rested and which the Supreme Court construed in Indra Sawhney and its successors.

### Q: How did the First Amendment change the restrictions on free speech in Article 19(2)?

The amendment substituted the whole of Article 19(2). As adopted in 1949, the clause saved laws relating to libel, slander, defamation, contempt of court, decency or morality, and matters undermining the security of or tending to overthrow the state; it contained no public order ground, which is why the bans in the Romesh Thapar and Brij Bhushan cases fell. The substituted clause permitted laws imposing reasonable restrictions on speech in the interests of the security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence. Two changes mattered most: the addition of public order, which handed back the power the Court had denied, and the addition of the word reasonable, which subjected every restriction to judicial review and became the hook for later doctrine.

### Q: What were Articles 31A and 31B, and how did they protect land reform laws?

Article 31A provided that laws for the acquisition of estates, the taking over of property management, the amalgamation of corporations and allied purposes could not be deemed void for inconsistency with Articles 14, 19 or 31, removing the three rights under which zamindari abolition had been attacked. Article 31B went further and operated retrospectively: it declared that the enactments listed in the Ninth Schedule were not, and had never been, void for inconsistency with any fundamental right, notwithstanding any judgment, decree or order to the contrary. The distinction is between a shield and an erasure. Article 31A protected a class of future laws against specified rights; Article 31B rewrote the legal past for named statutes, reversing the effect of judgments already delivered, including the Patna High Court's voiding of the Bihar Act. Together they made the land reform settlement litigation-proof.

### Q: What was the Brij Bhushan case, and why is it read alongside Romesh Thapar?

Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi (1950) SCR 605 concerned the Organiser, the weekly associated with the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh. On 2 March 1950 the Chief Commissioner of Delhi, acting under the East Punjab Public Safety Act, ordered the printer and publisher, Brij Bhushan, to submit all communal matter and all matter concerning Pakistan for official scrutiny before publication. The Supreme Court struck the pre-censorship order down on 26 May 1950, the same day as Romesh Thapar, holding that pre-censorship of the kind imposed fell outside the restrictions Article 19(2) then permitted. The two cases are read together because together they fixed the constitutional position the First Amendment set out to change: between them they protected a communist journal and a Sangh weekly, on the same principle, against governments of the same party, and the amendment's new public order ground answered both at once.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in Shankari Prasad in 1951?

In Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India, decided on 5 October 1951, a five-judge bench unanimously upheld the First Amendment against the challenge of zamindars whose estates the protected statutes abolished. The challengers argued that a constitutional amendment was law within Article 13, which voids laws abridging fundamental rights, and that an amendment abridging those rights was therefore itself void. The Court, in a judgment delivered by Justice Patanjali Sastri, held that Article 13 governed ordinary legislation, while the power under Article 368 was a constituent power of a different order, so Parliament could amend the fundamental rights themselves. The decision ratified the amendment pattern at its birth: the executive's method received the judiciary's certificate, and the settlement stood until Golak Nath questioned it in 1967 and Kesavananda Bharati refounded it, with the basic structure limit, in 1973.

### Q: How many laws did the original Ninth Schedule protect, and what kind of laws were they?

The original Ninth Schedule, as inserted in 1951, listed thirteen enactments. They were the zamindari abolition and land reform statutes of the states that had legislated first and been sued hardest, including the Bihar Land Reforms Act of 1950, the Uttar Pradesh Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of 1950 and the corresponding Madras, Madhya Pradesh, Bombay and Assam measures, together with connected acquisition provisions. Every entry concerned the abolition of intermediaries between the state and the cultivator or the reform of land tenure; none concerned industry, trade or elections. The narrowness of the founding list matters to the schedule's later history. Additions by the Seventeenth Amendment in 1964 and the amendments of the 1970s carried the list into nationalisation, reservation and general regulation, and past two hundred and eighty entries, a transformation the founding list's discipline throws into relief.

### Q: Did the First Amendment need ratification by the state legislatures?

No. Article 368 required ratification by the legislatures of not less than half the states only for amendments touching the provisions listed in its proviso, covering matters such as the election of the President, the extent of executive and judicial power of the Union and the states, the representation of states in Parliament and Article 368 itself. The First Amendment's subjects, the fundamental rights, the Ninth Schedule and the machinery provisions it adjusted, lay outside the proviso, so a special majority of the Provisional Parliament sufficed, and no state ratifications were sought. The point shaped the pattern's speed: the fastest amendments in Indian practice have generally been those the centre could carry alone, while the One Hundred and First Amendment of 2016, the Goods and Services Tax amendment, which touched the federal provisions, had to travel the slower road of state ratification and did so with consensus.

### Q: Who gave assent to the First Amendment, and when did it come into force?

President Rajendra Prasad gave his assent to the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 on 18 June 1951, and the amendment came into force on the same day. Prasad's role carries an irony the record preserves. As President of the Constituent Assembly he had presided over the adoption of the text the amendment was revising, and in the same season he was engaged in a celebrated constitutional disagreement with Nehru over the Hindu Code Bill, in which he questioned whether a President was bound to assent to measures he believed exceeded the government's mandate. On the amendment he assented, and the revision of the founding text became law seventeen months after its commencement, signed by the man who had signed the original. The bill had been moved by Nehru on 10 May 1951 and passed by the Provisional Parliament at the start of June.

### Q: Why does it matter that an unelected Provisional Parliament passed the First Amendment?

The Provisional Parliament was the Constituent Assembly sitting as the Dominion's legislature; its members had been indirectly elected in 1946 by provincial assemblies chosen on the restricted franchise of the Government of India Act, 1935, and it had never faced the electorate of the republic it governed. Its competence to amend under Article 368 was not in legal doubt, and it legislated for India across the whole interim period, so the amendment's validity was never the issue. The significance is one of constitutional morality and precedent. The most consequential early revision of the rights chapter was carried by a body whose democratic mandate predated universal suffrage, months before the first general election of 1951 to 1952, against opposition warnings recorded in its own debates. Later amendments were carried by elected houses, but the founding demonstration, that amendment was ordinary legislative business to be done at need and at speed, belonged to the unelected house.

### Q: What did Nehru say on 16 May 1951 in defence of the amendment?

Nehru's speech of 16 May 1951 in the Provisional Parliament is the fullest statement of the amendment's theory. He argued that the high courts, by their construction of the fundamental rights, had obstructed the government's programme on land reform and public order; that the judiciary could not be allowed to act as a third house of the legislature, reviewing policy under the guise of interpretation; and that where the courts' reading of the Constitution frustrated the social purposes the Constitution itself proclaimed in its Directive Principles, Parliament was entitled, and obliged, to bring the text into line. He treated zamindari abolition as a commitment on which the government's authority rested, and the speech restrictions as a necessity of a state facing communal propaganda and insurgency. The speech is rarely quoted in popular accounts, yet it is the founding document of the amendment pattern, stating in principle what later governments practised.

### Q: Apart from the rights provisions, which other parts of the Constitution did the First Amendment alter?

The Act carried a tail of machinery changes that are usually omitted from summaries. It amended the provisions governing the convening and prorogation of sessions, in Article 85 and Article 87 for Parliament and in Article 174 and Article 176 for the state legislatures, adjusting how often the houses had to meet and the addresses that opened their sessions. It amended Articles 341 and 342, which empower the President to specify the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in relation to each state, in ways connected with the lists' operation. It adjusted transitional arrangements in Articles 372 and 376, concerning the continuance of existing laws and the tenure of judges who had served in the predecessor courts. The tail matters to the pattern argument: from its first exercise, the amending power was used in one statute for high constitutional conflict and for housekeeping alike, treating the text as the ministry's working document.

### Q: How did the First Amendment affect zamindari abolition under Article 31?

Before the amendment, zamindari abolition statutes were exposed under Article 31, the property clause, and Article 14, the equality clause, and the exposure had already produced a voiding in Bihar and conflicting rulings elsewhere. The amendment answered at three levels. Article 31A declared that laws acquiring estates and allied rights could not be deemed void for inconsistency with Articles 14, 19 or 31. Article 31B validated the named statutes in the Ninth Schedule retrospectively, whatever any court had held. And although the amendment left Article 31's compensation language in place for ordinary acquisitions, setting up the next round of conflict that the Fourth Amendment addressed in 1955, the immediate effect was to clear the field for abolition: the Supreme Court upheld the Bihar Act in its main structure in May 1952, and the vesting of the great estates proceeded under the amendment's shelter.

### Q: Can a law placed in the Ninth Schedule still be struck down by the courts?

A law placed in the Ninth Schedule can be struck down, within limits settled by a nine-judge bench in I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu on 11 January 2007. The Court held that statutes inserted into the schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, are open to challenge where they destroy or damage the basic structure of the Constitution, tested through the fundamental rights in Articles 14, 19 and 21 read together. Insertions made before that date, including the thirteen original land reform statutes of 1951, were left undisturbed. The holding completed the long judicial negotiation with the First Amendment's boldest device: the schedule survives, the founding list is secure, but the device can no longer be used, as it was used in the 1970s, as a general warehouse for statutes a government wishes to place beyond the Constitution's discipline.
