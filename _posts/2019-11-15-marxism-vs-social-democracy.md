Marxism and social democracy are usually introduced as neighbours, two shades of the left placed side by side on a spectrum that runs from mild reform to total revolution. That picture is the first thing this article asks the reader to set aside. The two traditions are not separated mainly by temperament, by patience, or by the speed at which they hope to travel. They are separated by a disagreement about a fact, or rather about a prediction that a century of government has now tested from both sides. The question dividing them is whether the outcomes of capitalism can be substantially and durably improved while the ownership of productive assets remains where it is. Social democracy is the tradition that answered yes and organised a politics around redistribution, regulation, and universal provision inside capitalist property relations. Marxism is the tradition that answered no, holding that gains which leave ownership untouched remain concessions held on sufferance, revocable when the balance of forces shifts. Everything else in the comparison, the parties, the programmes, the settlements and the reversals, is evidence bearing on that single proposition.

Both sides of the argument possess real evidence, and an honest comparison has to hold the two bodies of evidence together without letting either dissolve the other. The social democratic evidence is a record: in the decades after the Second World War, governments of that tradition in Western Europe delivered sustained reductions in inequality, employment levels that approached full employment for an extended period, universal health and education provision, rising real wages, and a large expansion of social insurance, all without expropriating the owners of industry. No other tradition on the left can show anything comparable, and this article states that record plainly and early, because accounts written from within the Marxist tradition habitually understate it. The Marxist evidence is a reversal: from the nineteen seventies onward, much of that settlement was unwound, its taxes lowered, its industries sold, its employment commitment abandoned, its insurance trimmed, and the unwinding was possible precisely because the gains had never been secured in the ownership of productive assets. Accounts written from within the social democratic tradition habitually understate that. The reader who holds both facts at once has the whole comparison in miniature.

The test this article applies to both traditions is named here so that it can be used throughout: the reversibility test. A gain counts as secured, on this test, only if it survives the departure of the government that granted it and a sustained turning of the political balance against it. A gain that depends for its existence on the continued goodwill of electorates, markets, and successor governments is delivered but not secured, rented rather than owned. The distinction sounds like a debating point until it is applied row by row to the record, at which point it becomes the sharpest instrument the comparison possesses. It is also, unusually, a test that each tradition's own serious writers have at some point applied to themselves, which is why this comparison ends in a rarer verdict than most in the series: each side's central claim has received partial confirmation, and the partiality is itself the finding.

## The question the comparison actually asks

Begin with the divergence stated as a proposition rather than as a mood. Both traditions emerged from the same nineteenth-century labour movement, read many of the same texts, and organised many of the same workers. Both wanted security, dignity, rising living standards, and the civilising of industrial work. They parted over the means in a specific and testable way. One held that a determined government, armed with taxation, regulation, and public provision, could extract those goods from a capitalist economy indefinitely, and that ownership was a secondary matter because control had in any case drifted away from owners toward managers and the state. The other held that ownership was the load-bearing wall of the whole structure, that every concession extracted without touching it rested on the tolerance of those who retained the power to withdraw it, and that history would eventually run the experiment of withdrawal.

### What exactly did the two traditions disagree about?

They disagreed about one testable proposition: whether a capitalist economy's outcomes can be greatly and lastingly improved while its ownership structure stays as it is. Social democracy answered yes and built a politics on redistribution and provision. Marxism answered no, predicting that improvements resting on untouched ownership would remain revocable.

Notice what the disagreement is not. It is not a disagreement about whether working people deserved better lives, nor about whether capitalism in its unregulated form produced misery, nor even, in the early decades, about the ultimate destination named socialism, a word both traditions used on their banners. Nor is it adequately described as a disagreement about pace, the picture of two travellers on one road moving at different speeds. Pace implies a shared destination and a shared map. The actual dispute concerned the road itself: whether the parliamentary road, travelled patiently and in good faith, led through the territory of durable transformation or merely circled within the territory of concession. That is an empirical question, and the twentieth century answered it in a complicated way that neither tradition's propagandists find comfortable.

### Why is the spectrum picture of the two traditions misleading?

A spectrum arranges positions by intensity, as though social democracy were Marxism diluted and Marxism were social democracy concentrated. The picture fails because the two traditions made opposite claims about the same mechanism, whether gains could endure while ownership stood unchanged, and opposite claims cannot be arranged as stronger and weaker versions of one another.

The spectrum picture also smuggles in a judgement while pretending to be a description. To place the two traditions on a single line is to assume that the difference between them is one of degree, which is precisely the social democratic contention, that transformation differs from reform only in amount. The Marxist contention is that the difference is one of kind, that a reform which leaves the ownership of the means of production intact belongs to a different category of event from a transformation of that ownership, however large the reform and however small the transformation. A reader does not have to accept the Marxist contention to see that building it out of the picture at the start begs the question the comparison exists to examine. The honest starting point is two traditions, one origin, and a fork defined by a proposition about durability.

## One movement, two heirs: the shared origin

The fork came late, and it came to a movement that had been united for decades. The great socialist parties of the late nineteenth century, the German Social Democratic Party above all, were Marxist parties in their official doctrine. Their programmes quoted the analysis of capitalism as a system moving through its contradictions toward its supersession. Their newspapers taught historical materialism. Their theoreticians, Karl Kautsky pre-eminent among them, understood themselves as custodians of an orthodoxy that bore Marx's name. The trade unions that marched with these parties organised the industrial working class on a scale no previous movement had approached, and the parties' electoral machines grew with every extension of the franchise. Whatever social democracy later became, it began as the political form of Marxism itself, and the revision that eventually separated the two was a revision conducted from inside, by the movement's own intellectuals, against the movement's own inherited doctrine.

### Why did the parties begin as Marxist parties?

The parties began as Marxist parties because Marxism supplied the only systematic account available of why capitalism produced the misery their members lived, and of why organisation could end it. The German party's programme of the eighteen nineties stated the doctrine officially, and its theorists defended that doctrine as the movement's defining inheritance.

The Erfurt Programme, adopted by the German party in 1891 and drafted with Kautsky's hand on its theoretical section, declared the concentration of capital, the growth of the proletariat, and the necessity of social ownership in language drawn directly from the Marxist analysis. For the generation that built the mass parties, there was no rival framework of comparable scope. Liberalism explained the market but not the slum. Christian social teaching deplored the slum but defended the order that produced it. Marxism alone offered a theory in which the workers' suffering had causes, the causes had a history, and the history had a direction that organisation could accelerate. When later generations of social democrats abandoned the doctrine, they were abandoning the intellectual foundation on which their own institutions had been raised, which is one reason the abandonment took more than half a century to state openly.

The daily practice of those parties, however, already contained the seed of the fork. A party that contests elections, builds unions, negotiates wages, and administers municipalities acquires interests in the stability of the order it exists to overthrow. Its officials draw salaries from dues and, eventually, from the state. Its voters include pensioners, tenants, and skilled workers with something to lose. Long before any theorist announced a revision, the movement's practice was reformist in the plain sense that it pursued improvements within the existing order and measured its success by them. The theorist who finally said so, and built a doctrine on the saying, was Eduard Bernstein, a man formed inside the orthodoxy he revised, an exile who had edited the party's newspaper under the anti-socialist laws and who carried unimpeachable credentials as a Marxist. The revision could not be dismissed as an outsider's misunderstanding, because no outsider could have written it.

## Bernstein and the revisionist argument

Bernstein's statement of the revision, published at the end of the eighteen nineties, remains the founding document of the social democratic tradition considered as a doctrine rather than as a habit. His claims were empirical before they were strategic. Capitalism, he argued, was not collapsing under its contradictions as the inherited doctrine expected. Credit, cartels, and improved communications gave the system adaptive capacities the older analysis had not allowed for. Crises were becoming less catastrophic rather than more. The middle strata were not vanishing into the proletariat but persisting and mutating. Living standards for organised workers were rising in ways that could be measured in diet, housing, and hours. From these observations he drew the strategic conclusion that the movement should recognise what it already was: a party of democratic reform, pursuing socialism as the cumulative outcome of countless partial advances rather than as a single revolutionary rupture. His famous insistence that the movement mattered more than any final goal, as the phrase is usually rendered, was the doctrine of the fork stated in one line.

### How did Bernstein state the revisionist case?

Bernstein argued from evidence against expectation: capitalism was adapting rather than collapsing, the middle strata were persisting rather than disappearing, and organised workers were winning measurable improvements. Socialism should therefore be pursued as the sum of democratic reforms, each valuable in itself, rather than awaited as a single transforming event.

The reply from within the movement, Rosa Luxemburg's pamphlet on reform and revolution, stated the counter-position with a clarity the classical debate never surpassed, and this article leaves that exchange to the treatment it receives elsewhere in the series, because that argument has its own place in the article on the [reform or revolution debate](/2013/02/01/reform-or-revolution-debate/). What matters here is the shape of the fork the exchange revealed. Luxemburg held that reforms were valuable precisely as by-products of the struggle for power, and worthless as a substitute for it, because a movement that renounced the conquest of power would find its reforms administered back to it by the order it had declined to challenge. Bernstein held that the conquest of power, in the revolutionary sense, was a phrase surviving from an earlier epoch of politics, and that democratic institutions had changed what power meant. Both could point to evidence. Neither persuaded the other's tradition, and the argument they formalised is still, underneath every later restatement, the argument this article is grading.

It is worth pausing on the biographical irony, because it disciplines any temptation to treat the fork as a fall from purity. Bernstein was no renegade smuggled into the movement. He had been Marx and Engels's younger associate, Engels's literary executor in effect, a man whose Marxist formation was as deep as any in Europe. Kautsky, who answered him, had been his close collaborator. The revisionist controversy was a family quarrel among people who had built the same party, read the same proofs, and buried the same comrades. When the movement finally split, it split along a line that ran through friendships, editorial offices, and a shared past, which is why the bitterness of the eventual separation exceeded anything either side directed at the bourgeois parties. Heresy is always punished more savagely than opposition, because the heretic proves the doctrine could be read otherwise by someone who knew it from inside.

## The separation: war, revolution, and two internationals

Doctrine divided the movement on paper in the eighteen nineties. Events divided it in fact a generation later. In August 1914 the parliamentary parties of the Second International, pledged for decades to refuse support to a war between the great powers, voted war credits to their respective governments within days of one another. The German party's vote, taken under the discipline its deputies had sworn to uphold, announced that national loyalty outranked international solidarity at the moment of testing. Three years later the Russian Revolution posed the question of power in its sharpest form, and the movement's responses to that question completed the separation. Those who held that the Russian example showed the road, organisation through disciplined parties aiming at the conquest of state power and the expropriation of capital, gathered into the Communist International founded in 1919. Those who read the Russian example as a warning, that power seized without democratic consent would reproduce domination in a new form, rebuilt a separate international of democratic socialist parties. From that point the two traditions had separate organisations, separate martyrs, and increasingly separate vocabularies for describing the same events.

### Which event made the separation irreversible?

The war credits votes of August 1914 broke the International in practice, but the Russian Revolution made the break irreversible, because it forced every party to declare whether state power should be taken and held by revolutionary means. After 1919 the two traditions maintained rival internationals, rival parties, and rival accounts of the same upheavals.

The separation was sealed in blood in Germany, where a social democratic government in the winter after the armistice used irregular military formations against a revolutionary rising led by former comrades, and Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were murdered in the suppression. Whatever the provocations and whatever the alternatives available to that government, an event of that kind cannot be argued away by later programmes. The communist tradition stored it as proof that social democracy would defend the old order against the working class itself when the choice was forced. The social democratic tradition stored the same years as proof that revolutionary adventurism delivered movements to reaction and that democratic legitimacy was the only foundation on which gains could rest. Each tradition's founding memory of the other was a memory of betrayal, and founding memories of that kind do not fade with the generations that hold them. They are retold at congresses, printed in anniversary editions, and absorbed by recruits who never met anyone involved.

The doctrinal formalisation lagged far behind the organisational fact, and the lag is itself instructive. Communist parties codified their separation immediately, in conditions of membership that demanded acceptance of the revolutionary road. Social democratic parties, by contrast, continued for decades to carry Marxist language in their programmes while governing, when they governed, in a manner Bernstein would have recognised. The German party's programmes of the interwar years still spoke the vocabulary of class struggle and socialisation. The Swedish party that built the most durable social democratic order in Europe retained a programme with Marxist ancestry well into the post-war period. Doctrine, in the social democratic case, trailed practice by a generation, and the formal abandonment, when it came in 1959, ratified a separation that events had accomplished forty years earlier. The reader should keep that sequence in mind when the seeded question of when social democracy abandoned Marxism arises: the honest answer distinguishes the organisational separation of 1919, the practical separation visible in every interwar government, and the programmatic separation confessed at mid-century.

## The interwar test: what each tradition did with power

Between the wars both traditions held power somewhere, and the interwar record is the necessary preface to the post-war settlement because it shows what each did before the exceptional conditions of the post-war decades arrived. Social democratic governments in Weimar Germany administered a republic they had not designed, under reparations, inflation, and then mass unemployment, and their tenure is remembered less for transformation than for the defence of democratic institutions against enemies on both flanks. In Austria, the municipal socialism of Vienna built housing, clinics, and schools of a quality that visitors crossed Europe to study, inside a state whose national politics the party could not control, a demonstration in miniature of both the tradition's gift for provision and its vulnerability when the surrounding order turned hostile. In Sweden, the party that took office in the early nineteen thirties began the long construction that would make its country the settlement's fullest expression, learning in the process the arts of coalition, compromise with agrarian interests, and incremental institutional building that its post-war dominance would perfect.

The communist tradition's interwar tenure was of a different kind: one state, held without interruption, transformed by expropriation and planning at a pace and with a coercion that the social democratic record nowhere resembles. Industrialisation proceeded, literacy spread, and basic provision expanded from a desperate baseline, achievements the series records in the articles on the Soviet experience; and the same decades carried famine, terror, and the extinction of independent working-class organisation, which the social democratic tradition cited, not unreasonably, as the practical refutation of the revolutionary road. By 1939 each tradition could point to the other's record and find its worst expectations confirmed. Social democrats pointed east and saw where the conquest of power led. Communists pointed west and saw republics defended into collapse and movements that administered capitalism's crises on capitalism's behalf. The post-war settlement was built by men and women formed in that mutual education, and its architects understood themselves to be steering between two demonstrated failures: the failure of revolution to deliver freedom, and the failure of pre-war reformism to deliver security.

## The settlement defined: a period, a place, a set of institutions

The post-war settlement is the name this article gives to the configuration that governed much of Western Europe from the later nineteen forties into the nineteen seventies. It was not a single treaty or a single government's programme but a family resemblance among national arrangements, and precision about its boundaries matters, because one of the misreadings corrected later in this article is the habit of treating the settlement as universal when it was specific to a group of countries and a period. Its institutional core can be stated plainly. Governments committed themselves to maintaining high and stable employment as a goal of policy rather than a residue of the market. Welfare provision was organised on universal principles, as a right of citizenship rather than a relief of destitution. Collective bargaining between organised labour and organised capital was recognised, protected, and in several countries woven into the machinery of economic management. Public ownership held a significant but bounded sector, concentrated in utilities, transport, and in some countries banking and heavy industry. Taxation at levels that would have been unthinkable before the war funded the whole, and capital movements across borders were restricted by the monetary arrangements agreed among the allied governments, which gave national policy a sheltered space in which to operate.

### Which countries define the settlement's geography?

The settlement's heartland was north-western Europe: Britain, the Scandinavian countries, the Low Countries, Austria, and the western German state, with France a partial and distinctive case. Southern Europe joined late or not at all, North America built a weaker variant, and the arrangement everywhere depended on conditions specific to its decades.

Sweden represents the settlement in its most complete and durable form, governed by one party for most of the period and marked by an unusually encompassing union movement, centralised bargaining, and an active labour market policy that treated displaced workers as citizens to be retrained rather than casualties to be compensated. Britain shows the settlement in its most institutionally innovative shape, with a health service founded on universal and free provision that became the arrangement's most beloved single institution. The western German state displays the settlement in its most self-consciously anti-Marxist guise, a social market economy whose architects accepted welfare provision and codetermination while rejecting the tradition that had first demanded them. Austria practised a consociational variant, dividing influence between the great camps by agreement. The variation matters for the comparison, because it shows the settlement was not one party's property. It was an equilibrium that conservative and Christian democratic governments also administered, which is precisely what made its later unravelling so instructive: an arrangement accepted by all sides proved reversible anyway, once the conditions sustaining the acceptance decayed.

The settlement should also be distinguished from the mere existence of welfare states, which predated it. Bismarck's Germany had social insurance in the eighteen eighties, built expressly to undercut the socialist movement, and several countries had pension and accident schemes before the First World War. What changed after 1945 was the combination: universality as a principle, full employment as a commitment, organised labour as a recognised partner, and a fiscal scale that made the state a permanent redistributive presence in every household's life. A worker in the settlement's heartland encountered the arrangement not as an occasional benefit but as the frame of existence: insured against sickness and unemployment, housed increasingly by public or regulated provision, educated at public expense to levels previous generations had not reached, and pensioned at the end of a working life whose length and conditions were set by collective agreement. The comparison with Marxism turns on what that frame delivered and on what happened to it, and to both questions the article now turns.

## What social democracy delivered: the record stated plainly

The record comes first in this article's order of business, before the reversibility argument is allowed to qualify it, because the qualification has too often been used in Marxist writing as a solvent that dissolves the record before the reader has seen it. So let the record stand on its own for several pages. In the settlement's heartland and decades, the distribution of income and wealth narrowed substantially and sustained the narrowing, a compression visible in every reconstruction the economic historians have produced and described in the literature on the period as the great levelling of the middle twentieth century. Employment approached the full employment that policy had promised, and stayed near it for an extended period measured in decades rather than electoral cycles, a condition so unfamiliar to later generations that it requires an effort of imagination to recover: employers competing for workers, school-leavers choosing among offers, whole regions where the fear of the sack had receded from a daily terror to a remote contingency. Real wages rose across the period with a steadiness that transformed working-class life in its material texture: diet, heating, housing space, clothing, holidays, the furnishing of rooms, the ownership first of radios and then of televisions and washing machines and cars. Life expectancy climbed, infant mortality fell, and the diseases of poverty retreated before sanitation, nutrition, and medicine organised as public services.

### Did social democracy deliver more than Marxism?

on improvements in living conditions, yes, substantially. Post-war Western European settlements achieved sustained reductions in inequality, near-full employment, and universal provision without expropriation. The Marxist reply is that gains not secured in ownership proved reversible, and much of the settlement was subsequently unwound.

That short answer carries the article's whole architecture, and the sections around it exist to earn it. The affirmative clause is a statement about delivery: measured by what happened to the living conditions of working people, the social democratic record of the settlement decades surpasses anything the rival tradition can present from its own governments, and the margin is not narrow. The qualifying clause is a statement about durability: the Marxist tradition predicted, in advance and in writing, that gains of exactly this kind, resting on unchanged ownership, would be withdrawn when the conditions that made them prudent disappeared, and from the nineteen seventies a substantial part of the settlement was in fact withdrawn. Retain only the first clause and the result is social democratic advocacy; retain only the second and the result is its Marxist mirror. The discipline of this article is to hold both clauses in view at all times and to grade each tradition on the clause it emphasised and on the one it preferred to pass over.

### What did near-full employment rest on?

Near-full employment rested on a policy commitment backed by instruments: demand management that treated joblessness as a failure of government, capital controls that sheltered national policy, expanding public employment, and bargaining systems that traded wage restraint for security. It was an achievement of institutions, not a natural condition of the market.

Recover the strangeness of that achievement by remembering what preceded it. The generation that built the settlement had lived through the mass unemployment of the nineteen thirties, when in the industrial countries joblessness stood for years at levels that broke families, parties, and in several countries democracy itself. The men and women of 1945 regarded the return of that condition as the one outcome their politics existed to prevent, and they wrote the prevention into the operating instructions of the state. Governments accepted responsibility for the level of employment as they accepted responsibility for defence. Central banks, finance ministries, and planning offices were organised around the commitment. Employers, whatever their private preferences, operated inside a settlement whose legitimacy rested on delivering work, and unions traded moderation in wage claims for the guarantee that the work would be there. The result, sustained across a generation, was the closest approach to the abolition of unemployment that industrial societies have recorded, achieved without the direction of labour, without the abolition of markets, and without the expropriation of a single major industry beyond the bounded public sector the settlement itself defined.

The human meaning of the record deserves a paragraph that resists the language of indicators, because the traditions are ultimately arguing about lives. A skilled worker in the settlement's heartland, born around the end of the war, could expect to leave school for a job that was waiting, to be trained at an employer's or the state's expense, to rent and later perhaps to buy a dwelling built or financed under public programmes, to marry and raise children whose health care cost nothing at the point of need and whose schooling extended years beyond his own, to survive illnesses that had killed his grandparents, to retire on a pension that bore a planned relation to his earnings, and to die in a hospital whose bill no one presented to his widow. None of this required him to be a hero, a cadre, or a believer. It required him to be a citizen of a settlement country during the settlement decades. Set beside that biography the biography of his contemporary in any state governed in Marxism's name, and the social democratic objection to the whole Marxist project, stated later in this article at full strength, acquires its force. The objection is not theoretical. It is biographical.

## Universal provision: health and education

The settlement's most consequential design decision was to organise its central services universally, as entitlements of citizenship, rather than as reliefs targeted at the poor. The decision had a technical rationale and a political one, and the political rationale turned out to matter for durability, a point the reversibility sections will revisit. Services for the poor, the architects reasoned, become poor services: stigmatised in use, friendless in budget negotiations, and first in line when retrenchment comes. Services used by everyone, the doctor attended by the manager's family as well as the cleaner's, the school attended across the class structure, acquire a defending constituency that no finance ministry can easily override. Universalism was therefore not merely generosity. It was a fortification strategy, an attempt to build the settlement's gains into institutions that the whole society would defend because the whole society used them. That the fortification held in some sectors and failed in others is one of the most instructive patterns in the reversal, and the record and the reversal table later in this article is organised to display it.

### How was universal provision different from relief?

Relief is given to the certified poor, on proof of need, and carries the stigma of dependence; it can be tightened by adjusting eligibility whenever budgets tighten. Universal provision is held by citizens as a right, used by all classes, and defended by all classes, which changes both the experience of receiving it and the politics of cutting it.

The British health service became the type case of the universal principle and remains the settlement's most studied institution. Founded in the later nineteen forties on the rule that care would be free at the point of use and funded from general taxation, it abolished at a stroke the transaction that had defined medical poverty: the calculation, in a sickroom, of whether the illness justified the fee. Continental systems reached comparable coverage by different routes, through social insurance funds with near-universal membership and state underwriting, and the Scandinavian countries built tax-funded services on municipal foundations. The routes differed; the outcome converged. Across the heartland, the encounter between a citizen and a doctor ceased to be a market encounter for the great majority of care, and the fear of medical destitution, a staple dread of working-class life within living memory, was retired as an ordinary experience. Any account of what the settlement delivered that treats this as a minor administrative change has not understood what the fear of illness did to the generations who lived under it.

Education expanded on the same principle and with the same double effect, material and political. Secondary schooling, a minority privilege before the war in most of the heartland, became the common experience of the age group. Leaving ages rose. Grammar and technical routes multiplied and then, in several countries, were folded into comprehensive schools intended to end the sorting of children by class at eleven or twelve. Universities expanded from elite institutions into mass ones, with maintenance support that made attendance possible for students whose families could not have contemplated it a generation earlier. The expansion did not abolish educational inequality, whose resilience inside formally equal systems became one of the period's own research industries. It did transform the ceiling: the expectation that a worker's child might enter a profession moved from the exceptional biography to the ordinary family plan, and that transformation of expectation is part of the record the comparison must grade.

## Security at work: insurance, wages, and the balance in the workplace

Alongside the services stood the insurance state, the least photogenic and arguably the most life-changing of the settlement's constructions. Earnings-related pensions replaced the flat subsistence pension and the workhouse shadow behind it. Unemployment insurance, sickness benefit, family allowances, and industrial injury provision converted the classic catastrophes of working-class life, the accident, the layoff, the long illness, the death of the breadwinner, from family-destroying events into managed misfortunes. The conversion was never complete and the levels were never princely, and the literature of the period is full of documented gaps through which real people fell. But the direction and the scale are not in dispute among historians of any persuasion: the settlement decades saw insecurity, the defining texture of proletarian existence since industrialisation began, reduced further and faster than in any comparable span, and reduced by deliberate institutional construction rather than by the unaided operation of growth.

### What did the settlement change about the experience of work?

It changed the bargaining position underneath every working day. A worker who cannot easily be replaced in a tight labour market, whose family is insured against his layoff, and whose union is recognised by law negotiates from security rather than desperation, and the tone of the workplace, its hours, its safety, its courtesies, registers the difference.

The wage relation itself was civilised in specific, enumerable ways. Working hours fell across the period, holidays lengthened from days into weeks, safety regulation acquired inspectors and penalties, and the arbitrary powers of the foreman, dismissal at whim, fines, the blacklist, retreated before procedure and representation. None of this transformed who owned the factory, a fact the Marxist sections of this article will insist on. All of it transformed what it felt like to spend a life inside the factory, a fact the social democratic sections insist on with equal justice. The comparison's difficulty lives exactly in that double truth. A tradition that grades only ownership will call the settlement decades a gilded continuation of the old order. A tradition that grades only experience will call them the effective end of the old order's miseries. The reversibility test was devised, in this article's usage, to give the argument a third dimension that both traditions must answer: not only what was delivered, and not only what was left untouched, but what survived.

## The other record: what Marxist governments delivered

Fairness in this comparison requires the same plainness about the rival record, and the same refusal to let a qualification dissolve it in advance. Governments ruling in Marxism's name, across the twentieth century, industrialised backward economies at speed, spread literacy and basic medicine to populations that had known neither, mobilised for and won a war of national survival against invasion, and built the heavy industrial base of states that had been agrarian within living memory. The series treats those achievements in their own articles, and nothing in the present comparison requires denying them. What the comparison requires is the narrower question the traditions themselves posed: how the people who laboured under those governments actually lived, in security, in consumption, in freedom of movement and organisation, across the decades of rule? On that question the record runs heavily against the Marxist governments. Consumption was subordinated to accumulation for decade after decade, so that the promised abundance receded as a horizon while queues and shortages organised daily life. Housing was provided and was cramped, shoddy, and allocated by waiting lists measured in years. Full employment existed and coexisted with overstaffing, low productivity, and the absence of any right to organise independently of the state that employed everyone. Provision was universal in form and stratified in fact, with the ruling stratum's separate shops, clinics, and dachas constituting a standing refutation of the equality in whose name the system ruled.

Above all, the Marxist record contains a category of event that has no counterpart anywhere in the social democratic record: the organised violence of the state against its own working population, in famine, in terror, in the camp system, and in the suppression of independent labour movements from Berlin in the nineteen fifties to Gdansk in the nineteen eighties. Social democratic governments committed their own injustices, colonial and domestic, and the series does not canonise them. But no social democratic government starved its peasantry as a policy instrument, none built an archipelago of camps, and none required its workers to seek permission from a single party to form a union. The comparison of delivered outcomes, to anticipate the verdict this article will reach, is not close, and the closeness question is one the Marxist tradition's honest writers have themselves conceded when they stopped grading their own side by its intentions. Intentions are the one currency in which the Marxist record is rich. The comparison conducted here refuses that currency to both sides equally.

## The comparison basis: implementations against implementations

A methodological commitment governs everything in this article and should be stated before the argument proceeds further, because most published versions of this comparison violate it in one direction or the other. The comparison is between implementations and implementations: post-war Western European social democratic governments against twentieth-century Marxist governments, over comparable periods, on stated welfare dimensions. It is not a comparison of one tradition's practice with the other's programme, in either direction. The social democrat who sets the Swedish record beside the Marxist programme's promises, abundance, freedom, the withering of coercion, and declares the promises empty has compared a practice with a text. The Marxist who sets the Soviet record's intentions beside the settlement's compromises and declares the compromises a betrayal has made the mirror-image error. Texts are cheap in both traditions, and both traditions wrote beautiful ones. What each tradition did when it held power, for the people in whose name it held power, is the evidence this article admits, and the reader should hold the article to that rule as strictly as the article holds the traditions.

### What does comparing implementations against implementations rule out?

It rules out grading either tradition by its manifestos. A programme is evidence of what a tradition hoped; a government is evidence of what it did with power, resources, and opposition. Comparing the two kinds of evidence across the fork rigs the verdict in advance, which is why popular versions of this comparison so often satisfy only their authors.

The rule has a corollary that cuts against the side this article's reversibility argument will favour. If Marxist governments are to be graded by their records rather than by Marx's texts, then the distance between the texts and the records becomes a question the Marxist tradition must answer rather than a refuge it can occupy. The reply that true Marxism was never implemented is, under the implementations rule, an admission that the tradition's implementations failed to be true to it across every country and decade in which it held power, which is itself a finding about the tradition, about the gap between its theory of transition and any available practice of transition. Symmetrically, the social democratic reply that the settlement's unravelling was the work of its enemies rather than its own design is an admission that the design left the settlement dependent on the goodwill of those enemies, which is the reversibility finding stated from the other side. The implementations rule, honestly applied, leaves neither tradition a hiding place. That is its recommendation.

## Why Marxist accounts understate the record

A word about the sources, because the reader of this comparison will mostly encounter it in versions written by partisans, and the distortions are systematic enough to name. Marxist accounts of the settlement typically concede its existence in a paragraph and spend their energy on its limits: the persistence of exploitation, the integration of unions into management, the imperial foundations of European prosperity, the consumerism that allegedly pacified the class the movement existed to emancipate. Each limit is real, and several receive their due later in this article. But the proportions betray the method. A record that transformed the material life of a continent's working class within a generation is not adequately treated as a paragraph of concession on the way to a theory of why it did not matter. The understatement has a cause, and the cause is not dishonesty in the simple sense. To state the record at its true size is to raise the question the tradition least wants to face: if this much could be won without revolution, what was the revolution for? The question can be answered, and the reversibility argument is the tradition's answer. But the answer must be given after the record is stated, not instead of stating it.

### Why do Marxist accounts understate the record?

Because the record is the strongest evidence against the necessity of their central claim. If capitalism's outcomes could be transformed to that extent by reform, the argument that transformation requires expropriation loses its premise, so the record is minimised in advance and the argument proceeds as though the concession had never been forced.

Social democratic accounts distort in the opposite direction, and the symmetry should be registered now rather than discovered later. In the tradition's own telling, the settlement figures as a permanent achievement of democratic politics, a ratchet that history had fitted to the welfare state, and the unravelling from the nineteen seventies appears as an inexplicable weather event, a storm of ideology that blew in from across the Atlantic and will presumably blow out again. What that telling cannot accommodate is the structural question: why was the ratchet so easily reversed, if it was a ratchet? Why did institutions defended by majorities fall to governments elected by minorities of the electorate, and why did the defending constituencies that universalism was designed to create defend some institutions to the death and surrender others with barely a campaign? Those questions lead directly to the ownership argument the tradition exists to refuse, which is why its literature prefers weather metaphors to structural ones. The reversal sections of this article are written against that preference.

## The conditions that made the settlement possible

The Marxist explanation of the settlement, when it finally engages the record seriously, is an explanation by conditions. The settlement, on this account, was not the discovery of a permanent formula for civilising capitalism. It was the product of a specific and temporary configuration, and it lasted exactly as long as the configuration did. Four conditions do most of the work in the literature. First, reconstruction: the war had destroyed capital stock across the continent while leaving skills, institutions, and demand intact, creating a long boom in which investment and rising wages could advance together because there was so much rebuilding to do. Second, the monetary shelter: the Bretton Woods arrangements restricted capital movements, so a government that taxed and spent at settlement levels did not face the immediate verdict of mobile capital fleeing the jurisdiction. Third, a demographic and technological phase: young populations, expanding workforces, and the diffusion of a generation of accumulated technology through civilian industry supplied growth of a kind that made distributional conflict positive-sum, since a rising product allowed every claim to be met partly from the increment. Fourth, the geopolitical condition, the one the Marxist account treats as decisive and the social democratic account treats as embarrassing: the existence of a rival system to the east, armed, organised, and claiming to represent the same working class, which made concessions to labour in the west a matter of state prudence for governments of every colour.

### Which conditions made the settlement possible?

Reconstruction demand, sheltered national policy under capital controls, a favourable demographic and technological phase, and the presence of a rival system bidding for workers' allegiance combined to make generosity cheap. Each condition was temporary, and the settlement's architects built permanent institutions on foundations that history had already scheduled for removal.

Examine the fourth condition more closely, because it carries an irony the comparison should not pass over. In France and Italy, mass communist parties stood at the settlement's founding, fresh from the resistance, commanding the loyalty of a large fraction of the organised working class. Governments that wished to govern had to outbid them for that loyalty, and the currency of the bidding was the settlement itself: social security, nationalisation of commanding heights, works councils, the whole apparatus of provision. The welfare states of the Latin countries were built substantially by Christian democratic and centrist governments purchasing insurance against the Marxist alternative. The rival system thus contributed to the western record twice over: its existence disciplined western capital into concessions, and its example defined what the concessions had to outbid. When the rival system collapsed at the end of the nineteen eighties, the prudential motive for concession collapsed with it, a sequence the Marxist account reads as its prediction completing itself and the social democratic account prefers not to discuss. Even before that collapse, the erosion of the other three conditions had begun the unravelling, as the next sections describe.

### How did the rival system's existence shape concessions?

It made generosity prudent for governments that feared their own working classes defecting to a competitor. Western employers and states conceded welfare, bargaining rights, and full employment partly from conviction and partly as insurance, and the insurance motive weakened as the rival decayed, removing one of the settlement's silent supports.

There is a further condition, less often named because it embarrasses both traditions: the settlement rested on the organised strength of labour itself, on union densities and party memberships at historic heights, on shop steward organisations that could shut a plant by lunchtime, and on the living memory, among employers and officials, of what the nineteen thirties had done to societies that let mass unemployment return. Concession was prudent because the alternative to concession was not quiescence but mobilisation. The settlement, in other words, was not a gift of enlightened policy dropped into a passive society. It was an equilibrium of forces, in which a mobilised working class extracted terms from a capital that needed labour, feared its rival, and was sheltered from exit. Every element of that sentence names a variable, and every variable moved against the settlement in the decades that followed. Union density declined as employment shifted from the great factories to dispersed services. The memory of the thirties died with its generation. Capital acquired exit, first through the revival of international money markets and then through the formal abolition of the controls that had sheltered national bargains. The equilibrium did not need to be attacked in a single dramatic assault, though in Britain it was. It needed only to be outlived by its conditions.

## The reversibility argument stated in full

Now state the Marxist reply at its strongest, in the form its most careful analysts gave it, because the article's verdict will rest partly on it and it deserves to be tested at full power rather than in the caricature form. The argument begins from the observation that the settlement changed the distribution of income without changing the distribution of the assets from which income flows. The factory, the bank, the newspaper, the shipping line remained in the hands that had held them, and the state's new claims on the product were levies on a going concern whose continuance depended on decisions the state did not control: the decision to invest, to hire, to lend, to stay. A later literature gave this the name structural dependence. A government of any party, however radical its mandate, must persuade private owners to invest, because employment and tax revenue ride on that investment; owners who doubt the government's intentions can withhold investment, move capital, or simply wait, and the resulting downturn arrives in time to punish the government at the next election. The leash is not a conspiracy and requires no villains. It is a property of the structure, which is why the Marxist analysts who developed the argument, writers in the lineage that runs from Ralph Miliband's anatomy of the capitalist state through the state-theory debates the series treats in its article on [Marxist state theory](/2013/04/01/marxist-state-theory-today/), regarded the settlement not as a refutation of their tradition but as its most elegant confirmation: capital had accepted taxation and provision precisely because ownership, the decisive thing, had never been on the table.

From structural dependence the argument derives the reversibility prediction in a strict form. Gains that rest on a balance of forces are exactly as durable as the balance. When growth slows, the positive-sum politics of the increment collapses into the zero-sum politics of the share, and owners who accepted levies on a rising product resist levies on a stagnant one. When capital recovers its mobility, the sheltered space for national policy contracts, and governments begin competing for investment by advertising restraint. When the rival system decays, the prudential motive for concession decays with it. At that point the settlement's gains face hostile governments armed with a simple observation: everything the settlement built by statute can be unbuilt by statute, everything it built by taxation can be starved by taxation, and the owners of the underlying assets, who waited out the settlement decades with their title deeds intact, are available as allies, funders, and eventually ministers of the unbuilding. Nothing in the argument requires the unbuilding to be total, and the careful versions of it never predicted totality. They predicted direction: that the settlement would be rolled back along the gradient of its dependence on continued political control, sector by sector, at the speed politics allowed. The next sections test that prediction against the record of the unravelling, including the places where the prediction failed, which are as informative as the places where it held.

## The record and the reversal

The table below is the article's findable artifact, the record and the reversal set out row by row. It takes the settlement's five great policy areas in the order of their construction: inequality, employment, health, education, and social insurance. For each it states what the settlement period achieved, what was retained when the political weather changed, what was reversed, and what change in ownership would have been required to place the achievement beyond the reach of an ordinary hostile government. Read down the retained column and the reversal appears partial, which it was. Read down the reversed column and the pattern appears systematic, which it also was. The ownership column is the table's argument in miniature: in every row, the change that would have secured the gain is a change the settlement, by its founding decision, declined to make.

| Policy area | What the settlement achieved | What was retained | What was reversed | Ownership change that would have secured it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inequality | A sustained compression of incomes and wealth, a levelling across the middle decades without peacetime precedent | Part of the compression in earnings among the broad middle of the distribution | The steep fall in top tax rates, the renewed widening at the summit, the return of large inherited fortunes | Social ownership of the commanding assets, so that the surplus itself, and not only its distribution after the fact, stood under public claim |
| Employment | A generation of near-full employment maintained as a commitment of government | The machinery of labour market policy in the countries that built it most seriously | The commitment itself, abandoned as a goal and replaced by the management of joblessness | Public direction of investment, so that the level of hiring no longer rested on the confidence of private owners |

The health row deserves emphasis because it is the table's built-in control case. Health was the area where the settlement went furthest toward constituting provision as a public service in its own right, employing its staff, owning its hospitals, and supplying care outside the market rather than subsidising purchases inside it. Health was also, not coincidentally, the area where reversal proved hardest and least complete, where hostile governments with large majorities trimmed and reorganised but did not dare abolish, and where the universal constituency the founders designed performed exactly the defensive work it was designed for. The contrast with employment, where the settlement had changed nothing structural and merely committed governments to a goal, could hardly be cleaner. The commitment was reversed by the simple act of ceasing to hold it. Between those poles the other rows arrange themselves in the order the reversibility test predicts: the more a gain was constituted as public provision or public right embedded in institutions, the better it survived; the more it rested on policy stance, tax level, and the tolerance of owners, the faster it went.

## The unravelling from the nineteen seventies

The unravelling began as a crisis of the settlement's economics before it became a project of its enemies. The long boom faltered at the end of the nineteen sixties and broke in the nineteen seventies under shocks the settlement's machinery had not been designed to absorb: the collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary order, the abrupt rise in energy prices, and the arrival of stagflation, the simultaneous presence of stagnation and inflation that dissolved the period's governing trade-offs. Wage bargaining systems built for a growing product transmitted price shocks into wage rounds and wage rounds into price rises. Governments of the settlement's own tradition found themselves administering restraint, incomes policies, and eventually unemployment, the very conditions their politics existed to abolish. The political opening for a deliberate unravelling came when new governments of the right, elected at the decade's end and after, converted emergency into doctrine: the commitment to full employment was formally abandoned, capital controls were abolished in a single stroke in the British case, union law was rewritten in successive statutes, public industries were sold in a programme that transferred a substantial sector of the economy to private title, and the uprating of benefits was detached from earnings so that the insurance state shrank relative to the economy by formula rather than by vote.

### How did capital mobility change the settlement's defences?

Under capital controls, a government taxing at settlement levels faced grumbling but not flight, because exit was legally and practically restricted. Once controls fell and money markets globalised, every budget was drafted in the shadow of the bond market and the exchange rate, and the defence of national policy space had to be mounted daily or surrendered.

The French experience of the early nineteen eighties supplies the mechanism in its purest documented form. A socialist government elected on a programme of expansion, nationalisation, and redistribution attempted to grow its way out of the downturn while its partners contracted. Capital left, the currency fell in successive devaluations, and within two years the government reversed into austerity, wage restraint, and the discipline of the European monetary arrangement, a posture its finance minister named rigour. The episode matters to this comparison beyond France, because it isolates the variable. The government in question was not timid, its mandate was fresh, its parliamentary position was strong, and its programme was the settlement's logic extended. What it lacked was shelter: the controls that had once made national policy space possible were gone, and the owners of mobile capital, exercising no conspiracy and casting no votes, set the boundary inside which an elected government could act. Marxist analysts filed the episode under structural dependence. Social democratic analysts filed it under the lessons of interdependence. The filing differs; the episode is the same, and it is the single most instructive event in the comparison after the settlement itself.

### What was unwound first, and what survived?

The commitments held only as policy stances went first: full employment as a goal, capital controls, the earnings link in benefit uprating. Institutions with universal constituencies went last and least: flagship health services and the broad structure of social insurance survived hostile decades trimmed and reorganised but recognisably themselves.

Sweden's crisis of the early nineteen nineties extends the lesson to the settlement's heartland and its most competent administrators. A banking collapse and a severe recession forced retrenchment on a scale the Swedish model was supposed to have rendered unnecessary: benefit replacement levels trimmed, waiting days introduced, employment programmes cut, and the full employment commitment subordinated to price stability written into central bank mandates. A conservative government began the work and a returning social democratic government continued much of it, a sequence that matters for the argument, because it shows the unravelling proceeding not only by enemy action but by the tradition's own adaptation to conditions its model had not anticipated. New Zealand carried the logic furthest: there, a Labour government itself dismantled controls, floated the currency, corporatised and sold state assets, and rewrote the welfare state, demonstrating that the agency of reversal could wear the settlement's own colours when the structural pressures were strong enough and the party's doctrine had already migrated. The reversal, in short, was an international event with national variations, driven by the changed conditions the Marxist account enumerates and executed by governments of both traditions, though with very different degrees of enthusiasm.

## The reversal was partial: what hostile governments could not reach

Against the unravelling must be set, with equal plainness, the survival. The reversal was not complete, and any version of the reversibility argument that implies it was should be corrected by the record it claims to describe. The flagship health services survived decades of hostile or sceptical government with their founding principle intact: treatment without charge when illness struck, financed from taxation, available to the whole population. They were squeezed, reorganised, marketised at the edges, and made to wait for funds, and their survival in that battered condition is precisely the datum the verdict must weigh. Social insurance frameworks survived across most of the heartland, contributory in structure and broad in coverage, even where their generosity was eroded by formula. The educational expansion was never reversed at all in its structure: no government proposed to return secondary schooling to a minority or to shrink the universities to their pre-war scale, and the expectation of advancement the expansion created proved to be one of the settlement's most irreversible deposits. Even in Britain, the unravelling's most determined theatre, the health service emerged from two decades of governments committed to rolling back the state with its principle not merely intact but ritually reaffirmed by the very party that had privatised the commanding heights of industry.

### What would count as the settlement being owned rather than rented?

A gain would count as owned if it survived a change of government and a sustained shift in the balance of forces with its function substantially intact, not merely its name and façade: funding adequate to its purpose, eligibility un-narrowed, scope uncontracted, and no continuing dependence on the goodwill of the forces that opposed its creation.

That definition is doing deliberate work, and the reader should see the work. It distinguishes survival in function from survival in name, because the unravelling's subtlest technique was to preserve institutions as brands while hollowing their content: the insurance scheme whose uprating formula guaranteed its slow shrinkage, the public service whose budget was held below the growth of need until its queues performed the rationing its ministers denied intending, the university system expanded in numbers and starved in unit funding. A test that counted names would grade the settlement far more durable than a test that counts function, and much of the popular argument about whether the welfare state survived is an unacknowledged quarrel between those two tests. This article grades by function throughout. It is the stricter standard, and it is the one the settlement's founders, who built universalism precisely as a defence of function, would have recognised as their own.

Apply the functional standard honestly, however, and the survival column still does not empty. Health care in the flagship systems continued to treat the whole population free of charge whenever illness came, through every government of the unravelling decades; that is function, not facade, whatever the squeezes at the margin. Pensions continued to arrive, insurance continued to pay, schools continued to teach the children of the whole society in common institutions. The settlement's defenders are entitled to insist that a reversal which leaves the central institutions standing, funded, and universal after forty years of hostile weather is a strange kind of refutation of the tradition that built them. The Marxist reply, that the direction of travel was uniformly toward erosion and that the process was interrupted rather than concluded, is also entitled to its hearing. Both claims return in the verdict, where the defeaters are stated.

## Why ownership decides what a hostile government can reach

Underneath the narrative of survival and reversal sits the mechanism that explains the pattern, and the pattern is the strongest evidence in the entire comparison, because it was not arranged by either tradition. Sort the settlement's gains by how they were constituted and a law emerges. Gains constituted as flows, tax rates, benefit levels, policy commitments, were reversed at the speed of legislation, because a flow can be stopped by the same instrument that started it. Gains constituted as institutions with universal constituencies were reversed slowly, partially, and at political cost, because an institution used by everyone assembles its own defenders. Gains constituted as property, in the rare cases where the settlement touched property at all, were not reversed at all within the period, because property is defended by the legal order itself and reversing it requires the expropriation that hostile governments exist to prevent. The nationalised industries were sold, it will be objected, and the objection proves the rule's subtlety: they were sold precisely because they were property, transferable by the ordinary operations of the market the state itself conducted, and the proceeds and the precedent both belonged to the unravelling. What the state had owned as a portfolio it could sell as a portfolio. What it had built as a universal service it could not sell, because there was nothing to sell that the whole society did not regard as its own.

### Why does ownership decide what a hostile government can reach?

A hostile government reaches flows by statute and institutions by attrition, but an advance locked into who holds the society's assets can be undone only by reversing the legal order that protects its own supporters' property. Ownership is therefore the one foundation on which a gain stops depending on the next election.

This is the analytical core of the Marxist case, stripped of eschatology and stated as a proposition about institutions that any tradition could in principle accept. It does not say that reforms are worthless; the record refutes that laziness. It does not say that reversal is total or immediate; the survival column refutes that too. It says that durability is a function of constitution, that the settlement constituted its gains in the two weaker forms, flow and institution, while declining the stronger form, and that the subsequent history sorted the gains by their constitution with an almost experimental neatness. The social democratic tradition's most penetrating self-critics have at times conceded the mechanism while disputing its importance, arguing that institutions defended by majorities are security enough in democracies and that the demand for the stronger form mistakes the nature of democratic politics, in which nothing is or should be beyond the reach of a future majority. That rejoinder deserves respect, and it returns in the verdict, because it identifies the genuine philosophical divide underneath the empirical one: whether permanence is a legitimate ambition for democratic legislation at all. The Marxist tradition answers that a transformation which the next parliament can repeal is not a transformation. The social democratic tradition answers that in a democracy, everything is repealable, including constitutions, and that the demand for irreversibility is itself the authoritarian temptation wearing the mask of thoroughness. The reader should notice that this rejoinder, the strongest available, concedes the empirical claim while contesting its significance. Much of the verdict turns on that concession.

## The social democratic case at full strength

Every comparison in this series is required to state the strongest objection to its own governing argument, and in this article the objection is not a technicality. It is the social democratic case itself, stated by its own theorists at full strength, and it begins by refusing the terms in which the preceding sections have been written. You have graded our record, the objection runs, by a test of your own devising, durability under hostile government, and found it wanting. Grade it instead by the test that matters to the people who lived it: what did your life contain, and what would it have contained under the alternative? The revisionist tradition, from Bernstein at the century's turn to Anthony Crosland at its middle, argued that the transformation Marx predicted was unnecessary, because the goods that transformation had promised, security against the classic catastrophes, dignity in work and in dependence, a rising standard of life across a lifetime, and the gradual civilising of the wage relation, could be delivered within a reformed capitalism by democratic governments using taxation, regulation, and provision. The argument was not that capitalism was just. It was that capitalism was governable, that its outcomes were politically malleable to a degree the Marxist analysis denied, and that the proof would be furnished by governing.

A century of evidence, the objection continues, supports the theorists. Within living memory of the settlement's founding, the tradition delivered the largest sustained improvement in the conditions of working people ever achieved anywhere: longer lives, healthier children, educated daughters and sons, warm houses, paid holidays, pensions that arrived, workplaces governed by procedure rather than whim. It delivered these things without civil war, without expropriation, without a one-party state, without camps, without a single famine administered as policy, and without requiring the population to profess a doctrine. The governments that delivered them were elected, were criticised in a free press, lost elections, and handed over power, and the goods survived the handing over in substantial part, as this article's own survival column records. Against that record stands the rival tradition's: states that expropriated everything and delivered queues, that promised the withering of coercion and built its most extensive modern apparatus, that spoke in the name of the working class and forbade the working class to organise. A tradition whose implementations produced none of the goods and all of the coercion is not positioned, the objection concludes, to describe the settlement's gains as inadequate because a later generation of politicians trimmed them. Inadequate against what standard? Against the standard of the programme, which was never implemented anywhere without catastrophe, or against the standard of the record, which the settlement wins without serious contest?

The objection should be felt at its full weight before any answer is attempted, because it is not merely an argument but an accusation of bad faith directed at the entire genre of Marxist commentary on the settlement. The accusation is that the reversibility test functions in that commentary as a device for converting defeat into vindication: heads, the revolution was necessary; tails, the reforms were temporary. If the settlement had collapsed entirely, the tradition would claim its prediction. If the settlement endures, the tradition replies that it endures on sufferance and the claim is merely postponed. A test that cannot be failed, the objection observes with some justice, is not a test but a posture, and the closing sections of this article take that observation seriously enough to state, in explicit form, what evidence would defeat the article's own verdicts. The reader is entitled to hold the article to them.

## The answer: goods delivered, structure untouched

The answer begins by separating two questions that the objection, for all its force, runs together. The first question is comparative and biographical: which tradition's governments delivered better lives to the people they ruled? The second is analytical and predictive: what did each tradition claim about the durability of gains under unchanged ownership, and what happened? The social democratic case wins the first question outright, and this article concedes it without reservation: the comparison of delivered outcomes is not close, and Marxist accounts that proceed as though it were close, or that evade the record by changing the subject to intentions, forfeit the right to be heard on the second question. The concession is not a courtesy. It is the price of admission to the argument, and it is paid here in full.

On the second question, the record runs the other way, and the objection's own evidence helps to prove it. The goods were delivered, and then, within a generation of their delivery, a substantial portion of them was withdrawn by governments that did not need to storm anything, abolish anything, or suspend any constitution to do it. They needed only to win elections under rules the settlement itself accepted, and to legislate. The ease of the withdrawal is the datum. A transformation of ownership could not have been withdrawn by those means, as the objection's own tradition discovered when it contemplated the one serious attempt to make such a transformation by instalments, the Swedish wage-earner funds, and found the proposal treated by its opponents not as another policy to be trimmed but as an existential threat to be destroyed. The asymmetry is the whole argument in miniature. Policies are argued about; property is defended by the order itself. The settlement's gains were policies. That is why they were reversible, and their reversal, partial and resisted as it was, is the confirmation the Marxist prediction asked for: not that reform is futile, which the record refutes, but that reform which declines to touch ownership holds its winnings in a currency the next government can devalue.

Distinguish, finally, between the goods delivered and the transformation of ownership and control, because the objection's rhetoric depends on blurring them. Those goods, security against catastrophe, dignity in dependence as in work, a standard of life rising across a lifetime, and a wage relation civilised by law, are real goods, and the settlement delivered them in quantities no rival matched. Ownership and control are not goods of the same kind; they are the distribution of the power to decide what happens next, including the power to decide whether the goods continue. The settlement redistributed the product while leaving the power where it stood, and the subsequent history is what a redistribution of product without a redistribution of power looks like over time: generous while the balance holds, revocable when it shifts. The social democratic theorists promised that the goods could be delivered without the transformation. They were right about the delivery. The Marxist theorists predicted that delivery without transformation would not be secure. On the evidence of the unravelling, they were right about the security. Holding those two sentences together, without letting either annihilate the other, is the intellectual task this comparison sets, and it is harder than the polemics of either tradition suggest.

## Crosland and the claim that ownership had ceased to matter

The theoretical statement of 1956 that the brief for this comparison singles out is Anthony Crosland's book on the future of socialism, and it deserves treatment as the settlement's most intelligent self-understanding. Crosland wrote as a British Labour intellectual at the settlement's high tide, and his argument generalised Bernstein for the age of the managed economy. Ownership, he held, had ceased to be the decisive question, for two reasons. The first was the separation of ownership from control: the modern corporation was run by salaried managers whose interests and outlooks diverged from those of the shareholders who nominally owned it, so that the capitalist as a directing figure had already abdicated the position Marxist theory assigned him. The second was the transformation of the state: armed with Keynesian demand management, progressive taxation, and the welfare apparatus, a democratic government could achieve the substantive goals of socialism, full employment, security, and a narrowing of inequality, by fiscal and regulatory means, whatever the formal title to industry said. Socialism should therefore be redefined by its ends, above all equality, and pursued by the instruments that worked, with nationalisation demoted from a defining commitment to one tool among others, to be used where it served the ends and dropped where it did not.

### What separated Crosland's argument from Bernstein's?

Bernstein revised the doctrine from inside a movement that still expected capitalism to fail, arguing that it was adapting instead. Crosland wrote after the adaptation had been governed for a decade, and argued the stronger thesis that the system's commanding decisions no longer belonged to owners at all, making ownership a question of form rather than substance.

The argument's strength was its fit to the visible facts of the settlement decade, and its weakness was the same: it generalised a configuration. The managers who appeared autonomous in the nineteen fifties operated inside capital markets that were sleepy, segmented, and nationally contained, and inside firms whose size relative to the state made them partners in a negotiated order. When the capital markets woke, integrated, and began to discipline managers through takeovers, shareholder value doctrines, and the threat of exit, the separation of ownership and control revealed itself as a fair-weather phenomenon: control had not escaped ownership, it had merely been unexercised by owners who were content. Crosland's state, likewise, was the state of the sheltered decades, fiscally potent because capital was captive. The fiscal potency contracted as the captivity ended. None of this makes Crosland a fool; he remains the tradition's most formidable theorist, and his redefinition of socialism as equality rather than ownership gave the settlement a doctrine worthy of its practice. It makes him the clearest illustration of the article's central pattern, in which the social democratic tradition repeatedly mistook the conditions of its best decades for the permanent properties of the system it governed. His later work, written as the weather changed, shows a mind registering the shift, and the tradition he equipped spent the following decades arguing about whether his redefinition had been a liberation or a surrender, an argument that is the internal form of the fork itself.

The British sequel supplies the footnote. Labour's constitution had committed the party since 1918 to common ownership in a famous clause, and after the election defeat of 1959 Hugh Gaitskell, Crosland's ally, attempted to revise the clause away and failed against the party's own attachments. The clause survived as a totem for another generation, until a later leader rewrote it in the nineteen nineties, in a different world, as a statement of values with the ownership commitment removed. The sequence compresses the tradition's doctrinal history into one clause's biography: adopted when the party was Marxist in aspiration, defended as heritage when it had ceased to be Marxist in practice, and surrendered when the practice finally demanded a doctrine to match. Germany performed the same operation earlier, more honestly, and in a single document, to which the article now turns.

## Godesberg: the formal abandonment

In November 1959 the German Social Democratic Party, meeting at Bad Godesberg, adopted the programme that formalised the separation this article has been tracing. The Godesberg Programme abandoned the Marxist framework explicitly: no analysis of capitalism's necessary collapse, no commitment to social ownership as the definition of the goal, no identification of the party with a single class. In their place stood democratic socialism as an ethical commitment to freedom, justice, and solidarity, an acceptance of the market economy and of private property in the means of production, and the famous formula that the party wanted as much competition as possible and as much planning as necessary, a sentence that deserves its currency because it states the whole post-Marxist position in eleven words: the economy is an instrument panel, and the only question is the setting. The party re-presented itself as a people's party open to all citizens rather than the political arm of the working class, accepted the western alliance and rearmament it had opposed, and within a decade entered government, first in coalition and then at its head, governing the settlement's most prosperous large economy in a manner indistinguishable in its economics from the programme's formula.

### What did the Godesberg programme actually change?

It changed the doctrine to match a practice that was already two generations old. Social democratic parties had governed as reformers since the interwar years; Godesberg confessed in a programme what Bernstein had argued in a book, that the party no longer expected or sought the supersession of capitalism, only its civilised management.

The date matters for the seeded question about when social democracy abandoned Marxism, and the honest answer has three layers that should not be collapsed. Organisationally, the abandonment occurred in 1919, when the movement split and the parties that rejected the revolutionary road constituted themselves as a separate tradition. Practically, it occurred in every interwar and post-war government, none of which attempted the expropriation that the inherited doctrine prescribed. Doctrinally, it occurred at Godesberg and in the parallel revisions elsewhere, when the tradition finally rewrote its programmes to say what its practice had long announced. Each layer is a defensible answer to the question, and the choice among them is a choice about what a tradition is: its organisations, its deeds, or its documents. This article's own usage treats the deeds as primary, which is why it dates the fork to the separation of 1919 and treats Godesberg as the confession of a forty-year-old fact. But the confession mattered, because a doctrine retained on paper exerts a gravitational pull on practice, constraining what leaders can propose and what members will forgive. After Godesberg, the German party's leaders could govern as managers without the friction of a membership armed with a Marxist programme, and the absence of that friction is part of the explanation for the smoothness with which the tradition later administered the unravelling.

## The structural dilemma: the sharpest statement, written by a Marxist

The comparison's most analytically precise material comes from neither tradition's apologetics but from a work of political science published in 1985 by Adam Przeworski, a scholar formed in the Marxist tradition, whose book on capitalism and social democracy subjected his own side's electoral strategy to an analysis of unusual coldness. The brief for this article flags the irony, and it is worth dwelling on: the sharpest statement of social democracy's dilemma was written by a Marxist, just as the sharpest statements of the revolutionary tradition's dilemmas were so often written by its own dissidents. Traditions in this series are repeatedly understood best by their internal critics, and the present case is the comparison's governing example.

Przeworski's dilemma has two horns, and both must be stated exactly. The first horn is electoral arithmetic. A party that seeks power through elections in a society where manual workers are a minority, and a shrinking one, cannot win on the votes of its founding class alone. It must appeal beyond that class, to salaried employees, to professionals, to small proprietors, to pensioners, and every extension of the appeal dilutes the programme that made the party distinctive, because the new constituencies want the settlement's benefits without its founding project of transformation. The party therefore faces a choice between purity and power that has no resolution: remain a class party and lose, or become a people's party and govern as something other than the movement it was. The historical record of the tradition is the record of parties choosing the second horn and then discovering that the choice, once made, remakes the party itself, its membership, its career structures, its instincts, until the transformation it was founded to pursue no longer has an organisational bearer.

### What does the structural dilemma claim in its exact form?

It claims that electoral socialism is caught between its electorate and its project. Winning requires appeals beyond the working class, which dilute the programme; and workers themselves, offered a certain compromise against an uncertain transition, rationally choose the compromise. The route that leads to office therefore leads away from transformation, by arithmetic and by preference alike.

The second horn is the deeper one, because it operates even if the arithmetic is solved. Suppose the party wins, with whatever coalition. It now proposes to move beyond the settlement toward the transformation of ownership. The costs of that transition fall in the present and are certain: disruption, capital flight, falling investment, shortages during the reorganisation, borne by the very workers the transition is meant to emancipate. The benefits fall in the future and are uncertain, depending on the performance of institutions that have never existed. Workers confronting that prospectus are not suffering from false consciousness when they hesitate. They are reasoning correctly about risk, time, and the evidence available to them, and a party that respects their reasoning will moderate the transition, while a party that overrides it ceases to be democratic in the sense the tradition claims. The electoral road thus tends toward the settlement rather than through it, not because leaders are cowards or traitors, the explanation the revolutionary tradition prefers, but because the structure of electoral choice under capitalism systematically rewards the compromise and penalises the leap. The settlement, on this analysis, is not a stage on the way to socialism that timid men mistook for the destination. It is the equilibrium of the electoral strategy itself, the resting point toward which the strategy's own logic drives.

### Why do workers rationally prefer compromise to transition?

Because the compromise pays now and the transition bills now for a payment later that may never arrive. A worker with a family, a tenancy, and a skill tied to an existing industry discounts an uncertain collective future against certain private costs, and no amount of doctrine converts that discounting into an error.

Przeworski's analysis has a corollary that the Marxist tradition has found even harder to absorb than the dilemma itself. If workers rationally prefer the compromise, then the classical explanation of reformism, that the workers are misled by their leaders, their press, or their illusions, is not merely condescending but false, and the tradition's theory of consciousness requires revision at its root. The settlement was not imposed on a revolutionary class by a reformist bureaucracy. It was chosen, repeatedly and with open eyes, by electorates that included the class in whose name transformation was demanded, and chosen again in the unravelling decades when many of the same electorates voted for the parties of retrenchment. Any Marxism that cannot process that fact will keep mistaking its own unpopularity for a puzzle about deception. Any social democracy that processes it fully, however, confronts the dilemma from the inside: its mandate is real, and its mandate is for the settlement, not for what lies beyond it. The one serious attempt to escape the dilemma by changing the underlying structure gradually, rather than by exhorting the electorate to be braver, is the subject of the next section, and its fate is the comparison's most concrete single episode.

## The wage-earner funds: the one attempt to cross the line

In 1976 the Swedish trade union confederation adopted a proposal, developed by its economist Rudolf Meidner, that remains the only fully worked strategy by which social democracy proposed to transform ownership itself: the wage-earner funds. The design was elegant in a way that honoured both traditions' constraints. Companies above a certain size would pay a levy, assessed on profits, not in cash but in newly issued shares, into funds controlled collectively by wage earners. No expropriation decree, no compensation battle, no single dramatic transfer: the funds would accumulate shareholdings year by year, their voting power in the great companies growing with each levy, until over a span of decades the ownership of Swedish industry's commanding heights had passed, by instalments too gradual to provoke a crisis, into collective hands. The proposal answered Przeworski before Przeworski had published: it did not ask the electorate to leap, it did not ask workers to bear a transition's concentrated costs, and it did not depend on any future parliament's courage, because the mechanism, once legislated, would compound automatically like interest. It was, in the strictest sense, the reversibility problem addressed by the tradition that the reversibility argument indicts, an attempt to convert a flow settlement into a property transformation by the slowest imaginable instalments.

### How did the wage-earner funds propose to cross the ownership line gradually?

Profitable companies would issue new shares each year to collective funds as a levy on profits, so that wage earners' funds would accumulate voting stakes across industry without purchase, compensation, or decree. Ownership would migrate by arithmetic, a few points of equity at a time, until collective holdings commanded the great firms.

The response of Swedish capital remains the most instructive mobilisation in the comparison. The employers' federation, historically a partner in the settlement's centralised bargaining, recognised the proposal instantly for what it was: not another levy, which it could price, nor another regulation, which it could absorb, but a ratchet on ownership itself, the one mechanism in the entire social democratic repertoire that could not be reversed by a later parliament without expropriating the funds. It organised accordingly, funding research, campaigns, and eventually a mass demonstration of employers and their supporters through Stockholm, an almost unheard-of spectacle of capital in the streets. The political temperature of the proposal rose until the social democratic leadership, returned to office in the early nineteen eighties, enacted a drastically diluted version: a handful of regional funds, capped in size, limited in duration, financed in part by a payroll levy, and forbidden the open-ended accumulation that was the original design's whole point. The funds operated modestly for several years, were wound up after the change of government at the decade's turn, and passed into the literature as the road not taken. The unravelling of the diluted funds is sometimes cited against the reversibility argument, as proof that even property changes are reversible. The citation misreads the episode. What was reversed was a capped, time-limited scheme whose caps and limits were the price of its enactment; the original design was never tried, and the ferocity of the campaign against it is the best evidence of why: the owners of Swedish capital believed, and said, that the unamended funds would have worked.

### Why were the funds defeated rather than merely amended?

Because amendment was the defeat, administered in instalments. The design's power lay in its open-ended accumulation, so capping the funds' size and lifespan converted a ratchet into a gesture, and the employers' campaign aimed precisely at persuading the governing party to accept the gesture as the policy's fulfilment.

Draw the episode's lessons in order. First, the dilemma is real but not airtight: a strategy existed that addressed the ownership question within democratic and gradualist constraints, which refutes the lazy version of the Marxist claim that social democracy never even attempted transformation. Second, the strategy's fate confirms the deeper structure: the attempt to touch ownership mobilised opposition of a different order from the opposition to taxes and benefits, opposition that treated the proposal as a constitutional event rather than a budgetary one, exactly as the ownership sections of this article predict. Third, the decisive resistance came not only from capital but from within the governing tradition itself, whose electoral instincts, honed by the dilemma's first horn, recoiled from a fight whose costs were immediate and whose benefits belonged to a generation not yet voting. The funds are therefore the comparison in a single case: the record of delivery behind them, the reversibility argument around them, the structural dilemma inside the party that diluted them, and the ownership line drawn so clearly that both sides could see it. A student who understands the funds understands the article, which is why the exam guides in this cluster give the episode the prominence they do.

## The Swedish model in depth: solidarity wages and the active state

Sweden deserves a section of its own because it was the settlement's most self-conscious construction, the place where social democracy governed longest, thought hardest about its own methods, and eventually produced the wage-earner funds as the model's logical extension. The Swedish model's engine, designed by the union economists Gösta Rehn and Rudolf Meidner in the early nineteen fifties, was a pairing that still repays study. Solidaristic wage policy pressed for equal pay for equal work across firms and industries, regardless of each firm's profitability, which squeezed inefficient employers, forced capital toward its most productive uses, and prevented low-wage competition from undercutting the organised core. Active labour market policy then caught the workers displaced by that creative pressure and moved them, with training, allowances, and public works, toward the expanding sectors. Security was thereby attached to the worker rather than to the job, a distinction whose importance the whole comparison illustrates: the Swedish worker could let a firm die without terror, because the state had undertaken to carry him to the next employment. Full employment, compressed wages, rapid structural change, and low inflation were pursued as one package, and for two decades the package worked about as well as its designers promised.

The model's political foundations were as deliberate as its economics. The Swedish party governed in a society where the union confederation organised the overwhelming majority of workers, where a centralised employers' federation preferred one national bargain to a thousand local conflicts, and where an agreement of the late nineteen thirties had established the ground rules of industrial peace between the two. The state taxed heavily and spent visibly, and the visibility mattered: citizens who received child allowances, health care, housing support, and education from the same fiscal system that taxed them could see the exchange, and the party's electoral dominance rested on being the trusted administrator of that exchange. It is difficult to overstate how completely the Swedish case shaped the wider tradition's imagination. For social democrats everywhere, Sweden was the standing proof that the programme worked when it was actually tried, competently and for long enough. For Marxist critics, the same case was the standing problem: a capitalism so thoroughly civilised that the revolutionary argument had to explain why its beneficiaries showed so little appetite for going further. Przeworski's dilemma, encountered earlier, was in large part a generalisation of the Swedish experience, and Meidner's funds were the Swedish movement's own attempt to answer the question its success had posed.

## Britain: the settlement's most contested case

Britain built the settlement's most famous institution and then became the unravelling's most determined theatre, which makes it the comparison's most instructive national case after Sweden. The Labour government elected in 1945, amid exhaustion and debt, constructed in six years the health service, the national insurance scheme, a programme of council housing, and a public sector embracing coal, rail, steel, and the central bank. It did so while demobilising an army, servicing a vast debt, and losing an empire, and the construction's speed and completeness still startle historians who study the administrative record. The settlement that followed was genuinely bipartisan for a generation: Conservative governments of the nineteen fifties accepted the health service, the employment commitment, and the mixed economy, competing with Labour on competence rather than on the framework's existence. That bipartisan acceptance is sometimes cited as evidence that the settlement had transcended party, and so it had, for exactly as long as the conditions of the nineteen fifties and sixties held.

The British unravelling, when it came, was correspondingly theatrical. A Labour government in the nineteen seventies was driven by a sterling crisis to borrow from the International Monetary Fund and to accept spending restraint as the price, an episode the tradition's left read as structural dependence photographed mid-operation: an elected government, committed to expansion, reversed by the judgement of creditors. The decade closed in a winter of strikes that destroyed the government's authority and delivered the electorate to the most ideologically committed administration of the post-war period, which proceeded to abolish exchange controls, defeat the miners after a year-long strike that split the labour movement's morale, sell the council housing stock to its tenants at discounts, privatise the public sector built in the nineteen forties, and rewrite the legal position of unions in successive acts. The British case thus contains the whole argument in one national story: construction by a reforming government, consolidation by its opponents, crisis administered by the tradition itself under external compulsion, and reversal executed with a thoroughness that tested the reversibility prediction to its limit. What survived the test, the health service above all, survived in the form this article's mechanism predicts: as a universal institution with the whole society inside it, defended even by the government dismantling everything around it.

## The German variant: codetermination and the social market

The western German state complicates the comparison usefully, because there the settlement was built largely by the social democratic tradition's opponents, on foundations that included one genuine incursion into the governance of firms. Codetermination, legislated first for coal and steel in the early nineteen fifties under pressure from a union movement threatening a general strike, placed workers' representatives on supervisory boards and a labour director on management boards in the great heavy industries; a later statute of the nineteen seventies extended a diluted parity across large companies generally. German workers thereby acquired a voice inside the firm's constitution that their British counterparts, whose unions bargained from outside, never possessed. Marxist commentary has generally dismissed codetermination as integration machinery, a seat at the table in exchange for the renunciation of the table's ownership, and the dismissal contains its truth: codetermination changed who was consulted, not who owned, and its decades of operation coincided with the German economy's thorough integration into world markets. Yet the institution also changed the texture of German industrial life, the information available to workforces, and the conduct of restructuring, and it survived the unravelling decades with its legal basis essentially intact, protected, like the health services elsewhere, by having been constituted as part of the industrial constitution rather than as a policy of any government.

The social market economy that framed the German settlement was a deliberate ideological construction, an attempt by its Christian democratic architects to claim the settlement's benefits for an anti-socialist doctrine: markets disciplined by law, a strong currency guarded by an independent central bank, social insurance on the Bismarckian contributory pattern, and a rhetorical commitment to property as the foundation of freedom. The German case therefore tests the comparison's categories. If the settlement could be built and administered by a party that defined itself against social democracy, then the settlement was not social democracy's private achievement but a general equilibrium of the post-war decades, available to any government operating under the period's conditions. That conclusion strengthens one side of this article's argument and weakens another. It strengthens the conditions explanation: the equilibrium tracked the conditions, not the ideology of its administrators. It weakens any social democratic claim to sole authorship of the record. And it leaves the reversibility finding untouched, since the German settlement too was trimmed, its insurance formulas adjusted and its labour market restructured, when the conditions turned, by governments including one led by the social democratic party itself at the century's end.

## Housing: the settlement's most reversed sector

No sector displays the gradient of reversibility more cleanly than housing. The settlement treated housing as a social service: municipalities and non-profit associations built at scale, rents were regulated, subsidies flowed to construction rather than to individuals, and in several heartland countries a third or more of the population lived in publicly provided or publicly financed dwellings of a standard that shamed the private stock they replaced. Vienna's interwar blocks had shown the ambition early; the post-war decades generalised it, and the great estates, for all the later criticism of their architecture, housed a generation decently at rents geared to wages. Housing was also, however, the sector where the gain was constituted most purely as a stock of assets administered by policy, and when the unravelling governments looked for a popular instrument of reversal, they found it ready-made. The British sale of council houses to sitting tenants, at discounts scaled to tenancy, transferred a vast public asset into private hands one household at a time, wrapped the transfer in the language of aspiration, and made its reversal practically impossible, since no later government could propose to expropriate the purchasers. Waiting lists lengthened, the residual public stock concentrated the poorest households, and rents in the deregulated private sector climbed to levels the settlement's architects would have regarded as a return to the conditions they had abolished.

The housing story carries a lesson the reversibility debate sometimes misses, which is that reversal can proceed by conversion rather than by abolition. The dwellings still stood; the tenants, in many cases, still lived in them; what had changed was the tenure, the security, the rent's relation to income, and the asset's availability to the next generation of households in need. A gain measured by the existence of buildings appeared intact while the gain as a social arrangement had been dismantled. The article's insistence on grading survival by function rather than by name was written with housing in mind. It also explains why the sector became the unravelling's emblematic success for its architects: it demonstrated that a determined government could reverse a core settlement gain while increasing its own popularity, by distributing the asset to its current holders and leaving the costs to fall on future applicants who had no vote in the matter and, in many cases, had not yet been born.

## Pensions and the slow reversal by formula

If housing shows reversal by conversion, pensions show reversal by arithmetic, the quietest and in cumulative terms perhaps the largest of the unravelling's techniques. The settlement's pension promise was earnings-related security in old age: a replacement of a planned fraction of working income, uprated as earnings rose, so that pensioners shared in the prosperity their labour had built. Reversing that promise frontally, by cutting the pensions of current recipients, was understood by every government of the unravelling to be electorally impossible; the retired were too numerous, too organised in sympathy if not in unions, and too visibly deserving. The promise was therefore reversed prospectively and by formula. Uprating was detached from earnings and attached to prices, guaranteeing that each cohort of pensioners would fall a little further behind the working population with every passing year, without any single decision ever presenting itself as a cut. Eligibility ages began their long climb. Earnings-related state schemes were contracted out, wound down, or replaced by flat provisions supplemented by private saving, transferring risk from the collective to the individual saver and exposure from the finance ministry to the market. In several countries the funded private pillar grew until the pension system's centre of gravity had shifted from social insurance toward asset ownership, with consequences for inequality in old age that the settlement's design had existed to prevent.

The pension case refines the reversibility test in a way the verdict will use. A hostile government confronting a universal institution does not need to abolish it, and usually cannot; it needs only to adjust the parameters by which the institution's future generosity is computed, and then let demography and compound arithmetic perform the reversal across decades, at a pace too slow to organise an election around. The institution survives in name and largely in form. Its function, the maintenance of pensioners' relative standard of life, is reversed by degrees that no single parliament votes on as such. Defenders of the settlement who point to the survival of the pension systems are therefore both right and wrong: right that the framework stands, wrong that the promise does. The Marxist analysts who predicted reversal by erosion rather than by storm described this case in advance with uncomfortable accuracy, and the social democratic literature's tendency to count frameworks rather than promises is one of the evasions this article was commissioned to refuse.

## Unions: partner in the settlement, casualty of its fall

The organised working class was the settlement's co-author and became the unravelling's principal casualty, and the sequence deserves attention because it connects the record to the reversal by a human mechanism. At the settlement's height, union density across the heartland stood at levels that made organised labour a estate of the realm: consulted by governments, seated on boards and commissions, capable of delivering wage restraint in exchange for policy because its leaders could bind their members to a bargain. That partnership was the settlement's political machinery, and it depended on the industrial structure that sustained it, the great factories, mines, docks, and railways where thousands worked under one roof and one employer. The unravelling attacked the partnership from two directions at once. Legislation narrowed the unions' lawful armoury, restricted secondary action and the closed shop, imposed ballots and notice requirements, and in the British case defeated the movement's strongest battalion in a set-piece strike whose outcome announced that the state would no longer treat union power as a fact to be accommodated. Simultaneously the economy itself dissolved the partnership's base, as employment migrated from the unionised strongholds to dispersed services, small workplaces, and eventually to forms of engagement designed to sit outside employment law altogether.

Density fell, in some heartland countries catastrophically, and with density fell the political fact that had made concession prudent in the first place. Recall the conditions analysis: the settlement rested partly on the strength of the counterparty, on capital's knowledge that the alternative to negotiation was mobilisation. A labour movement reduced to a fraction of its former coverage no longer poses that alternative, and governments and employers adjusted their behaviour to the new reality with a speed that confirmed the structural reading of the old one. The unions' decline is thus not a separate misfortune that befell the settlement from outside. It is the mechanism by which the balance of forces, on which every flow gain depended, was itself reversed, after which the reversal of the gains followed as a matter of legislative routine. The Marxist tradition, which had always insisted that the working class's organisation was the real constitution beneath the paper one, could read the sequence as its sociology vindicated. The social democratic tradition, which had built its politics on that same organisation and watched its own governments sometimes administer the decline, has found the sequence harder to narrate, and its literature often treats union decline as a sociological weather pattern rather than as the dismantling of the settlement's load-bearing wall.

## The defence of the Marxist record, heard fairly

The implementations rule obliges this article to grade Marxist governments by their records, and the same rule obliges it to hear the defence those governments' serious advocates actually make, rather than the defence that is easiest to answer. That defence has three components, and each deserves its weight. The first is the development argument: the Marxist states began from levels of poverty, illiteracy, and industrial weakness that the settlement countries had left behind generations earlier, and they achieved in decades the industrialisation that had taken the capitalist pioneers a century, while carrying the costs of invasion and encirclement that no settlement country faced after 1945. Comparing a Swedish worker's biography with a Soviet worker's without comparing their grandfathers' is, the defence argues, a rigged comparison, and the rigging is the critic's, not the record's. The second component is the encirclement argument: the Marxist states developed under blockade, arms races imposed by richer adversaries, and in the Soviet case a war of annihilation that killed a generation, conditions that deformed their economies toward heavy industry and their politics toward siege discipline. The third is the baseline argument about the settlement itself: that the western record was purchased partly from the surplus of a world economy whose peripheries supplied cheap materials and labour under arrangements the settlement countries administered or benefited from, so that the comparison credits the metropole with virtues financed elsewhere.

Each component contains truth, and the article's verdict discounts the comparison accordingly rather than pretending the two records began level. Industrialisation from a peasant baseline is a real achievement; siege conditions really do deform; the world economy's hierarchies really did subsidise the heartland's comfort. But the defence, heard in full, still does not reach the comparison's centre, for three reasons the advocates themselves have struggled to answer. The development argument explains a lower starting point, not the direction of travel in the mature decades, when the Marxist economies' consumption, quality, and innovation fell further behind economies that had started the century no richer, in the divided-country comparisons the series examines elsewhere, where a single people was split between the systems and the starting point is controlled by the experiment itself. The encirclement argument explains deformation but not its specific forms: blockade does not dictate the collectivisation famine, the camp system, or the prohibition of independent unions, each of which was a choice with alternatives urged at the time by Marxists themselves. And the baseline argument, whatever its force against the settlement's self-congratulation, is an argument about the settlement's financing, not about the Marxist record's content: it could at most narrow the margin of the delivered-outcomes comparison, and the margin, as conceded earlier, is not narrow. The defence therefore mitigates the verdict on delivery without overturning it, and it leaves the coercion ledger, the category with no social democratic counterpart, exactly where it stood.

## The settlement and the wider world

The baseline argument deserves one further paragraph in its own right, because it is the point where this comparison touches the series' wider treatment of empire, and because both traditions have an interest in mishandling it. The settlement's decades coincided with the last years of the European colonial empires and with a world trading order whose terms favoured the industrial exporters. Cheap energy, cheap raw materials, and markets held open by political arrangements inherited from empire formed part of the material background against which European governments could afford generosity at home. Marxist critics have made the point into a general theory of the settlement as imperialism's domestic dividend, and social democratic historians have resisted it as a slander on a generation that also, in several countries, liquidated empires and extended the settlement's principles, haltingly, to the populations emerging from them. The measured conclusion is that the dividend was real but partial: the heartland's prosperity rested principally on its own productivity growth, its reconstruction, and its internal bargains, while drawing a genuine subsidy from the world order's asymmetries, a subsidy whose size historians continue to dispute. For the comparison, the point matters less for grading the record, which stands on either accounting, than for explaining the settlement's fragility. A generosity financed partly by favourable external terms was exposed when those terms turned, as they did with a vengeance in the energy crises of the nineteen seventies, and the governments that had distributed the dividend discovered that the domestic coalition built to receive it had no corresponding plan for sharing its absence.

## Chile and the electoral road pushed to its limit

One case belongs to neither the settlement's geography nor the Soviet record, and the comparison cannot be complete without acknowledging its gravitational pull on both traditions' imaginations. In Chile at the start of the nineteen seventies, an avowedly Marxist coalition won a presidential election and attempted the transition itself by constitutional means: nationalising copper and banks, extending agrarian reform, and raising wages and provision, while leaving the opposition's press, parties, and property otherwise intact. The experiment ended after three years in a military coup of exemplary brutality, and the argument over its meaning has never closed. For the Marxist tradition's revolutionary wing, Chile proved that the electoral road ends where the owners of the old order decide it ends, and that a government which leaves the army, the judiciary, and the commanding heights in hostile hands has rented its transformation on the shortest lease of all. For social democrats, the same case proved the recklessness of attempting transformation on a plurality mandate against a mobilised opposition, and confirmed the wisdom of the settlement's modesty. The series gives the Chilean case its own full treatment in the article on [Allende's Chile and the democratic road](/2018/12/01/allende-chile-democratic-road/), and the present article does not re-litigate it. It notes only that Chile is the limiting case of the reversibility argument: a transformation attempted by the rules, reversed by the suspension of the rules, in a sequence that each tradition reads as the other's confession.

## The state question inside the comparison

Beneath the argument about durability runs an older argument about the state, which the comparison presupposes and should briefly surface. The Marxist tradition, in its classical form, regarded the state in capitalist society as an instrument bound by its structure to the reproduction of that society, so that capturing its offices without transforming its foundations changed the administration of the old order rather than its nature. The social democratic practice implied a rival theory, rarely stated as boldly as it was enacted: that the state in a democracy is a neutral instrument available to any majority, its bureaucracy professional, its army obedient, its central bank a technician, so that the road to transformation runs through the polling station to the ministry. The settlement decades appeared to vindicate the rival theory, since democratic states did in fact tax, provide, and regulate at the movement's bidding for a generation. The unravelling complicated the vindication without reversing it. The state proved available for retrenchment as readily as for expansion, which supports the neutrality thesis in form; but the pattern of what could be expanded and what could be retrenched, and the discipline exercised over both by the owners of mobile capital and by institutions designed to sit beyond electoral reach, gave the structural thesis a second life in subtler forms. The series treats the theory of the state in its own right elsewhere, and the reader who wants the conceptual machinery should follow the state theory link given earlier in this article. For this comparison, the state's behaviour is evidence rather than theory: it behaved, across the whole period, like an instrument that any government could aim but no government could wholly own, and both traditions' stronger claims about it require that qualification.

## The operating system: demand management and the economics of the settlement

The settlement had an economics, and the comparison is incomplete without it, because the economics explains both the delivery and part of the fragility. The doctrine that governments could and should manage aggregate demand, smoothing the cycle, sustaining purchasing power through downturns, and treating mass unemployment as a policy failure rather than a natural visitation, supplied the settlement's operating instructions across the heartland regardless of which party held office. Its intellectual author was Keynes, no Marxist and no social democrat, a liberal economist whose wartime and post-war influence armed reforming governments with a technique that appeared to dissolve the old dilemma: if demand management could abolish the slump, then capitalism's most politically lethal failure, mass unemployment, could be engineered away without touching ownership at all. Crosland's generation built its doctrine on exactly that confidence. The state did not need to own the factory if it could guarantee the factory's market.

The settlement's economics worked while its preconditions held, and the preconditions were the conditions enumerated earlier wearing technical dress: a sheltered currency regime that let governments expand without instant punishment, investment opportunities abundant enough that public and private spending crowded in rather than out, and wage bargaining centralised enough that demand stimulus translated into employment rather than into a wage-price spiral. When inflation and stagnation arrived together in the nineteen seventies, the operating system had no procedure for the combination, and the doctrine's authority collapsed with a speed that surprised its holders. Into the vacancy stepped a rival economics that redefined unemployment as substantially voluntary or structural, money growth as the sole cause of inflation, and the settlement's whole apparatus as the disease it claimed to cure. The unravelling governments governed with that rival doctrine as the settlement governments had governed with Keynes, and the symmetry is worth noting: both traditions of policy believed their economics had dissolved politics, and both discovered that the economics had been a fair-weather vessel. For the comparison, the episode grades neither founding tradition directly, but it grades the settlement's self-understanding, which had mistaken a technique suited to one configuration of conditions for a permanent conquest of the economic problem.

## France: the late, statist, partial variant

France built a settlement with the same materials and a different architecture, and the differences illuminate the comparison's variables. The French social security system was founded in the immediate post-war years on insurance principles, administered substantially by the social partners rather than by the state directly, and layered over a dirigiste economic apparatus in which the state planned investment, owned the great banks and energy concerns, and directed credit with a confidence that owed nothing to social democracy and much to a technocratic tradition older than either party family. The French left's own contribution was divided against itself: a mass communist party commanding the largest union confederation stood outside government for most of the period, while the socialist party, weaker and more rhetorical, retained a Marxist vocabulary longer than any comparable party and practised, on its rare access to office in the period, an administration its own militants found tepid. The French settlement was therefore generous in provision, statist in method, and peculiar in politics: its benefits were delivered substantially by governments of the centre and right, its unions were strong in militancy and weak in membership, and its working class was represented in the main by a party committed, in doctrine, to the revolutionary road it had no prospect of travelling.

The French sequence of the nineteen eighties, the expansion and the turn to rigour already examined as the mechanism of structural dependence, completed the variant's lesson. France demonstrated that a settlement could be built without social democratic hegemony, defended without a encompassing labour movement, and disciplined, when the shelter failed, by the same external constraints that disciplined its neighbours. The case weakens any account of the settlement that rests on the virtues of particular parties and strengthens the account this article has built: a configuration of conditions, an equilibrium of forces, and a set of institutions whose durability varied with their constitution. It also supplies the comparison's most quotable irony, that the country whose socialist party kept the word Marxism longest in its rhetoric was the country where the gap between rhetoric and the administration of capitalism was most frankly displayed, in a presidency that began with nationalisations and ended as the monetary union's co-architect.

## The affluent worker and the embourgeoisement question

One celebrated episode in the settlement's sociology belongs in the comparison because it tested, empirically, a prediction each tradition had made about the other's electorate. As working-class incomes rose in the settlement decades, a thesis circulated widely in journalism and in the labour movement's own anxious discussions: that prosperity was dissolving the working class as a political force, that the affluent worker, with his house, car, and television, was becoming bourgeois in outlook and would vote accordingly, abandoning the parties of his class. The Marxist tradition had its own version of the anxiety, the theory of embourgeoisement through a labour aristocracy bribed by the system's surplus. A celebrated British research programme of the late nineteen sixties, studying affluent workers in a boom town's factories, tested the thesis directly and found it largely false in its simple form. The affluent workers studied were not becoming middle class in their sociability, their aspirations for their work, or their union loyalties. They were instrumental collectivists: attached to their unions as instruments for wages and conditions, voting for their party as an instrument likewise, and privatised in their leisure without being converted in their allegiances.

The finding matters here because it cuts against both traditions' favourite stories. Against the conservative story, prosperity had not dissolved class as a political identity; the settlement's beneficiaries knew which side had built their security and voted their gratitude and their interest together. Against the revolutionary story, the same workers' attachment to the settlement was instrumental and satisfied rather than transitional: they supported the parties of reform as consumers of delivery, not as recruits awaiting a further stage, which is Przeworski's second horn photographed at the level of the individual household. The affluent worker studies thus stand in the literature as the micro-sociology of the structural dilemma. A class that experiences the settlement as a good bargain has no grievance from which a politics of transformation can be built, and its rationality in holding that view is precisely what the dilemma's analysis predicts. When the bargain later deteriorated, the same instrumentalism detached as readily as it had attached, and the electoral foundations of the settlement parties crumbled in the deindustrialising regions with a speed that astonished observers who had mistaken the attachment for loyalty of a deeper kind.

## The settlement's hidden foundation: the household and its unpaid labour

A later generation of scholarship, much of it feminist, identified a foundation of the settlement that its architects had built upon without naming: the unpaid labour of women inside the household. The settlement's model worker was a man in full-time employment whose wage, supplemented by family allowances and services, supported a wife whose unpaid work of cooking, cleaning, nursing, and child-rearing reproduced the labour force daily and generationally at no cost to the fiscal system. The welfare state's celebrated generosity rested in part on that invisible subsidy. Its services assumed it: school hours that presupposed a parent at home, hospitals that discharged patients into family care, an insurance structure that derived most women's entitlements through their husbands' contributions. The Marxist tradition, for all its attention to unpaid labour in theory, administered its own states on a variant of the same foundation, adding the double burden of full-time employment to the household labour it did not socialise either. On this dimension the comparison grades both traditions down together, and the audit belongs in the record because it qualifies the delivered-outcomes verdict at its foundation: part of what was delivered to the model worker was delivered by transferring costs to a population the record's categories barely counted.

The Scandinavian countries, again, travelled furthest in correcting the foundation, and the correction is part of their settlement's second life. From the nineteen seventies the Nordic model rebuilt itself around the dual-earner household: individual rather than derived social rights, public childcare as a universal service, parental insurance shared between parents, and taxation of individuals rather than couples. The transformation raised female employment to levels unmatched elsewhere, enlarged the tax base that funded the services, and created in public care work a new constituency of employees with a direct interest in the welfare state's continuation. Scholars of the model note the elegant circularity: the services that freed women for employment employed women to deliver them, and the resulting political economy proved more resistant to retrenchment than the male-breadwinner variant it replaced. The lesson generalises beyond gender. The settlements that adapted their foundations survived better than those that merely defended their superstructure, a finding the verdict will carry, since it complicates any simple story in which durability belongs only to gains embodied in property. Adaptation, it turns out, is a second road to durability, and the social democratic tradition's best pupils found it in the one heartland where their movement remained strong enough to rebuild what it had built.

## Eurocommunism: the fork revisited from the other side

In the nineteen seventies the fork was revisited from the communist direction, in the movement historians call Eurocommunism, and the episode belongs in this comparison because it was the Marxist tradition's own attempt to cross to the comparison's other bank. The communist parties of Italy, Spain, and for a time France, mass parties operating inside democracies, progressively declared their independence from Moscow, their acceptance of pluralism and alternation in power, and their intention to pursue transformation by democratic consent rather than by the seizure of the state. The Italian party under Enrico Berlinguer travelled furthest, proposing a historic compromise with Christian democracy and building a reputation for municipal competence that made it, for a season, the most plausible governing force of the European left. The Soviet leadership read the development as apostasy, which in doctrinal terms it was: a Marxism that renounces the dictatorship of the proletariat, accepts that it may be voted out, and disavows the model of the existing socialist states has crossed most of the distance to the position the social democrats reached at Godesberg, approaching from the opposite direction.

The episode's fate instructs both traditions. The Eurocommunist parties won respect, votes, and local power; they did not win national transformation, and their trajectories dissolved into the general crisis of the European left, the Italian party eventually refounding itself as a post-communist democratic party indistinguishable in programme from the social democracy its founders had split from in 1921. Read one way, the convergence vindicates the social democratic claim that democratic politics has one resting point and every tradition that submits to it arrives there. Read the other way, the convergence vindicates Przeworski: the electoral road's logic operated on communist parties exactly as it operated on social democratic ones, diluting the project as the price of the electorate, until the parties that had begun at opposite poles of the fork met at the settlement's address and found the settlement itself in liquidation. The article records the episode without needing to choose between the readings, because the choice between them is the comparison's own unresolved remainder, the part of the verdict that the defeaters, stated shortly, are designed to keep honest.

## Decommodification: the scholarly measure of the settlement

Political science eventually gave the settlement a measure of its own, and the measure deserves a section because it converts this article's argument into the literature's terms and shows where the comparison sits in the scholarship. In a landmark study of 1990, the Danish sociologist Gøsta Esping-Andersen proposed that welfare states be graded by decommodification: the degree to which a person can maintain a livelihood without reliance on the market, without selling labour power on pain of destitution, without purchasing care and security as commodities. The concept is the settlement's achievement stated as a variable. At the settlement's height, in its social democratic heartland above all, sickness did not mean destitution, unemployment did not mean the workhouse's modern equivalents, old age did not mean dependence on children's charity or the market's mercy, because rights to income and service had been detached from the cash nexus to a degree no previous order had attempted. Esping-Andersen sorted the advanced welfare states into three worlds, a social democratic world of high decommodification and universalism, a conservative world of status-preserving insurance, and a liberal world of residual, means-tested provision, and the typology organised a generation of research.

For the present comparison, the measure's value is that it is neutral between the traditions in form and devastating to both in application. It is the social democratic record stated in units a scholar can count, and by those units the record is magnificent: the heartland's decommodification stood at levels that made the Marxist states' formal guarantees look, in lived experience, like their poorer cousins, since a right to work enforced by a state that also forbade independent organisation decommodified labour in a sense the concept's author would not recognise, the worker being unable to sell his labour freely yet equally unable to withhold it. And the same measure, tracked across the unravelling decades, records the reversal in its precise technical form: recommodification, the reattachment of livelihood to market performance, proceeded through every instrument this article has catalogued, tightened eligibility, shortened duration, lowered replacement, expanded the private pillars, until the heartland's scores converged downward toward the liberal world's. The scholarly literature and the polemical literature thus arrive, by independent routes, at the same two facts with which this article began: the delivery was real and historically unmatched, and a substantial part of it was given back. A comparison that began in advocacy ends, in the scholarship, as a measurement problem with the same shape.

## The fiscal contract: who paid, and why they consented

One further mechanism underlies the settlement's stability and its later troubles, and it is fiscal. The settlement was expensive, and it was paid for, in the main, by the broad population itself through taxes on wages and consumption that reached levels no previous peacetime state had sustained. The arrangement is sometimes described as the working class taxing itself to insure itself, and the description, while arithmetically pointed, misses the political economy that made the contract hold. The taxes were visible and heavy; the returns were visible and universal. A citizen of the heartland paid a large share of income to the state and received, in the same month, the school, the clinic, the allowance, the pension promise, and the knowledge that unemployment would not mean destitution. Consent rested on the exchange's legibility and on the growth that let the state's share rise without any group's absolute standard falling. Political scientists call the resulting formation the fiscal contract of the welfare state, and its stability conditions were the settlement's stability conditions restated in budgetary language.

The unravelling corroded the contract from both ends. Growth's slowing meant that maintaining the state's share now required someone's absolute loss, turning the boom's politics of a growing product into a politics of rival slices, in which pensioners, patients, claimants, and taxpayers could be played against one another by any government fluent in the idiom. Simultaneously the tax base itself grew mobile: capital and high incomes acquired exit options that wages, taxed at source, did not, so the burden of the contract shifted toward the immobile, breeding the resentment that retrenchment governments harvested. The fiscal sociology thus completes the conditions analysis. The settlement was not merely a set of institutions and a balance of industrial forces. It was a tax bargain sustainable while growth lubricated it and capital stayed home, and when the lubrication failed and the capital left, the bargain's parties began, rationally and miserably, to defect. The traditions' theories both contain this story in embryo: the Marxist version as the fiscal crisis of the state, a literature of the nineteen seventies that predicted the squeeze from the structure of accumulation, and the social democratic version as the permanent problem of consent, the need to renew the contract's legitimacy in every generation. On the evidence, the squeeze arrived on schedule, and the renewal of legitimacy is the task the tradition's survivors are still attempting.

## The Antipodes variant: protection by wages instead of welfare

The comparison's geography has a southern outlier that scholars use to test its categories, and the test is worth reporting. Australia and New Zealand built, from the turn of the twentieth century, a distinctive form of working-class protection that the political scientist Francis Castles named the wage-earners' welfare state. Where the European settlement taxed and transferred, the Antipodean model regulated and arbitrated: compulsory arbitration courts set wages with an explicit needs component, tariffs sheltered the industries that paid them, immigration was controlled to protect the labour market's scarcity, and the state's welfare apparatus remained comparatively residual because the wage itself was made to do the welfare work. New Zealand added, in the nineteen thirties under a Labour government of genuine radicalism, a comprehensive social security edifice that made it for a time the settlement's most ambitious small state. The variant matters to the comparison for two reasons. It shows the settlement impulse, the determination to decommodify the worker's existence by political means, operating through entirely different institutions where the historical materials differed. And it shows the reversibility gradient operating with special clarity, because a protection embodied in tariffs and arbitration awards proved exquisitely reversible when the shelter of a protected market was withdrawn: New Zealand's Labour government of the nineteen eighties, as noted earlier, executed the most thorough liberalisation in the democratic world, and Australia's labour governments of the same decades floated the currency, cut the tariffs, and decentralised the wage system their movement had built over eighty years.

The Antipodean cases also discipline the comparison's party-political moral. In Europe, the unravelling was substantially the work of the right, and a social democratic reader can sustain the story of a settlement murdered by its enemies. In Wellington and Canberra the instruments were wielded by labour governments themselves, staffed by the movement's own economists, arguing in the movement's own vocabulary of modernisation and necessity. The structural reading absorbs the fact without strain: when the conditions fail, the tradition's governments administer the adjustment because the alternative governments would administer it worse, and the choice between administering and opposing is the dilemma's final form. The apologetic reading, in which fidelity to the programme was available and merely betrayed, cannot explain why the betrayal recurred, under leaders of every temperament, in every country where the conditions turned. Structure, again, outperforms biography as an explanation, and the comparison's Marxist analysts are entitled to the point, which they have not been slow to take.

## The renunciations: how the parties rewrote themselves

Between Godesberg and the century's end, the social democratic parties of Europe performed a series of formal renunciations that together constitute the doctrinal history of the fork's second half. The Spanish case is the most dramatic and the least known in the English-language literature. The Spanish Socialist Workers' Party, refounded in exile and clandestinity under the dictatorship, emerged in the transition to democracy still defining itself as Marxist, and its young leader Felipe González concluded that the definition was an electoral impossibility in a country whose middle classes associated the word with the civil war's losing side. At the party's congress of 1979 he forced the question, resigned when the delegates initially refused to drop the Marxist self-definition, and was recalled months later by an extraordinary congress that adopted his terms. The party that then governed Spain for fourteen years, building much of a modern welfare state in a country the settlement had bypassed, did so as a party that had publicly renounced the doctrine in whose name its founders had died in exile and in prison. The sequence is the fork's whole later history in miniature: the renunciation was the price of the electorate, the electorate was the price of the delivery, and the delivery, health, education, pensions, infrastructure, was real.

Sweden's party revised its programme in stages, each revision moving the language of ownership further toward the margins, until the party of the wage-earner funds was describing itself in terms its nineteenth-century founders would have found unrecognisable. The British party's clause, defended in 1960 and surrendered in 1995, has been noted. The French socialists, refounded at Épinay in 1971 with a rhetoric of rupture with capitalism, governed twice and left the rhetoric in the congress hall. Read across the continent, the pattern is uniform enough to count as a law of the tradition's development: the parties survived by ceasing, in their official self-descriptions, to be what they had been founded to be, and the survival was purchased at the price the dilemma specifies. A Marxist reading calls the process liquidation. A social democratic reading calls it growing up. The comparison's colder vocabulary calls it adaptation to the electoral structure of advanced capitalism, and notes that no party that refused the adaptation retained either office or influence, a datum neither reading can afford to ignore.

## The crisis literature: overload, legitimation, and the fiscal squeeze

The settlement's breakdown generated a scholarly literature of its own in the nineteen seventies, and the comparison should register it, because the rival diagnoses of that decade map precisely onto the two traditions' theories. From the right and centre came the overload thesis, given international form in a report to the Trilateral Commission in 1975 whose authors worried that the democratic countries had become ungovernable: an excess of participation and demand, they argued, had loaded the state with commitments its authority and revenues could not sustain, and the remedy was a moderation of democracy itself, a cooling of expectations and a restoration of the distinction between governing and being governed. From the Marxist tradition came two companion diagnoses of greater analytical ambition. James O'Connor's account of the fiscal crisis of the state argued that the capitalist state was structurally required to spend in two directions at once, subsidising accumulation and purchasing legitimation, while being denied by the organisation of property any secure command over the surplus from which both expenditures must come, so that chronic fiscal crisis was not a policy error but a constitutional feature. Jürgen Habermas generalised the argument into a legitimation crisis: a state that steers the economy becomes responsible for its failures, consumes the loyalty it needs in the act of compensating for the outcomes it disavows, and finds its motivational resources, meaning, solidarity, civic privatism, depleting faster than its interventions can replenish them.

Set the three diagnoses side by side and the comparison's structure reappears inside the scholarship. The overload thesis blames the settlement's beneficiaries for demanding too much, which is the social democratic record read as a pathology of success. The fiscal and legitimation theses blame the structure the settlement declined to transform, which is the reversibility argument dressed in systems theory. All three agree on the datum, that the settlement in its classical form had become unsustainable by the middle nineteen seventies; they disagree on whether the unsustainability was a failure of discipline, of capitalism, or of the compromise between them. The subsequent decades graded the diagnoses as they graded the traditions, partially and without appeal. Fiscal crisis became a permanent feature of the states in question, as O'Connor's structure predicted. Legitimation did not collapse, as the stronger versions of Habermas expected; it thinned, into abstention, volatility, and the long decline of the mass parties, a slower pathology than the theory's drama suggested. And governability was restored, where it was restored, by governments that took the overload thesis as a manual, reducing the demands on the state by reducing the state's promises, which is to say by administering the unravelling as a cure. The seminar room, like the continent, ended by confirming each side in part.

## Inflation: the distributional war continued by other means

The settlement's breakdown had an economic signature that the comparison should name, because it reveals the class content of what looked like a technical malfunction. The inflation of the nineteen seventies was analysed by the period's best political economists, the Cambridge economist Bob Rowthorn prominent among them, as a distributional conflict fought through the price level: organised workers pressed money wage claims to defend and advance their share, employers passed the claims into prices to defend theirs, and the state, committed to full employment, validated the outcome by expanding demand rather than imposing unemployment as the arbiter. Inflation, on this account, was the form class struggle took inside a settlement that had abolished the older arbiters, the reserve army of the unemployed and the gold standard's discipline, without replacing them with any agreed rule for dividing the product. The settlement had promised that the division could be negotiated; the seventies demonstrated that the negotiation had rested on growth's surplus, and that when the surplus vanished the negotiation became a war of attrition in which each side's defensive action was the other side's provocation.

The resolution of the inflationary war is the unravelling's economic core. Governments of the new right restored the arbiter the settlement had retired: unemployment, deliberately raised and patiently maintained, disciplined wage claims more thoroughly than any incomes policy, while the legal reconstruction of union power weakened the claims' organisational vehicle. The cost was counted in the deindustrialising regions, in a generation of young workers who entered labour markets that had no place for them, and in the conversion of whole towns from the settlement's beneficiaries into its monuments. The Marxist tradition reads the sequence as its oldest text illustrated: when the compromise ceased to serve accumulation, accumulation dispensed with the compromise, and the state that had posed as the neutral manager of demand resumed its older office of disciplining labour. The social democratic tradition reads the same sequence as a tragedy of errors, in which its own union wing pressed claims the economy could not meet and thereby summoned the reaction that destroyed them both. The comparison notes that the two readings are not equally deep. The tragedy-of-errors account explains the timing; the structural account explains why the errors were available to be made, why the settlement's machinery converted distributional conflict into inflation in the first place, and why the resolution took the form of restoring the discipline the settlement had existed to abolish.

## The third way: the tradition administers its own inheritance

At the century's end the fork produced a final doctrinal formation that the comparison must grade, because it governed widely and because both traditions claim it as evidence. The third way, as its British and German architects named it in the late nineteen nineties, presented itself as the modernisation of social democracy for a globalised economy: acceptance of the market order and of the unravelling's central settlements, including flexible labour markets and fiscal discipline, combined with investment in education, activation in welfare, and a rhetoric of opportunity that replaced the older rhetoric of security. Its governments were not negligible. They raised employment, expanded childcare and early-years provision, introduced or raised minimum wages where none had existed, and directed real resources toward the health and education services. They also consolidated the reversal's structural core: the German Agenda reforms of the early two thousands, enacted by a social democratic chancellor, restructured unemployment insurance toward conditionality and shortened its duration in the largest single retrenchment of the German settlement, executed by the tradition that had built it, against the opposition of its own unions, at the price of splitting its own electorate for a generation.

The third way is the dilemma's purest product and therefore the comparison's most efficient exhibit. A party family that had completed the renunciations, freed of every doctrinal encumbrance, electorally triumphant in country after country at the same moment, used its freedom to administer the inherited order more humanely at the margins while accepting, and in the German case extending, the recommodification that the unravelling had begun. Nothing in the episode suggests bad faith; the architects believed, and argued with sophistication, that the global economy had foreclosed the older instruments and that opportunity was the equality a modern state could still deliver. The episode's grading is therefore not moral but analytical. It demonstrates that the constraint on the tradition was never principally its doctrine, since the doctrine had been surrendered, nor its electoral position, since the position was dominant, but the structure the dilemma describes: governments of the left, in economies they did not own, administering terms set by owners who could leave. The Marxist tradition is entitled to cite the third way as the fork's destination photographed. The social democratic tradition is entitled to reply that the photograph omits the childcare places, the minimum wages, and the hospital budgets, and that a tradition is graded by its record, which returns the argument, as every road in this comparison does, to the two clauses with which it began.

## After the rival fell: the settlement without its shadow

The collapse of the European communist states at the end of the nineteen eighties rearranged the comparison's conditions one final time, and the rearrangement's effects were the opposite of what the victors' rhetoric announced. The western left might have been expected to benefit from the removal of the rival whose record had discredited the very word socialism in the electorates' ears. Social democracy did indeed inherit the field: the communist parties collapsed, converted, or shrank to sects, and the social democratic parties became, by default, the whole of the organised left in most of the heartland. But the inheritance arrived with the prudential condition of the settlement abolished. So long as a rival system stood armed across the continent's divide, the western owners of capital had a standing reason to purchase social peace at settlement prices, and western governments of the right had a standing reason to defend welfare institutions as exhibits in a systemic competition. With the rival gone, the insurance motive for concession vanished, and the competition of systems was replaced by the competition of locations, in which states bid for mobile capital with restraint. The unravelling's second wave, the activation reforms, the pension restructurings, the labour market liberalisations administered so often by the left itself, belongs to the decades of the settlement without its shadow.

The Marxist tradition's own position after the collapse was stranger and bleaker, and honesty requires the comparison to say so. The tradition survived as an analysis in the universities, as a vocabulary in the movements that periodically filled the streets, and as the unacknowledged ancestor of much of the scholarship this article has used. As a political force capable of governing, it had been destroyed less by its enemies than by the record of its own implementations, a destruction the comparison of delivered outcomes has already recorded. What remained was the analytical core, and the core's condition after the rival's fall was paradoxical: its predictions about the settlement's reversibility continued to be borne out by governments of every colour, while its positive programme commanded no electorate anywhere. A tradition can be right about the mechanism and finished as a movement, and the history of the European left since the rival fell is substantially the history of that paradox working itself out in institutions built by people who had ceased to believe the programme and could not refute the mechanism.

## Michels and the iron law: the party as its own evidence

The fork's prehistory contains a study that both traditions have spent a century trying not to have written. Robert Michels, a young scholar sympathetic to the labour movement, examined the German Social Democratic Party, the most democratic mass organisation of its age, with its congresses, its press, its elected officials, and its constitution of internal democracy, and concluded in his book of 1911 that organisation itself generates oligarchy: that every party, however democratic its aims, develops a leadership stratum whose interests in the organisation's survival, and in their own positions within it, progressively displace the aims the organisation exists to serve. Who says organisation, says oligarchy, ran the famous compression of his argument. The German party was his evidence precisely because it was the hardest case: if oligarchy formed there, in a party of the oppressed, armed with democratic ideology and internal elections, it would form anywhere.

The iron law's bearing on the comparison is double and uncomfortable for both sides. Against the revolutionary tradition, Michels predicts the trajectory of its parties in power with a fidelity that the record of the one-party states did nothing to refute: the apparatus that monopolises organisation monopolises truth, and the dictatorship exercised in the class's name settles into the hands of those who administer it. Against the social democratic tradition, the law predicts the trajectory of its parties in office: a leadership stratum whose careers run through ministries and municipalities will experience the movement's founding project as an embarrassment of its youth, and the renunciations catalogued earlier become, in Michels's light, less a series of reasoned revisions than the predictable behaviour of an oligarchy shedding the doctrines that inconvenience its tenure. Przeworski's dilemma, arrived seventy years later by the road of electoral arithmetic, converges with Michels's law, arrived by the road of organisational sociology, on the same resting point: the settlement as the equilibrium of parties that have become institutions in the order they were founded to transform. That two such different methods, wielded by writers of such different sympathies, land on the same point is the kind of triangulation this series treats as evidence, and the reader should weigh it accordingly.

## The employers change their minds: the defection of Swedish capital

The settlement's bargains required two signatories, and the unravelling in its heartland began when one of them withdrew its signature. Swedish capital had accepted centralised bargaining for half a century because the arrangement served it: labour peace, predictable costs, a state that carried the burden of adjustment, and a union movement that disciplined its own side in exchange for full employment and rising wages. From the nineteen seventies the largest Swedish firms, increasingly multinational in their operations, recalculated. Their markets, their production, and their investment horizons had outgrown the national frame within which the bargain was struck, and the solidaristic wage policy that had once squeezed their inefficient competitors now squeezed their own margins against foreign rivals whose labour regimes were cheaper. The engineering employers broke ranks first, negotiating separately; the employers' federation then withdrew from the corporatist agencies through which the model had been administered, and finally from the centralised bargaining system itself, announcing that the Swedish model, in its classical form, was a arrangement its principal private signatory no longer wished to inhabit.

The episode deserves its place because it isolates the comparison's mechanism from the usual suspects. No hostile government ordered the defection; social democracy held office for much of the relevant period. No electoral verdict demanded it; the model's popularity with voters remained high. The settlement in its heartland was undone, in its industrial-relations core, by the private decision of the party whose consent the settlement had always required and whose consent the settlement's design had done nothing to make irrevocable. Here the reversibility argument ceases to be a prediction and becomes a description of a signature being withdrawn from a contract that only one side had ever been legally bound by. The unions and the party had built their half of the model into law, custom, and institution. Capital had built its half into a practice, and a practice can be discontinued at a board meeting. The asymmetry between what each side's consent was made of is, in the end, the ownership argument translated into industrial relations, and it is the single cleanest demonstration the comparison affords of why the form in which a gain is held decides whether it can be kept.

## The Low Countries and Denmark: adjustment by negotiation

Not every heartland country unravelled in the British manner, and the variations grade the argument's finer points. In the Netherlands, the settlement's crisis of the early nineteen eighties, mass unemployment, a bloated disability system used to warehouse redundant workers, and a fiscal position the bond markets watched with open scepticism, was answered by negotiation rather than by confrontation: the Wassenaar accord of 1982, struck between unions and employers with the government at the table's edge, traded wage moderation for working-time reduction and set the pattern for two decades of polder bargaining, in which employment grew impressively, much of it part-time, and the welfare state's generosity was trimmed by agreement rather than abolished by assault. In Denmark, the activation turn of the nineteen nineties rebuilt unemployment policy around obligation and training while preserving benefit generosity to a degree the British path never contemplated, producing the configuration later marketed as flexicurity: easy hiring and firing, generous insurance, and an active state, a settlement renegotiated rather than repealed.

The negotiated cases complicate the reversibility finding in a productive way. They show that where the labour movement remained encompassing and the bargaining institutions intact, adjustment could be distributed by agreement and the core of provision defended, at the price of accepting the new conditions' premises: competitiveness, activation, fiscal discipline. What survived in Amsterdam and Copenhagen was more, and more functional, than what survived in London, and the difference tracks the survival of the settlement's organisational foundations rather than the survival of its doctrines. The Marxist reading absorbs the cases as confirmation that the balance of forces decides, since the forces in the Low Countries and Denmark remained unusually balanced. The social democratic reading absorbs them as proof that the tradition, where it stayed strong, could steer rather than merely suffer the adjustment. Both readings are available because both are true, and the cases therefore belong to the article's governing pattern, the partial confirmation that the verdict will formalise. What the negotiated cases do not show is any route back to the classical settlement's terms: the full employment commitment in its original form, the sheltering of national policy, the expectation of ever-rising provision. The negotiation preserved the institutions by accepting the age; the age was the unravelling's, and negotiating inside it is a different thing from reversing it.

## A biography revisited: the worker of the settlement in the unravelling

Return, for one section, to the worker whose biography this article constructed at the settlement's height, and follow the same life into the decades that followed, because the comparison's abstractions, delivery and durability, are finally bookkeeping categories for lives like his. He retires, let us say, in the middle nineteen eighties, on the earnings-related pension the settlement designed, in the council house he was permitted to buy at a discount in the unravelling's most popular single act, an owner now, with an asset whose appreciation will quietly reclassify his family's politics. His pension arrives whole; its uprating, detached from earnings by a formula change he never heard debated, begins its slow arithmetic, and by the end of his life his income has fallen a noticeable distance behind the working population whose standard the pension was built to track. His widow keeps the house, keeps the health service, whose queues lengthen and whose charges nibble at the margins, dentistry and spectacles drifting out of the free provision he remembered, and keeps, above all, the expectation that the state stands behind her old age, an expectation no government of the period dared to falsify outright.

His children inhabit a different settlement, thinner in every dimension the table measures. The son's apprenticeship route vanished with the industry that offered it; he works in logistics, on terms his father would have recognised as casual labour dressed in new vocabulary, his pension a private account whose value moves with markets he does not follow, his housing a mortgage at a multiple of earnings that would have frightened the building societies of his father's day into refusal. The daughter works in the public care services, employed by the state her father's generation built, on a contract outsourced to an agency the state licenses, delivering to other people's parents the care the settlement once promised as a right of citizenship, at wages the solidaristic policy of another country and another age existed to abolish. Neither child is destitute; both are educated beyond their parents, healthier in expectation, richer in possessions and poorer in security, which is the unravelling's precise signature at the level of the household. Ask the family, across its generations, which tradition's account of the century it recognises, and the honest answer is both in sequence: the social democrats described their father's life, and the Marxists described what happened to it. A comparison that cannot hold a single family's three generations in view at once has no business grading either tradition.

## The geography of grief: the regions the unravelling made

The unravelling was not evenly spread across the map, and its geography became the heartland's defining political fact. The settlement had rested on industrial regions whose factories, pits, and yards concentrated the organised working class and its institutions: the union hall, the cooperative store, the party club, the brass band, the whole dense civil society of labour. Deindustrialisation, accelerated by the unravelling governments' monetary and industrial policies and by the global relocation of manufacturing, dissolved that world within a generation in region after region: the coalfields of northern England and the Ruhr's older districts, the steel towns of Lorraine and Wallonia, the shipbuilding coasts, the textile valleys. What replaced it was not a new economy of comparable density but a thinner landscape of logistics sheds, retail parks, call centres, and public employment, over a substratum of incapacity claims, informal work, and early death that the statistics of the period record with bureaucratic calm. The regions' political trajectory, from the settlement parties' safest territory to the seedbeds of every subsequent insurgency against the political order, is the unravelling's longest shadow, and it falls across the comparison in both directions.

For the social democratic tradition, the geography of grief is an accusation it has never answered: the party of the industrial working class presided, in office and in opposition, over the dissolution of the industrial working class's world, and its successor electorates in the old heartlands dwindled into a coalition of the metropolitan and the credentialed that its founders would not have recognised as their movement. For the Marxist tradition, the same geography is an accusation of a different kind: here, if anywhere, was the immiseration, the discarded population, the reserve army made permanent, from which a politics of transformation might have been built, and the tradition that had predicted the discard for a century found, when it arrived, that it possessed neither the organisations nor the credibility to gather the discarded, whose anger flowed instead into channels neither tradition controlled. The comparison records the double failure without deriving comfort from its symmetry. A settlement that could be dismantled over the heads of its beneficiaries, and a critique that could diagnose the dismantling for decades without assembling the coalition to stop it, are the two halves of the European left's modern condition, and the fork that began in the movement's youth ends, for the period this article covers, in their joint tenancy of the ruins.

## Why the golden age grew: the coordination thesis

The settlement's defenders possess one scholarly argument that this article has not yet given its due, and fairness requires it, because it converts the settlement from a distribution of growth's proceeds into a cause of the growth itself. The economic historian Barry Eichengreen, in a body of work that reorganised the literature on the post-war boom, argued that the golden age's exceptional growth was substantially an institutional achievement: the settlement's machinery, encompassing unions, employers' associations, and governments bound in repeated negotiation, solved the coordination problems that had wrecked the interwar economy. Unions that could bind their members traded wage moderation for investment; employers who believed the moderation would hold invested at rates that made the moderation rational; governments underwrote the bargain with full employment and the welfare floor. On this account the settlement was not a tax on the boom but one of its engines, a set of commitments that let every party make promises the others could bank. The Marxist conditions analysis and the coordination thesis agree on the machinery and differ on the moral: where the one sees an equilibrium of forces resting on temporary conditions, the other sees a technology of trust that wise societies might have maintained.

The unravelling, read through the coordination thesis, becomes a story of the technology's maintenance costs rising past what its operators would pay. Wage moderation is a promise about the future, and its price rose as the workforce's composition changed, as the frontier of catch-up growth closed, and as the employers' outside options multiplied with capital's mobility, until the parties to the bargain could no longer deliver their sides of it. The thesis matters to the grading because it rebuts the crudest version of the conditions argument, in which the settlement merely spent a windfall. The settlement helped make its windfall, through institutions that raised investment and productivity growth across a generation. It also, however, confirms the deeper structure of this article's argument, since a technology of trust among organised interests is exactly the kind of gain that lives and dies with the balance and density of the organisations that operate it. When the unions thinned and the employers defected, the coordination failed, not because anyone refuted it, but because its operators had left the room. The engine was real. So was its dependence on the crew.

## The Mediterranean latecomers: building the settlement as it closed

The settlement's geography has a final chapter that tests the comparison's sense of period. Spain, Portugal, and Greece emerged from dictatorships in the middle nineteen seventies, at the precise moment the heartland's model entered its crisis, and proceeded to build, under governments frequently led by socialists, the welfare states they had been denied: universal health systems where provision had been patchy and occupational, mass secondary and university education, pension systems extended across populations that had relied on family and charity. The latecomers built with the heartland's blueprints in full knowledge of the heartland's troubles, financed partly by their own growth and partly by the transfers of the European integration they joined, and their construction continued through decades in which the older settlements were being trimmed. A Spaniard or a Portuguese citizen of the late twentieth century experienced, in compressed form, the trajectory the heartland had spread over two generations: dictatorship's scarcity, then the settlement's arrival, then the fiscal weather that questioned its terms, all within one adult lifetime.

The latecomer cases refine the grading in two directions. They show the settlement model's portability: its institutions were a technology that could be installed late, quickly, and to real effect, by societies whose class structures and party systems differed markedly from the heartland's, which supports the social democratic claim that the model answered a general problem rather than a local accident. And they show the model's dependence on a sponsor: the latecomers built under the shelter of European integration, its funds, its markets, and its political conditionality, an external frame that performed some of the functions the vanished conditions had performed for the founders, sheltering national policy and subsidising its costs. When that frame itself turned disciplinarian in the debt crisis of the following century, imposing retrenchment on the latecomers as a creditor's condition, the comparison's oldest mechanism reappeared in modern dress: a settlement resting on a sponsor's tolerance is revised when the tolerance ends, and the populations that had received universal health care as citizens discovered how much of it they held as borrowers. The latecomers thus replay the whole argument at higher speed, and their experience belongs in the record beside the founders', neither as epilogue nor as exception, but as the comparison's most recent full run of the experiment.

## The social wage and the Accord: Australia's second experiment

Australia's labour movement, having watched its first protectionist settlement dismantled by the conditions' turning, attempted in the nineteen eighties a deliberate reconstruction that deserves the comparison's attention as the most self-conscious effort anywhere to rebuild a settlement by negotiation. The Prices and Incomes Accord, struck in 1983 between the labour party and the union confederation whose leader became the government's dominant economic minister, traded wage restraint, delivered through the arbitration system the movement still commanded, for a social wage: tax cuts targeted at wage earners, a national health scheme restored after its earlier abolition, superannuation extended toward universality as a deferred wage held in industry funds, and family payments restructured toward the working poor. The design's intelligence lay in its recognition that the wage bargain and the welfare state were one system, and that a movement which could still deliver restraint could purchase provision with it even inside an economy being opened to the world. For a decade the Accord's machinery delivered both disinflation and a rebuilt floor, an outcome the confrontation governments elsewhere conspicuously failed to match on either count.

The experiment's later course supplies the by now familiar codicil. Successive renegotiations of the Accord traded more restraint for thinner increments, enterprise bargaining dissolved the centralised machinery that had made the trades enforceable, and the superannuation pillar, the design's most durable element, endured precisely because it had been constituted as property, individual accounts in collectively governed funds, a form no successor government could reach without expropriating its own voters' retirement savings. The pattern will not surprise the reader of this article. The Accord's flow elements, its wage norms and tax trades, were revised as conditions moved. Its institutional element, a universal health scheme, survived changes of government in the now predictable manner. Its property element outlasted everything. Australia's second experiment thus reads as the comparison's argument restated by its most ingenious practitioners, who discovered, as the Swedes had before them, that the form in which a gain is lodged decides how long the gain is kept, and who lodged their most permanent gain, in the end, in the one form the argument recommends.

## Norway and the oil fund: the settlement's late experiment in owning

One heartland country conducted, late and almost by accident, the experiment the funds had proposed deliberately, and its result belongs at the centre of any honest grading. Norway's petroleum, developed from the nineteen seventies under a state that took a commanding share of the rents through taxation, direct participation, and a national oil company, presented a social democratic government with a river of revenue that no fiscal contract had ever contemplated. The Norwegian response, legislated as the boom matured, was to convert the river into a stock: a sovereign fund, owned by the state on behalf of present and future citizens, into which the petroleum surplus would be paid and from which only a disciplined fraction of the expected return would enter the annual budget. The fund grew into one of the largest pools of capital on earth, holding slices of thousands of companies across the world, and it did so under rules designed to place the asset beyond the reach of any single parliament's appetite: the principal was not to be spent, the spending rule was cross-party property, and the fund's existence was justified publicly as the conversion of a depleting resource into a permanent endowment.

Consider the fund through this article's instruments and its significance sharpens. Here at last was a gain constituted as property, a vast asset held collectively, whose income underwrites the welfare state's generosity and whose principal no finance minister may raid without breaking a rule that the whole political class treats as constitutional furniture. The reversibility gradient predicts that such a gain should prove the most durable of all, and Norwegian politics bears the prediction out: parties compete over the spending rule's interpretation, none proposes to consume the fund, and the country's welfare settlement, alone among the heartland's, faces the future without the fiscal anxiety that haunts its neighbours. Yet the case cuts the other way as well, and the comparison should record the cut. The fund socialised the rent of a natural resource that no one's labour had created and no domestic capitalist class had owned; it expropriated nobody, confronted no employers' federation, and required no electorate to endure a transition. Norway bought with geology what Sweden could not win by politics. The exception therefore proves the rule in its strict form: where a gain was embodied in collectively held assets, it endured as nothing else endured, and the only road to that embodiment that history actually opened ran through a hole in the seabed rather than through the transformation of an existing class's property.

## Kirchheimer and the catch-all party: the dilemma's organisational form

The dilemma acquired an organisational sociology a generation before Przeworski gave it an electoral arithmetic, in the work of the German-American political scientist Otto Kirchheimer, whose analysis of the western parties' transformation remains the indispensable companion to the comparison. Kirchheimer observed, in the middle nineteen sixties, that the great mass parties of the European tradition were mutating into what he called catch-all parties: organisations that shed their ideological baggage, loosened their bonds with their founding classes, professionalised their leadership, and reoriented themselves from the representation of a constituency toward the conquest of the median voter. The transformation was not a betrayal plotted in smoke-filled rooms. It was a rational adaptation to the same facts Przeworski later formalised, the shrinking and diversifying of the industrial class, the rise of a salaried electorate with cross-cutting loyalties, and the arrival of television politics, in which a party's programme mattered less than its leader's competence in the studio. The catch-all party is the structural dilemma wearing an organisational chart.

The concept grades both traditions without mercy. For social democracy, it explains the renunciations as organisational physics rather than moral failure: a catch-all party cannot carry a doctrine of transformation, because doctrine is precisely the baggage the form exists to shed, and its leaders are selected, by the form's internal labour market, for their skill at not frightening the constituencies the party must catch. For the Marxist tradition, Kirchheimer's analysis poses the mirror problem: the parties that refused the catch-all mutation, holding their doctrine and their class identity intact, were rewarded with the fate the dilemma predicts for purity, dwindling into sects, heritage societies, or museum pieces with excellent archives. Between the catch-all party that governs without a project and the doctrinal party that keeps the project without governing, the European left of the settlement's aftermath distributed itself almost without remainder. The reader should notice that the dilemma, stated at this level, no longer depends on any premise specific to Marxism or to social democracy. It is a theorem about mass parties in affluent democracies, which is why it has outlived both traditions' attempts to answer it, and why the comparison treats it as the closest thing the subject possesses to a law.

## The American shadow: the settlement that mostly was not

Across the Atlantic the comparison casts a shadow that illuminates by contrast. The United States built, in the New Deal of the nineteen thirties and the wartime and post-war decades, a partial settlement of its own: social insurance for the old and the unemployed, a statutory floor under wages, protection for union organising that produced a generation of industrial bargaining, and a mass prosperity underwritten by public investment in highways, housing credit, and higher education. The parallels are real, and the differences are more instructive. The American arrangement never included universal health provision, never rested on a labour party, never achieved the union coverage of the European heartland even at its peak, and was racially segmented in its design and administration in ways that limited both its generosity and its defending constituency. Its full employment commitment was shallower, its public sector smaller, its taxes lower, and its capital, organised nationally from the start and facing no rival system on its continent, correspondingly less concessional.

The American unravelling, when it came, was correspondingly swifter at the level of the wage relation and slower at the level of the elderly's insurance, and the pattern grades the article's mechanism once more. Union density, never high, collapsed toward the private sector's margins under legal and managerial assault. Wages for the typical worker stagnated across decades in which productivity climbed, a decoupling with no heartland equivalent at that scale. Yet the old-age insurance and health programmes for the elderly, the American settlement's nearest approach to universal institutions with mass constituencies, survived every administration that campaigned against big government, defended by the same politics of universal use that protected the European flagship services. The shadow case thus confirms the gradient from outside the heartland: what was constituted as a universal institution endured, what rested on bargaining power and policy stance dissolved, and the absence of the settlement's full form is explained by the absence of its conditions, an encompassing labour movement, a labour party, and a state tradition capable of universal design. A comparison that ignored the American case would risk mistaking the heartland's experience for the nature of things; including it shows how much of the settlement was a local achievement, purchased by local forces, and lost or kept by local constitutions of gain.

## Misreadings to correct

The comparison attracts a stable set of misreadings, and the article collects them here because each one, uncorrected, quietly rigs the grading. The first is the pace misreading, treated at the outset: that the traditions differ only in speed, the one patient and the other impatient, on a road both are travelling. The second is the innocence misreading: that social democracy was always non-Marxist, a liberalism with a human face that sprang fully formed from the brow of the welfare state. Their parties started life as Marxist parties, their founders argued in Marx's vocabulary, and the separation was an event in time with dates, 1919 for the organisations, 1959 for the German doctrine, not a timeless difference of essence. The third is the universality misreading: that the settlement was the general condition of the post-war west, when it was the achievement of a specific group of countries in a specific configuration of decades, with the Mediterranean south, the North American continent, and the Antipodes each building partial or alternative arrangements, and most of humanity building nothing of the kind. The fourth is the completeness misreading, in both its forms: that the reversal abolished the settlement, which the survival of its central institutions refutes, and that the settlement survives intact, which the formulas, eligibilities, and scopes of those institutions refute with equal ease. The fifth is the solution misreading: that the structural dilemma has been solved, by a leader, a doctrine, or a communications strategy, when every proclaimed solution of the past half-century has dissolved on contact with the arithmetic and the risk calculus that constitute it.

### Which misreading does the pace metaphor produce?

It produces the belief that the two traditions want the same thing at different speeds, so that the faster tradition is merely the slower one in a hurry. The record shows a disagreement about what reforms do to the order they reform, and a traveller who denies the road leads to the destination is not travelling it slowly.

Underneath the five misreadings sits a sixth, methodological, that this article has tried hardest to refuse: the habit of grading one tradition by its best decade and the other by its worst. The settlement at its height against the Soviet record at its depth is not a comparison but a verdict in costume, and the reverse costume, the revolutionary promise at its most eloquent against the settlement at its most squalid, is the same trick in the other wardrobe. The implementations rule, applied over comparable periods and stated dimensions, is the article's protection against the trick, and the reader who finds the article's conclusions unsatisfyingly double is invited to consider that the doubleness is what the evidence, protected from the trick, actually looks like. Partial confirmation is not the article's failure to reach a verdict. It is the verdict, and the next sections state it in its graded form.

## The untranslated shelf: a note on sources

A comparison is only as good as its evidence, and this one's evidence has a linguistic geography the reader should know. The German programme documents at the comparison's centre, the party programmes from Erfurt to Godesberg and the theoretical literature around them, are available in translation and have been Englished for generations of students. The Swedish materials are another matter. The wage-earner funds debate, the comparison's single most instructive episode, was conducted largely in Swedish: the union confederation's internal reports, the employers' federation's campaign literature, the party's anguished congress proceedings, and Meidner's own fuller statements exist in English only in summary, in the comparative literature's retellings, and in a small number of translated documents. The Anglophone reader therefore encounters the funds through the accounts of scholars, sympathetic and hostile, rather than through the debate's own texture, and should discount accordingly the confidence with which English-language versions of the episode are usually retailed. The French materials occupy a middle position, extensively translated in the theoretical register and patchily in the documentary one.

### What does the Swedish-language debate mean for the evidence?

It means the comparison's most important case is known to most readers at one remove, through scholars' summaries of a debate conducted in a language few of them read. The episode's outline is not in dispute; its emphases are, and the article has relied on the points where hostile and sympathetic accounts agree.

The source problem has a second layer, which is the traditions' own archives. Social democratic movements were bureaucracies of paper, and their records, congress reports, membership files, ministerial papers, are rich, catalogued, and open, so that the settlement can be studied from inside its own administration to a degree its architects might have found uncomfortable. The Marxist states' archives opened partially and briefly after their fall, long enough to transform the scholarship of their records and to confirm, from their own files, much of what the comparison of delivered outcomes asserts, before access narrowed again in several of the successor states. The reader should know that the grading in this article rests, where it matters most, on evidence the graded traditions themselves produced: the settlement's statistics and its critics' files, the funds debate's pamphlets and the employers' campaign archive, the programmes in which the parties said what they were and the budgets in which they showed what they did. Advocacy flourishes where archives are thin. On this comparison the archives are thick, which is one reason the article's double verdict, unwelcome to both traditions' advocates, is so difficult to argue away.

## War's levelling: the founding the traditions prefer to forget

The settlement's deepest precondition is the one its celebrants least like to examine: total war. The British social policy scholar Richard Titmuss, in his official history of the war's social policy and in the essays that made his reputation, argued that the experience of total mobilisation transformed what populations would accept and what states could do. A society that conscripted its young men, rationed its food by need rather than by purse, evacuated its children into strangers' homes across every class line, and bombed its cities into a common peril discovered, in the emergency, a solidarity that peacetime politics had never been able to manufacture, and emerged from the emergency unwilling to return to the pre-war distribution of sacrifice. The health service, the insurance scheme, the housing drive were, on this account, the peace terms a mobilised population exacted. Nor was the mechanism only sentimental. War levelled incomes directly, through taxation at confiscatory marginal rates, through the destruction of capital and the inflation that eroded rentier wealth, and through full employment imposed by the state's own demand. The great compression of inequality that the settlement is credited with sustaining was substantially accomplished by the war itself, and the settlement's achievement was to refuse, for a generation, to let it unwind.

The point disciplines both traditions' foundation myths. Social democracy's telling prefers a story of democratic persuasion, in which the electorate, educated by the movement, voted the settlement into being; the war's levelling suggests that the electorate had first been levelled by artillery and taxation, and that the settlement ratified a distribution the emergency had created. The Marxist telling prefers a story of class forces in their purity; the war suggests that the decisive force was the state's own mobilisation, which suspended the market's disciplines more completely than any party programme proposed and taught a generation of administrators, many of them conservatives, what a directed economy could deliver. When the unravelling came, its architects understood the lesson in reverse: no emergency would be permitted to re-level what policy had levelled, and the long peace of the market decades was also a long demobilisation of the solidarity the war had conscripted. The settlement, on this reading, was the institutionalised memory of a total war, and it lasted, give or take the negotiated survivals, about as long as the memory's living carriers held power.

## Demography: the silent partner in the settlement's accounts

Beneath the settlement's politics ran a demographic current so favourable that its beneficiaries mistook it for the nature of economic life. The post-war decades combined falling infant mortality and rising life expectancy with the great bulge of births that entered the labour market as the period matured, so that the ratio of workers to dependants stood at a historical sweet point: many contributors, few pensioners, schools full but workplaces fuller. Welfare states designed in that window promised pensions and care on assumptions about the ratio that demography was already scheduling for reversal, since the same bulge would retire together, live longer than any previous cohort, and draw on systems whose contributor base had narrowed as births fell. The point is not the familiar alarm about ageing, which belongs to a later debate. It is that the settlement's fiscal ease, the sense that generosity cost nobody anything absolute, was partly a demographic dividend, a one-generation transfer from a fortunate age structure, and the dividend's expiry belongs in the list of the settlement's conditions alongside reconstruction and capital controls.

Neither tradition's theory had much to say about this, and the omission is itself a finding. Marxist analysis, organised around production and class, treated the age structure as backdrop. Social democratic administration, organised around the next budget, treated it as somebody else's problem, although the Swedish system's actuaries saw the arithmetic early and said so in print. When the bills arrived, in the pension restructurings and retirement-age increases that run through the unravelling's second wave, they were administered as technical necessities by governments of both traditions, and the electorates' resentment at the discovery that the settlement's promises had been priced on a vanished demography fed the same anti-political currents that the geography of grief had already dug. The comparison records the demographic condition in the same spirit as the others: not as an excuse that acquits anyone, but as a reminder that the settlement was an achievement of a particular moment in the life of populations as well as of classes, and that its architects, like its critics, were luckier in their moment than either understood.

## The New Left's indictment: the settlement as administered society

The settlement was attacked in its own heyday from a direction neither founding tradition anticipated, and the attack belongs in the comparison because it identified, early, the discontents that the unravelling's politics would later harvest in a different idiom. The generation of 1968, students and young workers across the heartland and beyond, indicted the settlement not for failing to deliver but for the character of its delivery: a society administered from above, in which welfare arrived as bureaucracy, work remained stultifying beneath its new securities, consumption was stage-managed by advertisers, and the parties of the left had become managers of the very order their founders had promised to transcend. Herbert Marcuse, the Frankfurt School's emissary to the American campus, gave the indictment its theory, arguing that advanced industrial society absorbed its opposition by delivering goods, that the working class had been integrated by refrigerators and motor cars into the system its grandfathers had expected it to bury. The settlement's own children, raised in its schools and universities, were telling it that security without freedom of a more radical kind, freedom from boredom, from hierarchy, from the administered life, was a gilded cage.

The indictment was unfair in the way of generational indictments, and its unfairness is easy to document: the cage's bars were the health service and the pension, and the grandchildren of the uninsured were complaining about the quality of a security their grandparents would have regarded as miraculous. But the New Left saw two things clearly that the founding traditions, in their different ways, had missed. It saw that the settlement's legitimacy rested on delivery alone and would therefore not survive delivery's interruption, a prediction the unravelling verified when electorates raised inside the settlement declined to defend it as an ideal rather than as a service. And it saw that the labour movement's own institutions had become part of the administration, their leaders invested in the machinery they had won, which is Michels's law returning in the idiom of the seminar. The Marxist tradition absorbed the indictment selectively, keeping the critique of consumer integration and forgetting the critique of its own administered states, where the cage had no gilding. The social democratic tradition absorbed it hardly at all, and met its own children, in the party conferences of the following decades, as a management meets a shareholders' revolt. Both absorptions were failures of self-knowledge of the kind the verdict has already graded.

## Christian democracy: the settlement's other parent

The comparison has spoken of the settlement as social democracy's achievement, with corrections, and a further correction is owed to the tradition that co-authored it across much of the continent. Christian democracy, the party family that governed Germany, Italy, the Low Countries, and Austria for much of the settlement period, brought to the construction its own doctrine: Catholic social teaching, with its principles of solidarity and subsidiarity, its defence of the family wage and of intermediate associations, and its suspicion equally of the unregulated market and of the collectivist state. The German social market, the Dutch pillarised welfare institutions, the Austrian partnership of the camps, all bear the family's fingerprints alongside the labour movement's. The settlement in its continental form was a condominium, and its architects included men who regarded social democracy as an adversary to be outbid rather than a partner to be thanked, which returns the argument to the conditions analysis: the bidding was driven by competition for the working-class electorate, in which the communist alternative stood as the standing threat that concentrated every bidder's mind.

For the comparison, Christian democracy's role performs a useful experiment. Here was a tradition with no Marxist past, no doctrine of transformation, and no intention of touching ownership beyond a bounded public sector, which nonetheless built and defended welfare institutions of settlement scale for a generation. Its participation confirms that the settlement was an equilibrium of the age rather than the property of a doctrine. And its subsequent trajectory confirms the equilibrium's fragility from the other side: the Christian democratic parties, their confessional electorates dissolving and their doctrine thinning into a general conservatism, administered their share of the unravelling with no more resistance than their rivals, and in the German case supplied the chancellor under whom the settlement's monetary guardian, the Bundesbank's discipline, was elevated into a continental constitution. The condominium's other parent, it turned out, had also been renting.

## The end of ideology: the settlement's self-image at high tide

At the settlement's high tide, its most fashionable self-description was that ideology itself had ended. The American sociologist Daniel Bell gave the thesis its canonical form at the close of the nineteen fifties: the great nineteenth-century ideologies, Marxism above all, had exhausted themselves in the west, he argued, because the questions they answered had been settled by the mixed economy's success, and politics had become a matter of incremental adjustment among technicians who agreed on the framework. The thesis was the settlement's complacency dressed as sociology, and it deserves a paragraph in this comparison because both traditions' later embarrassments are visible in it. Bell was right that the revolutionary project had lost its western audience, right that the settlement framework commanded a consensus that crossed party lines, and wrong in the way that matters most: he mistook the consensus for a permanent property of affluent societies rather than a fair-weather formation of the conditions this article has enumerated, and the decade after his book appeared supplied the correction, in the streets as much as in the seminar rooms.

The Marxist tradition's response to the end-of-ideology thesis was to denounce it as itself ideological, the ideology of the satisfied, and the denunciation was correct and insufficient. It was correct because the claim to have transcended ideology is the oldest ideological manoeuvre in the archive. It was insufficient because the tradition's own house contained the same complacency in a different register, the assumption that history's direction was settled and the only question was the timetable, an assumption the tradition's states were about to falsify in the most public manner available. The settlement and its rival, at the moment of their joint maximum confidence, each believed the argument was over. The comparison this article has conducted is possible only because both were wrong, and because the decades since have graded their claims in the open, where the records, the archives, and the reversals can be inspected by anyone willing to hold two clauses in view at once.

## The reversibility test applied: grading both traditions

Now apply the article's instrument systematically, tradition by tradition and dimension by dimension, with the comparison basis held steady: implementations against implementations, the settlement decades and their aftermath against the Marxist governments' decades, on the welfare dimensions the traditions themselves chose as their battleground. On delivered improvements in living conditions, social democracy's grade is the highest any tradition in the series receives: sustained compression of inequality, near-full employment held for a generation, universal provision in health and education, rising real wages, and comprehensive insurance, delivered without expropriation and without terror. Marxism's grade on the same dimension, from its own governments, is poor: real achievements in industrialisation, literacy, and basic provision from low baselines, outweighed by consumption's subordination, provision's stratification, and the coercion ledger that has no counterpart on the other side. On durability, the grades invert in direction though not in completeness. The Marxist prediction that gains resting on unchanged ownership would prove reversible receives substantial confirmation: the employment commitment, the tax structure, the public industrial sector, the insurance formulas, and the wage relation's civilising statutes were all reversed in whole or in part once the conditions turned, and the reversal sorted itself by the constitution of each gain with an almost experimental fidelity to the theory. Social democracy's implicit prediction, that democratic majorities and universal institutions would hold the settlement, receives partial confirmation of its own: the flagship services and the insurance frameworks survived in function, battered but recognisable, and no electorate ever voted for the settlement's abolition as such.

### Is the partial confirmation a finding or a fudge?

It is a finding, and the distinction matters. A fudge splits a difference to avoid offence; a finding reports that each side's stated claim met the evidence the other side's history supplied. The delivery claim and the durability claim were both made before the events that graded them, which is what makes the joint partial confirmation information rather than diplomacy.

Grade, finally, the traditions on the quality of their self-knowledge, the dimension on which the comparison's strangest results cluster. Social democracy produced, in Bernstein and Crosland, theorists who described its own practice with a candour its opponents rarely matched, and then produced, in its decades of dominance, a governing class that mistook the settlement's conditions for the nature of the world and was genuinely astonished by the unravelling. Marxism produced, in the reversibility argument and in Przeworski's dilemma, the two most penetrating analyses of the other tradition's situation ever written, analyses whose accuracy its own political extinction did nothing to diminish, while remaining constitutionally unable to apply the same coldness to the record of its own implementations, where its literature still reaches, two generations after the events, for the vocabulary of betrayal and encirclement. Each tradition, in short, understood the other better than it understood itself, and the reader who wants the truth about either should read its rivals. That is not a counsel of despair about the traditions. It is the ordinary condition of intellectual movements, and the series' comparative method exists to exploit it.

## What would defeat the verdicts

This article's verdicts are stated with their defeaters, as the cluster to which it belongs now requires, so that the reader can distinguish a finding from a posture. The verdict that the settlement was rented rather than owned, that its gains proved reversible because they were never secured in ownership, would be defeated by evidence that its central institutions survived the period of hostile government substantially intact in function rather than merely in name, which the record of funding, eligibility, and scope would have to show. The article has graded that evidence as it stands: the health services come closest to meeting the test, the insurance frameworks meet it in form more than in generosity, and the employment commitment and the industrial public sector fail it outright, so the verdict stands as a graded finding rather than a slogan, and the reader who assembles the funding and eligibility record differently is invited to say where.

The brief for this article states the defeaters in a second, complementary form, and both are recorded here so that neither can be quietly dropped. The verdict that gains left on unchanged ownership prove reversible would be defeated by a case in which a comparable settlement was sustained across several decades and through a sustained shift in the balance of political forces without ownership change. No such case has been identified in the literature this article draws on; the negotiated survivals of the Low Countries and Denmark come nearest, and they survive as renegotiations on the new age's terms rather than as the classical settlement sustained. And the verdict that the alternative produced nothing comparable would be defeated by a Marxist government at national scale delivering equivalent improvements durably. That defeater, too, stands unmet on the record examined here, and stating it matters, because it specifies the evidence that would most change this article's conclusions and thereby shows the conclusions to be hostage to the record rather than to the author's sympathies. A verdict that names its own executioners can be trusted further than one that does not, and the cluster's exam guide will teach candidates to demand the same discipline of every comparison they write.

## Verdict: rented, not owned, and the concession that stands

The verdict can now be stated in its full, double form. The settlement's own authors, in their tradition's later and more reflective writing, came to judge their achievement in terms this article has borrowed: as rented rather than owned. The judgement was not imposed on them by their enemies. It arose inside the tradition, in the Swedish movement's long reckoning with the funds' defeat, in the British movement's post-mortems on the unravelling it had failed to prevent and sometimes administered, in the Spanish and German parties' discovery that the electorates they had won for delivery would not follow them one step toward security of tenure for what had been delivered. A generation of social democratic intellectuals who had begun by announcing that ownership no longer mattered ended by writing, in effect, the reversibility argument against themselves, with the funds as their evidence and the dilemma as their explanation. When a tradition's own heirs supply the verdict on its central claim, the comparison's work is mostly done, and what remains is to state the grading with the precision the evidence allows and to preserve, against the verdict's momentum, the concession that stands permanently on the other side.

### What did the settlement's own heirs conclude about its security?

They concluded, in their literature and in the funds debate, that what they had built rested on political control they could not guarantee, and that the one device which might have secured it had been refused by their own electorates and diluted by their own governments. Rented rather than owned is their verdict before it is this article's.

The concession that stands is the delivered record, and it stands unconditionally. Nothing in the reversibility finding returns the settlement's decades to the realm of illusion. The children were healthier, the old were warmer, the workers were safer and freer in their daily lives, the students climbed further, and the fear that had organised working-class existence for a century and a half receded, for a generation, to the margins. If the traditions are graded by what they did for the people alive under them, the comparison has a winner and the margin is wide, and the Marxist tradition's attempts to argue otherwise, by appeal to what its programmes promised or what its heartland might have done under kinder stars, are the least honourable pages in its literature. The reversibility finding grades a different thing: not what the settlement was, but what it was made of, and the answer, that it was made of statutes, taxes, and tolerances rather than of transformed property, is a finding about its construction, not a denial of its reality. A house can be both genuinely warm and genuinely rented. The tenants' comfort is not refuted by the lease, and the lease is not refuted by the comfort, and the whole of this comparison is contained in the discipline of holding those two sentences apart.

For the wider grading of the critiques to which this article belongs, the reader should place the comparison where the series places it: inside the family of assessments gathered in the [critiques of Marxism complete guide](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/), answered from the other side in the article on the [Marxist replies to the standard critiques](/2019/12/01/marxist-replies-to-critiques/), and reopened, for the present age's version of the question, in the article asking [whether Marxism is still relevant](/2022/02/15/is-marxism-still-relevant/). The present article's contribution to that family is the insistence that the social democratic comparison is the one case where the critique and the reply each possess a record, and where the records, honestly kept, confirm each side's central claim in part. The fork that opened in a shared movement in the eighteen nineties, over a proposition about durability that neither side could then test, has now been tested by the only laboratory available, the twentieth century itself. The laboratory's report is double, and this article has declined, on principle and on evidence, to make it single.

## The settlement's boundaries: who stood outside the warmth

The delivered record, graded at its true size in this article's early sections, requires one final qualification that the traditions' own literatures were slow to supply: the settlement's warmth had boundaries, and the populations outside them experienced the golden age differently. The heartland's labour shortage drew millions of migrant workers into its factories, from the Mediterranean's villages to Germany's factories under formal guest worker programmes, from the Caribbean and South Asia to Britain's mills and hospitals, from the former colonies to the metropoles that had ruled them. These workers built the settlement's infrastructure, staffed its health services, and paid its taxes, while standing, in varying degrees, outside its political and social citizenship: housed in hostels and the worst of the private stock, concentrated in the jobs the domestic workforce was vacating, their residence contingent, their families divided by immigration law, their presence treated by the settlement's own labour movements with an ambivalence that ranged from organising solidarity to a defence of the insiders' queue. The welfare state's universalism, so impressive within the national frame, stopped at the frame's edge, and the edge was not an accident of drafting. A settlement financed by a national fiscal contract and legitimised by a national solidarity had structural reasons to define its beneficiaries nationally, reasons the migrant's payslip could not answer.

The boundary matters to the comparison for more than completeness. The unravelling's politics drew heavily on the boundary's existence: the claim that the welfare state's generosity was being consumed by outsiders, however the statistics graded it, became the solvent in which the old solidarities dissolved fastest, and the parties of the settlement found themselves defending universal institutions before electorates increasingly invited to imagine them as a national patrimony under siege. The Marxist tradition, which possessed in its internationalism the vocabulary for this failure, had its own version of it to confess, since its states policed their boundaries and stratified their provisions with no greater generosity and considerably more coercion. Neither tradition built the transnational solidarity its rhetoric celebrated, and the populations who crossed the boundaries paid for the omission in both systems. A grading of the settlement that confined itself to its insiders would repeat the founding error. The warmth was real. So was the wall.

## The eastern mirror: the comparison as the Cold War's domestic argument

For four decades the comparison conducted in this article was also a live instrument of statecraft, argued not in journals but in exhibitions, broadcasts, and the staged encounters of the Cold War's competition for allegiance. Each system exhibited the other's working class as evidence. The western states displayed their workers' consumption, the cars, the appliances, the holidays, as the standing refutation of the rival's promises, most famously in the staged kitchen debate of 1959, when an American vice-president and the Soviet leader argued the merits of their systems across a model kitchen in a Moscow exhibition, and the appliances did the arguing. The eastern states displayed their workers' security, the guaranteed job, the subsidised rent and loaf, the free clinic, as the refutation of the west's anxieties, and pointed, not without effect, to the west's unemployment queues whenever the western economies faltered. The settlement stood at the centre of the western exhibit, and its governments understood its display value exactly: the welfare state was the west's answer to the question the rival posed to every western worker, and its funding carried, alongside its domestic justifications, the logic of a shop window in a contested street.

The mirror's effects ran deeper than propaganda. Western workers' knowledge of the eastern record, fragmentary and filtered as it was, disciplined the western left's imagination for two generations: the revolutionary road's destination had an address, and the address could be visited, written about, and eventually televised. Eastern workers' knowledge of the western record, equally filtered and all the more potent for its illegality, corroded the rival's legitimacy from within, as the comparison between the shop window and the queue became the Soviet bloc's most corrosive private joke and, in the end, one of the solvents of 1989. The comparison's historians sometimes write as though the two records developed in isolation and were graded later by scholars. In truth they developed in each other's sight, each priced partly against the other, each population conducting the comparison in its own idiom long before the seminar room formalised it. The settlement's generosity and the rival's stagnation were not independent variables. They were moves in a single argument, which is why the argument's end, when the rival collapsed, unsettled the winner's arrangements almost as much as the loser's, a paradox the article has already recorded in the prudential condition's disappearance.

## The public sector as employer: the settlement's second workforce

The settlement created, almost without intending to, a second organised workforce whose existence reshaped the comparison's later decades: the public employees. As health, education, social services, and administration expanded, the state became the heartland's largest employer, and its workforce acquired the characteristics of a class fraction in its own right, heavily unionised, disproportionately female, credentialed, and possessed of a direct material interest in the welfare state's continuation that no pamphlet needed to teach it. The unravelling governments understood the fact precisely, which is why their programmes paired fiscal retrenchment with the marketisation of public employment: contracting out, agency status, performance regimes, and the conversion of professional judgement into audited output were techniques for dissolving the settlement's most reliable constituency by dissolving the common conditions that made it one. Where the industrial working class had been the settlement's co-author, the public workforce became its garrison, and the unravelling's longest battles, over hospitals, schools, and the civil service itself, were fought against the garrison's resistance.

The garrison's defence had a mixed record that grades the article's mechanism from yet another angle. Public services proved harder to abolish than public industries, for the universal-constituency reasons already given, but the terms of public employment were successfully degraded across the period, pay restraint imposed by statute and by comparability's suspension, pensions restructured, security of tenure thinned, in a slow-motion version of the same gradient that governed benefits. The public workforce also complicated the traditions' categories in ways neither welcomed. To the Marxist analysis, a vast stratum employed by the state to administer care and education fitted awkwardly into a scheme of owners and workers, and the tradition's attempts to classify it, as unproductive labour, as a new middle class, as the welfare state's own clientele, never settled. To social democracy, the stratum was simultaneously its achievement, its electorate, and its embarrassment, since a party of the public workforce governs as the employer in every dispute with it, a contradiction the tradition's strikes against its own governments displayed in regular instalments. The comparison leaves the stratum where the evidence puts it: as the settlement's most durable human deposit, the living form in which its institutions acquired defenders, and the population whose slow recommodification measures, in household budgets rather than in statutes, how far the unravelling actually travelled.

## The built landscape: new towns, suburbs, and the architecture of the golden age

The settlement had a physical form, and its remains still organise the heartland's daily life, so the comparison should walk its streets for one section. Around the old industrial cities rose the new towns and the great estates, the tower blocks and the garden suburbs, built by municipalities and development corporations at a pace and scale that assumed, as a matter of course, that housing the population was a public function. The architecture was argued over then and has been prosecuted since, the towers indicted for their alienation and their shoddy systems building, the estates for their monotony, and some of the indictment is just. But the prosecution usually omits the counterfactual the buildings replaced: the slum courts, the shared privies, the rooms let by the night, the tuberculosis wards of the pre-war cities, which the settlement's bulldozers cleared by the square mile. A generation grew up in the new landscape taking its decencies for granted, hot water, a bathroom of one's own, a school within walking distance, a clinic at the estate's centre, and the taking for granted was the settlement's cultural signature, the conversion of its achievements into the unnoticed furniture of ordinary life.

The landscape's fate in the unravelling recapitulates the article's whole argument in concrete and brick. The best of the stock was sold to its tenants and passed into the ordinary market, where it performs still. The worst was residualised, concentrated with the households the labour market had discarded, and in several countries eventually demolished in televised explosions that served as the era's monuments in reverse. The new towns aged into ordinary suburbs whose residents defend, with great tenacity, the amenities the founding plans provided, an afterlife of the universal constituency in municipal form. And the housing shortage that returned to the heartland's prosperous cities, as construction's public engine was switched off and the market declined to replace it at the required scale or price, stands as the landscape's final commentary: a function the settlement had performed as a matter of course proved, on its withdrawal, to have been a function the alternative order could not perform at all, which is the delivered record's argument preserved in the built environment for anyone who cares to read a skyline as a text.

## The dress rehearsal: the people's home and the crisis programme

The settlement had a dress rehearsal, and it was Swedish. When the Swedish party took office in 1932, in the depths of the depression, it governed with a programme that anticipated the post-war settlement's economics by a decade: counter-cyclical public works financed by borrowing, defended theoretically by the economists of the Stockholm school, who reached the logic of demand management by their own road at the same time as Keynes, and framed politically by Per Albin Hansson's celebrated image of the people's home, a society ordered as a good household in which no member is a stepchild and the strong do not live off the weak. The folkhemmet metaphor deserves the comparison's attention because it performed the tradition's central translation in a single image: the solidarity of the class movement re-described as the solidarity of the nation, the workers' claim re-presented as the household's duty, in a vocabulary that the farmers with whom the party governed, and the middle strata it needed, could inhabit without conversion. The crisis programme worked, on its own terms and at its own scale: unemployment was fought rather than endured, the party's tenure lengthened into the long dominance that built the model, and the lesson the European movement drew, that a reforming government could master a slump by spending, became one of the settlement's founding convictions.

The rehearsal also foreshadowed the settlement's limits with a fidelity the later history would confirm. The crisis programme operated inside a small, open economy whose room for manoeuvre depended on the world's demand for its timber, ore, and engineering; its success was a success of management within conditions, not a repeal of conditions. The people's home, for all its warmth, was a national household, and its solidarity stopped, as the later section on boundaries recorded, at the household's door. And the party that learned to govern in 1932 learned the governing temperament so thoroughly that the transformation its programme still named receded, decade by decade, into the ceremonial parts of its congresses, long before Godesberg made the recession official elsewhere. Sweden's rehearsal thus contains the settlement entire, in embryo and in miniature: the economics, the national framing, the delivery, and the slow substitution of administration for transformation that the dilemma would later formalise. The comparison knows no better single paragraph of evidence that the fork's outcome was overdetermined, visible in the tradition's most successful national case from the moment of its first triumph.

## Two utopias: the report and the realm of freedom

Every tradition in this comparison carried an image of the society it ultimately wanted, and the images repay a side-by-side reading, because they explain what each side's supporters heard in the word socialism when the century began. The Marxist image, at its most eloquent, was of a realm of freedom beyond necessity: production organised by associated producers, the working day shrunk by productivity's advance to a fraction of its industrial length, and human energy released into the development of capacities for their own sake, art, science, love, and idleness dignified as creation. The image's power over generations of militants should not be underestimated by anyone grading the tradition's appeal: it promised not merely more goods but a different relation to time itself, the abolition of the condition in which life is spent earning the right to live. The social democratic image, at its most eloquent, was humbler and, its holders would say, more honest: the Beveridge report of 1942, the settlement's founding bestseller, promised a society that had abolished the five giants of want, disease, ignorance, squalor, and idleness, a Britain, and by extension a Europe, in which no citizen need fear the classic catastrophes and every child could climb as far as talent and effort reached. It promised security, not transfiguration; a floor, not a heaven.

The comparison of the utopias grades the traditions in a dimension the records cannot reach, and the grading is not one-sided. The Marxist image named a dissatisfaction that the settlement never answered and that returned, in the New Left's indictment, as the complaint of the settlement's own children: that a life of secure consumption inside unchanged hierarchies of work and command is not yet freedom, however warm the house and however reliable the pension. The social democratic image named a discipline the revolutionary tradition never learned: that a promise is a debt, that the giants could actually be slain by identifiable instruments within identifiable budgets, and that a utopia which cannot specify its first five years of administration is a poem rather than a programme. The century graded the poems and the programmes together. The programme was enacted and partly repealed. The poem was never enacted, and survives unenacted, which is why it can still be quoted without embarrassment at commemorations, while the programme's surviving clauses must be defended with budgets. A reader deciding what to carry from this comparison might carry exactly that asymmetry: the tradition that promised less delivered more and kept less of it than it hoped, and the tradition that promised everything delivered, where it ruled, something its own poets would have crossed the street to avoid, and kept its poem.

## Elections as the century's running tally

One crude instrument has graded the two traditions continuously throughout the period, and the comparison should consult it before closing its evidence: the ballot box. Across the heartland's century, the combined electorate of the two traditions traced an arc that no commentary can argue with. The social democratic parties rose from insurgency to become, in country after country, the natural parties of government, their vote consolidating around the settlement they administered and holding, through the unravelling's first decades, with a resilience that astonished their enemies: electorates punished them for particular failures and returned them as the default custodians of the institutions they had built. The western communist parties traced the inverse arc in its two variants: the French and Italian parties commanded, for a generation after the war, the allegiance of a quarter or more of their nations' voters, a mass base built on resistance credentials and municipal competence, and then declined, in France toward marginality, in Italy toward the dissolution and refoundation already described, as their eastern sponsor's record became indefensible and their own electorates aged, suburbanised, and defected. The tally's cruellest entry belongs to the unravelling's later decades, in which the social democratic vote itself began its long erosion in the old industrial heartlands, not toward the revolutionary left, which had nothing to receive it, but toward abstention and toward insurgent parties of an entirely different family, whose rise lies outside this article's period and whose existence registers, in the tally's cold arithmetic, the settlement parties' loss of the constituencies the settlement was built by and for.

The tally grades the dilemma one final time. A tradition whose theory required the working class's growing revolutionary temper was refuted by the returns for a hundred consecutive years, in free elections whose fairness its own participation certified. A tradition whose practice required only the electorate's continued preference for competent custodianship was sustained by the returns for two generations and then undermined by the same mechanism, as the class whose custodianship it was dissolved beneath it and the catch-all competition for the remaining electorate dissolved the distinctiveness that had made the custodianship worth voting for. The ballot box is a blunt instrument and it misses everything this article has said about coercion, about baselines, about the populations outside the franchise's warmth. But it is the one instrument both traditions agreed to be judged by when they chose, the one in 1919 and the other across the whole century, to seek power through it or against it. Its running verdict is the comparison's last piece of evidence, and it points the same way as the rest: toward a split decision, delivered by the only jury the subject allows.

## The long preparation: how the unravelling was thought before it was done

The unravelling is sometimes narrated as an improvisation, a series of governments reacting to crises with the instruments nearest to hand, and the narration understates the event's intellectual preparation in a way the comparison should correct. The ideas that governed the unravelling had been assembled over the same decades as the settlement itself, by a network that began in deliberate exile from the age's consensus: the society founded at Mont Pèlerin in 1947, where Hayek gathered the economists and philosophers who refused the settlement's premises, and the institutes that followed in its image, patiently funded, publishing, placing students, and waiting. For thirty years the network was a curiosity, its members treated by the governing consensus as survivals from a buried world. What they built in the wilderness was a complete alternative operating system for the state: a theory of inflation as monetary indiscipline, of unemployment as the price of interference, of unions as monopolies, of public enterprise as waste by definition, and a cadre of the convinced distributed through universities, journalism, and eventually the parties of the right. When the settlement's economics failed in the nineteen seventies, the alternative was not improvised. It was taken off a shelf where it had been kept, dusted and argued over, for a generation.

The comparison should draw the lesson the episode offers about its own subject, because the lesson cuts across the fork. The settlement's traditions believed that governing was a matter of holding office and administering well, and their intellectual life thinned accordingly, into programme drafting and the management of congresses. The network that undid them believed that governing was a matter of holding the premises from which officeholders, of whatever party, would reason, and invested accordingly, in the slow media of books, institutes, and the formation of a generation. When the crisis came, the side with the prepared premises won the interpretation of the crisis, and the interpretation governed the response more than the response's authors, several of whom were administering settlements they had been elected to defend. There is a Marxist moral available here, about the materiality of ideas and the superstructure's revenge, and the tradition's survivors have not been slow to draw it. There is also a social democratic moral, about the cost of treating thought as a luxury of opposition. The comparison records both, and notes that the settlement's intellectual disarmament, its conversion from a doctrine into a technique, was complete before the first unravelling government took office, which is one more reason the resistance, when it came, was fought with the weapons of nostalgia rather than with a counter-theory of the age.

## Care in old age: the settlement's last construction

One field of provision continued to expand even as the unravelling proceeded elsewhere, and its behaviour under pressure refines the article's findings in a useful direction. Long-term care for the very old, the slow catastrophe of dementia, frailty, and the exhaustion of family carers, arrived on the settlement's agenda late, because the demographic structure that made it a mass experience arrived late. The responses, where they came, were built in the settlement's classical idiom: Germany legislated a long-term care insurance in the middle nineteen nineties, a new branch of the social insurance edifice, contributory, universal in coverage, and administered through the sickness funds, as though the unravelling decades were a rumour the legislators had not heard. The Scandinavian municipalities extended their service model into domiciliary care on a scale that made the state the old person's principal carer. The new construction was real, and its timing is the datum: even in the age of retrenchment, a need that arrived universal, undeniable, and terrifying to every family that contemplated it could still summon the settlement's instruments into being, because the constituency for care in old age is, in the end, everyone who expects to live.

Yet the care field also displays the unravelling's techniques in their most intimate form. The new insurance was designed with its benefits capped below the cost of the need, so that families and the market retained the margin; the services were delivered, increasingly, by a low-paid and heavily migrant workforce whose own securities were the thinnest in the welfare state; and the family carer, usually a daughter, remained the system's uncounted foundation, her labour the subsidy on which the formal accounts balanced, exactly as her grandmother's had been in the settlement's first design. The care case therefore closes the comparison's evidence where the household section opened it: the settlement's achievements were genuine, its boundaries were real, and its last construction reproduced both in a single institution. A tradition grading itself honestly would find in long-term care the proof that its instruments still worked when the need was undeniable, and the proof that the conditions for using them, a universal constituency and an unanswerable need, had narrowed to the catastrophes no politics could talk its way around. The welfare state did not end. It retreated, over the decades this article covers, toward the ground where its necessity could not be denied, and held that ground with the old tenacity. What that ground is, and whether it can be held or extended, is the form in which the comparison's question survives into a century this article does not enter.

## The communists inside the settlement: an irony of authorship

The comparison's authorship question has one more twist, and it is the kind of fact that rearranges a reader's mental furniture. In France, the social security system founded in 1945, the settlement's French cornerstone, was designed and driven through by a communist minister: Ambroise Croizat, a metalworker and former union leader serving in the post-liberation government, whose name the French labour movement still attaches to the edifice it calls, simply, la Sécu. The French communists participated in government in the liberation years, held ministries, administered reconstruction, and left office in 1947 as the Cold War's alignments froze, having co-authored the most durable institution of the republic they were pledged, in doctrine, to transcend. In Italy, the communist party spent the post-war decades as the settlement's shadow government in the regions it controlled, building in its red belt a standard of municipal provision, childcare, health, and cultural life that visitors studied as the Italian road's shop window, while the national settlement was constructed by its Christian democratic adversaries. The western Marxist parties, in other words, did not merely oppose the settlement from outside. Where they were strong, they built parts of it, administered parts of it, and derived their credibility with their own electorates substantially from the competence of the building.

The irony grades both traditions' self-descriptions. The revolutionary tradition's account of the settlement as a bribe administered by capital's managers cannot accommodate its own ministers among the administrators, nor its own municipalities among the exhibits. The social democratic account of the settlement as its family's private achievement cannot accommodate the fact that the family's rivals supplied some of its most energetic builders and most of its pressure from below. What the irony actually demonstrates is the comparison's deepest theme stated at the level of personnel: the settlement was an equilibrium of the whole labour movement's strength, communist and social democratic, organised and insurgent, and its terms reflected the pressure of the entire movement, including the part that disavowed it. When the movement's revolutionary wing collapsed at the century's end, the equilibrium lost a component its other component had never acknowledged needing, and the settlement's surviving defenders discovered that the rival whose existence had disciplined capital and frightened centrists into generosity had also, all along, been holding up their end of the roof.

## Retirement: the life stage the settlement invented

The settlement's most intimate invention was a stage of life. Retirement, as a normal, expected, financed period of existence between the end of work and death, did not exist for the mass of the population before the settlement built it. The pre-war worker worked until capacity failed and then descended, through family dependence and the parish and the workhouse's descendants, toward a death that often followed capacity's failure closely. The pension systems of the settlement decades converted that descent into a stage with its own dignity, income, and social script: a boundary at a fixed age, an income bearing a planned relation to earnings, a status, the pensioner, that carried entitlement rather than shame. Life expectancy's rise then stretched the stage from a brief epilogue into a third of the life course, a transformation of the human biography as profound as the century's medical advances and far less celebrated. The three-part life, education, work, retirement, which the late twentieth century mistook for the natural order of things, was the settlement's artefact, designed in its ministries and financed by its fiscal contract.

The invention's fate in the unravelling is the comparison in biographical miniature once more. The stage was not abolished; no politics could abolish it, since its population is the electorate's most faithful segment and its abolition is unimaginable in a way the abolition of, say, an employment commitment was not. It was instead repriced and re-bordered: the boundary age pushed upward year by year, the income's relation to earnings loosened by the formula changes already described, the risk of the stage's financing transferred, through the private pillars, from the collectivity to the individual's investment fortune. The pensioner of the settlement's design held a claim on the community's future product, guaranteed by the community's tax base. The pensioner of the revised design holds a portfolio. The difference between those two holdings is the difference between the two traditions' worlds stated in a single asset, and the fact that the claim form survives, thinned but recognisable, in most of the heartland is the survival column's most personal entry. The stage the settlement invented will not be uninvented. What is contested, in the politics the article's period bequeaths to its successor, is only who bears its risks, which is to say that the fork's question, translated into the idiom of old age, remains exactly where the century left it.

## The administrators: the settlement's unglamorous authors

The comparison has credited parties, movements, and conditions, and it owes a section to the stratum that actually drafted the settlement into existence: the administrators. The welfare state's founding documents were not, in the main, party manifestos. They were civil servants' reports, actuaries' tables, and inspectors' memoranda, produced by officials whose names the public mostly never learned, Beveridge himself being the great exception, a Liberal economist in temporary civil service whose report became a bestseller by accident of timing and clarity. Around such men, and increasingly women, stood the permanent machinery: the insurance actuaries who priced the promises, the labour exchange managers who made full employment an administrative fact rather than a slogan, the medical officers and school architects and housing managers who converted statutes into surgeries, classrooms, and keys. The settlement was, in this dimension, a triumph of the file and the form, and its quality varied, country by country and town by town, with the quality of the administration that operated it, a variable neither tradition's theory had a category for.

The administrators matter to the grading for two reasons. The first is that their existence explains the settlement's bipartisan durability better than any doctrine can: the machinery, once built, was operated by a profession whose ethos of continuity served every government, and the conservative ministers who administered the settlement in the nineteen fifties were, at the level of the office, the same men who had drafted it under Labour, a fact that made the framework feel less like a party's property than like the state's own furniture. The second reason is the unravelling's corresponding technique: a settlement embodied in administration could be reversed administratively, by the reorganisation, the target regime, the efficiency scrutiny, the quango abolished and the function contracted, without any parliament ever voting on the principle at stake. The new public management of the unravelling decades was the administrators' world turned against the administrators' creation, a counter-reformation conducted in the same idiom of files and forms, and its victories were correspondingly quiet, cumulative, and hard to campaign against, since no single reorganisation ever presented itself as the settlement's repeal. Both traditions under-theorised this stratum for the same reason: each imagined history as the work of classes and parties, and the settlement's actual authors, the officials with their slide rules and their draft regulations, fitted neither's dramatis personae. The comparison restores them to the cast, and notes that the durability of what they built, which outlasted the parties that commissioned it and the doctrines that justified it, is perhaps the strongest evidence in the entire record for the proposition that institutions, once competently built and universally used, acquire a life that neither founders nor enemies fully control.

## The settlement's seasons: a periodisation in four acts

The comparison's evidence can be gathered into a periodisation, and students of the subject are entitled to one, since the argument's force depends on keeping the acts distinct. The first act is construction, running from the war's end to the early nineteen fifties: the founding statutes, the health services and insurance schemes legislated, the public sectors assembled, the employment commitment proclaimed, in a burst of institution-building whose speed still impresses the administrative historians. The second act is consolidation, running through the nineteen fifties and sixties: the bipartisan administration of the framework, the rising provision and falling inequality, the full employment that made the whole structure feel like the nature of things, and the theoretical codifications, Crosland's above all and Godesberg's as its party-political form, that announced the framework's permanence at exactly the moment of its maximum security. The third act is strain, concentrated in the nineteen seventies: the conditions' simultaneous failure, stagflation, the fiscal squeeze, the industrial conflicts that the framework could no longer mediate, and the crisis literature, overload from one side, legitimation from the other, that announced the framework's unsustainability before its enemies had won the elections to enact the announcement. The fourth act is the long unravelling and renegotiation, running from the decade's end through the period this article covers: the commitments abandoned, the formulas adjusted, the industries sold, the unions diminished, and alongside the losses the survivals, the services defended by their users, the negotiated adjustments of the strong-movement countries, the late constructions in care and in the Mediterranean, that make the act a story of gradient rather than of collapse.

The periodisation carries the argument's moral in chronological form. Notice where the traditions' confidence peaked: the social democratic codifications of permanence belong to the second act, written from inside the consolidation by men who had no reason to imagine the third. Notice where the Marxist prediction was framed: in the first act and before, by analysts who had watched the pre-war republics and drawn the moral that paper gains on an unchanged foundation are hostages. And notice what the fourth act did to both: it confirmed the prediction's direction while refuting its completeness, since the hostage-taking proceeded by negotiation as often as by storm, and some of the hostages, the beloved services, were never taken at all. A comparison that collapses the acts into a single tableau, settlement good or settlement doomed, loses precisely the information the periodisation preserves: that the same institutions were triumph, furniture, burden, and inheritance in succession, and that grading them requires saying which act one is grading. The verdict this article has reached is a grading of the whole run, and it is double because the run was.

## The residue: what a reader should carry away

Comparisons of this size deposit a residue in the reader that is worth distilling before the apparatus of method and balance sheet closes over it. Carry away, first, the habit of asking two questions of every political promise rather than one: not only what will it deliver, but in what form will the delivery be held, and who will be able to reach it when the ministry changes. The first question is the one campaigns ask. The second is the one history grades. Carry away, second, a suspicion of permanence in both its dialects: the governing tradition's dialect, in which the reigning settlement is the end of history and its conditions are the nature of the world, and the opposition's dialect, in which nothing real is ever achieved until everything is achieved and every partial gain is a snare. The century refuted both dialects with the same evidence, the record of gains that were real and the reversal that was partial, and a reader immunised against both will read the next century's promises with better instruments than either tradition supplied to its own followers. Carry away, finally, the image of the house that recurs through this article, warm and rented at once, because it holds in a single figure the truth that the polemics of a hundred years were designed to keep apart: that a thing can be worth building and worth defending with everything a movement has, and still stand on a lease whose terms the builder did not set. The tenants of the settlement lived better lives than any generation before them. The lease ran out on much of what they were given. Both sentences are the history. Neither is the whole of it.

## A note on method: how this comparison was conducted

The article closes its evidence with a word about its own construction, since the series' readers have learned to ask what a comparison has excluded. Three choices governed. The first was the implementations rule, stated early and applied throughout: governments against governments, records against records, with programmes admitted as evidence of intention and never as substitutes for performance. The second was the refusal of figures for the contested magnitudes, inequality, employment, wages, and public expenditure, in favour of directional statements with their scholarly literatures named, a protocol adopted because the precise magnitudes are themselves artefacts of measurement conventions that the rival literatures dispute, while the directions, the compression and its partial reversal, the employment commitment and its abandonment, are established beyond serious quarrel. The third was the grading of survival by function rather than by name, funding, eligibility, and scope rather than letterhead, without which the reversal's subtlest instruments, the formula and the squeeze, escape the record entirely.

What the method excludes should be stated as plainly. It excludes the counterfactuals that haunt the subject: the settlement that might have been built in the Mediterranean a generation earlier, the Marxist government that might have governed as its programme promised under conditions no Marxist government enjoyed, the funds enacted whole in a Sweden whose employers stayed home. Counterfactuals are the opium of this literature, allowing every tradition to be graded by the history it would have made in a world arranged for its convenience. The method also excludes the moral bookkeeping that totals suffering into a single ledger and declares a winner, because the coercion ledger and the delivery ledger are kept in different currencies and the article has declined to invent an exchange rate. What remains, after the exclusions, is the comparison the evidence supports: two traditions, one origin, a proposition about durability tested by a century of government, and a verdict that arrives, unusually and on the record, in two parts. The balance sheet that follows gathers the grading into one view, and then the article is done.

## The balance sheet: the grading in one view

Set the whole comparison out in summary, dimension by dimension, as the article's sections have established it. On delivery, social democracy stands first in the series: the largest sustained improvement in working people's lives ever administered by elected governments, achieved without expropriation or terror. On durability, the Marxist prediction stands substantially confirmed and the social democratic expectation substantially disappointed: the settlement was reversed along the gradient of its constitution, flow first, institution under attrition, property not at all, with the flagship services' survival marking the gradient's far end rather than refuting it. On self-knowledge, the honours divide strangely: each tradition's best analysts understood the other's situation better than their own, Bernstein and Crosland describing reform's possibilities with a candour the revolutionary literature rarely matched about power, the reversibility argument and the structural dilemma describing reform's limits with a precision the governing tradition has never answered on its merits. On the coercion ledger, there is no comparison to make, and the article has not pretended otherwise. On the question with which the comparison began, whether capitalism's outcomes can be substantially and durably improved without transforming ownership, the century's answer is now legible in full: substantially, yes, beyond the dreams of the reformers' grandfathers; durably, only in the forms that approached property or that universal use made too costly to touch, and otherwise at the pleasure of the conditions, which is to say, on lease. That sentence is the article's verdict compressed, and everything in the preceding pages exists to earn its two clauses and to prevent either from being quoted without the other.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The most analytically precise material in this comparison, and the most rewarding to teach, is the structural dilemma, because it converts a familiar historical narrative into an argument students can test. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Marxism and social democracy?

The two traditions share an origin in the nineteenth-century labour movement and part over a single proposition. Social democracy holds that a democratic government can substantially and lastingly improve capitalism's outcomes through taxation, regulation, and universal provision, while ownership of productive assets stays where it is. Marxism holds that such improvements remain concessions granted on sufferance, because the power to withdraw them stays with the owners of those assets, and that durable transformation requires changing who owns. Nearly every other difference, over parties, programmes, and the state, is a working out of that fork.

### Q: Did social democracy deliver more than Marxism?

Measured by what happened to ordinary lives under each tradition's governments, the answer is yes, and the margin is wide. The Western European settlements of the post-war decades compressed inequality, held employment near full for a generation, made health care and schooling universal, raised wages steadily, and insured the classic catastrophes of working life, all through elected governments that could be and were dismissed. Governments ruling in Marxism's name industrialised and educated from low baselines, but subordinated consumption for decades, stratified their provision, and added a record of state violence against their own populations that has no equivalent on the other side.

### Q: When did social democracy abandon Marxism?

The honest answer has three dates, because a tradition can abandon a doctrine organisationally, practically, and on paper at different times. Organisationally, the break came in 1919, when the movement split into rival internationals over the Russian Revolution. Practically, it came with the movement's spells in office between and after the wars, when no ministry it supplied ever moved to expropriate, whatever the inherited programmes prescribed. On paper, it came at mid-century, emblematically in the German party's Godesberg Programme of 1959, which struck the Marxist framework from its doctrine and accepted the market order as the field of its politics. Each date answers a different version of the question.

### Q: Are the social democratic gains reversible?

In substantial part they proved to be, and the proof sorts itself by how each gain was constituted. Commitments held as policy, such as full employment and the level of taxation, were reversed by simple legislation once governments changed. Benefits were eroded quietly by altering uprating formulas and tightening eligibility. Public industries were sold. Against that, institutions used by the whole society, the flagship health services above all, survived decades of hostile government with their founding principles functioning, and the educational expansion was never structurally undone. The accurate statement is neither that everything was reversed nor that nothing was: the reversal was real, patterned, and incomplete.

### Q: What is the structural dilemma facing social democratic parties?

In the form given it by Adam Przeworski, the dilemma has two horns. A party of the working class cannot win elections on that class's votes alone, since industrial workers are a minority of the electorate, so it must broaden its appeal, and broadening dilutes the programme that made it distinctive. And even in office, the party confronts its own voters' rational caution: a transition's costs are immediate and certain, its benefits distant and doubtful, so workers themselves tend to choose a secure compromise over a hazardous transformation. The electoral road, on this analysis, leads toward a settlement rather than through it, by arithmetic and by preference at once.

### Q: What were the wage-earner funds?

They were the Swedish labour movement's proposal, adopted by its union confederation in 1976 from the design of its economist Rudolf Meidner, to transfer ownership of industry gradually into collective hands. Profitable companies would pay a levy on profits in the form of newly issued shares, voted into funds controlled by wage earners, so that collective holdings would compound year by year until they commanded the great firms, without expropriation, compensation battles, or any single dramatic transfer. Employers recognised the design as a ratchet on ownership itself and mobilised against it on a scale Sweden had never seen. The enacted version was capped, time-limited, and diluted, and the funds were wound up after a change of government.

### Q: What did Crosland argue about ownership?

Anthony Crosland, in his theoretical statement of 1956, argued that ownership had stopped being the decisive question in capitalist society. Control of the great corporations, he held, had passed from owners to salaried managers whose outlook differed from the classical capitalist's, while the democratic state, armed with demand management, progressive taxation, and welfare provision, could reach the substantive goals of socialism by fiscal and regulatory means. Socialism should accordingly be redefined by its ends, equality above all, with public ownership demoted to one instrument among several. The argument fitted the sheltered conditions of its decade; when capital markets later reawakened and disciplined the managers, the separation of ownership from control on which it rested looked considerably less secure.

### Q: Why did the post-war settlement unravel?

Because the conditions that had made it cheap to sustain gave way together. The long boom ended in the nineteen seventies amid monetary disorder and energy shocks, and stagflation dissolved the trade-offs its economic management relied on. Capital recovered the mobility that the post-war monetary order had restricted, so governments lost the sheltered space in which national settlements had been negotiated. The rival system whose existence had made concessions prudent decayed and then vanished. Union density fell as industry shrank. Facing that combination, governments of the right converted crisis into doctrine and dismantled the settlement's commitments, while governments of the settlement's own tradition, in several countries, administered much of the adjustment themselves.

### Q: Can social democracy transform ownership?

The historical record contains exactly one fully designed attempt, the Swedish wage-earner funds, and it was defeated in the manner this article describes: recognised by its opponents as a change in kind rather than degree, campaigned against as a constitutional event, and diluted by its own side before enactment. Lesser incursions exist, codetermination in Germany, bounded public sectors everywhere, but none altered the underlying distribution of productive assets in a way that survived as a ratchet. Whether a differently designed attempt could succeed under different conditions is a question the record leaves open. What the record establishes is that the tradition, across a century, produced one serious design and could not carry it.

### Q: Is social democracy a form of socialism?

The answer depends on which definition of socialism is doing the work, and the dispute over the word is itself part of the comparison. If socialism is defined by who holds the means of production, then social democracy in its governing practice does not qualify, since it expressly renounced that goal in its programmes and never pursued it in office beyond a bounded public sector. If socialism means the democratic pursuit of equality, security, and the decommodification of life's necessities, then the tradition is socialism's most successful historical form. The parties themselves once answered yes under the first definition and later rewrote their programmes to answer yes under the second. The word survived; the referent changed.

### Q: How does the reversibility test grade the comparison between the two traditions?

The test asks of any gain whether it would still be standing, function intact rather than façade preserved, after the ministry that created it had fallen and the political wind had blown against it for a generation. Applied to the comparison, it produces the article's double grade. Social democracy scores at the top of the series on delivery, the improvements it made to the lives of the people under its governments, and markedly lower on durability, since much of what it delivered was held as statute, tax level, and tolerance. Marxism scores poorly on delivery from its own governments, while its prediction about the other tradition's durability is substantially borne out. The test's value is that it forces both grades onto the same page.

### Q: What should a student separate between a gain that is delivered and a gain that is secured?

A delivered gain exists in people's lives now: the benefit paid, the treatment received, the wage negotiated. A secured gain exists in the structure that guarantees its continuation: the institution a hostile government cannot reach, the right embedded deeply enough to defend itself, in the limit the changed ownership of the assets from which the gain flows. Students go wrong by treating delivery as proof of security, because a gain can be entirely real and entirely revocable at once, as a warm house can be genuinely warm and genuinely rented. In an answer, the separation earns its marks when it is applied to cases, gain by gain, rather than announced as a slogan.

### Q: Why do gains that leave ownership unchanged face a distinctive risk?

Because everything about them remains within reach of ordinary politics. A benefit level can be lowered by the same kind of statute that raised it; a tax can be cut by the majority that once raised it; a policy commitment can be abandoned by a successor's speech. The owners of the underlying assets, whose cooperation the gains depended on, retain both the motive to press for reversal when conditions favour them and the structural leverage to obtain it, since governments must keep their confidence to keep investment and employment. A gain embodied in a changed distribution of assets faces no equivalent exposure, because reversing it would require expropriating property, the one act the legal order exists to prevent. The risk is distinctive because it is constitutional, not incidental.

### Q: How does the record and the reversal table order the policy areas?

The five rows follow the sequence in which the settlement built them, beginning with how incomes and wealth were spread, proceeding through work, and closing with the insurance system. Across each row run four judgements: the height the settlement reached, the portion still standing after hostile decades, the portion dismantled, and the alteration in who holds productive assets that would have put the achievement out of a successor government's ordinary reach. The arrangement lets the reader trace the argument's gradient without commentary, since the rows where provision was embodied in a public service hold their ground in the retention column, while the rows resting on a governing commitment alone show the deepest losses.

### Q: What separates comparing delivered outcomes from comparing the durability of outcomes?

Delivered outcomes are measured in lives at a moment: incomes, health, security, schooling as people actually experienced them under a tradition's governments. Durability is measured across the governments that follow: whether those outcomes persisted when power changed hands and conditions turned. The two comparisons have different winners in this case, which is why conflating them produces such confident nonsense on both sides. A writer who compares only delivery will call the argument settled in social democracy's favour and never meet the reversal. A writer who compares only durability will call the settlement an illusion and never meet the record. The comparison this article defends holds the two measures apart, grades each tradition on both, and reports the split verdict that results.
