Structuralist Marxism and Marxist humanism were the names of two camps in an argument that ran through Marxist thought from the late 1950s to the end of the 1970s, filling journals, splitting reading groups, and forcing everyone who used the vocabulary of Marx to answer a question that sounds abstract and proved to be anything but. The question was whether there is such a thing as a human subject of history: a being with a nature of its own, whose characteristic powers can be developed or stunted, fulfilled or violated, and whose standpoint gives criticism its ground. The humanist side answered yes, and built its Marxism on the young Marx's account of alienated labour and species-being. The structuralist side answered no, and held that speaking of a human essence was a leftover from the philosophy Marx had broken with, an ideological notion rather than a scientific one. On the structuralist account, individuals are bearers of relations they did not choose, and what looks like a subject is an effect produced by the structures within which a life is lived.

The argument mattered politically because each side accused the other of a political crime, not merely a scholarly error. To appeal to human values and a human essence, humanists said, was the only honest way to criticise the bureaucratic regimes that ruled in Marx's name, since a theory that cannot say what a human life is for cannot say what those regimes were doing wrong. To dissolve the subject into structures, structuralists said, was the only honest way to explain why well-intentioned revolutions had produced those regimes in the first place, since a theory that trusts in human goodness will be surprised by obedience every time. Philosophy of history, the reading of Marx's texts, the theory of ideology, and the strategy of parties were all pulled into the quarrel, because the quarrel was never only about texts. It was about what kind of theory Marxism is: a science of social formations, or a critique mounted in the name of a humanity that capitalism deforms.

This article states each side in its own terms, at the strength its proponents would recognise, before it judges anything. It sets out the battlegrounds table, one row for each contested issue, with the position of each camp and what each camp has since conceded. It then gives a verdict, and the verdict has a name: the mutual necessity. The claim is that the dispute ended not by refutation but by concession on both sides, and that the working position most Marxists actually use can be stated precisely rather than gestured at. Humanism supplies the standard by which a social arrangement can be judged and found wanting, a standard structuralism cannot generate from inside a theory where subjects are effects. Structuralism supplies the explanation of why arrangements persist, reproduce themselves, and survive the discontent of the people living under them, an explanation humanism, reasoning from frustrated capacities, chronically lacks. Neither half is optional. A criticism without an explanation of durability becomes moralising; an explanation without a standard of judgement becomes a description of machinery that no one is entitled to call unjust. The sections that follow earn that verdict slowly, because a verdict about a fifty-year argument is worth having only if the argument itself has been heard fairly first.

## What structuralist Marxism and humanism disagreed about

Strip away the polemics, the party contexts, and the very different national settings, and the dispute concerns a single fork. Either history has a human subject, a being whose essential capacities are realised or violated by the arrangements under which it lives, or subjects are effects of social structures with no essence standing behind them, prior to them, or underneath them. Every other disagreement in the debate, about texts, about ideology, about experience, about strategy, is a consequence of which side of that fork a thinker took. The humanist reading begins from the early Marx, from the manuscripts of 1844 in which labour under capitalism is described as estranged from the worker, and from the concept of species-being, the distinctly human power of free, conscious, world-transforming activity. If human beings have such a nature, then history can be read as the story of its alienation and its possible recovery, and socialism is the name of the recovery. The structuralist reading begins from the later Marx, from Capital and from the claim that around 1845 Marx broke with his early philosophical anthropology and founded something else: a science of modes of production, of structures and their effects, in which talk of Man and his essence is a pre-scientific survival.

The canonical definition of the dispute, then, is this. Humanist Marxism holds that Marx's work is unified by a conception of human essence, that the mature economics is the early philosophy worked out in detail, and that this conception is what licenses criticism of any order that cramps human powers. Structuralist Marxism holds that Marx's work is divided by an epistemological break, that the science begins where the philosophy of the subject ends, and that importing the young Marx's vocabulary into the mature theory corrupts the science with ideology. Each side therefore needed a different Marx, and each side produced one, with editions, translations, and commentary traditions arranged to support the reading. Readers who want the contested anthropology itself should go to the article on [species-being](/2011/05/15/marx-species-being-explained/), which this comparison links rather than repeats, and readers who want the textual quarrel over the break should go to the article on [the young Marx and the mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/). The wider tradition that houses both positions is mapped in the series' [Western Marxism complete guide](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/). The business here is the confrontation between the two completed positions, what each was for, what each could not do, and what the settlement between them looks like when it is stated exactly.

### Is there a human nature in Marxism at all?

Both sides claimed Marx for their answer. Humanists pointed to species-being, the idea that human beings have characteristic powers whose free exercise is their fulfilment. Structuralists answered that Marx abandoned talk of a human essence in 1845 and that what exists are historically specific forms of individuality, not a nature underneath them.

The quarrel over human nature is older than the labels. Marx himself gave both camps their proof texts. The sixth thesis on Feuerbach, written in 1845, declares that the human essence is no abstraction inherent in each single individual, that in its reality it is the ensemble of the social relations. Structuralists treated that sentence as a founding deed, the moment Marx stopped philosophising about Man and started analysing relations. Humanists replied that the same Marx went on writing about the rich human being, about the development of human powers as an end in itself, about labour as life-activity, and that a thinker who had truly discarded the category of human nature would not keep reaching for its vocabulary in the notebooks of his maturity and in the pages of Capital itself. The dispute could not be settled by quotation because the corpus genuinely contains both registers, a point each side handled by demoting the other's texts: juvenilia and Feuerbachian residue on one side, rhetorical concessions and unexamined survivals on the other. What the texts could not decide, the century decided politically. Human nature was not only a philological question. It was the question whether Marxism contains, inside itself, a standard against which a socialist state could be measured and condemned. After 1956, that was not an academic matter at all, and the heat the question generated for thirty years cannot be understood apart from it.

## The dissident setting: humanism east of the divide

The humanist camp is often introduced as a Western affair, a matter of Parisian seminars and London history workshops, and that introduction is half false. Some of its bravest and most consequential work was done inside the socialist states, where the appeal to human values against the regime had a price attached that no Western polemicist ever paid. The thaw that followed Stalin's death, and above all the revelations and the Hungarian rising of 1956, created a generation of Marxist philosophers in Poland, Yugoslavia, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia who reached for the young Marx as a weapon of internal criticism. If the essence of socialism was the free development of human powers, then a state that regimented its citizens stood condemned by the doctrine it professed, and the manuscripts of 1844 became, in those hands, a charter for dissidence written by the founder himself. The Yugoslav philosophers gathered around the journal Praxis and the summer school on the island of Korcula built an entire school on this ground, analysing self-management as an institutional attempt to overcome alienation and criticising bureaucratic power as a new form of the old estrangement. In Poland, philosophers such as Adam Schaff worked, for a time with official tolerance, on what a Marxist account of the individual could be, while Leszek Kolakowski moved from revisionist humanism towards a devastating internal critique of the whole tradition. In Budapest, the circle around the elderly Lukacs, including Agnes Heller, developed a theory of needs and of everyday life that asked what a human existence worthy of the name would require.

The structuralist side never had an equivalent presence in the East, and the asymmetry is instructive. Structural Marxism was a doctrine of the Western academy and of one great Western party's intellectual periphery; its account of subjects as effects offered no vocabulary with which a dissident could address a regime in the language of violated human dignity, and its suspicion of humanist rhetoric sounded, in Warsaw or Prague, like an apology for whatever the apparatus chose to do. Humanists made exactly that charge, and it was among the most damaging of the whole debate because it was sometimes true in effect even where it was false in intent. A theory that dissolves the subject has difficulty saying who is wronged when a ministry stamps a file, and the dissident humanists pressed the point relentlessly: their structuralist opponents in Paris replied that sentimentality about Man had coexisted happily with every terror, and that the gulag's administrators had spoken fluent humanism when it suited them. Both charges landed, which is one reason the debate never cooled. Each side believed the other's philosophy had blood on its abstractions, and each could point to a decade in which the belief looked plausible.

The Eastern setting also explains a feature of the debate that puzzles readers who meet it as pure philosophy: its tone of personal accusation, the reviews written as indictments, the conference interventions that ended friendships. Where philosophy decides whether criticism of a regime is loyalty or treason, arguments are not games. The Western participants imported some of that charge through their own party loyalties and Cold War alignments, and the result was a thirty-year conversation in which almost no one was only talking about texts. The sections below keep the political causes in view, as the brief of a fair comparison requires, without reducing either position to those causes. A position is not refuted by its circumstances, and both sides were frequently right about the circumstances while remaining, in the article's eventual judgement, half right about the theory.

## The humanist case, stated in its own terms

The humanist position can be stated as a chain of connected claims, each of which its holders defended in print. First, Marx's thought is a unity. The manuscripts of 1844 are not an embarrassing prelude but the foundation, and Capital is their continuation by other means: the analysis of the commodity, of the working day, of accumulation, is throughout an analysis of what happens to human beings and their powers under a specific set of relations. Second, the centre of that unity is a philosophical anthropology. Human beings are distinguished by species-being, by the capacity to make their own life activity an object of will and consciousness, to produce universally and freely, and to recognise themselves in a world they have shaped. Third, capitalism is criticisable, and criticised by Marx, because it alienates that activity: the worker is estranged from the product, from the process, from fellow workers, and from the species-life that should be his fulfilment, and this estrangement is not a metaphor but a describable deformation of real powers. Fourth, history is therefore intelligible as a drama with a subject: humanity producing itself through labour, losing itself in class society, and positioned, in the proletariat, to recover itself. Fifth, and decisively for the camp's self-understanding, this anthropology is what makes Marxism a critique rather than a mere science of society. A science can describe a factory. Only a theory with a conception of undeformed human life can call the factory a mutilation.

The personnel of the camp was international and generational. Its founding ancestor was the Lukacs of History and Class Consciousness in 1923, who had arrived at a Marxism of consciousness, totality, and reification independently of the 1844 manuscripts, which were still unpublished, a fact humanists later cited as proof that the anthropology was in the logic of the method and not a youthful accident. Ernst Bloch built his vast philosophy of hope on the not-yet-conscious of human longing. In France, Henri Lefebvre spent a long career insisting that Marxism was the critique of everyday life and the knowledge of man in his totality, and Jean-Paul Sartre, in the Search for a Method and the Critique of Dialectical Reason of 1960, attempted the grand synthesis: an existential Marxism in which individual praxis, the free project of a situated agent, remained the intelligible motor of history even inside scarcity and the practico-inert weight of worked matter. Erich Fromm carried the anthropology into psychoanalysis and into the Anglophone world with Marx's Concept of Man in 1961, whose translations of the early manuscripts reached readers no party journal could touch, and his edited symposium Socialist Humanism in 1965 displayed the camp's range across continents. The Praxis philosophers, Kosik in Prague with his Dialectics of the Concrete, the Budapest school, and in Britain the social historians and cultural critics grouped loosely around E. P. Thompson, who insisted that class is made in experience and not read off from position, completed a camp that shared no headquarters and much temperament. What united them was a conviction that a Marxism which loses the human subject loses the reason anyone became a Marxist.

### What did the humanists mean by alienation?

Alienation named a deformation, not a mood. A worker's activity belongs to another, the product stands against its maker as an alien power, and capacities meant for free exercise are used as means of survival. The concept gave criticism a measuring rod: arrangements could be graded by what they did to human powers.

The measuring rod metaphor deserves pressure, because it contains the whole humanist claim to indispensability. A measuring rod is not part of the thing measured. The humanist held that Marxism possesses, in its anthropology, a standard that stands apart from any existing arrangement and can be applied to all of them, to Manchester in 1844 and to any later industrial order, capitalist or avowedly socialist, with the same result where the deformation is the same. This is what allowed dissident humanists to turn the doctrine against its custodians without importing liberal premises from outside. They could say: judge this society by its own founder's standard. It also allowed the Western humanists to resist a certain complacency in the labour movement, the habit of treating the growth of production as self-evidently emancipatory. If the standard is the free development of human powers, then a larger output of goods produced by stunted producers is not progress, whatever the statistics say. Critics of the camp, structuralists prominent among them, replied that a measuring rod which never changes while everything measured changes around it is not a discovery but a postulate, smuggled in from the philosophy of Feuerbach and Kant. The reply has force, and the humanist answer to it, that the powers in question are precisely those that history itself develops so the rod grows with the measured, is the beginning of the sophisticated humanism of a Kosik or a Heller rather than the end of the argument. The battleground, in other words, was never whether to have a standard; it was whether the standard was a finding of science or a commitment of philosophy, and what follows for the status of criticism either way.

### Did humanism mean believing that people are naturally good?

No, and the camp's main writers denied it with some irritation. Their claim concerned capacities, not virtues: human beings can act freely, consciously, and in common, whatever use they make of those powers. Alienation described powers blocked or bent, and a blocked power is not an innocent one; it can turn cruel, servile, or destructive.

The misunderstanding matters because it converts a philosophical anthropology into a sentimentality, and sentimentality is easy to defeat. The serious humanist position is almost the opposite of optimism about human conduct. It holds that the same species-powers which make cooperation and creation possible also make domination effective, since only a being that can internalise norms can be governed through them, and only a being that can imagine alternatives can be corrupted by false ones. Fromm's work on the authoritarian character, whatever its later reputation, was an attempt within the camp to explain why masses of people had clung to their own subordination, and it conceded to the structuralists in advance that human beings are not reliably on the side of their own emancipation. What the humanist would not concede was the inference from that record to the non-existence of the subject. A capacity thwarted is still a capacity, and the thwarting still needs a name and a judge. When structuralists replied that invoking a capacity that never appears unthwarted is indistinguishable from invoking nothing, the argument reached the bedrock on which it would rest for twenty years: two different accounts of what an explanation is allowed to contain.

## The structuralist case, stated in its own terms

The structuralist position, associated above all with Louis Althusser and the circle that worked with him in Paris in the 1960s, can likewise be stated as a chain. First, Marx's corpus is not a unity but a development punctuated by a rupture. Around 1845, in the Theses on Feuerbach and The German Ideology, Marx broke with the Feuerbachian problematic of essence and alienation in which his earliest work had moved, and began the long labour that produced a new science, the science of history, whose mature statement is Capital. Althusser, borrowing a term from the philosophy of science, called the rupture an epistemological break, and he mapped the corpus by it: works of youth before, transitional works around it, works of maturity after. Second, the science is distinguished from ideology as its other. Ideology, in this usage, is not false opinion but the imaginary relation of individuals to their real conditions, a relation every society produces and no society escapes, including a socialist one. Humanism, when it presents itself as theory, is ideology in this precise sense: the lived self-image of the subject mistaken for knowledge. Hence the camp's deliberately provocative banner of theoretical anti-humanism. It did not announce indifference to human beings, a point Althusser stated explicitly and his critics often skipped. It announced that the category of the subject could not serve as the foundation of a science of history, because what requires explanation is precisely how subjects, their desires, their self-understandings, their experienced freedoms, are produced by the relations they inhabit.

Third, the science has its own concepts, irreducible to anthropology: mode of production, forces and relations of production, base and superstructure reworked as instances of a structure in dominance, determination in the last instance by the economy combined with the relative autonomy of the political and ideological instances, and overdetermination, the principle that every contradiction is affected by all the others so that no contradiction ever works in a pure state. Fourth, individuals enter this account as bearers, supports of relations defined by the structure; the famous formulation is that history is a process without a subject or goals, a sentence from the Reply to John Lewis of 1973 which the camp's enemies quoted for a decade and its friends explained for just as long. Fifth, the political consequence drawn was austere: because subjects are made by ideologies, liberation cannot be entrusted to the spontaneous self-expression of an essence. It requires theory, organisation, and an unflinching analysis of the apparatuses, including the ideological state apparatuses anatomised in the essay of 1970, through which consent and identity are manufactured. The [profile of Althusser](/2015/12/15/louis-althusser-intellectual-biography/) in this series carries his biography and the fate of these ideas; the present article is concerned with what the position, at its strongest, claimed against the humanists.

The camp around the position was narrower than the humanist coalition and more disciplined, which its members counted a virtue and its enemies a symptom. Étienne Balibar contributed the rigorous reconstruction of historical materialism's basic concepts in Reading Capital of 1965. Nicos Poulantzas carried the framework into the theory of the state and classes. A younger generation, Pierre Macherey in literary theory, Jacques Rancière before his break, the students of the Cahiers pour l'Analyse, worked the seam between the science and its others. Outside France the position travelled through journals and translation series into Britain, Latin America, and the Anglophone academy, where it met, in the late 1970s, the most famous reception in the whole debate. Its attraction for a generation of younger scholars was real and should be recorded in the camp's own terms: it seemed to offer a Marxism that was rigorous where the older Marxism was rhetorical, that could explain the resilience of capitalism after 1945 instead of predicting its imminent collapse, that accounted for nationalism, racism, and the authoritarian personality of masses without treating them as inexplicable failures of consciousness, and that promised to replace moral denunciation with knowledge of mechanisms. The promise of mechanism is the thread to hold. Everything the structuralists conceded later, they conceded because mechanism alone could not do one job, and everything the humanists conceded, they conceded because denunciation alone could not do another. The battlegrounds table sets the jobs side by side.

### What was the epistemological break?

The break was Althusser's name for the rupture he located in Marx's work around 1845, when the vocabulary of essence and alienation gave way to the concepts of a new science. It did double duty: a claim about the corpus, which texts belong to the science, and a claim about knowledge, that science begins by breaking with ideology.

Philologically, the break thesis was vulnerable in ways its author half acknowledged. The Grundrisse of 1857 and 1858, those vast notebooks of the mature economist, are saturated with the language of alienation and of human development, and the third volume of Capital contains the celebrated passage on the realm of freedom beginning beyond labour determined by necessity, with the shortening of the working day as its prerequisite, language indistinguishable in spirit from the manuscripts of 1844. Humanists read such passages as continuity proved. Althusser's defence was subtler than his slogan: a break in problematic, he argued, does not require the instant disappearance of old words, since concepts can survive as metaphors or be refunctioned inside a new structure of questions, and the Grundrisse in particular he treated as a transitional text where the new science is being built with tools not yet its own. The defence is not absurd, and serious readers still divide over it, but it purchased the theory at the price of a criterion problem. If the presence of humanist vocabulary in a late text does not count against the break, what would count, and if nothing would count, the break has ceased to be a finding and become a decision about how to read. That problem returns in the final sections, because it is one of the places where the dispute's settlement was reached by attrition: scholars stopped asking whether the break happened and started asking what each reading of Marx made possible, which is a different and more honest question.

### Does structuralism remove human agency?

It makes subjects effects of structures rather than their authors, which removes agency as a starting point. Structuralists replied that structures are also sites of struggle, and their critics argued this concession is not supported by the framework. The dispute was never resolved in theory.

Consider what the denial of the starting point was meant to achieve. Humanist history-writing, in the structuralist critique, oscillated between two failures. Where people acted as the theory wished, their action was credited to the awakening of the subject; where they did not, their inaction was debited to false consciousness, a notion that explained everything after the fact and predicted nothing before it. Against this, the structural account proposed to explain conduct, including resistant conduct, by the positions agents occupy and the ideologies that interpellate them, to use the camp's term for the process by which individuals are recruited as subjects who recognise themselves in an identity offered to them. The schoolchild, the conscript, the believer, the worker who experiences the wage relation as a fair exchange: each is a subject, fully real as an experience, and each subjectivity is an effect with causes that can be studied. The gain in explanatory reach was genuine. The loss, as critics from Thompson onwards insisted, is that struggle becomes hard to locate in the picture. If subjects are effects, whence comes the force that bends structures rather than reproducing them? Althusser's answer, that class struggle is the motor and that structures exist only in their effects and in the practices they organise, was felt even by sympathetic readers to name the difficulty rather than dissolve it. The concession flagged in the snippet answer, that structures are sites of struggle, entered the camp's vocabulary under pressure and sat there uneasily, a humanist-shaped patch on a structuralist garment. The settlement described later in this article can be read as the two camps agreeing, without ever signing anything, to let the patch stay.

### Did theoretical anti-humanism mean indifference to human beings?

Althusser denied it in so many words. The adjective theoretical did the work: at the level of theory, he argued, one cannot found a science on the concept of Man, while at the level of practice and politics, humanist watchwords could retain a role as ideology, judged by their effects rather than their truth.

The distinction is easy to mock and was mocked for decades, but it deserves a fair paraphrase. Every mature science, the argument ran, had to expel the self-image of its object from its foundations: astronomy does not found itself on the experienced centrality of the earth, and biology does not found itself on the experienced unity of the living self. A science of history, if there is to be one, cannot found itself on the experienced freedom of the subject, since that experience is itself among the phenomena to be explained. Anti-humanism named that expulsion at the level of foundations. It did not license cruelty, and its author, a man whose own biography resists any simple telling, insisted that ideological humanism, the practical language of protest against suffering, kept a function he had no wish to abolish. What his critics heard, and what many of his followers enacted, was colder than the doctrine: a manner in which concern for persons was treated as theoretical naivety, and the humanist vocabulary of dignity and mutilation was met with a raised eyebrow. Doctrines are responsible for their manners only up to a point, but the point had been passed often enough that the humanist charge, anti-humanism slides towards indifference, entered the record as a standing warning rather than a refutation. Both things are true, and keeping both in view is the discipline this comparison tries to practise throughout.

## The battlegrounds table

Arguments of this size are hard to hold in the mind whole, and the debate's literature, which runs to hundreds of interventions, defeats summary by narrative. The device used here is a table: one row for each issue on which the camps actually fought, the position of each camp stated in a sentence, and a final column recording what each side has since conceded, since the concessions are where the settlement lives. Six battlegrounds cover the war. There is the status of the early writings, the texts on which the humanist reading was founded. There is species-being, the anthropology itself. There is the theory of ideology, where the camps offered rival accounts of why people believe what they believe. There is the theory of history, expressive totality against structured whole. There is the status of experience, Thompson's chosen ground. And there is political strategy, the battleground on which all the others were secretly being fought, since the question of what theory is for was the question neither camp could leave alone.

| Battleground | The humanist position | The structuralist position | What each side has since conceded |
|---|---|---|---|
| The early writings | The manuscripts of 1844 are the foundation; the later economics unfolds their vision of alienated and recovered humanity. | The early works belong to a Feuerbachian problematic that Marx broke with; the science starts after the break. | Humanists grant that the vocabulary and the questions change markedly across the corpus. Structuralists grant that humanist themes persist in the mature notebooks and in Capital itself. |
| Species-being | Human beings possess characteristic powers whose free, conscious exercise is their fulfilment; alienation is the deformation of those powers. | There is no human essence prior to social relations; individuals are produced as subjects by the relations they bear. | Humanists grant that the powers are historically shaped and appear only in specific forms. Structuralists grant that some account of human need and capacity is required for criticism to bite. |
| The theory of ideology | Ideology is false consciousness, a distortion that knowledge and experience can dispel as agents come to see their situation clearly. | Ideology is the imaginary relation of individuals to their real conditions, eternal in form, reproduced by apparatuses, and constitutive of subjects themselves. | Humanists grant that belief is materially reproduced and does not melt before facts. Structuralists grant that ideology is contested terrain and that subjects can be formed against the grain. |
| The theory of history | History is the self-production of humanity through labour: the alienation of the subject and its possible recovery give history its intelligible shape. | History is a process without a subject or goals, driven by contradictions within and among the instances of a structured whole. | Humanists grant that outcomes are not guaranteed by any logic of the subject. Structuralists grant that their formula risks dissolving the agency it needs to explain change. |
| The status of experience | Experience is where structures are lived, tested, and sometimes refused; it is evidence no theory may override. | Experience is an ideological effect, the least reliable witness to the structures that produce it. | Humanists grant that experience is interpreted through inherited categories and can mislead. Structuralists grant that a theory which cannot hear experience loses contact with its object and its audience. |
| Political strategy | Emancipation is the self-emancipation of the subject; organisation must prefigure the free association it seeks. | Emancipation requires theory and organisation imposed on spontaneity; the party as bearer of science guards against ideological relapse. | Humanists grant that spontaneity alone dissolves before organised power. Structuralists grant that a theory accountable to no one's experience becomes a closed clerisy. |

Read the table slowly, because each row conceals a library. The first row, on the early writings, was fought with editions and translations as weapons: the humanist side promoted readings of the complete Marx, the young and the old in one binding, while the structuralist side taught students to begin from the break and to treat the manuscripts as a document of what the science had to escape, so that two generations of readers literally held different books under the same author's name. The second row conceals the philosophical anthropology discussed above, and with it the deepest water in the debate, since a concession in that row costs each side its foundation. The third row, ideology, is where the structuralist side made its most durable contribution, because the account of ideology as material, institutional, and subject-forming outlasted the camp that proposed it and passed into the common stock of social theory, used by many who never called themselves structuralists. The fourth row states the fork itself. The fifth row was the historians' war, fought over whether the felt reality of a crowd, a chapel, a strike, is data or decoration. The sixth row is the one to watch if the others seem scholastic: every position in the upper rows implies a holder of power in the bottom row, a party, a class, a council, an editor, and the debate's participants understood one another's rows perfectly in that respect, whatever they said.

The concessions column is the article's evidence for its verdict, and it should be read as evidence, not as politeness. These concessions were not extracted in a single negotiation; nobody convened a peace conference. They accumulated across the 1970s and the decades after, in retractions and softenings, in the spectacle of leading structuralists criticising the theoreticism of their own school, in humanists absorbing the institutional reproduction of belief into their accounts of consciousness, in the plain fact that the polemics stopped while the problems continued. A dispute settled by argument ends with a winner's text. A dispute settled by attrition ends with both sides quietly using each other's tools, which is what the record shows. The verdict of mutual necessity simply names that record and draws its consequence: if each side's tools are needed to do the other's job, then the working position is a division of labour, and the remaining quarrel, over whether there is a subject at the level of philosophical anthropology, is real but no longer load-bearing for most of the theory built on top of it.

## Ideology: the battleground where the structuralists advanced furthest

On no issue did the two camps produce accounts as unequal in afterlife as on ideology. The humanist inheritance, descending from the young Marx's critique of religion and from the Lukacs of reification, treated ideology primarily as distortion: a veil of ideas, generated by a deformed social order, which misrepresents that order to the people living in it and which criticism, experience, and organisation can progressively tear. The model has a knower and a known, a subject who can in principle see clearly, and a set of illusions interposed between them. Its political programme follows: enlighten, expose, demystify, and let the agent act on the world as it is. The structuralist account cut deeper and colder. Ideology, for Althusser, is not a veil over reality but the medium in which individuals live their relation to reality at all, an imaginary relation to real conditions, and as such it has no outside for the individuals it forms and no history of its own abolition: there will be ideology in any society, including a classless one, because subjects must be formed somehow and formation is ideology's work. The mechanism is interpellation, the hailing by which institutions recruit individuals into identities they experience as their own innermost selfhood, and the machinery is the ideological state apparatuses, family, school, church, law, media, and party among them, which produce the subjects a social order requires far more reliably than its police produce obedience.

The humanist counterattack was swift and not wrong. If ideology is eternal and subjects are its effects, then the critic's own standpoint becomes mysterious: from where does the structuralist scientist observe the machinery, if observation is itself interpellated? The camp's answer, that science is produced by a break with ideology and sustained by a practice of theory with its own criteria, persuaded few outside the circle, and Thompson turned the question into ridicule with unmatched fluency. Yet the humanists absorbed the substance even while rejecting the system. No serious humanist writing after the confrontation treated belief as a cobweb that facts could brush away. The reproduction of common sense by institutions, the materiality of the school and the screen, the formation of desires themselves by the order that frustrates them: these entered the common stock, and the historian of experience himself wrote about the making of consent with tools the other camp had forged. The pattern of the whole debate is visible in miniature on this row of the table. The structuralist advance was real, the humanist objection to its foundations was also real, and the successor theory, visible in the cultural studies that Stuart Hall and others built from Gramsci and Althusser together, simply used both: ideology as the terrain on which subjects are formed, and struggle over that formation as the space where agency re-enters.

### How does each side handle the critic's own standpoint?

The humanist answers with the standpoint of the subject: the proletariat, or the critic speaking from undeformed human interests, can see what ideology hides because its situation gives it an interest in truth. The structuralist answers with the break: theory produces knowledge by its own procedures, not by occupying a privileged place in society.

The two answers fail in mirror image, which is why the exchange ran so long. The standpoint answer risks circularity, since the class that is to see clearly must first be identified by the theory it is supposed to validate, and its actual recorded opinions, which often disappoint the theory, must then be discounted as the very false consciousness the standpoint was meant to escape. The break answer risks clericalism, since a knowledge validated only by its own procedures can mistake its enclosure for rigour, and the history of the structuralist circle, with its internal excommunications performed in the vocabulary of science, gave the risk a biography. The settlement, again, is practical. Working critics borrow the standpoint's moral location, criticism speaks from somewhere and for someone, and the break's methodological suspicion, that somewhere may be an effect of the machinery under criticism. Holding both is uncomfortable and is also simply what careful work in this tradition looks like when it is honest about its instruments.

## History without a subject, history as a subject's story

The theory of history is the row of the table where the fork shows its full depth, because here the anthropology becomes a philosophy of the whole process. The humanist philosophy of history is a drama in three acts, and its author never quite hid the theological architecture it inherited. Humanity externalises itself in labour and in the world it builds; that world turns against its makers, confronting them as capital, as the state, as dead labour dominating living; and the possibility exists, embodied in the class that suffers the alienation most completely, of a recovery in which the producers take conscious possession of their collective powers. Even writers who scorned the word eschatology used the shape, because the shape is what converts economics into meaning: it says what the suffering is for, and who bears the future. Against this the structuralist philosophy of history offered an anti-drama. A social formation is a whole articulated in dominance, its instances moving at different tempos, its contradictions overdetermined, its transitions occurring when a rupture generalises from the weakest link rather than when a subject awakens. There is no protagonist. There are structures, conjunctures, and the class struggle as motor, a motor that belongs to no one. The slogan that history is a process without a subject or goals was aimed precisely at the three-act drama, at every philosophy that had smuggled a destination into the evidence.

Each side's philosophy of history explains the other's existence, a symmetry the extra questions of any good seminar eventually reach. The humanist drama, said the structuralists, is what Marxism looks like when it functions as the ideology of a movement that needs hope and a protagonist, which is why it flourished in parties and in prison cells and in the prefaces of popular editions. The structuralist anti-drama, said the humanists, is what Marxism looks like when it functions as the ideology of an academy that needs a science and a priesthood, which is why it flourished in seminars and in the footnotes of difficult books. Both sociologies are plausible and both are ungenerous, and the mature judgement is that a philosophy of history is not refuted by identifying its congregation. What can be said, and the concessions column records it, is that events disciplined both. No awakening of a universal subject arrived to close the humanist drama on schedule, and the structuralist machine, asked why anything ever changes at all, kept reaching for the vocabulary of struggle and contradiction in ways that reintroduced agency by the back stairs. The division of labour proposed in this article assigns the drama's third act to the status of a normative hope, something criticism may hold and history owes nobody, and assigns the conjunctural analysis to the status of method, the only honest way to study how formations actually persist and break. That assignment is not a synthesis. It is a demarcation of what each inheritance is good for, drawn by watching each fail at the other's task.

## Experience: the ground the historians defended

The debate's most quoted English voice belonged to a historian, and his choice of ground was experience. E. P. Thompson's life work, from The Making of the English Working Class in 1963 onwards, embodied a methodological conviction: that class is not a thing or a slot in a structure but a historical relationship, something that happens when people, out of common conditions, come to feel and articulate an identity of interests against others whose interests oppose theirs. Experience, in this vocabulary, is the middle term the structuralist schema lacked. Between the structure and the act stands the lived handling of the structure, in inherited languages of fairness, in chapels and clubs, in the moral economy of a crowd that riots for a just price rather than plundering at random. A theory that treats that middle term as ideological froth, Thompson argued, has not transcended humanism; it has amputated its object. People are not puppets of structures that theorists alone can see, and a Marxism that addresses them as such will deserve the indifference it receives. His polemical essay of 1978 gathered these convictions into an assault on the whole structuralist enterprise, and its target section below takes up the confrontation directly; here the point is the narrower one about evidence. For the humanist side, experience was the court in which theoretical claims could be cross-examined, and the structuralist refusal to appear in that court was, to them, the confession that the theory could not survive contact with its subject matter.

The structuralist rejoinder was philosophically serious, and flattening it into a sneer at ordinary people, as the polemics often did, misrepresents the camp's best argument. Experience, the rejoinder ran, is not raw. What a person lives arrives already categorised by the languages, rituals, and self-images their formation supplies, and the feeling of immediacy is itself an achievement of ideology, which works precisely by presenting its products as nature. The crowd's sense of a just price was real as an experience and was also, as Thompson's own research showed in exquisite detail, a inherited script, learned from custom, sermon, and statute, so that the historian's evidence for spontaneous agency turns out on inspection to be evidence for the depth of cultural formation. That tu quoque did not settle the matter, because Thompson could accept the script and still ask who performs it, varies it, and sometimes refuses the part, but it permanently raised the standard for humanist appeals to experience. After the exchange, experience could be cited as evidence only by writers who had also accounted for its formation. The historians, in the main, rose to the standard, and social history's golden decades were written by scholars who treated experience as both irreducible and produced, which is the settlement in this row stated as a method.

### Where does experience fit in each account?

For humanists, experience is the tribunal: the place where structures are lived, judged, and occasionally refused, and therefore evidence a theory must answer to. For structuralists, experience is an exhibit produced by the accused: real as feeling, unreliable as testimony, since ideology's success consists in making its constructions feel like direct knowledge of the world.

A working example keeps the distinction honest. Take the experience of the wage as a fair day's pay for a fair day's work, felt sincerely on both sides of a factory gate. The humanist analysis asks what that feeling suppresses: the working day contains hours for which no equivalent returns, and the feeling of fairness is the form in which the suppression is lived quietly. The structuralist analysis asks how the feeling is manufactured: by the legal form of the contract between equals, by the separation of the economic and the political that makes the gate a boundary between a realm of freedom and a realm of command, by schooling in the virtues of the bargain. Both analyses are true of the same morning. They differ in what they trust. One trusts the grievance that eventually breaks the feeling, reading it as the subject stirring; the other trusts the mechanism that reproduces the feeling, reading the grievance itself as an event inside the mechanism's tolerances. A reader deciding between the camps should notice that the two questions, why does the feeling persist, and by what right do we call it unjust, are both real questions, and that neither camp's apparatus answers both. That noticing is the seed of the verdict this article defends.

## The English confrontation and the measured reply

The debate had burned in France for fifteen years before it produced its most famous single explosion in English, and the explosion is worth examining closely because it is both the clearest and the most misleading document of the whole quarrel. In 1978 the Merlin Press published The Poverty of Theory and Other Essays by E. P. Thompson, whose title essay, a vast polemic of some two hundred pages, undertook the total demolition of Althusser and of the structural Marxism that had gathered around him. Verification of the bibliographic facts is straightforward and worth the habit: the polemic appeared in 1978, and the substantial reply, Arguments within English Marxism by Perry Anderson, editor of New Left Review, followed in 1980 from the same publishing milieu, a reply whose defining feature is that its author agreed with neither side entirely and said so in detail. Between those two dates the English-speaking Marxist world conducted, in reviews, conferences, and correspondence columns, a brawl whose energy has not been matched since, and whose smoke still obscures the issue it was ostensibly about.

Thompson's charges, stripped of a invective that even his admirers conceded was operatic, were four. He charged the structuralists with theoreticism, the production of concepts in a sealed circuit answerable only to itself, a machinery he likened to an orrery, a clockwork model of the heavens that moves beautifully and touches nothing. He charged them with ahistoricism, with a schema into which any evidence could be fed and from which no surprise could emerge, so that history as a discipline, with its stubborn archives, was replaced by the illustration of a structure. He charged them with the elimination of moral agency and therewith of moral judgement, leaving a Marxism that could analyse domination in exquisite detail while lacking any ground on which to object to it, and he connected this to a politics he despised: the mandarin authority of a theoretical vanguard, answerable to its own rigour, which he recognised as the philosophy of a certain kind of party wearing academic dress. And he charged the whole enterprise with being, despite its slogans, an idealism, since its structures existed nowhere but in the discourse that posited them while claiming the authority of materialism. The essay's peroration defended experience, moral agency, and the discipline of historical evidence as the true inheritance, and it did so in prose that remains, whatever one thinks of the argument, the most readable hundred pages the debate produced.

Anderson's reply is the document serious students should read second, because it performs the settlement in embryo. Anderson defended Thompson's politics and his historiography against the structuralists on several counts, conceded the force of the charge that Althusserian theory had become self-confirming, and documented the political evasions of the Paris circle with an insider's authority. And then, having sided with the historian against the philosopher, he turned and administered a correction of equal weight: Thompson's own alternative, he argued, relied on a notion of experience and of free agency that could not bear the explanatory load placed on it, romanticised the self-activity of the oppressed, and left English Marxism without a theory of the structures, state, economy, ideology, within which experience is formed and constrained. The editor agreed with neither side entirely because, on his showing, each side was in possession of the refutation of the other and neither was in possession of a whole position. Readers who know Anderson's later work will recognise the stance; what matters here is that the most authoritative referee the English debate produced declined, in print, in 1980, to award the match, and gave reasons that pointed directly at the mutual necessity. The confrontation is thus the clearest episode and the least clear verdict in the whole story, which is exactly the competitive gap this article exists to fill: narrating Thompson versus Althusser is easy, and the internet is full of the narration, but stating what each side needs from the other is the only part of the story that still teaches anything.

### What did Thompson actually object to?

Beneath the famous invective, Thompson's objection was that structural Marxism had abolished the court in which its claims could be tested. A theory that treats experience as ideology's product and history as its own illustration cannot be corrected by evidence, and what cannot be corrected cannot be knowledge, only doctrine with a technical accent.

His positive case deserves the same economy. History as a discipline, he argued, proceeds by a dialogue between concept and evidence in which the evidence can win: the archive surprises, the dead refuse their assigned roles, and a category that cannot accommodate the refusal must be revised or dropped. That discipline, unglamorous and slow, was for Thompson the actual materialism of the historian, against a materialism of incantation that pronounced matter and moved in a world of pure concepts. The point connects to his socialism, which was of the libertarian and dissenting kind: a movement that cannot be corrected by the experience of its own constituents will reproduce, inside itself, the unaccountable authority it exists to abolish. Whatever the verdict on his reading of Althusser's texts, and specialists have found it cavalier in places, the objection generalises beyond its target. Any theory of ideology that seals itself against the testimony of the ideologised has arranged to be right at the price of being empty, and later practitioners of the structural inheritance, notably in cultural studies, rebuilt their methods precisely to reopen the court Thompson demanded: audiences, readers, and crowds studied as makers of meaning within structures, not as the structures' ventriloquists.

### What did the mediating reply concede to each side?

Anderson's Arguments within English Marxism conceded to Thompson that the Paris school had evaded political judgement and sealed its theory against evidence. It conceded to the structuralists that English Marxism lacked any adequate theory of structures and that experience, unexamined, could not serve as a foundation for one.

The reply's method is as instructive as its judgements. Anderson treated both camps as holders of partial truths whose partiality could be specified, and he specified them with a bibliographer's patience, citing the actual texts in their actual sequence, refusing the polemical shortcuts of quotation out of context that both camps had used on each other and on Marx. The result was a book that partisans of either side could raid for ammunition and neither side could claim, and its reception confirmed the diagnosis: each camp reviewed the concessions to itself with gratitude and the concessions to its opponents with suspicion, and went on much as before. Settlements by attrition are like that. They are announced early by the clearest heads and adopted late by everyone else, without acknowledgement, in the footnotes of work that simply proceeds on the settled basis. The measure of the reply is that the subsequent practice of Marxist scholarship, in history, in sociology, in literary studies, looks far more like Anderson's demarcation than like either camp's manifesto. Subjects are studied as formed and forming; structures are studied as reproduced and contestable; and the pure positions survive mainly as teaching devices, which is where this article, too, has presented them before complicating them.

## The French setting: a party, a philosophy, a schism

To understand why the quarrel's French theatre was fought with such peculiar intensity, begin with the institutional facts. The French Communist Party was, for the middle decades of the century, one of the largest political organisations in the country, with an intellectual apparatus to match, and it maintained an official philosophy as parties of that kind do. After 1956, when the secret speech and the crushing of Hungary forced every Western party to find a language for de-Stalinisation, the language the French party reached for was humanism: socialist humanism, a Marxism with a human face avant la lettre, in which the crimes were betrayals of the doctrine's human essence rather than products of its structure. Roger Garaudy, the party's house philosopher, built the official version, and the Central Committee at Argenteuil in 1966 gave the humanist vocabulary its imprimatur. It was against this official humanism, from inside the party he never left, that Althusser wrote his great interventions of the early 1960s. His anti-humanism was thus, among other things, an internal opposition's weapon: to call the party's humanism ideology was to accuse its leadership of dissolving the science of Marx into a moralising rhetoric that could bless any policy, including the ones that had filled the camps, provided the intentions were humanely phrased. The accusation stung because it inverted the dissident charge from the East. There, humanism was the language of the accused against the apparatus. In Paris, structuralism claimed to be exactly that, wielded against an apparatus whose humanism was its alibi.

Sartre's position in the same city made the geometry triangular and the tempers worse. The most famous philosopher in France had rebuilt his existentialism as a Marxism of praxis, insisting that Marxism was the untranscendable philosophy of the epoch and that within it the individual project remained the unit of intelligibility; the structuralists, several of whom had sat in his rival's seminars rather than his, replied that this was precisely the ideological humanism the science had to expel, and Claude Lévi-Strauss, from anthropology's neighbouring chair, closed his own masterwork of 1962 with a critique of Sartre that the Marxist structuralists happily borrowed. When the events of May 1968 arrived, the geometry was tested in the street and found, by many of the young, wanting on all sides: the party condemned the students, the structuralists' most famous name was caricatured on walls as a mandarin of stasis, and the humanist vocabulary of liberation floated free of every organisation, available to anyone. The camps drew opposite morals. Humanists said that a theory of subjects as effects had disarmed itself before a festival of subjects in revolt. Structuralists said that a festival was not a revolution, that the structures absorbed the insult within two electoral cycles, and that only a theory of reproduction could explain the absorption. Both morals contained the other's refutation in embryo, and both were drawn, which is why the row outlived the decade that produced it.

### Why did each camp hear the other as a political threat?

Because in the party cultures where the debate lived, a philosophical position was a factional act. To defend humanism in Paris was to defend the leadership's post-1956 settlement; to attack it as ideology was to accuse that leadership of bad faith. In the East the valences reversed, and the same words armed dissidents against their apparatuses.

The English theatre had its own version of the geometry, milder in stakes and sharper in prose. The historians around Thompson had broken with their party in 1956, in the same season as the dissidents of the East, and had built the first New Left partly on the humanist ground of that break: socialist humanism was the banner under which they had refused to excuse the tanks in Budapest. To be told, twenty years later, by a younger generation of theorists formed on Parisian texts, that their founding vocabulary was ideology and their historiography a pre-scientific empiricism, was to be told that the moral basis of their lives' work was a confusion. The fury of 1978 becomes intelligible, and forgivable, in that light. Conversely the younger theorists, who had watched the movement of 1968 dissipate and the far left fragment, heard in the historians' humanism a refusal to think rigorously about why defeats recur, and heard it as sentimentality armed with seniority. Generational succession, the politics of journals, and the Cold War's long shadow did the rest. None of this explains the positions away. It explains why a disagreement about the early writings of a nineteenth-century economist could empty a conference hall into factions, and why the participants so often wrote as if an error in the theory of ideology were a species of treason.

## What humanism gives that structuralism cannot generate

The humanist contribution that no successor theory has replaced is a standard of judgement internal to Marxism itself. Consider what criticism requires. To call an arrangement unjust, mutilating, or unworthy, one must possess a conception of the being it wrongs: of what that being is for, what its flourishing would look like, and in what the wrong consists when flourishing is blocked. Mainstream moral philosophy supplies such conceptions from its own traditions, and Marxists may borrow them, but the borrowing has a cost the humanists identified precisely: the resulting critique is external, a liberal standard applied to a socialist object, and it surrenders the founding claim that Marx's analysis contains its own indictment. Species-being, for all its philological embarrassments, is the place where the indictment lives inside the analysis. Labour is the human essence in action; alienated labour is that essence turned against itself; the critique of political economy is therefore also, in the same breath, the measurement of a world against the beings who make it. Remove the anthropology and the measurement loses its unit. One can still describe the factory's mechanisms with unmatched precision. One cannot, from the mechanism alone, derive that anyone is wronged there, since wrong is a relation between an arrangement and a being with claims, and the theory has defined such beings out of its foundations.

The structuralist camp was not insensible to this difficulty; it was, in a way, built on refusing to meet it cheaply. Its answer was that the normative work is done by the class struggle itself and by the standpoint of the proletariat, whose interest in abolishing the order gives criticism its direction without any anthropology. The answer relocates the standard rather than supplying it. An interest is not a standard until one has said why its satisfaction is good, and the proletariat's recorded interests have included, at various times, demands no theorist of the camp would endorse, which the theorist must then discount by the usual machinery of false consciousness, reintroducing a judge above the judged: the theorist, armed with a standard he declines to name. The humanist charge, pressed by Thompson and by the Praxis philosophers with equal force from different worlds, is that the un-named standard is always smuggled humanism, the theorist's own intact sense of what a human life should be, enjoying diplomatic immunity from the theory that dissolves everyone else's. A division of labour that names the standard openly, and assigns it to the humanist inheritance, is not an evasion of the difficulty. It is the difficulty, confessed and organised.

### Why does a theory of structures need a standard of judgement?

Because description cannot grade. A structural account can show how an arrangement reproduces itself, how its subjects are formed, and where its stresses gather, but the step from that showing to the verdict that the arrangement should be abolished requires a conception of what the arrangement does to the beings inside it.

The point can be tested against the debate's own history. The structuralist literature of the heroic period is magnificent on reproduction and nearly silent on emancipation's content, and when its practitioners turned to politics they reached, as politicians do, for the vocabulary of dignity, freedom, and human need that their theory had placed under suspicion. Conversely, consider what happened when a structuralist did attempt to generate the normative from the structural: the theory of the state derived functions from the needs of capital with great ingenuity, and then faced the question why meeting capital's needs badly, in crisis, should be anyone's opportunity rather than merely everyone's misfortune. Crisis is a standard-neutral event. It becomes a revolutionary situation only for beings who could live otherwise and know it, which is the humanist subject returning through the tradesman's entrance. None of this shows that structuralism is false. It shows that it is incomplete in a specific, locatable way, and that its incompleteness is shaped exactly like the other camp's foundation, which is what a claim of mutual necessity means when it is being careful.

## What structuralism explains that humanism chronically cannot

The complementary incompleteness is just as locatable. Humanism explains, better than any rival, what is wrong with a durable injustice. It is chronically weak at explaining the durability. Ask the humanist account why an arrangement that frustrates the powers of nearly everyone inside it, and that nearly everyone inside it can be brought to criticise in the abstract, nevertheless renews itself each morning: why the workers arrive, why the forms are filed, why the children of critics are schooled into the same common sense, why revolts subside into restorations that the revolted then administer. The humanist repertoire contains answers, false consciousness chief among them, but they are names for the puzzle rather than solutions to it, since they must explain why consciousness is false in exactly the ways the arrangement requires, generation after generation, without a conspirator arranging it. Structuralism's entire apparatus exists to answer this question, and its answer is the camp's permanent contribution to social theory. Arrangements persist because they produce the conditions of their own continuation: the relations of production train the capacities they need, the apparatuses form subjects who experience the order's requirements as their own identity, the instances support one another in dominance, and the whole is reproduced, not by anyone's intention, but as the resultant of practices each of which makes local sense to its performer. Reproduction, not repression, is the deep explanation of order, and it was the structuralists who made that sentence unavoidable.

The explanatory gain shows in the debate's treatment of defeats. Humanist historiography, for all its archival glory, handles defeat as tragedy or betrayal: the subject was ready, the leaders failed, the moment passed. Structural analysis handles defeat as information: the conjuncture was misread, the apparatuses held, the alliance of instances that a rupture requires did not assemble, and the same actors with the same virtues would have lost again. The second kind of explanation is colder and more useful, and any movement that intends to win rather than to be commemorated needs it in its bones. It also shows in the analysis of consent under obvious domination, of nationalism's grip on the classes it disadvantages, of the authoritarian enjoyments that humanist psychology could only pathologise. Wherever the puzzle is the stability of the intolerable, the structural inheritance is the one that works. A division of labour that assigns durability to it, openly, is again not a compromise between two brands. It is an allocation of questions to the apparatuses that can answer them, which is what the attrition settlement had already achieved in practice before anyone stated it as a verdict.

### Why do unjust arrangements persist if nobody intends them to?

Because persistence does not require intention. Structures reproduce through the ordinary practices they organise: contracts signed, children schooled, roles inhabited, each act reasonable to its performer. The order's requirements are met as the by-product of everyone pursuing intelligible local ends, which is why denouncing the order's wickedness leaves its machinery untouched.

This is the point at which the two camps' concessions interlock most visibly. The humanist who has absorbed reproduction no longer expects exposure to emancipate, and designs criticism accordingly, aiming at the apparatuses and the practices rather than at the veil. The structuralist who has absorbed the standard no longer pretends that identifying the machinery settles what to do about it, and states his politics in the open, where they can be argued with. The resulting figure, the critic who knows both why the world persists and by what right he condemns it, was not a member of either camp in 1970. By the time the polemics burned out, he was the ordinary product of both, and the literature of the following decades is unintelligible except as his work.

## The strongest objection to the settlement, stated fairly

The verdict of mutual necessity has an obvious enemy, and honesty requires stating the enemy's case at full strength before answering it. The objection runs as follows. A division of labour between a normative humanism and an explanatory structuralism is incoherent, because the two positions are not tools for different jobs but contradictory answers to the same question. Either there is a human subject with an essence that history expresses and violates, or there is not. If there is, then structuralism's foundation is false, and its apparatus is an elaborate machine built on a denial of the being whose activity the machine purports to explain. If there is not, then humanism's standard is a fiction, and the criticism mounted in its name is a sermon borrowing the prestige of a theory it has refuted itself by accepting. To combine the two is not wisdom but convenience: it holds the anthropology on weekdays for the purposes of denunciation and the anti-anthropology on Sundays for the purposes of explanation, and a position that can only be used in alternation is not a position at all. The objection adds, with some justice, that the historical settlement proves nothing about coherence. Scholars are tired animals; a generation stops fighting for biographical reasons, and the peace of exhaustion is then retroactively baptised as a synthesis by commentators who prefer harmony to thought. On this view the mutual necessity is a fudge with a table.

The answer begins with a concession, and the concession is not tactical. At the level of philosophical anthropology, the incompatibility is real and unresolved. There is no consistent doctrine in which the subject both is the expressive origin of history and is exhaustively an effect of structures, and the attempts at a higher synthesis, including some famous ones, succeed by quietly deciding the question in one direction while keeping the other's vocabulary. If the reader demands a single, contradiction-free metaphysics of the subject, the dispute is still open, and the article's verdict does not pretend otherwise. But the objection proves too much when it moves from that concession to the charge of fudge, because division of labour is not alternation between contradictions when the questions are genuinely different. It is not contradictory to hold that an arrangement is to be judged by its effects on human capacities, and also that its persistence is to be explained by mechanisms that operate regardless of anyone's judgement of them, any more than it is contradictory for a physician to grade a fever as dangerous and to explain it by mechanisms that are indifferent to danger. The standard and the mechanism answer different interrogatives: by what right do we condemn this, and by what means does it continue. Contradiction would require the two answers to collide on the same question, and the history of the debate shows the collision happening only when one camp annexed the other's question, when humanists tried to derive persistence from frustration alone, or structuralists tried to derive condemnation from structure alone. Each annexation failed in public. The settlement is the map of those failures, drawn as borders. That is a verdict, not a fudge, because it is falsifiable in a way a fudge is not: produce a humanist explanation of reproduction that works, or a structuralist derivation of the standard that does not smuggle, and the borders move.

### Can the two positions be combined without contradiction?

They can be combined as a division of labour, not as a single doctrine. The combination assigns judgement to the humanist standard and explanation of persistence to the structural account, and it concedes openly that the underlying question about the subject remains unsettled at the level of philosophical anthropology.

One more consideration belongs to the objection, because it is the part its proponents usually leave out. The demand for a contradiction-free foundation assumes that a theory's parts must all be derivable from one answer to one question, and that assumption is itself a philosophical commitment, not a law of thought. Marx's own corpus, on any reading, combines a critique mounted in the name of human flourishing with an analysis of mechanisms that grind on without reference to it, and the refusal of the corpus to resolve into either camp's purity is the datum the whole debate started from. A settlement that preserves both registers, assigns each its jurisdiction, and marks the unresolved seam in plain sight may be less satisfying than a system. It has the compensating virtue of resembling its object.

## A break in a corpus, or a development within it?

Underneath the anthropology ran a philological dispute that any student of the debate must learn to separate from it, because the two are constantly confused in the secondary literature and the confusion serves both camps' rhetoric. The philological question is whether Marx's writings display a rupture around 1845 or a continuous development from the manuscripts to Capital. The anthropological question is whether there is a human essence. Althusser's construction fused them: the break was the event in which the essence was discarded, so that settling the philology settled the philosophy. His opponents accepted the fusion and fought on the same ground, hunting the late texts for surviving humanism as a miner hunts a seam. The mature treatment separates what the polemic joined. A break in a corpus is a claim about texts: about vocabulary, about the structure of the questions asked, about what a thinker at one date could and could not have written. A development within a corpus is equally a claim about texts. Either could be true while the anthropology goes either way, since a thinker may discard a vocabulary and keep the commitment the vocabulary carried, refunctioning it inside new concepts, or keep a vocabulary and empty it. The evidence, taken without the fusion, supports a verdict more precise than either camp's slogan. The vocabulary of Feuerbach, essence and alienation as technical terms, does recede after 1845, and the structure of the questions does change, from the critique of estrangement to the analysis of forms. The commitments carried by the old vocabulary, the condemnation of labour's deformation and the horizon of human powers freely developed, demonstrably persist into the work of the final decades, in the realm-of-freedom passage, in the treatment of the working day, in the notebooks. Rupture in the letter, continuity in the commitment, development in the concepts that mediate between them: that is the philological settlement as the best subsequent scholarship, including work by readers with no camp allegiance, has converged upon it.

Norman Geras gave the settlement its sharpest scholarly form in 1983, in a short book whose title announced its thesis: Marx and Human Nature, with the subtitle Refutation of a Legend. The legend was the claim, by then common property of both camps in different versions, that the mature Marx had abandoned the idea of a human nature altogether. Geras, a careful reader with philosophical training and no stake in either camp's politics, showed that the sixth thesis on Feuerbach denies a specific abstraction, an essence inherent in each isolated individual, and not the existence of transhistorical human needs, capacities, and vulnerabilities, which the mature texts invoke constantly and require for their arguments about exploitation and flourishing to make sense. The intervention mattered because it came from outside the exhausted geometry: here was the anthropology's minimal core, a nature thin enough to survive the structuralist critique of the substantial subject and solid enough to bear the humanist standard. Not everyone accepted the reading, and the debate about Geras became a small literature of its own, but the terms had shifted permanently. After 1983, the question was no longer whether Marx had a conception of human nature, but how thick it was and what work it could do. A dispute that ends by refining its question has not been fudged. It has been, in the only sense scholarship knows, resolved by being outgrown.

### How can a reader tell a rupture from a growth?

A break is a change in the structure of the questions: the old problems are not answered but dissolved, and the old key terms lose their jobs. A development is a change in the answers, where problems persist and are worked on with better instruments. Marx's corpus shows the first in its vocabulary and the second in its commitments.

The distinction pays rent beyond this debate, which is why it earns its place among the extra questions attached to this article. Students meeting any thinker with a long career and a polemical posterity, a Wittgenstein with his early and late phases, a Freud revising his topography, face the same fork, and the same temptation to let a philosophical preference dictate a philological verdict. The discipline the debate eventually taught is to run the two enquiries separately and to let each be interesting. Whether Marx broke with his youth is a question for the texts and the calendar. Whether there is a human essence is a question for philosophy. The camps' shared error, the fusion of the two, was not stupidity; it was strategy, since each camp's politics needed the founder's authority and the founder's authority could only be monopolised by owning the correct account of his development. When the monopoly ceased to matter, because the movements that needed it had themselves dissolved, the questions separated of their own weight, and scholarship got on with both.

## Not the same debate: agency and structure in general sociology

A persistent confusion, which the misreadings list in any good syllabus flags, identifies the humanist and structuralist quarrel with the agency and structure problem of general sociology. The two overlap, and the overlap is genuine: both ask how much of social life is made by acting subjects and how much is imposed by the orders within which they act. But they are not identical, and treating them as identical dissolves what is distinctive in each. The sociological problem is methodological and perennial; it arises for any social science, in any political colour, and its standard treatments, structuration theory among them, are tools for research design as much as for philosophy. The Marxist quarrel was fought inside a specific corpus and a specific movement. Its stakes were the correct reading of Marx's development, the scientific status of historical materialism, the authority of parties that governed in Marx's name, and the permissibility of condemning those parties in Marx's vocabulary. A sociologist can adopt a position on agency and structure at breakfast and change it by lunch without excommunicating anyone. A Marxist in 1970 who adopted the wrong side of the break might lose a journal, a party card, or a chair. The ferocity belongs to the second debate, and so does the settlement: the mutual necessity is a claim about Marxism's internal economy, about what its critique and its science each require, and it neither needs nor offers a solution to sociology's general problem, though sociologists are welcome to the tools, as several generations have helped themselves.

### Is the agency and structure problem the same argument?

It is a neighbouring argument, not the same one. The sociological problem is a standing methodological puzzle about explanation. The Marxist dispute was a struggle over a founder's corpus, the status of a science, and the right to criticise regimes in the founder's name, with parties and journals as its instruments and casualties.

The distinction also protects the article's verdict from an easy misapplication. Readers sometimes leave the debate narrative believing that the sensible position on everything is a bit of both, agency for the optimistic pages and structure for the pessimistic ones, allocated by mood. That is the fudge the objection warned against, and it is worth naming to exclude it. The division of labour defended here is jurisdictional, not atmospheric: the humanist standard governs every judgement in the analysis, at every point, and the structural account governs every explanation of persistence, at every point, and the analyst is not invited to swap them when one becomes inconvenient. What the analyst may not do is derive either from the other. Mood has nothing to do with it, and a reader who finds the combination unsatisfying should press on the seam marked earlier, the unresolved anthropology, rather than dissolving the jurisdictions into a general both-and.

## Sartre: the synthesis attempted from inside humanism

No account of the humanist camp is complete without its most ambitious internal dissenter, the thinker who agreed that the camp as he found it was inadequate and tried to rebuild it at a scale the structuralists could not dismiss as journalism. Sartre's Critique of Dialectical Reason, published in 1960 with the Search for a Method as its prospectus, accepted the structuralist era's central embarrassment before the structuralists had fully stated it: humanist Marxism, as practised, could not explain seriality, inertia, the way free projects congeal into counter-finalities that dominate their authors, the practico-inert field in which praxis returns to its makers as an alien power wearing no one's face. His solution was not to abandon the subject but to complicate its world: praxis remains intelligible and free at the level of the individual project, while worked matter, scarcity, and the series explain how millions of freedoms sum to an order none of them chose. The group-in-fusion, his account of a crowd becoming a collective agent under threat, and its decay into institution and oath, offered the humanist camp something it badly needed, a theory of how agency is real and yet produces structures that then govern agency, without either term being dissolved into the other.

The structuralists were not persuaded, and their reasons are instructive. From the Paris of the seminars, Sartre's edifice looked like the anthropological foundation rebuilt at greater expense: the translucency of praxis to itself, the intelligibility of the individual project as the atom of history, remained postulates of a philosophy of consciousness, and the whole vast machinery of the Critique struck its critics as an attempt to save the subject by describing, very brilliantly, everything that refutes it. Lévi-Strauss's closing attack in The Savage Mind of 1962, aimed at the primacy Sartre gave to history and to the dialectic over analytical reason, functioned within the Marxist debate as the structuralist manifesto's respectable elder brother. And yet the judgement of attrition has been kind to the attempt. The practico-inert is a concept of exactly the kind the settlement requires, a structural effect theorised by a humanist without surrendering the subject, and the series and the group have entered the vocabulary of writers who would not call themselves Sartreans. Sartre's own political itinerary, from fellow-travelling through the break over Hungary to the Maoist enthusiasms of his last decade, displayed every vice the structuralists attributed to humanism, voluntarism above all, and he displayed them so publicly that the camp's critics could treat the itinerary as the philosophy's refutation. It was nothing of the kind, and the separation of a position from its holder's biography is a discipline this article has applied to graver cases elsewhere in the cluster. The Critique stands, two generations on, as the proof that the humanist inheritance contained the resources for its own correction, which is the strongest thing that can be said for any inheritance.

## The two poles: a young man's manuscript and a mature preface

Every long dispute acquires sacred objects, and this one had two: a set of notebooks written by a twenty-six-year-old exile in Paris in 1844 and not published until 1932, and a preface of a few pages that the fifty-year-old author attached to his Critique of Political Economy in 1859. The early manuscripts and the mature Preface became the debate's two poles because each camp could make its entire case by choosing which object to stand beside. Beside the manuscripts, Marx is the philosopher of estranged labour and species-being, indignant, Hegelian, drunk on the recovery of man's essence, and the later work is the long footnote in which that vision acquires an economics. Beside the Preface, Marx is the scientist summarising his guiding thread in lapidary prose: the mode of production of material life conditions the social, political, and intellectual life process in general, it is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence but their social existence that determines their consciousness, and epochs are to be distinguished by the economic foundations that crack their legal and political superstructures open. The Preface does not mention an essence. The manuscripts do not mention a mode of production. Two Marxes, two relics, and a generation of argument about which relic was the true cross.

The polemic obscured what a calmer scholarship can state plainly: the two texts are different instruments written for different occasions, and the opposition between them was manufactured by the uses to which they were put. The manuscripts are exploratory notebooks, a young thinker arguing with Hegel and Feuerbach and the economists simultaneously, never prepared for publication and unknown to every Marxist, including Engels in his later editorial labours and including Lenin and Lukacs, until the twentieth century was a third over. Their publication in 1932 was itself an event inside the prehistory of the debate, for it handed the humanists their charter at the very moment the orthodoxies were freezing: here was the founder, in his own hand, saying that communism is the riddle of history solved, in a vocabulary the official doctrine had forgotten. The Preface, by contrast, is a public summary, retrospective and compressed, written to orient readers of a difficult book and to stake a claim of discovery; authors' prefaces are advertisements for their own method, and treating five pages of advertisement as the whole science is a mistake no structuralist would make with any other author. Each camp therefore chose its relic for extrinsic reasons and then read the corpus from it. The manuscripts served those who needed a Marx who could condemn the present in the name of man's essence. The Preface served those who needed a Marx who had superseded philosophy. The corpus, inconveniently, kept supplying the other camp's quotations, which is the empirical reason the dispute could not be closed by scholarship and had to be closed, eventually, by the demarcation this article defends.

### Why did the manuscripts and the Preface become the two poles?

Because each is short, quotable, and totalising in a different direction. The manuscripts offer a complete philosophy in embryo, essence, estrangement, recovery; the Preface offers a complete method in miniature, foundations, superstructures, determination. A camp could found itself on either text and read the rest of Marx as commentary on its relic.

There is a lesson in the selection that students should be taught to notice, because it recurs in every tradition that argues over a founder. The texts that become poles are rarely the founder's largest or most considered works; Capital, which neither camp could digest into a slogan, sat in the middle of the quarrel being quoted by both and owned by neither. Poles are chosen for portability. A position that must be carried into party schools, seminar rooms, and the prefaces of translations needs a charter that fits in an anthology extract, and both relics fit. Recognising the portability principle does not discredit either charter. It discredits the inference from a charter's convenience to its completeness, and it explains why the settlement, when it came, took the form of returning the relics to the shelf of a much larger library and getting on with the work.

## Lukacs, the ancestor both camps had to claim

The strangest figure in the debate is the one who belonged fully to neither camp because he had anticipated both before either existed. The Lukacs of 1923, writing History and Class Consciousness without knowledge of the 1844 manuscripts, produced in the essay on reification the humanist camp's deepest concept from purely Hegelian and Weberian materials: the commodity form penetrates consciousness itself, the world appears to its inhabitants as a second nature of calculable things, and the standpoint of the proletariat is epistemically privileged because that class experiences itself as commodity and can therefore know the whole from inside its own objectification. The structuralists could not dismiss this book, since it was the acknowledged ancestor of every Western Marxism, their own included, and Gramsci's parallel construction descended from the same season. They therefore performed an act of partition. The young Lukacs was enrolled as a precursor whose Hegelianism the science had to overcome, his concept of totality was retained in the structural form of the articulated whole, and his account of class consciousness as imputed, ascribed to the class by theory rather than read off from its members' heads, was quietly recognised as the ancestor of their own anti-spontaneism, the point where the humanist saint had been a structuralist before the word. The humanists performed the mirror partition: the later Lukacs, recanting under pressure and labouring in the orthodox vineyard for decades, was treated as a tragedy of the party intellectual, while the book of 1923 was kept as the charter. The article on Lukacs and reification in this series treats the man whole; the comparison needs only the partition's lesson, which is that the ancestors of a dispute are always conscripted, and that conscription is itself evidence about the dispute. A camp that must steal the other's founder, or partition its own, is conceding that the founder's work contains both registers. Marx's corpus contains both. So does Lukacs's single book. The settlement's demarcation is written into the sources from the beginning, waiting for the polemics to exhaust themselves enough to read it.

## Gramsci, the third presence in the room

Between the expressive subject of the humanists and the subjectless process of the structuralists stands a figure both camps recruited and neither could digest whole. Gramsci, writing his notebooks in a fascist prison in the decade before the manuscripts' humanism and the break were codified as alternatives, built his theory around hegemony, the organisation of consent, and his account has the peculiar property of satisfying each camp's deepest demand while violating each camp's foundation. Consent is manufactured: through the institutions of civil society, through intellectuals who elaborate a class's worldview into common sense, through the slow war of position in the trenches of culture, which is the structuralist insight about reproduction stated without the structuralist metaphysics. And consent is organised by subjects who can be won, lost, and re-educated: the national-popular collective will is an agent with a biography, formed in struggle and capable of forming itself, which is the humanist demand stated without the humanist anthropology, since Gramsci's subject is a historical bloc and not an essence. The later quarrel between the Gramscians and the Althusserians, treated in [its own article](/2015/04/15/gramsci-vs-althusser/) in this series, was the camps' proxy war over the inheritance, and its inconclusiveness is diagnostic. Hegemony theory flourished precisely where the two camps' successor practices merged, in the cultural studies that took ideology from Paris and audiences from the historians, because the concept itself is the demarcation wearing a single word: a structure that exists only in the subjects it forms, and subjects who exist only in the structures that form them, with the argument relocated to the level of institutions where it can be researched rather than the level of essences where it can only be repeated.

### Why does hegemony resist assignment to either camp?

Because the concept was built to describe the middle region both camps under-theorised: the institutions where consent is produced and contested. It grants the structuralists their machinery of formation and grants the humanists their forming subjects, and it declines to found either grant on a philosophy of the subject or on its abolition.

The Gramsci case generalises into a rule of thumb for reading the whole debate. Whenever a concept from the period continues to do work in later scholarship, inspect its structure, and it will usually be found to straddle the fork: hegemony, the practico-inert, the moral economy in its sophisticated uses, ideology as terrain of struggle. The concepts that died with the camps were the pure ones, the expressive totality in its undiluted form and the subjectless process in its undiluted form, because pure concepts could organise a polemic but not a research programme. Posterity's vote, cast in citations rather than manifestos, went to the straddlers. That vote is not philosophy and does not settle the anthropology. It does settle the practical question of where a student should invest reading time, and it corroborates, from an independent direction, the division of labour this article proposes: the tradition's own survivors are the concepts that refused to choose between the standard and the machine.

## Political strategy: the bottom row, where the abstractions cashed out

The table's last row deserves its own section, because participants on all sides privately agreed that the other five rows were fought as proxies for it. Strategy is where a theory of the subject becomes an organisation chart. If history has a subject whose self-emancipation is the content of socialism, then the party is at most the subject's midwife and school, dangerous exactly in proportion as it substitutes itself for the class whose experience is the tribunal, and the humanist tradition accordingly shades towards councilism, towards the early Lukacs's party as the form of the class's consciousness rather than its command, towards Thompson's fierce libertarianism and the Praxis school's self-management. If subjects are effects and ideology is the medium of their formation, then spontaneity is the name of the trap, the party or the theoretical instance must stand in a relation of exteriority to the class it serves, and the structural tradition accordingly shades towards a defence of organisation as the bearer of a science the class cannot generate unaided, a defence its critics heard, not always unfairly, as the philosophy of the apparatus. The terrible resonance of the second position in the century of the apparatuses needs no underlining, and the humanists underlined it relentlessly. The symmetrical weakness of the first position, its chronic inability to explain why the subject keeps failing to emancipate itself on schedule and why midwives keep becoming jailers, was the structuralists' standing reply.

The honest summary is that neither camp produced a strategy that survived contact with the century. The self-emancipation tradition inspired the century's most attractive movements and watched them dissolve or be crushed, blaming the conjuncture with diminishing returns. The party-of-science tradition produced organisations of formidable durability whose durability became their refutation, since the apparatuses reproduced themselves magnificently and emancipated no one the humanists could recognise. The settlement's demarcation offers strategy a discipline rather than a doctrine: judge every organisational form by the humanist standard, does it enlarge or cramp the capacities of the people inside it, and explain every outcome by the structural method, which apparatuses formed the actors, which instances held, where the rupture failed to generalise. A movement educated in both questions would at least fail differently. That the two camps' strategic heirs, the libertarian socialists and the party Marxists, spent the decades after the debate mostly talking past a world that had stopped organising itself around either banner is a fact about the century, not about the questions, which return whenever people organise against an order and ask what they are doing and why it is so hard.

## The science question underneath the anthropology

The anthropology was the debate's flag, but its engine room was a dispute about knowledge: what kind of knowing is Marxism, and what entitles it to obedience? The structuralist answer was the more ambitious. Marx had founded a science, the science of history, as Galileo had founded a mechanics and Darwin a biology, and the mark of the foundation was the break with the object's self-image. On this account the theory's authority is the authority of a discipline: its concepts are produced by theoretical practice according to criteria internal to the practice, its results are knowledge effects verifiable in the practice's own terms, and disagreement is adjudicated as sciences adjudicate, by the fertility and rigour of the work. The party's claim to lead received, in this construction, an epistemological warrant with a long pedigree in the tradition: if there is a science of society, those who possess it possess something the class needs and cannot spontaneously generate, a warrant Lenin's theory of the vanguard had asserted politically and the structural reconstruction asserted philosophically. The humanist answer denied the premise. Marxism, on the humanist account, is a critique, a form of knowledge that knows itself to be inside its object: the theorist is a member of the society analysed, the categories are weapons in a struggle the theory itself joins, and the criterion of truth includes the practical one, whether the analysis arms the subject it describes. Authority in this construction is democratic in tendency if not always in practice: the theory must answer to the experience of those it concerns, because they are its object and its jury in one.

Neither answer survived the century's epistemology intact. The structuralist philosophy of science, drawn from a French tradition of historical epistemology that remains respectable in its home discipline, was received in the Anglophone world in the decades when the demarcation between science and non-science was itself dissolving under historical scrutiny, and the picture of a knowledge sealed inside its practice struck outsiders as a description of every closed doctrine that has ever existed, theology included. The camp's own best students felt the draught: within a few years of the heroic period, former Althusserians were publishing self-criticisms of theoreticism, of the guarantee the theory had issued to itself, and the master's own later writings conceded that the science and ideology distinction had been drawn with a rationalist's overconfidence. The humanist epistemology fared no better in the opposite direction. Answering to experience is a fine criterion until the experiencers disagree, as they reliably do, at which point the theory must either discount the experience it dislikes, reimporting the vanguard through the back door in the person of the historian who knows which testimony counts, or dissolve into a parliament of standpoints with no procedure for decision. The settlement's demarcation extends to this level too: the structural inheritance survives as a demand for mechanism and for suspicion of self-images, including the critic's own; the humanist inheritance survives as a demand for accountability, that the mechanism's description be brought back to the people it describes and tested against their recognition. A Marxism that keeps both demands is harder to practise than either camp's doctrine. The record suggests it is also the only version that kept producing knowledge after the camps dissolved.

### What kind of truth did each side claim?

The structuralist claimed the truth of a science: impersonal, produced by a practice, indifferent to the comfort of its objects. The humanist claimed the truth of a critique: reflexive, located inside its object, vindicated when the subject it describes recognises itself in the description and acts on the recognition.

The two claims generate different literatures, and the debate's bibliography divides accordingly with almost comic neatness. Structuralist writing is systematic, architectonic, given to the reconstruction of concepts and the exposure of problematics; it reads like engineering and ages like engineering, impressively and with obsolete parts. Humanist writing is essayistic, historical, biographical, given to the recovery of lost voices and the moral weighing of episodes; it reads like literature and ages like literature, unevenly and with permanent passages. A student deciding where to begin should know that the quarrel of the camps survives in the physical form of their books, and that reading only one shape of book is the surest way to inherit one camp whole.

## The two Freuds: depth psychology as a proxy war

One of the debate's strangest theatres was psychoanalysis, which each camp conscripted as its psychology and read in opposite directions. The humanists had Fromm, for whom Freud's discovery, corrected of its mechanistic biology, revealed a human nature with needs for relatedness, rootedness, and transcendence that a sick society frustrates and a sane one could satisfy; the Sane Society of 1955 is the camp's anthropology with a clinician's casebook attached, and its enormous readership carried Marxist humanism into suburbs that never heard of the epistemological break. The structuralists had the Freud of the return to Freud, mediated by Jacques Lacan: a Freud whose unconscious is structured like a language, whose subject is split and constituted by the signifier, an effect of a symbolic order that precedes and produces it, which is to say a theoretical anti-humanism with a couch. That the same founder could equip both camps is not an accident of Freud's ambiguity alone. Psychoanalysis is the discipline that promises knowledge of the subject's interior, and a dispute about whether there is a subject will always fight over the discipline that seems to have the subject's measurements. The humanists measured a nature and its needs; the structuralists measured an effect and its determinations. The theatre closed as the others did, by attrition and absorption: the social character of Fromm and the symbolic order of the Lacanians both passed into the common stock of a cultural theory that used whichever it needed, and the question of which Freud was the real one joined the question of which Marx was the real one in the museum of unanswerable inheritances, where the correct label, in both cases, is that the founder contained the quarrel, as founders do.

## The third party nobody invited: official doctrine

Accounts of the debate usually stage it as a duel, but both camps defined themselves against a third presence that neither invited and both needed as a foil: the official dialectical materialism of the Soviet textbooks, the catechism of matter in motion and stages of history that governed the philosophy faculties of the socialist states and the education of party cadres everywhere. Against that catechism, humanists and structuralists were allies before they were enemies, and remembering the alliance corrects a common misreading of both. The humanists hated the catechism because it turned Marx into a cosmology and a police science, dissolving the subject into laws of nature and society whose administrators needed no one's consent. The structuralists hated it because it was, in their vocabulary, an ideology in scientific dress, an empiricist evolutionism that had never undergone a break with anything. Each camp's founding texts spend real ammunition on the catechism: the early Althusser's polemics against economism and the humanists' polemics against the textbook's iron stages are aimed at the same target from different ridges. The alliance failed because the successor doctrines they built were incompatible, but the shared enemy explains a feature of the debate that baffles readers who meet it cold, namely its participants' insistence that enormous stakes rode on fine distinctions. They were not only arguing with each other. Each was arguing that the other's doctrine, whatever its merits against the catechism, would in power become a new catechism, and the century gave each side's prophecy enough confirmations to keep the argument supplied with dread. A fair comparison records the third party's presence for a further reason: the camps' positions look more moderate, and more alike, when the textbook is in the room. Both were, by the standards of the official doctrine, heresies of remarkable sophistication, and the energy of their quarrel was partly the energy of heretics who agreed about the church.

## The Frankfurt complication

A further complication for any tidy map of the camps is the tradition the map usually flattens: the Frankfurt School, treated across this cluster of the series in its own articles, whose founding figures agree with each camp against the other in a pattern that scrambles the partition. Horkheimer and Adorno wrote, in the Dialectic of Enlightenment, an account of the administered world in which the subject is indeed produced, damaged, and integrated by the machinery it inhabits, an analysis the structuralists could cite as a precedent for their anti-humanism arrived at by a different road. The same book is a threnody for the mutilated subject, written in the conviction that what the machinery destroys is precisely the human possibility whose loss the humanists existed to name, and its younger colleague Marcuse built his most famous work on a Freudian anthropology of needs, false and true, that belongs squarely in the humanist column while reaching conclusions about one-dimensionality that the structuralists recognised as their own thesis in another vocabulary. The Frankfurt figures predate the camps' codification and never enlisted, and their work therefore serves the comparison as a control experiment. Thinkers who never read the quarrel's terms into their problems arrived, under the pressure of the same century, at positions that straddle the fork: the machinery theorised, the subject mourned and demanded, the standard kept and the psychology of its defeat analysed. The straddle is what independent arrival looks like, and it supports the article's verdict from an angle independent of the camps' own concessions. Where serious people, unbribed by either camp's institutions, worked on the same realities, they produced the division of labour spontaneously, because the realities required both halves.

## The debate beyond its European theatres

The quarrel was Parisian and London-centred in its famous episodes, but its materials circulated worldwide through the translation networks of the Marxist left, and the receptions abroad were not copies. In the United States the humanist side arrived first and strongest, carried by Fromm's bestsellers and by the independent socialist journals that had been printing the young Marx since the 1950s, Raya Dunayevskaya's Marxism and Freedom of 1958 being the remarkable American monument of the tendency, a work that built an entire organisation's doctrine on the humanism of the manuscripts and the Hegelian dialectic. Structuralism arrived later, through the universities in the 1970s, and found a country with no mass party to fight over, which changed its function: where in Paris the doctrine had been a factional weapon, in the American academy it became a research style, valued for its rigour in literary and film studies long after its party context had evaporated, and immune there to several of the political charges that had wounded it at home. In Latin America the reception fused with the debates over dependency and revolution: the humanist vocabulary of liberation, amplified by the region's own traditions and by a theology that reached similar conclusions from another founder, armed movements of the dispossessed, while structural categories, mode of production, social formation, the articulation of instances, equipped the great controversy over whether the continent's countryside was feudal or capitalist, a controversy with lives riding on its answer. The camps' categories thus had second careers in places where the original quarrel's stakes were unknown, and the second careers confirm the settlement's logic from a third direction: the tools were adopted piecemeal, by need, by movements and disciplines that had never signed either manifesto, and a tool adopted piecemeal by strangers is a tool whose usefulness does not depend on its founder's system.

## Two public tests: a spring and an autumn

Twice in the debate's lifetime, events staged a public experiment on the rival philosophies, and both camps' handling of the results is part of the record a fair comparison must keep. The first test was the spring of 1968 in France, when a student revolt detonated a general strike of ten million workers and the party and union apparatuses, in the structuralists' own city, worked to contain and conclude the movement their philosophy had not predicted. Humanists drew the moral of the subject: history's agent had appeared, unbidden by any structure's timetable, and a theory of subjects as effects stood refuted by the barricades, while its author was mocked on the walls of his own institution as a fossil of the order. Structuralists drew the opposite moral from the sequel: within weeks, the apparatuses had reabsorbed the explosion, the elections restored the government with an enlarged majority, and the decade that followed belonged to the reproduction the theory described, so that the festival had demonstrated exactly the distance between an event and a transformation. The second test was the autumn of 1989 and its sequels, when the regimes of the East dissolved with a speed no camp had foreseen. The dissident humanists' heirs could claim a vindication of the standard: orders that cramp human powers had been judged, in the streets of their own capitals, by the beings inside them. The structuralists' heirs could claim a vindication of the method: the dissolutions were conjunctural cascades through weakened apparatuses, nationalisms interpellating the crowds that the language of universal humanity had expected to speak, and what followed was decided by structures of property and geopolitics, not by the recovered essence of man. Both claims are true, and they are true of different aspects of the same events, in the same proportions the verdict predicts. The standard judged; the machinery decided the judgement's consequences. A reader who can hold the two sentences together has understood the debate's outcome better than either camp's obituaries understood it.

## Totality: the concept both camps needed in incompatible forms

If the debate has a single technical term on which everything else pivots, it is totality. Both camps were holists against the empiricism that studies society in slices; both insisted that a fact means what it means only inside the whole that produces it. They differed on the whole's architecture, and the difference is the fork stated in systems language. The humanist whole is expressive: each part expresses the essence of the whole as a microcosm expresses a macrocosm, the factory expresses the society, the society expresses the stage of the subject's self-alienation, and understanding runs from the essence outward, which is why the young Lukacs could call his method the standpoint of totality and mean by it the proletariat's capacity to know society as its own product. The structuralist whole is articulated: the instances are irreducible to one another, each has its own effectivity and tempo, no part expresses the whole, and the whole exists only in the differential play of its parts under a dominance that the economy assigns in the last instance, an instance whose lonely hour, in the camp's most quoted joke against itself, never comes. Karel Kosik, the Czech humanist whose Dialectics of the Concrete appeared in 1963, attempted the period's most serious repair from the humanist side: totality as concrete, as the structured and developing whole that is neither a sum nor an expression but a reality known through praxis, the concept of the pseudo-concrete naming the world of fetishised appearances that structural analysis at its best also dispelled. The repair did not reunite the camps, since the structuralists heard praxis and reached for their suspicion of the subject, but it marked the spot where a reconciliation would have to be built, and the settlement's descendants, in the realist philosophies of science that later gave Marxist social theory a second academic life, built near the spot: wholes that are structured and open, mechanisms that are real and laminated, subjects that are formed and, in forming, causal. The vocabulary is no longer the camps' vocabulary. The architecture is the demarcation, inherited.

### What did the two camps mean by totality?

An expressive whole, for the humanists: every part manifests the essence of the whole, so that to grasp the essence is to grasp the parts. An articulated whole, for the structuralists: the parts are distinct instances with their own effects, joined under a dominance none of them expresses, so that the whole is known only by mapping the joins.

The totality dispute is the place to test a reader's understanding of the whole comparison, because it resists translation into the language of sides. Nobody in the debate defended fragmentation; the empiricist who studies the strike, the school, and the sermon as unrelated topics was the common enemy, mocked by Thompson as a collector of antiquarian facts and by the Parisians as a positivist. The quarrel was between two holisms, and two holisms can share an enemy for decades without sharing a doctrine. That is also why the dispute kept recruiting fresh combatants from adjacent disciplines: any field that must decide whether its object is an expression or an articulation, a text, a mind, an economy, will find the camps' arguments waiting for it, pre-built. The literature of the mind, of the novel, of the firm, all have their expressive and articulated parties, usually ignorant that the templates were machined in the Marxist workshops of the 1960s. Recognising the template is part of what this series exists to teach.

## The missing subject arrives: feminist interventions

Both camps were eventually indicted by a movement that found their shared map missing the territory it knew best. Feminist theorists of the 1970s observed that the humanist subject, whose essence and emancipation were at issue, was described in a masculine grammar that its holders rarely noticed, and that the structuralist machinery, for all its account of how subjects are formed, had remarkably little to say about the formation of women in the family, the first apparatus on its own list. The intervention that mattered most to the debate's internal history came from inside the structural inheritance: Juliet Mitchell, whose essay of 1966 on women as the longest revolution and whose Psychoanalysis and Feminism of 1974 took the Althusserian and Lacanian tools, ideology, the symbolic order, the constitution of the subject, and turned them on the question the camps had neglected, arguing that women's oppression was overdetermined by several structures, production, reproduction, sexuality, and the socialisation of children, no one of which could be reduced to another. The humanist tools served the same generation differently: the recovered vocabulary of experience and of consciousness-raising treated women's testimony as evidence no theory could override, in Thompson's spirit if not at his direction. The socialist-feminist debates that followed, over dual systems and unified theories, restaged the camps' entire geometry on new ground, which is the sincerest form of succession. The point for this comparison is not to award the debate a posthumous relevance it did not earn in its own terms. It is to note that when a genuinely new subject of history presented its credentials, the tools it reached for came from both camps' boxes, chosen by the work they could do. Attrition's settlement, once again, was ratified by strangers with no stake in the quarrel, and a settlement ratified by strangers is as close as intellectual history gets to a proof.

## The concessions, counted in the record

The battlegrounds table asserted concessions; this section exhibits them, because a verdict resting on concessions should show its evidence. Begin inside the structural citadel. Althusser himself, in the self-critical texts of the mid-1970s collected as Essays in Self-Criticism, conceded that his earlier formulations had been marked by theoreticism, a rationalist deviation in which the theory had effectively guaranteed its own results, and he re-described the break and the science in chastened terms, insisting that philosophy was, in the last instance, class struggle in the field of theory, a formula that reintroduced the politics his system had bracketed. The concession did not dissolve the school, but it withdrew the school's charter of infallibility from the master's own hand. His students drew their own conclusions at speed. Jacques Rancière, a contributor to Reading Capital, published in 1974 a book-length repudiation of the master's lesson, arguing that the doctrine of the break had functioned to silence the speech of the workers it claimed to serve, a humanist indictment written from inside the seminar. In Britain, Barry Hindess and Paul Hirst, whose Pre-Capitalist Modes of Production of 1975 had been the most rigorous English exercise in the structural manner, followed it within two years with a self-demolition, Mode of Production and Social Formation, rejecting the theoretical framework of their own book root and branch; whatever the second book's merits, the episode taught a generation that the structural system, pursued honestly, consumed its own foundations. The camp's machinery survived these defections, as machinery does, in other people's workshops. Its doctrine of itself did not.

The humanist side's concessions are quieter, because the camp had less system to retract, but they are as real. Thompson himself, the polemicist of 1978, spent the following decade in campaigns whose practice embodied the structural lesson: the peace movement he helped to lead analysed the arms race as a structure, a self-reproducing system of exterminism with its own institutional logic, in an essay of 1980 that structuralists could admire and did, and his later historical work grew ever more attentive to the languages and rituals that form experience from inside. The historians who succeeded him built the formation of experience into their method as a matter of course; the tribunal of experience continued to sit, but it now took evidence about its own constitution before hearing the case. The dissident humanisms of the East, after 1989, faced the harder reckoning that their standard, triumphant as judgement, had predicted nothing about what would follow the regimes they condemned, and the survivors' memoirs are full of the discovery that a measuring rod is not a map. Meanwhile the figure who embodied the settlement most completely had been at work since the beginning of the period's end: Stuart Hall, formed by the first New Left and by Thompson's circle, took from Althusser the concepts of articulation and overdetermination, stripped them of the system's guarantees, and built with them, and with Gramsci, a Marxism without guarantees, his phrase and his programme, in which structures constrain and enable, subjects are formed and form themselves, and no last instance arrives to settle the account. Hall's enormous influence on the study of culture is the settlement's largest institutional fact. The camps have museums and syllabi. The settlement has descendants.

### Why does each camp keep producing the other?

The structuralists explained humanism as the ideology of the movement itself: the self-image a rising class and its intellectuals require, flattering and false. The humanists explained structuralism as the ideology of the academy and the apparatus: a doctrine that dissolves inconvenient subjects suits institutions that prefer their subjects dissolved. Each explanation is partly true and wholly insufficient.

The symmetry is worth pausing over, because it marks the exact spot where the dispute became incapable of self-resolution. Each camp possessed a theory of error powerful enough to explain why the other camp existed, and a theory that explains its opponents' existence explains away their arguments without answering them, since a answered argument must first be heard as an argument and not as a symptom. The debate's worst passages are all written in the symptomatic mode, on both sides: reviews that diagnose instead of refuting, the opponent's class position, generation, or institutional interest produced as a substitute for his reasons. The debate's best passages, Thompson on the orrery when he is actually engaging the conceptual machinery, Anderson almost throughout, Althusser in his concessions, share the opposite discipline of taking the other side's strongest form as the target. The settlement became possible when the second discipline became habitual in the successor generation, who had been taught by both camps and owed obedience to neither. There is a general lesson here that the series has met before in other clusters: a dispute is usually ended not by the side with the better arguments but by the arrival of readers who need both sides' tools for work neither side foresaw. The feminist intervention, the cultural studies synthesis, the conjunctural analyses of the long crisis of the 1970s, were that work. The tools were taken up, the banners were left in the hall, and the quarrel of the subject passed into the keeping of comparisons like this one, whose duty is to keep both the tools and the reasons for the quarrel in working order.

## What the quarrel did to the reading of Capital

The debate's most technical legacy lies in how its survivors learned to read the book both camps claimed. The structural reading had taught a generation to approach Capital as a constructed object: to attend to the order of the exposition, to the concepts' systematic relations rather than to the narrative of suffering the surface offers, and to treat the opening chapters on the commodity as a theory of the value-form whose logic governs the whole. In Germany a parallel movement, the new reading of Marx associated with Hans-Georg Backhaus and Helmut Reichelt, reached a similar anti-historicism from the text itself, arguing that the value theory had been flattened by friend and foe into a labour-embodied accounting and that the monetary form was the theory's beating heart. The humanist reading had taught the complementary discipline: to read Capital as a book about people and their hours, to keep the working day chapter and the chapters on machinery and accumulation at the centre, where the abstraction repeatedly lands on bodies, and to treat the theoretical architecture as the servant of an indictment the architecture exists to deliver. The successor scholarship, at its best, simply declined to choose: the value-form analysis now proceeds with full structural rigour in monographs whose prefaces announce a commitment to the critique of political economy as a critique of a world that mutilates its makers, and nobody involved experiences the combination as a contradiction, because by the jurisdictional settlement it is none. The form is the machinery; the mutilation is the standard; the book contains both, as it always had.

## The nature question: the subject both camps forgot was not only human

A later generation's most searching complaint against both camps is that they fought over the human subject while the non-human world waited outside the seminar door. The point is largely just, and its partial exceptions illuminate the camps once more. The structural apparatus, with its instances and its dominance assigned in the last instance by an economy conceived as a social relation, had no obvious place for nature as an actor with its own recalcitrance; matter entered as raw material and as the forgotten condition of every instance. The humanist tradition contained one great exception: Alfred Schmidt, a Frankfurt figure, whose study of the concept of nature in Marx, published in 1962, showed the founder's materialism to be a philosophy of the metabolism between society and a nature that is never exhausted by its social forms, and did so with the humanists' characteristic attention to the early writings. Schmidt's book was read by both camps and owned by neither, the structuralists finding its mediation talk too Hegelian, the humanists finding its conclusion, that nature sets limits no praxis abolishes, too cold for the philosophy of hope. When ecological crisis moved the metabolism question to the centre of Marxist work decades later, it was Schmidt's exception and the late Marx's own notebooks on soil and chemistry that the new work built on, and the camps' mainstreams had little to contribute. The episode is recorded here not as a reproach but as a calibration of the verdict. The mutual necessity governs the ground the camps actually fought over, the subject and the structure. It does not canonise their shared blind spots, and a settlement that cannot list its own borders invites the next dispute to be fought by its heirs in the same symptomatic style. The borders, then: the anthropology is demarcated, not solved; the machinery is inherited, not worshipped; and the world outside the subject, the nature in which every social formation metabolises, awaits the synthesis that neither camp attempted and that any successor theory must supply from materials both would have recognised and neither possessed.

## The Italian prequel: a science against humanism before Paris

The French quarrel had an Italian prequel that its participants acknowledged and its historians sometimes forget, and the prequel matters because it shows the fork arising independently wherever the same conditions assembled: a mass party, an official philosophy, and a generation determined to purge it. Galvano Della Volpe, working from the 1950s, had already opposed to the Hegelian and humanist Marx of the Italian tradition a Marx of determinate abstractions and Galilean method, a logic of the concrete concept against the dialectic of the subject, and his student Lucio Colletti sharpened the construction into the period's most formidable anti-Hegelian Marxism, arguing that the materialism of Marx was incompatible with the dialectical machinery the humanists loved and that Kant, not Hegel, was the philosophy's true interlocutor. When the Parisian texts arrived, Italian readers therefore met them as reinforcements in a domestic war, not as revelations, and the Italian debate proceeded on its own track, with its own casualty list: Colletti himself eventually walked out of Marxism altogether, judging that the science he had reconstructed could not bear the politics built on it, an exit the French camps studied with the attention due to a possible future. Italy also generated the period's most original strategic heresy from the humanist side of the street: the workerism of Mario Tronti and the journals around him, which inverted the structural picture by fiat of the will, insisting that the working class's struggles are the independent variable and capital's restructuring the dependent response, so that the subject the Parisians dissolved returned as the motor of the whole analysis, wearing overalls. Workerism's descendants carried the inversion into theories of autonomy that conquered parts of a later left. The Italian theatre thus compresses the comparison's whole argument into one national case: the science and the subject arose as rival purges of the same official doctrine, each conquered a province, each overreached, and the work that lasted was done by those who raided both.

## Alienation goes to the laboratory

One measure of the humanist camp's reach is the journey of its central concept out of philosophy altogether. In 1959 the American sociologist Melvin Seeman, working in a discipline with no stake in the camps, operationalised alienation into five measurable dimensions, powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, isolation, and self-estrangement, and launched a thousand questionnaires. Robert Blauner's Alienation and Freedom of 1964 then took the concept into the factory with survey data, comparing technologies and finding the assembly line's workers the most estranged and the continuous-process operators the least, a result the humanist philosophers read with complicated feelings. Their concept had achieved empirical respectability at the price of its soul: the measuring instruments captured the worker's reported feelings, which the structuralists could have told the sociologists, and did tell anyone who asked, are the least reliable data about the worker's situation, while the philosophical core of the concept, the deformation of powers that might not be felt as deprivation at all, drained away between the questionnaire items. The episode is the standard's career in miniature and deserves its place in the record because it poses the demarcation's practical question in a form a student can test. When alienation is measured as feeling, the humanist inheritance has been reduced to psychology and the structural objection stands unanswered. When alienation is refused measurement altogether as a philosopher's essence, the standard floats free of the world it judges and the empiricist's objection stands unanswered. The work that honoured both demands, the studies of the labour process that followed Harry Braverman's intervention of 1974, used the standard to choose their questions and structural method to answer them: which machinery, which division of labour, which destruction of craft, and by what right the destruction is named a loss. The name of the loss is the humanist's contribution. The account of the machinery is the structuralist's. The book that holds both is the settlement, shelved in a different aisle.

## How the camps travelled: journals, translations, generations

Intellectual camps are carried by machinery, and a comparison that has praised machinery as an explanation should apply it to the debate itself. The structuralist ascendancy in the Anglophone world was built by identifiable apparatuses: a review in London whose editorial collective, after 1962 under Anderson's direction, made itself the transmission belt for continental theory; a publishing house attached to it that issued the translations in uniform editions a student could collect; a reading group culture in the new universities where difficult texts conferred distinction on their initiates. The humanist ascendancy had older and warmer machinery: the history workshop movement that carried Thompson's methods into hundreds of local groups, the paperback editions of the manuscripts that put the young Marx into coat pockets, the adult education classes where the standard was taught as a birthright. Generational succession oiled both machines. To be young in a university in 1970 was to experience the humanist elders as the establishment, however scarred by exile from their parties, and structuralism arrived as the insurgent's rigour, a way of being more radical than one's teachers that required only the mastery of a difficult vocabulary. To be young in 1980 was to experience the structuralist idiom as the establishment's password and to rediscover, in the historians and the dissidents, the insurgent's warmth. The camps' philosophical contents rode on these successions without being reducible to them, and the participants who understood the machinery best, Anderson pre-eminent among them, were precisely those who could see their own camp as a formation and were therefore least surprised when the formation dissolved. The sociology does not refute the philosophy. It explains the philosophy's career, which is the structuralist half of the settlement applied to the settlement's own history, and the application is left standing as this article's small act of methodological symmetry.

## Why the question keeps coming back

The final misreading to correct is the gentlest and the most consequential: that the dispute belongs to the history of ideas, a period piece to be summarised and shelved. The dispute's surface is dated, certainly. Its parties are dissolved, its journals are archives, its slogans require footnotes. But the dispute was never, at bottom, about its slogans. It was the Marxist form of a question that no theory of society escapes: when people act, is the actor the explanation's beginning or its result, and when an order is condemned, whose injury does the condemning? Every successor vocabulary re-asks it. The theorist of ideology who explains a belief by its function must still say why the explanation is a criticism. The theorist of agency who celebrates resistance must still explain why resistance is rare, local, and so often absorbed. The analyst of platforms and algorithms inherits the apparatuses in new materials and faces the old fork at the first page: are the users the subjects of the system, whose thwarted powers measure it, or its effects, whose desires the system manufactures and harvests? Positions taken in ignorance of the old debate are usually the old positions with the footnotes removed, which means the weaknesses arrive unannounced. The debate is therefore taught, in this series and in this comparison, neither as scripture nor as archaeology but as a map of where the bodies are buried along a road that is still being travelled. A reader who finishes the map knowing the location of the seam, the standard on one side and the machine on the other, and the exact point where derivation from either to the other has always broken down, possesses the debate's whole usable estate.

## A test object: the Poulantzas and Miliband exchange

Debates conducted at the level of first principles benefit from being watched at work on a single object, and the period obligingly supplied one: the capitalist state, argued over in the exchange between Ralph Miliband and Nicos Poulantzas that began in 1969 and continued, with later participants, through the following decade. Miliband's book of 1969 documented, with a wealth of empirical detail, the social composition of the state's personnel and the channels through which a capitalist class staffs and influences the machine that governs it; his critics named the method instrumentalism, the state as an instrument in identifiable hands. Poulantzas, reviewing the book from the structural position, objected that the entire problematic was misconceived: the state serves the reproduction of the relations of production not because of who staffs it but because of what it is, a structure whose form, its separation from the immediate producers, its organisation of the dominant classes into a bloc and its disorganisation of the dominated, secures the order's longevity whoever occupies the offices, which is why replacing the personnel without touching the form changes so little. Miliband replied with some heat that a structure which acts without actors had become a super-agent more mystical than any subject the humanists had posited, and the exchange's later rounds, joined by others and refined by both men's subsequent books, Poulantzas's last work conceding the state as a material condensation of relations of force, Miliband's later work conceding the weight of structural constraint on any government of the left, converged towards the by now familiar geography. The state must be judged, the humanist half insists, by what it does to the people in its cells and queues, and no account of its form may be allowed to launder its acts. The state must be explained, the structural half insists, by the form that selects its acts before its personnel arrive, and no inventory of villains may be allowed to substitute for that account. Any reader who has followed a modern controversy about why a reforming government governs like its predecessors has watched the exchange continue under other names.

### What did the state debate show about the two methods?

It showed each method's characteristic blindness at close range. The inventory of personnel could not explain why the machine outlasts its staff. The theory of the form could not say, without embarrassment, who is responsible when the machine harms someone, or why anyone should risk anything to change it.

Responsibility is the nerve. A politics needs addresses for its indictments, persons and offices that can be named, resisted, and replaced, and a theory that dissolves every address into a relation courts a distinctive quietism even when its author is a militant, since the reader who has learned that the form does it all has learned, as a side effect, that no one in particular does anything. The humanist method never lacked addresses; it lacked an account of why the same indictments recur against successive officeholders of every intention. The exchange's convergence is therefore the settlement in uniform, and it is no accident that state theory became the sub-discipline where the division of labour was first normalised into textbooks. The object forced the issue. A state is at once a machine that must be explained and a power that must be judged, and a generation of scholars who tried to do only one of those things to it left the other half visibly undone on the page.

## Raymond Williams and the refusal of both inheritances

The British scene contained a figure who watched the camps form and declined both, and his refusal is valuable to the comparison because it was built from the inside of the same culture that produced Thompson. Raymond Williams, the Welsh critic whose work remade the study of culture, proposed in Marxism and Literature of 1977 a cultural materialism that rejected the base and superstructure model in every version, humanist and structuralist alike, as a reification of what in reality is a single, continuous process of production and meaning. His key instruments were forged against both camps' habits: the concept of structures of feeling named the lived, not yet articulated experience of a generation, a reality the humanists honoured but could not theorise without an essence and the structuralists theorised away as ideology; his insistence that culture is material, a signifying system that is itself productive, dissolved the hierarchy of instances on which structural explanation relied, while his account of the dominant, the residual, and the emergent gave historical change a grammar subtler than either the subject's awakening or the structure's reproduction. Williams's distance from the camps was not neutrality; he had his own commitments, democratic and socialist, and his own blind spots, which his critics enumerated. His value here is as a demonstration that the fork, however deep, was never a law of nature: a thinker of the first rank, in the same decades and the same islands, built on different foundations entirely, and the buildings still stand. The settlement this article defends is a settlement between the camps, not a claim that the camps exhausted the possibilities. It says what the heirs of humanism and structuralism each owe the other. It does not say that Marxism owed them both its whole future, and Williams's shelf is the standing proof.

## The working concepts: formation, instance, dominance

Because the structural vocabulary entered general circulation in a diluted form, a comparison owes its readers one careful pass through the machinery at working strength. A mode of production, in the camp's usage, is an abstract object: a combination of forces and relations of production, defined at the level of theory, which never exists anywhere in its purity. What exists is a social formation, a concrete society, in which several modes and their remnants coexist under the dominance of one, articulated with political and ideological instances that have their own structures and effectivities. The formation's instances are relatively autonomous, a phrase the polemics wore smooth: it means that the political and the ideological are not reflections of the economy, that they act and react with real force, and that their articulation, not their reduction, is the object of analysis. Dominance names the hierarchy among the instances in a given formation, which instance organises the whole in a period; determination in the last instance by the economy names the claim that the economy assigns the dominance, deciding which instance rules, while never itself ruling directly in the manner of a puppet master, and the camp's formula from Engels, that the economic movement asserts itself as necessary in the last instance amid an endless host of accidents, was quoted on all sides because all sides needed it. Overdetermination, whose own article in this series carries the concept's full weight, names the condition of every contradiction inside such a whole: affected by all the others, condensed and displaced, never pure. Set out at working strength, the machinery's attraction is obvious. It promises a Marxism that can talk about a school, a church, a novel, a riot, and a wage bargain in one grammar without reducing any of them to any other, and its defeats in practice, the taxonomies that multiplied instances while history declined to consult them, belong to the record of its application rather than to the elegance of the design.

### Why did the machinery attract a generation?

Because it replaced a metaphor with a research programme. Base and superstructure had told scholars where to rank things. The articulated formation told them what to map: which instances, in which dominance, with which contradictions condensed at which points. A map can be wrong in ways a metaphor cannot, and the possibility of being wrong looked like science.

The humanist vocabulary deserves the same charity of precision, since its terms suffered equal dilution. Species-being is not a warm feeling about humanity; it is a technical claim about the difference between human production and animal production, that the animal produces under the dominion of immediate need while the human being produces universally, in accordance with the standards of every species and in freedom from direct compulsion, so that the worker's estrangement can be specified as the inversion of that universality into its opposite. Alienation is not dissatisfaction; it is the fourfold structure of estrangements the manuscripts enumerate, each with its mechanism. Class consciousness, in the strong Lukacsian version, is not what workers happen to think; it is the consciousness imputed to the class from its position, the thoughts and feelings its members would have if they assessed their situation to the end, a construction whose authoritarian potential the structuralists were right to probe and whose explanatory intent, to distinguish a class's situation from its mood, the humanists were right to defend. Read at working strength, both vocabularies are formidable, and the debate between them was a debate between serious instruments, which is why its outcome could not be a victory. Instruments are retired by better instruments for the same job. Each of these sets turned out to be the better instrument for a job the other could not do, and the inventory of jobs, standard on one side and machine on the other, is the settlement this comparison has stated from its first pages and now, with the evidence assembled, can state as a conclusion rather than a promise.

## Editions as artillery: how the complete Marx changed the war's logistics

The debate's logistics ran through editions, and the eventual arrival of the complete scholarly edition of Marx and Engels altered the war's supply lines in ways neither camp had budgeted for. The projected historical-critical edition, resumed in earnest in the 1970s on foundations laid before the war, began publishing the manuscripts, notebooks, and drafts in their full extent and in their chronological disorder, and the picture that emerged embarrassed every simple map. The notebooks of the supposedly mature economist are full of the young philosopher's concerns pursued by other means; the drafts of the supposedly pure science were revised in the light of anthropology, chemistry, and the study of communal forms in the author's last decade, when the founder of historical materialism filled notebooks with the ethnology of peoples his system had supposedly consigned to prehistory. None of this hands the victory to the humanists, since the same edition confirms the real discontinuities of vocabulary and problem that the structuralists had mapped. What it dissolves is the use of the corpus as a court that can be made to issue a single verdict, because the corpus, fully published, is visibly the record of a mind arguing with itself for forty years and leaving the argument unfinished by design and by death. The camps had needed a settled founder; the edition produced an unsettled one, permanently. There is a wider lesson in the logistics, and the series has met it in other clusters: traditions fight their wars with the texts they can obtain, and the history of a debate is partly the history of a supply chain, of which manuscripts were in print, in which languages, under whose editorship, at what price. The humanism of the 1950s was built on a manuscript published in 1932 by editors whom the orthodoxies despised. The structuralism of the 1960s was built on a selective canon defended as the science's true extent. When the supply became complete, the war of the selections ended, and the demarcation that replaced it can be read as the peace treaty of a conflict whose material basis, scarcity of the founder's words, had been abolished.

## The essence in the dock: the philosophical audit of both foundations

Stripped of their politics and their parties, the two foundations can be audited as philosophy, and the audit clarifies what the demarcation keeps and what it leaves in dispute. Against the humanist essence, the standing objections are three. A transhistorical nature risks explaining nothing, since a constant cannot explain variation: if the essence is always present and always thwarted, the differences between orders must be explained by other means, and the essence retires to the role of a spectator at its own drama. The essence risks circularity, since the catalogue of human powers is usually compiled from the critic's own culture and century, so that the standard by which all orders are judged is one order's self-image universalised, an objection the feminist and anti-colonial critics pressed hardest and which the dissident humanists, to their credit, often pressed against themselves. And the essence risks conservatism of form: a fixed nature sets fixed limits to emancipation, so that the philosophy of liberation smuggles in a doctrine of what human beings eternally are, which future generations may experience as a cage built by their liberators. Against the structural dissolution, the standing objections are likewise three. A subject exhaustively produced by structures cannot account for the production's failures and variations, the non-identical outcomes the same structures yield, without smuggling in an unanalysed residue that does the subject's work under another name. The dissolution cannot state its own normative ground, as the earlier sections showed, and its attempts to derive criticism from contradiction alone confuse a system's stresses with a victim's claim. And the dissolution cannot describe its own practice: the theorist who writes, chooses, and exhorts is a subject performing exactly the authorship the doctrine denies, a performative tension its holders handled with irony more often than with argument.

The audit's balance is the reason the article's verdict is jurisdictional. Each foundation fails as a total philosophy, in locatable ways, and each succeeds as the guardian of one indispensable function: the essence, thinned to the minimum of needs, capacities, and vulnerabilities that Geras defended, is the only ground yet proposed on which criticism inside this tradition can stand; the dissolution, kept as method rather than metaphysics, is the only discipline yet proposed that keeps criticism honest about reproduction. A philosophy that needs a ground and a discipline, and possesses a doctrine that supplies each at the price of the other, does not have a synthesis problem to be solved by cleverness. It has an estate to be administered by demarcation, which is what its heirs, in practice, proceeded to do.

## The verdict: the mutual necessity, stated as a demarcation

The evidence is now assembled, and the verdict can be stated with the precision the brief of this article demanded from its first paragraph. The dispute between humanist and structuralist Marxism turns on a single question: whether there is a human subject that history expresses, or whether subjects are effects of structures with no essence prior to them. That question, at the level of philosophical anthropology, was not resolved, and this article has conceded the fact without varnish. What was resolved, by concession on both sides accumulating across the 1970s and ratified by every successor practice that mattered, is the division of the question's estate. Humanism supplies the normative ground of criticism: a conception of human powers, needs, and vulnerabilities by which any arrangement can be measured and found wanting, including arrangements built in Marxism's name, and structuralism cannot generate that ground from within itself, as its own practitioners' recourse to the forbidden vocabulary whenever they turned to politics demonstrates. Structuralism supplies the explanation of durability: the account of how arrangements produce their subjects, reproduce their conditions, and survive the frustration they cause, and humanism cannot generate that explanation from within itself, as its repertoire of veils and betrayals, names for the puzzle mistaken for its solution, demonstrates. The working position of most Marxists since the polemics, stated as doctrine rather than observed as habit, is therefore a demarcation with two jurisdictions and one marked seam. Judge by the standard. Explain by the machine. Never derive the standard from the machine, and never expect the standard to do the machine's work. At the seam, where the anthropologies contradict, say so, and proceed; the seam does not load-bear, because the jurisdictions were drawn precisely so that nothing built in either requires the seam to be closed.

The claim's name in this article, the mutual necessity, is meant to carry the asymmetry that a phrase like synthesis dissolves. Necessity runs in both directions but not in the same currency. The humanist needs the structuralist as a condition of being right about the world; the structuralist needs the humanist as a condition of being entitled to judge it, and the difference between being right and being entitled is the whole of the matter, since the century's catastrophes in this tradition were produced in about equal measure by movements that were entitled and wrong, marching behind an intact standard into machine-blind disasters, and by apparatuses that were right about reproduction and entitled to nothing, administering the machine they understood into a permanence that mocked every standard they had dissolved. A student who grasps the asymmetry has the debate in a form that can be carried into other rooms. A one-sided answer, in an examination or in an argument, can be graded by it directly: identify which jurisdiction the answer occupies, credit it fully there, and then ask for the other jurisdiction's work, the explanation if the answer judges, the standard if the answer explains. The answer that supplies both, and marks the seam honestly where its two halves meet, is the answer the mutual necessity predicts will convince, and the examiners of this tradition, whether they know the phrase or not, have been rewarding exactly that shape since the polemics died.

### How should a reader use the battlegrounds table?

As a grading instrument and a reading instrument at once. For any text in the tradition, locate its row, its battleground, and ask which column's work it is doing and which column's work it is neglecting. The table's six rows will classify most arguments in the literature within a minute, and the neglected column is usually the argument's verdict on itself.

There is a final observation to make about the debate's manner of ending, because it generalises beyond this cluster and the series has had occasion to note it in other territories. The dispute was settled by attrition rather than by argument, says the brief of this article, accurately, and attrition is a disreputable word, suggesting that the truth was decided by exhaustion and demography. The record supports a more honourable paraphrase. The dispute was settled by use: by the migration of each side's instruments into the hands of practitioners who had problems the instruments solved, who cared for the founders' war only as it bore on the problems, and who thereby performed the only adjudication intellectual history reliably offers, the adjudication of the workbench. Arguments end disputes among philosophers. Instruments end them among everyone else, and a tradition is fortunate when, as here, the instruments turn out to be complementary rather than rival, so that the workbench inherits the whole estate. The two camps went into the museums of the history of theory, where they are preserved in the sharp colours this article has used for them. The standard and the machine went into the workshop, where they remain, in daily use, by hands that mostly do not know the names of the quarrel that bequeathed them. That is not a tragedy of forgetting. It is what a successful inheritance looks like from the inside, and it is the proper note on which to hand the reader over to the three audiences this series always keeps in view.

## Lucien Goldmann: the bridge built before the war

The most instructive might-have-been of the whole comparison is the thinker who constructed, before the camps had names, a position engineered to hold exactly what each of them prized, and who was consequently claimed by both and followed by neither. Lucien Goldmann, a Romanian-born scholar working in Paris and Brussels, called his method genetic structuralism, and the name is a peace treaty in two words: structures are real, as the structuralists would demand, but they are genetic, made and remade by the praxis of groups, as the humanists would demand, and the subject of the process is neither the individual of liberal philosophy nor an essence behind history but the transindividual subject, the group whose mental structures a great work expresses at the level of a world vision. His study of Pascal and Racine, The Hidden God, had demonstrated the method in 1955 by deriving the architecture of tragedies and fragments from the world vision of a social stratum, with a rigour the structuralists admired and a fidelity to meaning the humanists required. Goldmann had studied with Lukacs's texts when Lukacs was beyond the pale, corresponded with the Frankfurt exiles, and stood in personal succession to the whole pre-war Western Marxism that the later camps divided between them like an inheritance neither would share. When the quarrel codified itself, his position satisfied the letter of neither camp: too structural for the existential humanists, who found the group subject a dissolution of the person by instalments; too humanist for the Parisians of the break, who found the world vision a relapse into expressive totality with the group substituted for the essence. He died in 1970, just as the war he had declined to join reached its fiercest pitch in his own city. The settlement's demarcation is not Goldmann's system, and his system had real difficulties of its own, above all in specifying how a group's vision is individuated without circularity. But his fate is the comparison's cleanest exhibit of the camps' joint defect: a culture of polemic that could recognise a construction holding both halves only as a failure to choose. Posterity, which keeps his book in print and his method in the sociologist's toolkit, chose otherwise, as posterity tends to do when the shooting stops.

## The analytical Marxists: the map redrawn in another idiom

In the decade after the English confrontation, a group of philosophers and social scientists, mostly Anglophone, redrew the entire map in a different idiom, and their intervention matters here because it tested the settlement from outside the shared premises of both camps. The analytical Marxists, as they were labelled, demanded that Marxist claims be stated with the clarity of analytic philosophy and tested with the instruments of modern social science, and they were even-handed in their demolitions. Against the humanists they rejected dialectical logic as a substitute for argument and treated the philosophy of the subject as a literature of edifying obscurity. Against the structuralists they rejected functional explanation that named no mechanism and theoreticism that answered only to itself, and G. A. Cohen's defence of historical materialism, the camp's founding monument of 1978, rebuilt the theory of history on premises its Parisian contemporaries would have found starkly traditional. What happened next is the settlement's neatest external confirmation. Cohen himself, the school's most rigorous mind, spent his later career migrating from the explanation of history to the philosophy of justice, concluding that Marxism's critique of capitalism had always rested on normative premises it had not examined, self-ownership and equality chief among them, and that the examination could no longer be postponed. The school's founder thus walked, in public and over decades, from the machine's jurisdiction to the standard's, announcing at the end of the journey that the standard had been the load-bearing member all along and that its theory had yet to be written. The humanists could not have scripted a better witness, and the structuralists could not dismiss him as one of the other camp's sentimentalists. The demarcation, once again, was ratified by a practitioner who owed it nothing.

## The genealogy: Hegel's ghost and Feuerbach's gift

Both camps were, at one remove, arguing about two German inheritances that Marx himself had never finished sorting, and a reader who sees the inheritances sees why the argument could not be closed by an appeal to the founder. From Hegel came the expressive totality in its grandest form: Spirit externalising itself in history, alienated in its own creations, and recovering itself through them, a drama whose protagonist is a subject writ large enough to be a world. Every humanist construction in the debate is that drama with humanity cast in Spirit's role, and the structuralists' polemic against the expressive whole was, as they knew, a polemic against the Hegelian ghost in the machine of their own tradition. From Feuerbach came the species-being in its original form: the essence of man located in the species' communal nature, religion explained as that essence projected and worshipped in alienated form, and philosophy's task defined as the essence's restoration to its owners. The young Marx took the gift and transformed it, grounding the essence in production rather than in contemplation, but the structuralist charge was precisely that a gift so marked by its giver could not found a science, that Feuerbach's man survives in every humanist Marxism as an unexamined deposit. The founder's own texts sustain both genealogies because the founder's own formation contained both, and his mature work neither simply repeats the drama nor simply cancels it; it reworks the drama's energies into an analysis whose protagonist keeps dissolving into relations and whose relations keep issuing in a critique addressed to beings who suffer them. Two camps, each the legitimate heir of one strand of a double inheritance, contested the estate for thirty years. The demarcation is the will the founder never wrote, drawn up by the executors after the estate had been fought over long enough for its contents to be inventoried by use.

## Fromm's book and the humanism of the paperback

The debate's largest single readership was won by a book that specialists in both camps affected to find elementary, and its career belongs in the evidence because a comparison of positions should record what the positions did in the world. Erich Fromm's Marx's Concept of Man appeared in 1961 with translations of the 1844 manuscripts as its appendix, the first widely available English rendering, and it presented the founder to the Anglophone public as a humanist philosopher of alienation whose economics served his anthropology. The book's influence is difficult to overstate: for a generation of readers in the Americas and beyond, Fromm's Marx was Marx, the prophet of the sane society against the organisation man, and the humanist camp possessed thereby an asset no journal could match, a founder already installed in the public mind before the structuralist translations reached the bookshops a decade later. The Parisian response, when it came, was contempt of a purity that betrayed its anxiety: the paperback Marx was denounced as a falsification, a Feuerbachian bedtime story, and the denunciations are now read as the period's most eloquent testimony to the humanists' real strength, which was never primarily theoretical. A camp that owns the public image of the founder can lose every seminar and keep the century. The structuralists' counter-asset was the university, where their Marx became, for a different generation, the rigorous one, and the two publics, the paperback's and the seminar's, coexisted for decades with remarkably little trade between them. When trade resumed, in the successor practices this article has tracked, it was on the demarcation's terms, the standard walking out of the paperback and the machine walking out of the seminar to meet in the workshop. The sociology of the two publics is the debate's last lesson in its own method: ideas travel in material carriers, carriers select audiences, and audiences decide which of a theory's halves the future will keep in circulation. Both halves survived their carriers. The quarrel did not.

## Fanon and the humanism of the colonised

The camps' map of the world had a blank space where most of humanity lived, and the thinker who filled it did so in the humanist vocabulary at its most incandescent, which the comparison must record both as evidence and as reproach. Frantz Fanon, the Martinican psychiatrist who became the Algerian revolution's most famous voice, closed The Wretched of the Earth in 1961 with an explicit summons to a new humanism: the colonised, dehumanised by a Manichaean order, would in liberating themselves have to invent the human anew rather than imitate the Europe whose humanism had coexisted with the whip. His publisher's preface was Sartre's, the alliance of the existential humanism with the anti-colonial struggle sealed in print, and his analyses of the colonised subject's interior life extended the humanist anthropology into territory neither European camp had entered: the subject here is not thwarted in the factory but unmade in the colony, and its recovery is violent because the order that unmade it was. The structuralist camp had, by comparison, almost nothing to say about empire in its heroic texts, an omission its critics catalogued and its heirs repaired with the tools of formation and articulation applied to the world scale by the dependency theorists. The demarcation absorbs the case without strain, and is strengthened by it. Fanon's standard is the humanist inheritance at maximum intensity, a humanity to be created rather than recovered, which silently corrects the essence's conservatism from inside the camp. Fanon's weakness is the inheritance's weakness: the national bourgeoisie's career after independence, which his own book predicted with terrible clarity and his philosophy of the subject could not prevent, is the machine's revenge, the reproduction of domination by new personnel in an untouched form. The anti-colonial century is the debate's largest unwritten chapter, and it grades the camps exactly as the verdict predicts: the standard travelled and judged; the machine stayed home and was needed.

## Bloch, or humanism with a metaphysics

Ernst Bloch is the humanist camp's standing refutation of the charge that its philosophy was a soft anthropology for essayists. His system, built across The Spirit of Utopia of 1918 and the vast Principle of Hope composed in exile, is an ontology of the Not Yet: reality itself is unfinished, matter is shot through with latency and tendency, and the human subject's anticipatory consciousness, its daydreams, hopes, and utopian surpluses, is not decoration on the process but the process becoming aware of its own front line. Where the structuralists heard in such language the return of every idealism, Bloch heard in theirs the return of a dead mechanism, a world in which nothing genuinely new could ever occur because every event was the rearrangement of a structure's elements. The exchange was never staged as a formal debate, he belonged to the older generation and to the other Germany, but the positions confront each other across the camp boundary with unusual purity. Is novelty real? A theory of structures explains the new as recombination, and does so with increasing ingenuity as the cases harden. A philosophy of hope insists that recombination is not the whole of it, that the world's fertility exceeds its inventory, and stakes the claim on the same evidence the humanists always cited: that the beings inside the arrangements keep producing demands the arrangements did not script. The demarcation assigns Bloch his jurisdiction without annexing his metaphysics. The standard needs a future tense, a reason to treat the possible as more than the actual rearranged, and Bloch is the camp's great supplier of that tense. The machine needs his warning as a limit: an explanation of durability that cannot in principle register the genuinely new will mistake every dawn for a rearrangement, and the camps' history contains dawns it so mistook.

## Lefebvre and the everyday: humanism as a research programme

Henri Lefebvre spent the longest career in the debate insisting that the humanist inheritance was not a mood but a method, and his critique of everyday life, launched in 1947 and continued for four decades, is the humanist camp's most durable research programme. The everyday, the residual zone left when the specialised activities are subtracted, work, ritual, leisure administered from above, is where alienation is actually lived and therefore where it must be studied: in the commute, the advertisement, the housing estate, the festival that survives its organisers. Lefebvre's Marx is accordingly the philosopher of totality and of moments, of the possible that flashes inside the administered routine, and his long quarrel with the structuralists, who had been his students' generation and who dismissed the everyday as the ideological's home address, was fought on the terrain of what counts as a fact. For the structural method, the everyday is the least promising object, a fog of lived illusions to be dispelled by the concepts of the formation. For Lefebvre it is the only object where the formation can be caught in the act of being lived, reproduced, and occasionally refused, and a science that disdains it has disdained its evidence. His students and neighbours included the Situationists, who carried the critique of everyday life into a practice of provocation the old professor regarded with mingled pride and alarm, and his later work on the production of space founded a field outright. The demarcation reads Lefebvre as the humanist camp's bridge to the workshop: his programme took the standard's questions into the machine's territory, the reproduction of the ordinary, and demonstrated that a humanist could study reproduction without ceasing to judge it. That the structuralists never produced an equivalent ethnography of the ordinary, preferring the school and the family as diagrams, is the omission their heirs in cultural studies spent twenty years repairing with his tools.

## The labels themselves: two borrowed words

The camps fought under borrowed banners, and the borrowing shaped the fight. Humanism arrived with a pedigree from Renaissance scholarship and a fresh coat from 1946, when Sartre's lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism made the word the property of every philosophy that put man's self-making at its centre, to the annoyance of the orthodox and the confusion of librarians ever since. In Marxist mouths the word gathered the dissident charge described earlier: to call oneself a humanist after 1956 was to announce that the doctrine's centre was the human being and that any regime could be audited against it. Structuralism arrived from linguistics, from the Saussurean discovery that a language is a system of differences with no positive terms, by way of anthropology's application of the model to kinship and myth, and it carried into Marxism the aura of the exact sciences at the moment of their maximum prestige: here was a method that had dissolved the speaking subject of the linguists and the savage subject of the ethnographers into systems of relations, and could do the same for the subject of history. The labels were thus asymmetric in origin and in vulnerability. Humanism could be accused of being a mood, a centuries-old piety with a party card. Structuralism could be accused of being a fashion, the season's Parisian method applied to an object it did not fit, and both accusations were deployed with the confidence of camps that had not examined their own banners. Althusser, to his credit, examined his: theoretical anti-humanism was his attempt to specify the banner's meaning so narrowly that the mood and the fashion charges would miss it, and the failure of the specification to hold in his followers' usage is one more entry in the ledger of doctrines outrun by their labels. A comparison is bound to use the labels, since the debate used them and the literature is shelved by them. It is equally bound to note, once, that the thing fought over was older and deeper than either word, and that the words themselves are now the dispute's most dated artifacts, needing exactly the kind of unpacking this article has attempted for the positions beneath them.

## Two ways of writing history: the crowd and the longue durée

The camps' quarrel reproduced itself inside historiography as a quarrel of temperaments with methods attached, and the reproduction is instructive because historians, unlike philosophers, must eventually produce narratives that readers can check. The humanist temper wrote history from below: the crowd, the workshop, the chapel, the strike committee, recovering the experience of people the record was designed to ignore and treating that experience as the engine and the evidence of the story. Its monuments are narratives of making, of classes constituting themselves in action, and its besetting sin, which its own masters named, is romance: the temptation to find the subject forever rising, to read defeats as preludes, to let sympathy edit the archive. The structural temper found its historical ally outside Marxism altogether, in the French historians of the longue durée, for whom the event is foam on the deep structures of geography, demography, and price, and whose suspicion of the biographical and the episodic matched the seminar's suspicion of the subject point for point. Its Marxist monuments are analyses of formations and transitions, its besetting sin the one Thompson flayed: a schema so complete that the archive can only illustrate it, and men and women appear as the foam's temporary shapes. The peace, in historiography as elsewhere, was a demarcation arrived at by practitioners: the social history that flourished after the quarrel learned to write narratives of experience whose formation it had also mapped, to treat the crowd as both author and artifact, and the synthesis is visible in the period's best single-authored works, which pass from the price series to the sermon and back without apologising to either camp. It is sometimes said that the historians settled the philosophers' quarrel by ignoring it. The fairer statement is that they settled it by obeying the evidence's own jurisdiction rules, since an archive, unlike a seminar, will not let a researcher judge without explaining or explain without judging for very long.

## The shared ground the polemics concealed

A reader who knows only the polemics will wonder how the camps could occupy one tradition at all, and the answer is that their agreements were extensive, deep, and systematically understated by both sides, since agreement is useless in a fight. Both camps rejected the official catechism of dialectical materialism and the empiricism that treated Marx's texts as a quarry of quotations. Both held that capitalism is a system to be explained as a whole, not an accumulation of abuses to be deplored one by one, and both held that its appearances are systematically misleading, that the wage form, the commodity form, and the legal form conceal the relations they express. Both therefore placed ideology near the centre of their work, however rival their theories of it, and both despised the evolutionism that promised socialism as the automatic fruit of productive growth. Both camps, finally, were staffed by people who had staked their lives' work on the proposition that Marx's writings contain a resource for understanding and changing the modern world that no rival tradition supplies, and the ferocity of their quarrel was partly the ferocity of co-religionists who agreed on the scripture's importance and could not agree on its sense. Noting the shared ground is not a plea for false harmony. The disagreements this article has mapped were real, and one of them remains open. It is a correction to the polemical optics, in which two armies face each other across an empty field, where the record shows two schools on one estate, quarrelling over an inheritance both were busy spending. The settlement's plausibility rests partly on this: a demarcation is possible between jurisdictions inside a shared enterprise, and it was the shared enterprise, never in dispute, that made the demarcation worth drawing.

## The Fragment on Machines: one page of the Grundrisse, two futures

No single page of Marx illustrates the camps' optics better than the passage in the notebooks of 1857 and 1858 that posterity named the Fragment on Machines. There Marx, following capital's drive to replace living labour with machinery and science, imagines the point at which direct labour ceases to be the measure of wealth and the free development of individuals becomes production's real content. The humanists read the passage as the mature Marx confirming the young one: automation as the material basis for the recovery of species-powers, the realm of freedom drafted in the economist's own hand, proof that the anthropology governs the economics at its most visionary point. The structuralists read the same sentences as a theory of the transformation of the mode of production, in which the subject of the process is capital's own logic and the human material is its bearer, and they warned against extracting a philosophy of liberation from a passage whose grammar assigns agency to the system. The page supports both readings because it was written by a thinker in whom both registers operated simultaneously, which the camps experienced as an affront and the settlement experiences as the normal condition of the corpus. The fragment's afterlife repeated the family pattern: it became a charter for theories of immaterial labour and of post-work futures, each generation discovering in it the Marx its own questions required. A comparison need not adjudicate the fragment. It need only point out that the two camps' readings of it are the debate in miniature, standard against machine on a single page, and that any account of Marx that cannot hold both readings in view at once has purchased its clarity by amputation. Students meeting the fragment in an anthology should be told which reading their anthology's headnote has silently adopted, since the choice is always made and rarely announced, and spotting the silent adoption is the beginning of reading the tradition rather than inheriting it.

## Did Marx condemn capitalism as unjust? The morality sub-dispute

The standard's jurisdiction has a precise scholarly address: the long-running dispute over whether Marx himself condemned capitalism as unjust. The question sounds pedantic and is anything but, since on its answer depends whether the tradition contains its own tribunal or must borrow one. In the early 1970s the philosopher Allen Wood argued the austere thesis: Marx did not condemn capitalism for injustice, because justice, on Marx's own account, is a juridical notion internal to a mode of production, a standard by which transactions are assessed against the rules of the system they occur in, and by that standard the wage exchange is just, an exchange of equivalents with no robbery at the point of contract, whatever exploitation the production process performs behind the contract's back. The condemnation Marx actually issues, on this reading, is not moral but systemic and prudential: capitalism fetters the productive forces, produces crises and misery, and thwarts human flourishing, none of which requires the vocabulary of rights and deserts that the founder treated as ideological furniture. Geras answered, at length and with the corpus open on the desk, that the distinction would not hold: the texts repeatedly describe the appropriation of surplus labour in the language of theft, robbery, and embezzlement, language that is meaningless without a standard of entitlement the system violates, and a theory that describes a transaction as robbery while acquitting it of injustice has not clarified its categories but broken them. The dispute ran for decades in the journals of moral and political philosophy, recruiting fresh combatants and refining both cases, and its shape is this article's verdict in scholastic dress. Wood's Marx is the structural inheritance perfected: a critic who needs no anthropology because his condemnation works entirely within the machine's own malfunctions. Geras's Marx is the humanist inheritance defended at the level of the sentence: a critic whose machine is real but whose standard is real too, smuggled into no corner, legible in his verbs. The sub-dispute's inconclusive longevity, among scholars with no parties to serve, is final evidence that the fork is in the founder's texts and not in the parties' needs. Whoever settles it for a seminar should be required to say which jurisdiction is doing the work, which is all, in the end, that the mutual necessity ever asked.

## A human face: Prague and the standard in power's antechamber

The humanist standard once came within sight of being a government's programme, and the episode is the camp's proudest and saddest exhibit. The reform movement that culminated in the Prague Spring of 1968 marched under a slogan that could serve as the whole debate's emblem: socialism with a human face. The phrase was not decorative. The Czechoslovak reformers, economists, philosophers, and party officials among them, with Karel Kosik, the theorist of the concrete totality, sitting in the party's leading bodies, proposed to be judged by exactly the humanist criterion: whether the order enlarged or cramped the lives of the people inside it, their speech, their work, their everyday world. Censorship loosened, the victims of the purges were rehabilitated, and for one season the standard by which the dissident humanists had condemned the regime from outside was being applied, haltingly, from inside the antechamber of power. The Warsaw Pact invasion of August ended the experiment by the method the structuralists had theorised: not by winning the argument about faces but by moving the machinery, divisions across the border in the night, the apparatuses of five states demonstrating in practice what the reproduction of an order looks like when persuasion fails. Each camp mined the episode for its moral for a decade. Humanists: the standard had been affirmed by a whole society the moment it could speak, and only force had answered it. Structuralists: the episode proved that a standard without a theory of the machine, of alliances, instances, and the geography of power in Europe, is a petition addressed to tank crews. The demarcation's cold comment is that both morals are correct and that the season's tragedy is their conjunction. A politics that had held the standard in one hand and the conjunctural map in the other might still have lost in 1968; the map offered no winning move that year. But it would have lost knowing why, and priced its choices accordingly, which is the most the settlement ever promises anyone.

## Eurocommunism: the standard becomes a party line

The debate's vocabulary had one more public career, in the last attempt of the Western parties to reinvent themselves. The Eurocommunism of the 1970s, associated above all with the Italian party under Enrico Berlinguer and with the Spanish party's theorists, adopted a language of pluralism, democracy, and human values that amounted, in the camps' terms, to the humanist standard promoted to a strategy: socialism would be measured by the freedoms it preserved and extended, and the Eastern regimes were criticised, from inside the movement, for failing that measure. The structuralist suspicion arrived on schedule: the new vocabulary was ideology in the technical sense, the lived self-image of parties adapting to electoral respectability, and the disappearance of the science's hard concepts from the programmes marked the relapse the theory predicted. Events graded the exchange with their usual even-handedness. The parties that adopted the standard gained moral credibility and lost, within two decades, their reason to exist as communist parties at all, dissolving or renaming themselves when the Eastern anchor vanished, while the parties that kept the old language kept their apparatuses and their irrelevance in equal measure. The standard had judged the East correctly and could not save the West. The machine explained both outcomes and could endorse neither. The demarcation, reading the episode, finds its two jurisdictions at work in the open and its seam exactly where it always was: between knowing what a movement is for and knowing what a movement is up against.

Your task with this debate is separation before synthesis. First, separate the philological question, whether Marx's writings break or develop, from the philosophical question, whether there is a human essence; examiners reward the separation and punish the fusion, because the camps' own confusion of the two is the classic error in this territory. The article on [young Marx vs mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/) gives you the textual battleground in full, and the article on [species-being](/2011/05/15/marx-species-being-explained/) gives you the anthropology the humanists built on it, so read those two before attempting an answer that mentions either. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The battlegrounds table was designed to be lifted out of this article and put to work as a structured debate. Assign the six rows to six pairs of students, each pair required to argue one row from both columns in successive weeks, so that every student performs the structuralist case on the row where they are a humanist and the reverse. The exercise teaches the debate's real lesson faster than any lecture: the columns are jurisdictions, not teams, and the student who has had to explain reproduction on Monday and defend the standard on Thursday has understood the settlement in the muscles. Two staging recommendations from experience with this material. Begin not with the polemic of 1978, whose brilliance seduces students into treating the debate as a prizefight, but with the two relics, the notebooks of 1844 and the Preface of 1859, read side by side in a single session; the fork is visible there without any commentary. And reserve the confrontation, and Anderson's reply to it, for the end of the sequence, where the reply can be assessed as what it is, the settlement drafted early by the debate's best referee. Assessment follows the article's grading rule: full credit requires an answer to occupy both jurisdictions and to mark the seam between them honestly.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier in this territory is no longer the camps' war but the supply chains and successor practices through which the war was transmitted and settled. The two completed positions are best pursued in depth through the series' dedicated treatments: the profile of Louis Althusser for the structuralist side in its biographical and institutional setting, and the article on [Marxist humanism explained](/2011/07/15/marxist-humanism-explained/) for the opposing tradition in its own right, each of which carries bibliographies this comparison deliberately does not duplicate. Three problems repay attention in the primary record. The reception histories are still being written from the archives of the journals and publishing houses that carried the camps, and the translation record, which texts crossed which borders in which years under whose editorship, remains the least exploited and most decisive evidence for why the debate took the shapes it did. The Eastern humanisms, whose papers are dispersed and whose principals left memoirs in several languages, await the synthesis their Western counterparts have received. And the settlement itself, the migration of instruments into cultural studies, state theory, and the philosophy of social science, is a case study in how intellectual disputes actually end, of interest well beyond Marxism's borders. Journalists covering revivals of either vocabulary in public argument will find the battlegrounds table a serviceable checklist for identifying which half of the old estate a revived slogan is spending, and which half it is quietly borrowing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between humanist and structuralist Marxism?

The two are rival readings of Marx organised around the status of the human subject. The humanist reading treats the corpus as a unity centred on the early account of alienated labour and on species-being, humanity's characteristic powers, which provides the measure by which any social order can be condemned for deforming them. The structuralist reading treats the corpus as divided by a rupture in the mid-1840s, after which Marx founded a science of social formations in which individuals figure as occupants of places defined by relations, and in which appeals to a human essence count as ideology rather than knowledge. From that single divergence flow the camps' opposed views of ideology, of history, of experience, and of political organisation.

### Q: Which texts does each side rely on?

Humanists build from the Paris notebooks of 1844, where estranged labour and species-being are set out, and they read the later economics as those themes developed: the notebooks of 1857 and 1858 with their vision of free development, the chapter on the working day, the passage on the realm of freedom. Structuralists build from the rupture documents of 1845, the Theses on Feuerbach and The German Ideology, from the retrospective Preface of 1859 with its architecture of foundations and superstructures, and from the form-analysis of the opening of Capital. Each camp also keeps a secondary armoury: the humanists cite the ethnological notebooks of the last decade, the structuralists cite Engels's letters of the 1890s on determination in the last instance.

### Q: Does structuralism remove human agency?

It depends on what agency is being asked to do. As a first principle of explanation, yes, it is removed: conduct is to be accounted for by the positions people occupy and by the identities institutions confer on them, not by appeal to an originating self. As a phenomenon, no: people deliberate, resist, and invent, and the theory must register all of that as data. The camp's formula is that agents are real but produced, so their initiatives have conditions and limits that the analysis is obliged to specify. Critics answer that a produced agent who can nevertheless bend the producing structure is the old subject returning under guard, and that the theory never decides how much bending it permits. The question's long life comes from that undecided quantity.

### Q: What was the Thompson and Althusser confrontation about?

It was a confrontation conducted in books rather than in person. In 1978 the historian E. P. Thompson published a long polemic against Althusser and the structural Marxism gathered around him, charging the school with building a self-sealing conceptual machinery indifferent to evidence, with dissolving moral agency, and with supplying a mandarin politics in philosophical dress. The confrontation became the English-speaking debate's defining episode because it forced every underlying issue into one text: science against history, structure against experience, system against judgement. Two years later Perry Anderson's book-length reply assessed both positions and found each in possession of the other's refutation, which is why the episode is still read as the debate in miniature rather than as a victory for its louder party.

### Q: Did either side win the argument?

Neither side won in the manner of a proof, and no text exists that partisans of the losing camp accepted as decisive. What happened instead was a settlement by concession and absorption. The structuralists, beginning with their founder's self-criticism, withdrew the claim that theory could guarantee itself, while the humanists absorbed the institutional reproduction of belief and the formation of experience into their own accounts. Successor practices in cultural studies, state theory, and social history proceeded on a basis that mixes both inheritances: standards of judgement drawn from the humanist side, explanations of persistence drawn from the structuralist side. The strictly philosophical question at the root, whether there is a human essence, was left formally open, and it remains the one part of the quarrel that honest summaries should report as undecided.

### Q: Is the humanism and structuralism dichotomy overdrawn?

As a map of persons, yes; as a map of problems, no. The personnel refuse to stay in their columns. Gramsci's theory of hegemony, Sartre's dialectic of praxis and inertia, Goldmann's genetic structuralism, and the Frankfurt analyses of the administered world all straddle the division, and each camp contained internal variety that the labels flatten. But the fork beneath the labels is genuine: a theory either grounds its criticism in a conception of the human or it does not, and either explains order by mechanisms indifferent to intention or it reaches for the intentions of subjects. Writers can migrate between the resulting positions, and the wisest did. The dichotomy is best used the way this article uses it, as a pair of jurisdictions against which any particular text can be located, rather than as a pair of tribes to which authors are assigned for life.

### Q: What does humanism give that structuralism cannot?

It gives criticism its measuring rod: a worked-out conception of what human beings are and what their flourishing requires, against which an arrangement can be found wanting in itself and not merely in its malfunctions. Structural analysis can demonstrate that a factory system reproduces itself, disrupts itself, or contradicts itself; it cannot, from the demonstration alone, extract the conclusion that the system wrongs the people inside it, because wrong requires a being with claims that the arrangement defeats. Humanism names that being and itemises the claims: free and conscious activity, development of capacities, association without domination. Without such a ground, criticism in this tradition must borrow its standards from elsewhere or use them unexamined, and the borrowing and the smuggling are both visible in the structuralist literature whenever it turns from analysis to politics.

### Q: What does structuralism explain better?

It explains persistence. Why does an order that frustrates most of its members renew itself every morning without a conspiracy to renew it? The structural answer traces the circuits: relations that train the capacities they require, institutions that form the subjects who will find the order natural, instances that support one another so that a challenge at one point is absorbed at another. Humanist vocabularies handle the same facts with notions like false consciousness, which label the outcome without exhibiting the mechanism. The structural apparatus also explains the shape of defeats, why movements with justice on their side and numbers in the street subside into restorations administered by the defeated themselves. Wherever the puzzle is the stability of something widely resented, the structural toolkit has outperformed its rival for two generations, which is why even its opponents' successors keep it in service.

### Q: Why was the debate so bitter?

Because the philosophy was never only philosophy. Positions in the argument functioned as factional acts inside parties and movements: the vocabulary of humanism was, in one capital, the official language of a leadership's renewal and, in another, the banner of dissidents against their regimes, while anti-humanism could be heard as an attack on a leadership's alibi or as an apology for its apparatus. Generational succession sharpened everything, as younger scholars used the difficult new idiom to displace elders whose authority rested on the old one, and the Cold War supplied a standing charge each side could lay against the other, complicity with the camps or complicity with surrender. Add the genuine moral weight of the questions, what may be said about regimes ruling in the doctrine's name, and the wonder is not the bitterness but the amount of serious work the bitterness failed to prevent.

### Q: Which position do Marxists hold today?

The position most working Marxists operate with is the combined one this article calls the mutual necessity, usually held as practice rather than proclaimed as doctrine. Criticism is mounted in the humanist register: arrangements are judged by what they do to human powers, needs, and dignity, and regimes of every label are liable to the judgement. Explanation is conducted in the structural register: outcomes are traced to institutions, relations, and the reproduction of consent, not to the virtues or failures of the actors alone. Pure versions of either camp survive chiefly as teaching devices and as the idioms of small schools. The combination is not offered as a reconciliation of the underlying philosophies; its users have generally learned to mark the point where the two inheritances contradict, and to build on either side of that point without pretending it is not there.

### Q: How does the mutual necessity claim grade a one-sided answer?

It grades by jurisdiction. A one-sided answer is first located: is it judging an arrangement, or is it explaining an arrangement's persistence? Credit is given in full for the work done inside that jurisdiction, since each inheritance is genuinely strong at home. The deduction is then specified rather than general. A judging answer loses marks for the missing machinery: it has not shown how the condemned order reproduces itself, so its condemnation cannot guide action. An explaining answer loses marks for the missing standard: it has not said by what measure the explained order fails anyone, so its explanation cannot ground criticism. The distinction between a strong and a weak one-sided answer is whether the omission is acknowledged. The full answer occupies both jurisdictions and flags the seam where the anthropologies collide.

### Q: What should a student separate between the young Marx and the mature Marx in this debate?

Three things that the polemics delighted in fusing. Separate first the documentary record: which writings exist from which years, and what vocabulary each actually uses, the notebooks of 1844, the rupture texts of 1845, the great draft of 1857 and 1858, the Preface of 1859, the published volume of 1867. Separate second the literary-historical question of whether the record shows a rupture or a growth, which is answerable, at least in part, from the documents and their dates. Separate third the philosophical question of whether human beings have an essence that history can violate, which no amount of dating will settle. Marks in this territory are lost by letting a verdict on the third question dictate a reading of the first two. They are won by exhibiting each layer cleanly and showing where the camps soldered them together and why.

### Q: Why did the early manuscripts and the mature Preface become the debate's two poles?

Portability and symmetry did it. The notebooks of 1844 are brief, vivid, and contain a whole philosophy in germ: a human essence, its estrangement under private property, and the prospect of its recovery. The Preface is equally brief and contains a whole method in summary: material production conditioning consciousness, foundations bearing superstructures, epochs turning on their contradictions. A movement philosopher or a seminar leader could found a reading of the entire corpus on either text and carry the charter in a coat pocket, which is more than can be said for the thousand pages of the mature economics that both charters claimed to gloss. Their late and accidental character helped as well: the notebooks were unknown until 1932, so they arrived as a revelation unencumbered by older commentary, while the Preface's status as the author's own retrospective summary gave it an authority no other short text could rival.

### Q: How does each side explain the other's existence?

Each side keeps a theory of the other's error that doubles as a sociology of the other camp. Structuralists treated humanism as the spontaneous ideology of the labour movement and of dissidence: the flattering self-image of subjects who need to believe in their own authorship, bound to recur wherever Marxism functions as a faith rather than a science. Humanists treated structuralism as the ideology of two institutions, the academy and the party apparatus: a doctrine dissolving responsible subjects suits professors who prefer systems to archives and officials who prefer obedience to judgement. Both sociologies capture something real about the camps' recruitment and tone. Neither answers the other side's arguments, and the settlement became possible only when readers stopped accepting explanations of an opponent's existence as substitutes for engagements with his reasons.

### Q: What separates a break in a corpus from a development within it?

The test is what happens to the problems. In a development, the thinker's questions persist across the years and are pursued with better instruments: answers change, examples accumulate, earlier formulations are corrected by later ones working on the same difficulties. In a break, the questions themselves are dissolved and replaced: the old problems are reclassified as artefacts of a discarded framework, and the old key terms lose their employment even where the words linger as decoration. Applied to Marx, the record shows the vocabulary of essence and estrangement losing its employment after 1845, which supports the break, while the normative commitments carried by that vocabulary keep their employment to the end, which supports development. Holding both findings at once, in their separate registers, is the separation the whole debate eventually taught.
