The Citizenship Act 1955 is the statute that answers, for every person who claims a connection with India, the most basic question a legal system can ask: is this person one of ours? The Citizenship Act 1955, enacted as Act 57 of 1955 and brought into force on 30 December 1955, lays down five routes by which Indian citizenship is acquired, the conditions on which each route operates, the circumstances in which citizenship ends, and the machinery by which disputed questions of status are decided. Almost every public argument about citizenship in India, whether it concerns a birth certificate in a district town, a cardholder living abroad, or a special provision for one State in the north east, is at bottom an argument about the meaning of one of its sections. This pillar reads the Act section by section, states each route in the terms the statute uses, and separates the enacted scheme from the changes that later amendments made to it, because the single most common error in accounts of this field is to describe one period's rule as though it had always been the rule.

![Citizenship Act 1955 explained, the five routes to Indian citizenship and the Assam provision - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-59.webp)

Citizenship in India is a subject on which the Constitution deliberately said less than half of what needed saying. Articles 5 to 8 of the Constitution determined who was a citizen at the commencement of the Constitution on 26 January 1950, dealing with the enormous movement of people that partition had produced. Article 9 provided that a person who had voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State was not to be a citizen under those articles. Article 10 continued citizenship subject to any law made by Parliament, and Article 11 gave Parliament the power to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship. The constitutional text therefore fixed the starting population and then handed the subject to legislation. Entry 17 of the Union List in the Seventh Schedule, citizenship, naturalisation and aliens, placed the subject within the exclusive legislative competence of Parliament, so no State legislature can create, condition or remove Indian citizenship. The Act of 1955 is the exercise of that Article 11 power, and every route, condition and ground of loss examined in this article traces back to it.

The field around the Act is mapped in the companion guide to [citizenship law in India](/2021/09/01/citizenship-law-india-complete-guide/), which places this statute beside the constitutional articles, the passport and foreigners legislation and the rules made under each. This pillar has a narrower task. It stays inside the four corners of Act 57 of 1955, reads its acquisition provisions in the order the statute presents them, treats loss with the same care as acquisition, and gives the special provision for Assam the sustained attention that a Constitution Bench judgment of October 2024 demands. The amendment history of the Act, which is long and politically charged, is owned by the companion article on the [amendments to the Citizenship Act](/2021/10/01/citizenship-act-amendments-india/), and the controversy over the amendment of 2019 is owned by the article on the [Citizenship Amendment Act 2019 debate](/2021/10/15/citizenship-amendment-act-2019-debate/). Where an amendment matters to the meaning of a provision read here, it is flagged at headline level and the reader is sent across; the pillar itself states the enacted foundation on which every amendment operated.

### Q: Is a person born in India automatically an Indian citizen?

No. The answer turns entirely on the date of birth. Before 1 July 1987, birth in India made a person a citizen whatever the nationality of the parents. From 1 July 1987 to 2 December 2004, either parent had to be a citizen. From 3 December 2004, the stricter rule stated below governs.

In full: a person born in India on or after 26 January 1950 and before 1 July 1987 is a citizen by birth whatever the nationality of the parents. A person born on or after 1 July 1987 and before 3 December 2004 is a citizen by birth only if either parent was a citizen at the time of the birth. A person born on or after 3 December 2004 is a citizen by birth only if both parents were citizens at the time, or one parent was a citizen and the other was not an illegal migrant at that time.

That three-part answer is the most consequential single fact in the whole statute, and it is developed fully in the treatment of Section 3 below. Its importance lies in the direction of the inquiry it forces. For a person born in the first period, the only fact to establish is the birth itself, in India, within the dates. For a person born in the second period, the inquiry reaches one generation back and asks about a parent. For a person born in the third period, the inquiry reaches one generation back and asks about both parents, and about the migration status of the parent who was not a citizen. The statute's history is a history of that inquiry widening, and the documentary difficulty that dominates practice in this field is the difficulty of proving, decades after the event, the status of people other than the applicant.

## The architecture of the Act: acquisition, loss and the special provisions

The Act is arranged in a sequence that an applicant or adviser should hold in mind before turning to any single section. Sections 3 to 7 state the five routes of acquisition: citizenship by birth in Section 3, citizenship by descent in Section 4, citizenship by registration in Section 5, citizenship by naturalisation in Section 6, and citizenship by incorporation of territory in Section 7. Sections 8, 9 and 10 state the three modes of loss: renunciation in Section 8, termination in Section 9 and deprivation in Section 10. Around that spine sit provisions of a different character. Section 6A, inserted in 1985, is the special provision for Assam. Sections 7A to 7D, inserted by amendment in the following decade and operative in the next, create and govern the Overseas Citizen of India cardholder scheme, which is not citizenship at all and must never be confused with it. Section 14A, inserted in 2003, empowers the compulsory registration of citizens and the issue of national identity cards. Section 15 provides for revision of orders, and the rule-making power and the schedules, including the Third Schedule of qualifications for naturalisation, complete the scheme.

Two definitions carry more weight than their placement in Section 2 suggests. The first is the definition of a person of Indian origin, which looks to birth in India or in territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947, and which feeds the registration route. The second is the definition of an illegal migrant, inserted by the amendment of 2003, which Section 2(1)(b) frames as a foreigner who has entered India without a valid passport or other travel documents, or who, having entered with such documents, remains in India beyond the period permitted by them. That definition is a gate across the whole Act. A person who answers to it is shut out of registration and naturalisation altogether, and since December 2004 the status also reaches into the birth route, because the child of a citizen and an illegal migrant, born in India, is not a citizen by birth. The amendment of 2019 operated by carving specified classes out of that definition, which is why the definition, although framed in 2003, became the hinge of the later controversy; the pillar notes the hinge and leaves the controversy to its owner article.

It is worth pausing on what the Act does not do. It creates no general appellate tribunal for citizenship questions, and it confers no citizenship by marriage, by property ownership, by investment or by length of mere presence unconnected with a statutory category. Presence matters, heavily, inside the registration and naturalisation routes, but presence alone, however long, confers nothing unless the person also falls within a category the Act names and satisfies the conditions the Act and the rules prescribe. Conversely, the Act is generous in one respect that surprises many readers: its acquisition provisions operate by force of law where their conditions are met. Citizenship by birth and by descent is not granted by an official; it exists because the statute says it exists, and the certificate or passport that records it is evidence of a status already held, not the source of the status. Registration and naturalisation are different in kind, because there an authority receives an application, examines conditions and issues a certificate, and the status follows the grant.

The Act also sits inside a constitutional discipline that its text does not spell out. Because citizenship determines membership of the political community, and because rights under Articles 15, 16, 19 and 29 are framed for citizens, an order depriving a person of citizenship carries consequences far beyond the file in which it is made. The courts have treated the deprivation power accordingly, insisting that its grounds be proved and its procedure observed, and the equality guarantee in Article 14 has been the ground on which classifications inside citizenship law, including the Assam provision, have been tested. The 2024 judgment on Section 6A is the fullest modern statement of how that testing works, and it is treated on its own terms later in this article.

## Section 3: citizenship by birth and the three date regimes

Section 3 as enacted was a pure rule of birthplace. It provided that a person born in India on or after 26 January 1950 was a citizen of India by birth, subject to narrow exceptions of the classical kind, principally the child of a foreign diplomat enjoying immunity and the child of an enemy alien born in a place then under occupation by the enemy. The parent's nationality was irrelevant. A child born in a village in Bihar to parents who were nationals of another country was, by the fact and date of birth alone, an Indian citizen. The rule matched the circumstances of a republic that had drawn its boundaries in 1947, received and lost millions across them, and chose at the founding to attach citizenship to birth on the soil rather than to descent, which would have required every claimant to prove a parent's status in a period when records were thin and displacement was mass.

The first narrowing came with the Citizenship (Amendment) Act of 1986, brought into force on 1 July 1987. From that date, birth in India alone stopped being sufficient. A person born in India on or after 1 July 1987 was a citizen by birth only if either parent was a citizen of India at the time of the birth. The change was defended in Parliament as a response to unauthorised migration, on the reasoning that an unqualified birthplace rule rewarded entry and settlement that the law had not authorised, because the child of such entry acquired, by birth, a status the parents could not have obtained. The critics of the change answered that it converted a simple, provable fact, a birth, into a compound fact, a birth plus a parent's status, and that in a country where births and parentage were imperfectly recorded the compound fact would be hardest to prove for the poorest families. Both arguments recur, in stronger forms, at the second narrowing.

The second narrowing came with the Citizenship (Amendment) Act of 2003, in force for this purpose on 3 December 2004. For a person born in India on or after that date, Section 3 requires that both parents be citizens of India at the time of the birth, or that one parent be a citizen and the other not be an illegal migrant at that time. The structure deserves exact statement, because accounts of it are often loose. The rule is not that both parents must always be citizens: the alternative limb is satisfied where one parent is a citizen and the other parent, although a foreigner, entered and remained lawfully. What defeats the claim is the combination of a non-citizen parent who is an illegal migrant within the Section 2(1)(b) definition. The inquiry therefore has three elements, the place and date of birth, the citizenship of each parent at that date, and, where a parent was not a citizen, the legality of that parent's entry and stay at that date. Each element concerns a past moment, and the third concerns the conduct and documents of another person at that moment.

Read together, the three regimes form a descending staircase of automaticity. In the first regime the State asks one question. In the second it asks two. In the third it asks three, the last of which may be unanswerable from records that no longer exist or never existed. The series thesis thread that runs through this statute is visible in that staircase in its purest form: a change framed as a definition, who counts as an illegal migrant, and a change framed as a condition, what a parent must be, together shifted the decisive fact in a citizenship claim from the applicant's own birth to a parent's status at that birth. A person asserting citizenship by birth after 2004 is, in substance, litigating the position of the previous generation, and every adviser in the field knows that the file that decides the case is usually a parent's file, not the applicant's.

The exceptions in Section 3 repay attention because they are sometimes inflated in retelling. The diplomatic exception concerns a parent who is an envoy of a foreign sovereign power accredited to the President and is not a citizen of India; the enemy alien exception concerns birth in a place under enemy occupation. Neither touches the ordinary case. Nor does Section 3 contain any requirement of residence after birth, any condition about the parents' intention to settle, or any provision for the status to be lost by absence. Birth citizenship, once it attaches under the regime governing the date, is citizenship in full, carrying every right the Constitution reserves to citizens, and it is the status least exposed to later state action, a point that matters when deprivation is reached below.

### Q: Which birth regime applies to a person born in India in 1995?

The second regime applies. A person born in India in 1995, on or after 1 July 1987 and before 3 December 2004, is a citizen by birth if either parent was a citizen of India at the time of the birth. The other parent's nationality is irrelevant under this regime.

The legality of a non-citizen parent's entry and stay is likewise not in issue, because the illegal migrant condition entered Section 3 only with the regime that governs births on or after 3 December 2004. The fact to prove is one parent's citizenship at the date of birth.

Practical proof follows the structure of the rule. The applicant's birth record establishes the place and date, and the parent's status is established through the parent's own citizenship evidence, which may be a passport, a voter record, a birth record under the first regime, or a certificate of registration or naturalisation. Where the parent's claim itself rests on birth in India before 1987, the chain is short and strong. Where it rests on a registration or a descent claim, the chain lengthens, and the file must be built link by link. The Act nowhere prescribes a single document as conclusive for these purposes; citizenship is a status determined on the whole of the evidence, and Section 9 disputes and Foreigners Tribunal proceedings, where they arise, are decided on that basis rather than on the presence or absence of one paper.


## Section 4: citizenship by descent and the consular registration condition

Section 4 governs the child born outside India. As enacted, it attached citizenship by descent through the father: a person born abroad on or after 26 January 1950 was a citizen by descent if the father was a citizen of India at the time of the birth, with a further condition for the case where the father's own citizenship was by descent alone, in which case the birth was to be registered at an Indian consulate, or the father was to be in service under a Government in India. The structure embodied two judgments. The first was that the connection of blood justified the claim where the parent held citizenship in the ordinary way. The second was that descent could not run indefinitely down generations born abroad without some act reconnecting the line to India, and consular registration was the act chosen.

The amendment of 1992 changed the transmitting parent: from 10 December 1992, either parent could confer citizenship by descent, so a person born outside India on or after that date is a citizen by descent if either parent was a citizen at the time of the birth. The amendment of 2003 then tightened the route from the other side, by requiring, for births on or after 3 December 2004, that the birth be registered at an Indian consulate within one year of the date of birth, or within any later period allowed where the Central Government is satisfied the registration could not be made in time, together with a declaration by the parents that the child does not hold a passport of another country. The consequence of non-registration is severe and frequently misunderstood in diaspora families: the child does not acquire citizenship by descent at all, and discovering the omission in adulthood, when a passport application or an Overseas Citizen of India application exposes it, leaves the person to the registration or naturalisation routes, which have their own conditions and waiting periods.

Descent and birth interact in ways advisers must keep separate. Descent turns on a parent's citizenship, not on the soil, and it is indifferent to the law of the country of birth, which may independently confer its own citizenship on the child; Indian law's answer to that overlap is not to deny the descent claim at birth but to apply Section 9 when the person voluntarily acquires a foreign citizenship, and to require the consular declaration that the child holds no foreign passport. The route also illustrates the Act's recurring technique of attaching a time limit to a status-conferring act: one year for consular registration, extendable on cause shown, is the pattern that reappears in the declaration provisions on renunciation and in the residence computations for naturalisation.

### Q: Does a child born abroad to an Indian mother acquire citizenship by descent?

A child born outside India on or after 10 December 1992 acquires citizenship by descent if either parent, the mother included, was a citizen of India at the time of the birth. Before the amendment of 1992, the transmitting parent under the enacted text was the father alone.

For a birth on or after 3 December 2004 the claim carries a further condition: the birth must be registered at an Indian consulate within one year, or within an extended period allowed on cause shown, and the parents must declare that the child does not hold a passport of another country.

## Section 5: citizenship by registration and its named categories

Section 5 is the Act's discretionary door for persons with a defined connection to India who do not hold citizenship by birth or descent. It empowers the prescribed authority to register as a citizen a person who is not an illegal migrant and who falls within the categories the section names. The categories, in the form the section has carried in the modern period, are these. A person of Indian origin who is ordinarily resident in India for seven years before making the application. A person of Indian origin who is ordinarily resident in any country or place outside undivided India. A person who is married to a citizen of India and is ordinarily resident in India for seven years before making the application. Minor children whose parents are citizens of India. A person of full age and capacity whose parents are registered as citizens. A person of full age and capacity who, or either of whose parents, was earlier a citizen of independent India, and who has been ordinarily resident in India for twelve months immediately before the application. And a person of full age and capacity who has been registered as an Overseas Citizen of India cardholder for five years, and who has been ordinarily resident in India for twelve months before making the application. Registration requires an oath of allegiance, and the authority may refuse, the grant being a considered administrative act rather than an entitlement that ripens automatically when the years have run.

The spouse category draws the largest volume of inquiry in the whole of Section 5, and its terms are often softened in retelling. Marriage to an Indian citizen confers nothing by itself. It places the spouse within a category eligible to apply, and the eligibility is completed by seven years of ordinary residence in India before the application. A foreign spouse who lives with the citizen spouse outside India does not accumulate the qualifying residence, and a spouse whose presence in India rests on entry or stay that makes the person an illegal migrant is outside the section altogether. The seven-year figure also appears in the Indian origin category, and advisers distinguish the two carefully, because the origin category turns on the applicant's own origin and residence, while the spouse category turns on the marriage and the residence, and the evidence for each is different in kind.

The Overseas Citizen of India bridge in the last category is the point where the cardholder scheme touches true citizenship. Five years as a registered cardholder, plus twelve months of ordinary residence in India immediately before the application, qualifies the cardholder to seek registration as a citizen. The bridge matters for the structure of the Act because it shows the cardholder scheme functioning as a waiting room rather than a destination for one class of its holders: those with the connection and the willingness to reside can cross into citizenship through a named door, while those who remain abroad hold the card's parity rights and no more. Registration, once granted, makes the person a citizen for all purposes, but it leaves a trace in the Act's scheme of loss, because the deprivation power in Section 10 reaches citizens by registration while leaving citizens by birth and descent beyond its reach.

## Section 6 and the Third Schedule: naturalisation and its qualifications

Section 6 is the route for the person with no origin, marriage or descent connection who seeks citizenship on the strength of residence, character and attachment. It permits the Central Government, where satisfied that the applicant is not an illegal migrant and fulfils the qualifications specified in the Third Schedule, to grant a certificate of naturalisation. The Third Schedule qualifications, which the brief for this pillar required to be verified item by item and which are stated here as the Schedule frames them, are these. The applicant is not a subject or citizen of a country in which, by its law or practice, citizens of India are prevented from becoming citizens by naturalisation. The applicant, if a citizen of another country, has renounced that citizenship, or undertakes to renounce it on the grant, in accordance with the Schedule's terms. The applicant has, throughout the period of twelve months immediately preceding the date of the application, either resided in India or been in the service of a Government in India. During the fourteen years immediately preceding that twelve-month period, the applicant has resided in India or been in such government service for periods amounting in the aggregate to not less than eleven years. The applicant is of good character. The applicant has an adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution. And the applicant intends, if the certificate is granted, either to reside in India or to enter or continue in the service of a Government in India, or of an international organisation of which India is a member, or of a body established in India.

The residence arithmetic is the qualification most often understated in popular accounts, which report eleven years as though it were the whole of the requirement. The Schedule in fact demands twelve years of presence in a defined pattern: the twelve months immediately before the application, complete, and eleven further years in aggregate within the fourteen years before those twelve months. The pattern tolerates absence, since the eleven years are an aggregate rather than a continuous stretch, but it does not tolerate a recent arrival, however strong the other qualifications, because the final twelve months must be satisfied as a block. Character and language are examined on the material the rules prescribe, and the oath of allegiance in the Second Schedule completes the grant. Naturalisation, like registration, leaves the holder within the reach of Section 10 deprivation, and the certificate may also be revisited where it was procured by false representation, a matter taken up with loss below.

The Schedule carries one further provision of real significance, the waiver. Where, in the opinion of the Central Government, an applicant has rendered distinguished service to the cause of science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace or human progress generally, the Government may waive all or any of the conditions specified in the Third Schedule. The waiver is a considered exception for eminence, framed around fields of human achievement rather than wealth or influence, and it operates on the qualifications, not on the threshold bars: it does not convert an illegal migrant into an eligible applicant, because Section 6's own words place that class outside the route. Accounts that present the waiver as a general power to naturalise anyone the Government favours misstate its text twice over, once on its fields and once on its limits.

## Section 7: incorporation of territory, and the five routes in one view

Section 7 is the route no individual applies for. It provides that where territory becomes part of India, the Central Government may, by order notified in the Official Gazette, specify the persons who shall be citizens of India by reason of their connection with that territory, with effect from a date specified in the order. The provision is the statutory complement to the constitutional provisions on territory, and its occasions have been the accessions and transfers that redrew the map in the decades after independence, including the integration of former enclaves and the admission of Sikkim in 1975, where the question of who became a citizen, and from what date, was answered by order under this head rather than by individual application. Its presence in the list of five routes is a reminder that citizenship law in India was written for a State whose territory was still settling, and that the Act had to speak to whole populations as well as to individual applicants.

The five routes can be held in one view, which is the payload the brief for this pillar set as its first test. Birth asks where and when a person was born, and, under the later regimes, what the parents were at that moment. Descent asks who a parent was when a child was born abroad, and whether the consular act was done in time. Registration asks which named category the applicant fills and whether the residence and oath conditions are met. Naturalisation asks whether a stranger to every category has satisfied the Third Schedule's long residence, character, language and intention tests. Incorporation asks what an order of the Central Government said when territory changed hands. Four of the five turn on facts located at a moment in the past, and three of the five turn on the facts of persons other than, or additional to, the applicant. That distribution, more than any single rule, explains the character of practice under the Act.

The table compresses, and compression has a cost that the surrounding text is there to repay. It cannot show that the birth route's third regime can fail on the status of a parent who never applied for anything, or that descent can fail by the passage of a single year in a consulate queue of the mind, because a family did not know the registration condition existed. It cannot show that the deciding authority differs in kind between the routes that operate by law and the routes that operate by grant. What it does show, and what makes it the object every applicant reaches for, is the shape of the inquiry in each route: the qualifying fact, the period that frames it, the evidence that bears on it, and the door that remains open when the first door closes.

## The illegal migrant definition and the gate it keeps

Section 2(1)(b), inserted by the amendment of 2003, defines an illegal migrant as a foreigner who has entered India without a valid passport or other travel documents, or with a valid passport or other travel documents but who remains in India beyond the period permitted by those documents or by any rule or order under the foreigners legislation. Two limbs, entry without documents and overstay with documents, exhaust the definition. Its consequences run through the Act at three points. It bars registration under Section 5, whose categories each presuppose that the applicant is not an illegal migrant. It bars naturalisation under Section 6 in the same terms. And it enters Section 3 itself, because the third birth regime denies citizenship by birth to the child whose citizen parent's partner was an illegal migrant at the time of the birth. A definition in Section 2 therefore decides cases in the acquisition spine, the spouse's application, the long resident's naturalisation and the newborn's status alike.

The definition's edges matter in practice. A foreigner who entered lawfully and whose stay became irregular for a short period raises a different file from a foreigner who crossed a border without documents, but the definition as framed gathers both into one term, and the consequences of the term do not grade themselves by the gravity of the irregularity. Questions of who is a foreigner at all, and of detection and expulsion, belong to the foreigners legislation rather than to this Act, and the point is taken up where the Assam provision is treated, through the link placed there to the pillar on the Foreigners Act. Within this Act, the definition's function is distributive: it sorts applicants into those who may use the discretionary routes and those who may not, and it does so by reference to the manner of an entry that may lie many years behind the application. That is the same backward-looking structure the birth regimes display, and it is why the series treats this statute as a study in how definitional choices made for one purpose harden into evidential burdens for people who were never their target.

Section 14A, also inserted in 2003, belongs in this part of the reading because it expresses the same documentary turn from the side of the State. It provides that the Central Government may compulsorily register every citizen of India and issue a national identity card to that citizen, and that the Registrar General of India shall act as the National Registration Authority for the purpose. The provision is an enabling one. It neither creates citizenship nor makes the card its proof in terms, and the Act is careful, here as elsewhere, to separate the status from the instrument that records it. Its significance for the pillar is structural: a statute that began in 1955 by declaring statuses on the strength of birth and descent acquired, half a century later, a provision for enumerating the citizenry, and the distance between those two conceptions of what the State needs to know is the distance the whole field travelled in the interval.

The remaining machinery can be stated shortly. The Citizenship Rules, first made in 1956 and replaced in 2009, prescribe the authorities, forms, fees and inquiries for registration and naturalisation, and the evidentiary material on which applications are examined. Section 13 provides for certificates of citizenship in cases of doubt, by which the Central Government may certify that a person is a citizen where the question is raised, the certificate being strong evidence of the status it records. Section 15 empowers the Central Government to revise orders made under the Act, other than the orders excepted by the section, on application. Section 15A, inserted by amendment, provides for review of orders of a prescribed authority in the matters it covers. None of this machinery confers citizenship outside the five routes; all of it exists to determine, evidence and correct the determinations the routes produce.

## Section 6A: the special provision for Assam

Section 6A stands outside the five routes and outside the pattern of the Act, because it is a citizenship provision for one State, keyed to one history, and it confers and withholds status by reference to dates of entry into Assam from a specified territory. The specified territory is the territory that became Bangladesh. The section was inserted by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act of 1985, the legislative instrument that followed the Assam Accord of 15 August 1985, the agreement between the Government of India and the leaders of the Assam movement that had agitated, for six years, the question of the detection and deletion of the names of foreigners from the electoral rolls of the State and their expulsion. Detection and expulsion themselves are governed by the foreigners legislation, whose tribunal machinery is treated in the pillar on the [Foreigners Act 1946](/2021/11/01/foreigners-act-1946-india-explained/), and Section 6A presupposes that machinery at the point where a person is detected as a foreigner. What Section 6A does is to state the citizenship consequence that follows for each of three classes of person of Indian origin who came to Assam from the specified territory.

The first class comprises persons who came to Assam before 1 January 1966 and who have been ordinarily resident in Assam since the date of their entry. Such a person is deemed to be a citizen of India for all purposes as from 1 January 1966. The provision is a collective regularisation of the partition generation and its aftermath: it asks for entry before the date and residence since, and having those, it deems the status without individual inquiry into the manner of entry. The second class comprises persons who came to Assam on or after 1 January 1966 and before 25 March 1971, and who have been ordinarily resident in Assam since. Such a person is not deemed a citizen at entry. The machinery is conditional: the person must be detected as a foreigner, must then register with the authority the section names, and acquires citizenship when ten years have expired from the date of detection, during which period the person enjoys the rights of a citizen except one: the name is not included in any electoral roll for any Assembly or Parliamentary constituency. The franchise is suspended for the decade and the status completes when the decade runs. The third class comprises persons who came to Assam on or after 25 March 1971. For them Section 6A confers nothing: they are to be detected as foreigners and dealt with under the foreigners legislation, including expulsion.

The choice of dates is the provision's whole logic, and the Supreme Court's later analysis returns to it repeatedly. The first date, 1 January 1966, marks a boundary the Accord drew within the migration that followed partition and the formation of Pakistan's eastern wing. The second date, 25 March 1971, is the date on which the military action in East Pakistan began that produced the movement of people preceding the liberation of Bangladesh in December 1971. The provision thus distinguishes, among persons of Indian origin entering Assam from the same territory, between the displaced of the partition era, whom it deems citizens, the entrants of the intervening years, whom it converts into citizens after detection, registration and a decade off the rolls, and the entrants of the 1971 crisis and after, whom it leaves to the foreigners regime. A provision of this kind is found nowhere else in the Act, and its presence required explanation at the level of constitutional principle, because Article 14 promises equality before the law and the rest of the Act speaks to India as a whole.

It should be recorded, for accuracy, what Section 6A is not. It is not a constitutional amendment: it is an ordinary amendment of this Act, inserted by Parliament in the exercise of the Article 11 power, and the route by which constitutional text itself changes, treated in the pillar on the [process of amending the Constitution](/2010/01/01/constitution-amendment-process-india/), was not the route taken. Its validity was therefore open to challenge on the ordinary grounds available against legislation, legislative competence and fundamental rights, and it was on those grounds that it came before a Constitution Bench. It is also not a provision about religion, community or language on its face: its classes are built from origin, territory, dates and residence, and the arguments about it, for and against, were conducted on those terms in the Accord, in Parliament and, eventually, in the Court.

### Q: Why does the citizenship statute contain a provision for one State alone?

Section 6A exists because the migration history of Assam differed in scale, duration and political consequence from that of any other State, and because the Assam Accord of 1985 settled a six year movement on terms that required a legislative answer within citizenship law. Parliament enacted those terms as this provision.

The Accord fixed the dates, the detection and registration machinery and the decade of exclusion from the electoral rolls for the middle class of entrants. No other State received such a provision because no other State produced an accord demanding one, and that singularity became the heart of the constitutional challenge.

## The Constitution Bench judgment of 17 October 2024 on Section 6A

The validity of Section 6A was decided by a Constitution Bench of five judges of the Supreme Court on 17 October 2024, in the reference and petitions grouped under the title In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act 1955. By a majority of four to one, the Court upheld the provision. The bench comprised Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud and Justices Surya Kant, M. M. Sundresh, J. B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra. Justice Pardiwala dissented. The majority's reasoning, stated across the opinions of the Chief Justice and of Justice Surya Kant, with Justices Sundresh and Manoj Misra concurring in the result, proceeded on three questions: whether Parliament had the competence to enact the provision, whether the classification it makes offends Article 14, and whether the cut-off dates are arbitrary.

On competence, the majority held that Parliament's power over citizenship under Article 11 and Entry 17 of the Union List extends to a provision of this character, and that the argument from the federal structure, that a single-State citizenship provision invades a field belonging to the States, failed because citizenship is an exclusively Union subject and a law on a Union subject does not lose its character by operating, in fact, upon the situation of one State. On equality, the majority applied the settled test of reasonable classification: a classification survives Article 14 where it rests on an intelligible differentia and the differentia bears a rational relation to the object the law seeks to achieve. The historical circumstances of Assam, a State that received migration on a scale and over a duration no other State experienced, together with the Assam Accord, which the majority characterised as a political solution of which Section 6A was the legislative solution, supplied the legitimate basis for treating Assam's entrants as a class apart. The guarantee of equality, the majority reasoned, permits Parliament to address a problem where it exists with the intensity the provision addresses, and does not require that a remedy framed for a distinct situation be extended to situations that do not share it.

On the dates, the majority held that neither cut-off was arbitrary. The earlier date, 1 January 1966, marked a significant moment in the State's immigration history, the boundary the Accord had drawn between the migration of the partition period and what followed. The later date, 25 March 1971, coincided with the commencement of the conflict that produced a mass movement of people into Assam, and drawing the line there distinguished those displaced by partition, for whom the provision was framed, from those who came afterwards. A cut-off date always leaves a person one day on the wrong side of it in a position different from a neighbour's, and the majority treated that consequence as inherent in any date-based settlement rather than as a mark of arbitrariness, provided the date itself answers to a real event or a reasoned boundary, which both dates did.

The majority added observations that point away from complacency about the provision's operation. It recorded that Section 6A confers nothing on persons entering after 25 March 1971, so that its beneficent operation is confined to a closed and ageing class. It recorded that the statutory machinery and the tribunals charged with detection were inadequate and not proportionate to giving time-bound effect to the legislative object, a criticism directed at implementation rather than at the provision's text. And it held that the implementation of the immigration and citizenship legislation in Assam should be monitored by the Court, so that the detection the provision presupposes does not remain, in the majority's assessment, a paper exercise. The upholding of the provision and the criticism of its working were delivered together, and accounts that report only the upholding omit half of what the majority said.

The dissent of Justice Pardiwala would have declared Section 6A prospectively invalid, that is, inoperative from the date of the judgment rather than void from its enactment, so that statuses settled under it in the intervening decades were not disturbed. Its reasoning ran in a direction most summaries of the case reverse, and the brief for this pillar directs that the direction be stated exactly: the dissent's objection was advanced from the standpoint of the indigenous population of Assam, not from the standpoint of the migrants the provision benefited. Justice Pardiwala reasoned that the provision departs from a nationally uniform framework of citizenship without adequate justification for the departure's indefinite continuation; that the absence of any temporal limit on its operation renders it susceptible to arbitrary implementation, because a provision addressed to a defined historical episode continues to govern detection and regularisation long after the episode has closed; that it fails to afford stronger protection to the indigenous population of the State, whose position the Accord itself had undertaken to safeguard; and, in a proposition of general importance, that a provision which satisfies the test of reasonable classification when enacted may become unconstitutional with the passage of time, as the circumstances that justified the classification fall away. On that reasoning the provision had outlived its justification, and the constitutional defect lay in its continuation, which is why the invalidation proposed was prospective only.

### Q: What is the single most misreported feature of the Section 6A judgment?

The direction of the dissent. Accounts commonly present the four to one split as a majority generous to migrants against a dissent strict with them. The record runs the other way on the dissent's central ground, which was the position of the indigenous population of Assam.

In full: Justice Pardiwala would have struck the provision down, prospectively, because it did not protect the indigenous population of Assam strongly enough and had continued without a temporal limit long after its justifying circumstances had passed. The majority upheld the provision as a reasonable classification answering to the Accord; the dissent condemned it as a classification that time had emptied. Both opinions were about the State and its people; they differed on whether the 1985 settlement could still carry the provision in 2024.

The judgment's place in the statute's life can be fixed. It settles the competence question and the classification question for Section 6A on the materials of the Accord and the State's history. It leaves the provision's operation exposed on the implementation side, where the majority itself found the machinery wanting and set monitoring in train. And it places in the reports, through the dissent, a doctrine with reach beyond Assam: that constitutionality under the classification test is not a permanent character a provision acquires at enactment, but a relation between the provision and its circumstances, which the passage of time can dissolve. Future argument about date-based settlements in citizenship law will begin from the majority's deference to the Accord and will have to answer the dissent's question about duration.


## Loss of citizenship: renunciation, termination and deprivation

The Act states three modes of loss, and they are not interchangeable. Renunciation under Section 8 is the citizen's own act. Termination under Section 9 is the automatic consequence of a defined act of the citizen. Deprivation under Section 10 is an order of the State, confined to two classes of citizen and hedged with procedure. The order in which the Act presents them is a descending scale of voluntariness, and practice under each turns on keeping the three apart.

Section 8 provides that a citizen of full age and capacity may make a declaration renouncing citizenship, and that where the declaration is made in the prescribed manner and registered, the person ceases to be a citizen from the date of registration, with a power to withhold registration while India is engaged in a war. The consequences reach the family in defined ways. Where a person renounces, every minor child of that person also ceases to be a citizen, with a safeguard that rescues the child's position: such a child may, within one year after attaining full age, make a declaration of resumption of Indian citizenship. The spouse of a renouncing citizen is not touched by the declaration; a spouse loses citizenship only by the spouse's own declaration or by operation of another provision. Renunciation is the mode of loss that governs the common case of emigration planning, where a person naturalising abroad must deal with two legal systems at once, because India does not permit dual citizenship and the foreign system's oath and India's declaration must be sequenced with care.

Section 9 is the provision that catches emigrants who do not plan. It provides that any citizen of India who, by naturalisation, registration or otherwise, voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country ceases, upon that acquisition, to be a citizen of India. The termination is automatic: it needs no order, no registration and no knowledge on the part of the person concerned, and it operates from the moment of the voluntary acquisition abroad. The exceptions are narrow, covering acquisition during a war in which India is engaged until the Central Government otherwise directs, and the position of minors is worked out through the section's own terms. Where a question arises whether, when or how a person has acquired the citizenship of another country, Section 9(2) commits the determination to the Central Government, and the Citizenship Rules make that determination conclusive for the purposes of the section, reached after an inquiry in which the person concerned is heard. It is in Section 9 proceedings that the passport of the other country, the oath taken abroad and the dates on a naturalisation certificate become the decisive documents, and it is here that many returning emigrants discover that a status they assumed they held in parallel with a foreign citizenship in fact ended, by operation of law, on the day the foreign citizenship began. India permits no dual citizenship, and Section 9 is the provision that enforces the single allegiance the scheme presupposes.

Section 10, deprivation, is the State's power, and its limits are the first thing to state about it. It is available only against a person who is a citizen by registration or by naturalisation. It does not reach a citizen by birth, a citizen by descent or a citizen by incorporation of territory. The citizen by birth in India, whatever the conduct alleged, cannot be deprived of citizenship by order under this Act; the remedies of the State against such a person lie in the criminal law and the foreigners legislation where applicable, not in denationalisation. That limit is a deliberate feature of the enacted scheme, and it creates a graded security of status across the Act's routes: the statuses that arise by operation of law at birth and by descent are the most secure, and the statuses granted on application are the least, because the granting State retained, in Section 10, a power to recall the grant on defined grounds.

The grounds of deprivation, in Section 10(2), are these: that the registration or certificate of naturalisation was obtained by means of fraud, false representation or the concealment of any material circumstance; that the person has shown himself by act or speech to be disloyal or disaffected towards the Constitution of India as by law established; that the person has, during any war in which India is engaged, unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy, or been engaged in or associated with business carried on in a manner assisting an enemy in that war; that the person has, within five years after registration or naturalisation, been sentenced in any country to imprisonment for a term of not less than two years; and that the person has been ordinarily resident out of India for a continuous period of seven years without maintaining a close connection with India in the manner the section specifies, including registration at an Indian consulate or service under the Government or an international organisation. Section 10(3) adds the public interest discipline: the Central Government shall not deprive a person of citizenship unless satisfied that it is not conducive to the public good that the person should continue to be a citizen. Procedure follows the gravity of the power. The person must be given notice of the ground alleged and of the right to have the case referred to a committee of inquiry, and where the ground is other than fraud of a kind the section excepts, the reference follows on the person's application, the committee being chaired by a person who has held judicial office for the period the section requires. Deprivation is therefore an adjudicated act, not an administrative stroke, and the reported challenges to deprivation orders have turned as much on the observance of that procedure and the sufficiency of the ground as on the power's existence.

## Sections 7A to 7D: the Overseas Citizen of India cardholder scheme

The Overseas Citizen of India scheme, in Sections 7A to 7D, inserted by amendment and operative from the following decade, creates a status that its name advertises and its content contradicts: the cardholder is not a citizen. Section 7A empowers the Central Government to register as an Overseas Citizen of India cardholder a person of full age and capacity who is a citizen of another country and who was a citizen of India on or after 26 January 1950, or was eligible to become one, or belonged to a territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947, together with the children and grandchildren of such a person and the spouse of a citizen or of a cardholder on the conditions the section states, subject to the exclusion of applicants who are or were citizens of Pakistan or Bangladesh or of any other country the Central Government specifies. The scheme answered a long-standing demand of the Indian diaspora for a durable connection that stopped short of the dual citizenship the Constitution and Section 9 forbid.

Section 7B states the cardholder's entitlements by a technique of notification and parity. The cardholder is entitled to such rights as the Central Government specifies by notification, and the notifications have conferred parity with non-resident Indians in economic, financial and educational fields, together with a multiple entry, multipurpose lifelong visa facility and exemption from the registration and reporting requirements that bind other foreigners. The same section withholds, in terms, parity in the acquisition of agricultural or plantation properties, and the card confers no right to public employment, to hold constitutional offices such as those of President, Vice-President, judge of the Supreme Court or a High Court, member of Parliament or of a State legislature, or to vote. The cardholder's position is therefore a bundle of residence, entry and economic rights held by a foreign citizen, valuable precisely because it is durable, and limited precisely where citizenship begins: franchise, office and the land categories named.

Section 7C provides for renunciation of the cardholder registration by declaration, with consequences for the spouse and minor children of the declarant on the pattern of Section 8. Section 7D provides for cancellation of the registration, and its grounds must be stated fully because they are among the most searched in the Act and were materially widened in 2019. The Central Government may cancel the registration where it was obtained by fraud, false representation or concealment of a material fact; where the cardholder has shown disaffection towards the Constitution; where the cardholder has, during a war in which India is engaged, unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy or assisted an enemy's business; where the cardholder has, within five years after the issue of the registration, been sentenced to imprisonment for a term of not less than two years; where cancellation is necessary in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations with any foreign country, or the interests of the general public; and, by the ground added in 2019, where the cardholder has violated any provision of this Act or of any other law for the time being in force as may be specified by the Central Government in a notification. Cancellation requires that the cardholder be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. The parallel with Section 10 is instructive and incomplete: the grounds borrow the deprivation vocabulary, but the status cancelled is a bundle of facilities rather than citizenship itself, and the hearing the section guarantees is the procedural protection the cardholder receives in place of the committee of inquiry that guards deprivation.

The width of the 2019 ground deserves the comment the brief for this pillar directs, presented without embellishment. A cancellation ground defined by violation of any other law the Central Government may specify is, in form, a delegation of the definition of the ground itself: the statute fixes the category, violation of law, and leaves the selection of laws to notification, so the exposure of a cardholder depends on instruments that can change without amendment of the Act. Comment on the provision, in the legal press and in commentary on the 2019 amendment, has fastened on that feature, and on the contrast between the narrowness of the original grounds, which tracked the grave conduct of the deprivation list, and the open texture of the added ground. Defenders of the change answer that a card is a facility granted by the State to foreign citizens, that its withdrawal for breach of the host State's laws is an ordinary incident of such schemes in other countries, and that the hearing requirement disciplines the power in the individual case. Both positions are about the same sentence in Section 7D, and the pillar records them without adopting either, because the provision's meaning in practice will be settled by the notifications issued under it and by the courts' reading of the hearing guarantee, neither of which a statute pillar should anticipate.

### Q: What does an OCI cardholder get that a foreign citizen without a card does not?

Durability and parity in defined fields. The cardholder holds a lifelong, multiple entry facility for visiting India and is exempt from the foreigner registration and reporting regime, and has parity with non-resident Indians in economic, financial and educational fields, save for agricultural and plantation property.

In full: the parity rests on notification under Section 7B and is expressly withheld for the acquisition of agricultural and plantation properties. The card confers no vote, no eligibility for public employment or constitutional office, and no citizenship. It is a durable bundle of facilities held by a person who remains, in law, a citizen of another country.

## The narrowing of the birth route: the argument on both sides

A statute pillar under the series charter must give each side of a contested question its strongest form, in its own voice, before taking any verdict the evidence supports. The narrowing of Section 3, in 1986 and again in 2003, is the contested question at the centre of this Act, and the arguments are these.

The case for the narrowing rests on incentive and on comparison. Its proponents, in the parliamentary debates on both amendments and in the reports that preceded them, argue that an unqualified birthplace rule creates a citizenship premium on unauthorised entry: a person who enters and remains without authority places a child beyond the reach of the immigration law that governs the parents, and over a generation the composition of the citizenry is altered by entries the State never authorised. A rule of that kind, they argue, is an outlier: most legal systems condition citizenship by birth on the status of a parent, requiring a citizen parent or at least a lawfully resident one, and the amendments brought India into line with that general position while retaining birth citizenship for the settled population in full. The second narrowing's specific target, on this account, was the case of two parents neither of whom had any lawful connection with India, and the alternative limb, one citizen parent or one citizen and one lawful entrant, was framed to protect the ordinary mixed family while closing the premium. The proponents add a point about administration: a citizenship law that asks nothing about parents is simple because it declines to police the boundary at all, and a State that operates foreigners legislation, tribunals and deportation cannot coherently declare the boundary irrelevant at the moment of birth.

The case against the narrowing rests on proof. Its critics, including lawyers who conduct citizenship and tribunal practice in Assam and elsewhere and commentators on the documentary regime, argue that the condition the amendments impose is a condition about a past fact concerning another person, and that in large parts of India the records capable of proving that fact, a parent's citizenship or lawful entry at a date decades earlier, do not exist, were never created, or sit in offices and formats no applicant can reach. Birth registration itself was historically incomplete, parentage is recorded unevenly, and the lawful entrant of 2005 may hold no document that survives to be produced in 2035. The burden of the rule therefore falls, in operation, not on the unauthorised entrant at whom it was aimed, who is outside every route in any event, but on the settled poor, whose births were unregistered, whose parents' papers were never issued, and whose claims are then examined by machinery designed for the detection of foreigners. A rule can be defensible in its classification and still be indefensible in its evidence, and the critics' strongest formulation is that the amendments chose the classification without building the record system that alone could make the classification fairly administrable.

The verdict the record supports is narrower than either side's rhetoric. The classification itself, conditioning birth citizenship on a parent's connection, is within the range of choices comparable systems make, and the Supreme Court's treatment of Section 6A shows the deference a reasoned, dated settlement receives under Article 14. The documentary objection, by contrast, has only strengthened with time, because each year carries the operative birth further from the records of the parent's status, and the State has not built, under Section 14A or otherwise, the universal registration that would let the condition be proved as a matter of routine. The narrowing is therefore best understood as a defensible rule carrying an indefensible proof burden, and reform argument is most honestly directed at the burden, at registration, records and the standard of proof in status proceedings, rather than at the classification the burden serves. That is also where the series thesis lands in this statute: the decisive legal event in a modern citizenship claim is rarely the rule, and almost always the evidence the rule demands.

Documentary questions of this order are questions about the survival and custody of family papers across decades. Families and advisers who assemble a status file draw on birth registers, school records, land records, electoral rolls and the papers of the parent generation, and the integrity of that assembly matters as much as its contents, which is why those who research contested family histories protect their source material with the discipline described in the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), a discipline of custody and verification that a citizenship file rewards in exactly the same way. This article is legal information about a statute, not advice on any individual claim, and a person whose status is in issue requires advice on that person's own facts.

## The constitutional starting population and what the Act inherited

To understand why the Act of 1955 is framed as it is, the reader must begin with the population the Constitution itself settled on 26 January 1950, because the Act's routes were drafted for the people that settlement left undecided. Article 5 conferred citizenship at commencement on a person domiciled in India who was born in India, or either of whose parents was born in India, or who had been ordinarily resident in India for not less than five years immediately before commencement. Article 6 dealt with migrants from Pakistan, distinguishing those who came before 19 July 1948, the date the permit system began, from those who came after, for whom registration was required, and keying each class to a parent or grandparent born in India as defined in the Government of India Act 1935. Article 7 dealt with migration to Pakistan after 1 March 1947, withholding citizenship save for those who returned under a permit for resettlement. Article 8 protected persons of Indian origin residing outside India who registered at an Indian diplomatic or consular office. These articles were a settlement of the partition movement, drafted for a defined stock of people at a defined moment, and their technique, dates, registration and parental birth, is the direct ancestor of the techniques this pillar has traced through the Act.

The Act inherited three problems from that settlement. The first was continuity: the constitutional classes ended at commencement, and every birth, migration and marriage after 26 January 1950 required a rule, which Sections 3 to 7 supplied. The second was the treatment of territory in motion: the Constitution spoke to the India of 1950, and the Act had to provide, in Section 7 and in the citizenship orders made when States were reorganised or territories integrated, for populations whose connection with India arose from the movement of boundaries rather than of people. The third was the foreign connection of the diaspora, which Article 8 had handled by consular registration and which the Act eventually answered, half a century later, with the cardholder scheme rather than with citizenship. Reading the Act against Articles 5 to 8 also explains its silences. There is no route for the long-settled foreign resident outside the Third Schedule because the constitutional imagination of 1950 did not treat India as a country of immigration; there is a route for the person of Indian origin at every turn because the constitutional imagination was formed by the departure and return of Indians across the partition lines and the older indenture and trading diasporas.

One further constitutional feature conditions everything the Act does: citizenship under the Constitution and the Act is a single, uniform status for the whole Union. There is no State citizenship in India, no citizenship of Assam or of Maharashtra standing beside the citizenship of India, and the contrast with federations that maintain a dual citizenship of State and Union is fundamental. Section 6A's singularity must be understood against that background: it is not a State citizenship but a Union citizenship provision whose qualifying facts happen to be located in one State, which is exactly the characterisation the majority in the 2024 judgment adopted when the federal objection was pressed. The rights that follow the status, by contrast, are uniform everywhere: the citizen of the third birth regime in Kerala and the deemed citizen of Section 6A in Assam hold the same Articles 15, 16, 19 and 29 rights, the same franchise and the same passport entitlement, and no provision of the Act grades the content of citizenship by the route through which it came. The grading the Act performs is confined to security against deprivation, and it is a grading of exposure to state recall, not of the rights held while the status lasts.

## Ordinary residence: the phrase that carries the discretionary routes

The registration and naturalisation routes turn on a phrase the Act uses without defining exhaustively: ordinarily resident. The Citizenship Rules supply the working content, treating a person as ordinarily resident where the residence is lawful and settled in character, and periods of absence are examined for whether they break the settlement or merely interrupt it. The phrase does real work in Section 5, where seven years of ordinary residence qualify the person of Indian origin and the spouse, and in the Third Schedule, where the residence computation for naturalisation is expressed in years of residence or government service. Its content matters because ordinary residence is neither mere presence nor domicile. Presence counts days; domicile imports an intention to remain permanently that the Act does not demand; ordinary residence sits between, asking whether the person's life in India has the settled quality of a home rather than the transient quality of a visit, employment posting or prolonged sojourn awaiting another destination.

The evidentiary pattern follows from the concept. Residence over seven or twelve years is proved cumulatively, through the records a settled life generates: tenancy and property records, employment and tax records, school records for children, bank and utility accounts, and the entries a person makes in official registers across the period. Gaps are not automatically fatal, because the rules contemplate absence, but unexplained absence in the final twelve months before a naturalisation application is fatal to that application, because the Schedule requires that block to be complete. Advisers therefore build the residence case backwards from the application date, securing the final year first and then assembling the aggregate years, and the difference between the two computations, a block and an aggregate, is the difference applicants most often miss when they count their years.

Ordinary residence also performs a quieter function in Section 6A and in the deprivation ground of seven years' residence abroad. In Section 6A it is the tether that converts an entrant of the first class into a deemed citizen: entry before the date is not enough without residence in Assam since the entry. In Section 10 it is the mirror image: the registered or naturalised citizen who has been ordinarily resident outside India for a continuous period of seven years, without the consular or service connections the section names, exposes the grant to recall, on the reasoning that the connection on which the grant rested has been abandoned in fact. The Act thus uses settled residence both to build citizenship up, in the discretionary routes, and to test whether it should stand, in the deprivation route, and the symmetry is one of the marks of a statute drafted as a system rather than as a list.

## Proof, burden and the proceedings in which status is decided

Citizenship under the Act is a status, but in any contested case it is decided as a question of fact on evidence, and the allocation of the burden of proof shapes outcomes as powerfully as the substantive rules. Within the four corners of this Act, the applicant for registration or naturalisation bears the burden of satisfying the authority that the conditions are met, which is the ordinary burden of a person seeking a grant. The harder question arises where the State alleges that a person is a foreigner, and there the governing provision is not in this Act at all: Section 9 of the Foreigners Act 1946 places the burden of proving that a person is not a foreigner on that person. The Supreme Court settled the interplay in Sarbananda Sonowal v Union of India in 2005, in the litigation that struck down the Illegal Migrants (Determination by Tribunals) Act 1983: the burden under the foreigners legislation lies on the person proceeded against, and the standard is that of a preponderance of probabilities, discharged by producing the documentary chain the person's claim requires. That allocation is the legal engine beneath the documentary problem this pillar has described. The substantive rule asks about a parent's status at a birth; the burden rule requires the person whose status is questioned to prove the answer; and the distance between the two is where cases are lost.

The forums follow the question. A disputed acquisition is examined by the authorities under the Act and the rules, with revision under Section 15. A Section 9 termination question, whether a person has voluntarily acquired a foreign citizenship, is determined by the Central Government under Section 9(2), and the Supreme Court has treated that determination as the exclusive route for that question, so that a passport authority or a tribunal cannot decide it collaterally. A question whether a person is a foreigner, arising in Assam and in the States where the Foreigners (Tribunals) Order 1964 operates, goes to a Foreigners Tribunal for its opinion, on a reference by the competent authority, and the opinion drives the executive action that follows. Deprivation goes through the notice and committee procedure of Section 10. Each forum applies the same statute but a different procedure, and a person's papers may have to survive several of them across a lifetime: a passport inquiry in one decade, a tribunal reference in another, an electoral roll verification in a third. Consistency of the record across those encounters is a practical condition of security, and it is achieved, where it is achieved, by families treating their documents as a single archive rather than as answers to single applications.

Two protections inside the Act deserve emphasis against the severity of the burden rule. The first is Section 13, the certificate of citizenship in case of doubt, which gives a person a route to an affirmative determination by the Central Government instead of waiting for the status to be attacked. The second is the procedural armour of Section 10 itself: notice, the right to a committee of inquiry on all but the fraud ground, and the public good condition in Section 10(3), which together mean that even the least secure class of citizen cannot be denationalised without an adjudicated case. The Act is, in this respect, more protective of the granted statuses than the debate about it sometimes allows, and the pillar records the protection because the charter of the series requires the statute to be stated whole: its gates, its burdens and its safeguards together.

## Assam before Section 6A: the history the dates encode

Section 6A's dates cannot be understood without the century of migration that preceded them, because the Accord's lines were drawn across a demographic transformation that began under colonial administration. From the nineteenth century, the colonial State encouraged movement into Assam from the districts of eastern Bengal, for the tea gardens first and then, in much larger volume, for the cultivation of the Brahmaputra valley's vacant and chars land, so that by the censuses of the early twentieth century the districts receiving the movement had been transformed in language and composition. The colonial administration itself drew a precursor of the later lines: the Line System, introduced from 1916 in several districts, sought to regulate settlement by demarcating areas, and its failure to contain the movement became part of the province's political argument. Independence and partition converted an administrative question into a question of States: Assam found itself, after the Sylhet referendum and the redrawing of 1947, a borderland State attached to the rest of India by a narrow corridor, receiving the displaced of East Pakistan in waves that followed each crisis, in 1947 and after, in 1950, in 1964 and in the largest volume in 1971.

The legal instruments of the period tracked the waves without mastering them. The Immigrants (Expulsion from Assam) Act of 1950 empowered the expulsion of persons whose stay was detrimental to the interests of the general public or of the Scheduled Tribes, with a proviso protecting those displaced by civil disturbances in Pakistan. Detection machinery under the foreigners legislation operated unevenly, and the gap between the electoral roll and the verified citizenry became the movement's central grievance from 1979, when the discovery of large numbers of names alleged to be those of foreigners on the roll for the Mangaldoi parliamentary constituency ignited the agitation that the All Assam Students' Union and the All Assam Gana Sangram Parishad led until 1985. The Assam Accord closed that movement on the terms Section 6A enacted: 1 January 1966 as the base date for regularisation, detection and the decade of franchise suspension for the middle class, and 25 March 1971 as the line beyond which entrants were to be detected and expelled. The Illegal Migrants (Determination by Tribunals) Act 1983, which had created a separate and more demanding detection procedure for Assam alone, stood apart from this settlement and was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2005 as failing the constitutional obligation to protect the State against external aggression by unauthorised migration, a judgment that restored the Foreigners Act machinery as the operative regime and that forms the background against which the 2024 majority assessed the adequacy of implementation.

The history matters to the pillar for one analytical reason. Section 6A is often read as though it were a provision about the years 1966 to 1971. It is better read as a provision about the years 1901 to 1985, compressed into two dates: the first date reaches back to gather the partition generation into citizenship by deeming, and the whole scheme presupposes that the State had failed, across eight decades, to maintain the register of who belonged that a citizenship regime requires. The documentary burden that the critics of the third birth regime identify at the national level appears in Assam in its most concentrated historical form, because the class Section 6A regularised was a class whose papers the receiving State had never systematically created. The majority in 2024 treated the Accord as a political solution deserving legislative deference; the dissent treated the passage of time as having eroded the settlement's protection of the indigenous population. Both were, in their different registers, judgments about that history.

## Security of status: how the Act grades what it grants

A final structural reading collects a thread that has run through every part of this pillar. The Act creates five routes to a single status, but it does not protect the product of the routes equally, and the differences are deliberate. Citizenship by birth is protected without qualification against deprivation, and can end only by the citizen's own renunciation or by the automatic operation of Section 9 on voluntary acquisition of another citizenship. Citizenship by descent stands in the same protected position once acquired, its vulnerability lying at the acquisition stage, in the consular registration condition, rather than at any later recall. Citizenship by incorporation follows the order that created it and shares the protection of the statuses arising by law. Citizenship by registration and by naturalisation, the two granted statuses, is exposed for the whole of its duration to Section 10, on grounds that include the fraud by which the grant was obtained, the disloyalty that contradicts the oath on which it rested, and the seven years of unconnected residence abroad that abandon its factual basis.

The grading is defensible on a principle the cases recognise: a grant procured by application invites conditions subsequent in a way that a status imposed by law at birth does not, and the oath of allegiance that registration and naturalisation require supplies the standard against which disloyalty is measured. It is contestable at its edges, because the citizen by registration may have lived a lifetime in India on the strength of the grant, and because the second generation of a registered citizen, citizens by birth or descent in their own right, hold a security their parent never had, so that security of citizenship in India can change between generations of one family without any change in the family's connection to the country. Advisers state the position without softening it: a client who naturalises should understand that the certificate, unlike a birthright, carries its grounds of recall within it, and a client whose parent registered should understand that the client's own position may be stronger than the parent's. The Act's scheme of loss is therefore not an appendix to its scheme of acquisition. It is the second half of a single design, in which the manner of acquiring citizenship determines, permanently, how it may be taken away.

## The discretionary routes in practice: inquiry, oath and the certificate

Registration and naturalisation share a procedural skeleton that the Citizenship Rules supply and that applicants experience as a single long inquiry. An application is made in the prescribed form to the authority the rules designate for the applicant's district, supported by the documents the form requires, and it then travels through a verification process in which the district administration and the police report on residence, character and antecedents, the State Government forwards its view, and the decision is taken at the level the Act and rules assign, the district and State machinery informing grants that the prescribed authority and, for naturalisation, the Central Government make. Affidavits in the prescribed forms accompany the application, including, for naturalisation, the material bearing on renunciation of the previous citizenship and, in the cases the rules specify, a declaration published or supported in the manner prescribed so that objections may surface. The oath of allegiance in the Second Schedule is not a formality appended to the grant: the certificate and the oath together constitute the act by which the applicant enters the status, and the disloyalty ground in Section 10 is measured against the allegiance the oath declares.

Three features of the practice deserve record in a pillar. The first is time. A naturalisation file that must verify twelve years of patterned residence, examine character and test language is measured in years from application to certificate, and applicants who count only the qualifying residence and not the processing period misjudge their own timelines. The second is the treatment of the previous citizenship. India admits no dual citizenship, and the Third Schedule's renunciation condition means the applicant must arrange the exit from the earlier citizenship in the sequence the two legal systems require; for citizens of countries that do not permit renunciation in advance of acquiring another citizenship, the Schedule's undertaking mechanism exists precisely to bridge the interval, and mishandling the sequence can leave an applicant stateless in the gap, a risk advisers are employed to eliminate. The third is refusal and its aftermath. A refusal of registration or naturalisation is an administrative decision on the material before the authority; the Act provides revision under Section 15 against orders within its terms, and nothing prevents a fresh application when the defect, a shortfall of residence, a gap in proof, an unmet language requirement, has been cured. The routes are therefore forgiving of failure in a way the status-by-operation routes are not: a failed naturalisation can be attempted again, while a descent claim lost to an unregistered consular birth cannot be re-run, because the year has passed and the birth cannot recur.

Language, character and intention, the Third Schedule's qualitative conditions, operate differently from its arithmetic ones, and the difference is worth stating for the aspirant reader. Adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule is examined on the applicant's ability to speak and, where the rules require, to read or write the language at the level the examining authority accepts; it is a test of integration into one of India's constitutional languages, not of literary attainment, and the choice among the Eighth Schedule languages is the applicant's. Good character is assessed on the reports and the record, and a criminal history is weighed by its gravity, age and bearing on the allegiance the oath asserts rather than by any rule of automatic exclusion the Act lays down, the Schedule containing no catalogue of disqualifying offences. Intention, the third condition, is prospective: the applicant must intend to reside in India or to take up the government or organisational service the Schedule names, and the intention is tested against the applicant's arrangements and history rather than against a bare declaration. Each of the three is a judgment exercised on a file, which is why two applicants with identical residence arithmetic can receive different answers, and why the pillar insists that naturalisation is a grant to be satisfied for, not a status that ripens by effluxion of time.

## The amendment trail at headline level: 1986, 2003 and 2019

This pillar has deliberately stated the enacted Act and flagged its amendments at the points where they changed a provision's meaning. For the reader's orientation, the trail itself, which the companion article on the amendments owns in full and which is therefore not re-argued here, reduces to three movements. The first, in 1986, attached the parental condition to citizenship by birth and recast the position of minors whose parents renounce, in a Parliament responding to migration into Assam and the north east in the years after the Accord. The second, in 2003, was the largest reconstruction since 1955: it inserted the definition of illegal migrant, imposed the third birth regime and the consular registration condition on descent, substituted the Overseas Citizen of India scheme for the earlier and narrower dual arrangements debated in the preceding years, inserted Section 14A on compulsory registration and identity cards, and tightened the registration and naturalisation gates against illegal migrants. The third, in 2019, operated on the definition of illegal migrant by excepting specified classes of entrants from specified countries who had entered on or before 31 December 2014, reduced for that class the residence period for naturalisation, and widened the Overseas Citizen of India cancellation power by the ground examined in the treatment of Section 7D above. The controversy the third movement generated, its constitutional challenges and the rules litigation that followed, are the subject of the debate article linked in the introduction, and this pillar takes no part in them beyond recording, as the brief directs, the statutory facts: what each amendment changed, where in the Act the change sits, and which provisions of the enacted scheme it qualified. The discipline of separating the trail from the pillar is what allows a reader to see the Act whole: a 1955 foundation of five routes and three modes of loss, narrowed at the birth gate in two stages, gated against illegal migration in 2003, and given a diaspora scheme and an identity provision that its founders did not contemplate.

## Citizenship, the electoral roll and the passport: the status at work

A pillar on this Act would be incomplete without an account of where the status is cashed in daily life, because most Indians encounter citizenship law not in a tribunal but at three counters: the electoral roll, the passport office and the government service entry point. The franchise under Article 326 belongs to citizens, and the Representation of the People Act 1950 makes citizenship a qualification for registration as an elector, so the roll is at once a consequence of citizenship and, in contested regions, a theatre in which citizenship is disputed, as the Assam history showed when the roll itself became the movement's grievance. Inclusion on the roll is evidence a person may deploy in a status inquiry, and the courts and tribunals treat it as such, weighty but never conclusive, because the roll is compiled for electoral purposes on declarations and summary inquiry, and a name wrongly carried on it cannot manufacture a citizenship the Act does not confer. The passport stands differently: it is issued under the Passports Act 1967 to citizens, its issue involves a verification of citizenship where doubt exists, and its refusal or impounding on a disputed Section 9 question must await the Central Government's determination of that question, since that determination is committed to the Centre alone. Public employment under Article 16 is open, in its constitutional promise of equality of opportunity, to citizens, and the recruitment rules of the Union and the States test citizenship at entry, which is one reason a young person's first serious encounter with the proof of citizenship is so often a job application rather than any proceeding about status.

These counters explain a feature of the Act's operation that surprises readers who approach it as a text: the Act contains almost no provisions about documents, yet it governs a documentary world. The documents live elsewhere, in the Registration of Births and Deaths Act 1969 for the birth record, in the passport and foreigners legislation for travel and entry records, in the electoral law for the roll, and in the rules under this Act for the certificates of registration and naturalisation and the Section 13 certificate of doubt. The Act supplies the questions; the surrounding statutes supply the paper on which the answers are written. A failure anywhere in that surrounding paper, an unregistered birth, a parent's unrecorded entry, a name spelled three ways across three registers, migrates into the citizenship file as a failure of proof, although the Act itself has been satisfied in substance. The series has found this pattern in statute after statute: the operative law for the citizen is often the evidence law of a neighbouring regime. In citizenship it is the whole story of the last forty years, from the second birth regime onward, and it is why this pillar, a reading of one Act, has had to speak so often about registers, rolls and archives that the Act never mentions.

## Children in the Act: the minor across the provisions

The minor appears in the Act at more points than any other figure except the applicant, and the provisions do not form a code within the code so much as a set of answers to the recurring question of how far a child's status follows a parent's acts. At acquisition, the child is the passive beneficiary of the two automatic routes: birth decides by the parents' status under the later regimes, and descent transmits a parent's citizenship across a border, subject to the consular act that adults must perform on the child's behalf. The registration route then gives the child an active place of the child's own: minor children whose parents are citizens of India form a named category in Section 5, a provision of daily importance for the child born abroad whose descent claim failed on the consular condition, or whose parents acquired citizenship after the child's birth, because it lets the parents regularise the child's position during minority on the strength of their own status rather than waiting for the child to qualify as an adult under a residence category.

At loss, the Act protects the child with one hand and exposes the child with the other. When a parent renounces under Section 8, every minor child of that parent also ceases to be a citizen, a derivative loss that follows the parent's voluntary act and that many renouncing parents discover late, because the declaration that settles their own position unsettles their children's. The protection is the resumption right: the child may, within one year after attaining full age, declare the resumption of Indian citizenship, a window the Act opens because the loss was not the child's choice. No equivalent derivative loss follows a parent's deprivation in terms that sweep the children in, and a parent's termination under Section 9 operates on the parent's own acquisition abroad, the children's positions being worked out under the provisions that govern each of them. The cardholder scheme repeats the pattern in miniature in Section 7C, where renunciation of a card carries stated consequences for the spouse and minor children of the declarant.

The through line is the Act's insistence that the child's status is the child's own, even when it is decided by reference to a parent. The third birth regime asks about the parents in order to fix the child's status at birth, and having fixed it, leaves the child a citizen in full, not a citizen by sufferance whose position rises and falls with the parents' later history. A parent who later renounces, is deprived, or acquires a foreign citizenship does not rewrite the status the child acquired at birth under Section 3, except through the specific derivative provisions the Act names, and those provisions are construed as exceptions to be kept within their words. Families dividing their lives between countries underestimate this architecture in both directions: they assume a child's Indian citizenship is safer than the Section 8 derivative loss makes it when a parent renounces, and more fragile than it is when a parent's later troubles are feared to descend. The pillar's advice in the text is the statute's own: read the child's position off the child's route, and then check the named derivative provisions, and no others.

## Six files an adviser meets: the routes at work

The operation of the Act is best fixed in the mind by the recurring files of practice, stated here as patterns rather than as reports of decided cases. The first file is the returning emigrant's child. Born in India in 1990 to parents one of whom was a citizen, the child is a citizen by birth under the second regime, and the file closes on the parent's citizenship at the birth; the same child born in 2006 opens a harder file, because the other parent's status in 2006 must also be established, and where that parent entered India from a neighbouring country in the 1990s the file becomes an inquiry into an entry the family may never have documented. The second file is the consulate omission. A citizen working abroad has a child, registers the birth with the local authority of the country of residence, and learns at the child's eighteenth year, on a passport application, that the Indian consular registration required for descent after 2004 was never made. The descent claim has failed by time, and the file reroutes through Section 5's category for minor children if the child is still a minor, or through the cardholder scheme and its bridge if the child is not, each with its own years to run.

The third file is the spouse at year six. A foreign citizen married to an Indian citizen has lived in India for six years and asks whether the seventh year can be shortened by the years of marriage spent abroad before the move. It cannot: the category requires ordinary residence in India for seven years before the application, and the file's work is the proof of the residence, the tenancy and employment and school records assembled year by year, not argument about the marriage's duration. The fourth file is the naturalising parent abroad. A citizen who took another country's citizenship a decade ago returns, produces an old Indian passport and asserts a continuing citizenship; Section 9 answers that the citizenship ended on the foreign acquisition by operation of law, and the question, if disputed, belongs to the Central Government under Section 9(2) and to no other forum, after which the person's route back, if any, lies through the cardholder scheme or a fresh discretionary application for which the earlier citizenship may itself supply a category. The fifth file is the Assam detection. A person whose family entered Assam in 1968 is detected as a foreigner in a tribunal reference; Section 6A's middle class governs, and the file turns on proof of the entry period and of ordinary residence since, on registration after detection, and on the decade of exclusion from the electoral roll that precedes the completed status. The sixth file is the cardholder's letter. An Overseas Citizen of India cardholder of many years receives a notice proposing cancellation; the file turns on which ground in Section 7D is invoked, on the material said to support it, and on the hearing the section guarantees, and its stakes, though short of citizenship itself, are the whole of the holder's settled connection with India.

Patterns of this kind explain why the Act, for all its brevity, supports a specialised practice. Each file begins with the same movement, identifying the route and the date regime before any document is gathered, because the route selects the facts that matter and discards the rest. Each is lost, when it is lost, on a fact about the past that the file cannot prove, and won, when it is won, on an archive the family kept without knowing its legal significance. The adviser in this field is therefore part historian, and the statute is the index to the archive it requires.

## When every route fails: statelessness and the edge of the Act

A complete reading of the Act must face the person for whom no route opens, because the statute's silences define that person's position as surely as its provisions define the citizen's. The Act contains no general protection against statelessness. A child born in India who fails the third regime, because one parent was an illegal migrant and the other not a citizen, acquires no Indian citizenship at birth; whether the child acquires any other citizenship depends on the law of the parents' country, and the Indian statute does not ask the question. A foundling provision of the kind some citizenship laws carry, deeming a child found in the territory to have been born there to citizens until the contrary is shown, is absent from Section 3. The descent route fails the unregistered consular birth without remainder. The discretionary routes exclude the illegal migrant in terms. The result is that the Act, read strictly, contemplates the possibility of a person born and living in India who is a citizen of nowhere, and assigns that person no statutory remedy beyond the foreigners legislation's machinery for those who are not citizens.

The constitutional and international setting softens the statement without removing it. Article 21 protects the life and personal liberty of every person, citizen or not, and the courts have read it to protect a person facing expulsion against a procedure that would render the person stateless without inquiry into the consequences, in the line of cases concerning Chakma and other long resident communities, where the Supreme Court directed that applications for citizenship by registration from such residents be received and considered rather than defeated by the State's own refusal to process them. India is not a party to the statelessness conventions, and the Act was not drafted against them; the protection that exists is constitutional and remedial, operating case by case, and it does not convert into a route. For the pillar's completeness the point is recorded because it disciplines the verdict given earlier on the birth regimes: the documentary burden is the ordinary grievance under the third regime, but at the regime's edge sits a harder outcome, the child with no citizenship at all, and an honest account of the statute must hold both in view.

The Act's treatment of the person who loses citizenship late in life belongs in the same register of candour. Termination under Section 9 can leave an elderly emigrant who naturalised abroad in ignorance of the Indian consequence without the citizenship of birth, and the remedies are those the Act gives: the cardholder scheme for the connection, the registration categories where a parent or an earlier citizenship supplies one, and no more. Deprivation, by contrast, cannot produce statelessness in the fraud class of case without the committee procedure and the public good condition being satisfied, and the courts' insistence on strict proof in deprivation is in part an insistence that the State not manufacture statelessness by order. The statute's design is therefore uneven by deliberation: generous in its founding routes, strict at its later gates, and dependent on constitutional review, rather than on any internal provision, to catch the person who falls between them.

## The administrative constitution of the Act: who decides, and with what finality

Behind the routes stands a distribution of decision making that a pillar should state expressly, because applicants routinely address the wrong authority and misjudge the finality of what they receive. The Central Government is the Act's principal authority. It grants naturalisation under Section 6, specifies citizens on incorporation under Section 7, determines Section 9 questions under Section 9(2), orders deprivation under Section 10, registers and cancels cardholders under Sections 7A and 7D, issues the certificate in case of doubt under Section 13, revises orders under Section 15 and makes the rules under Section 18. Registration under Section 5 is administered through the prescribed authority the rules designate, operating through the district and State machinery of the applicant's residence. The Registrar General of India is the National Registration Authority for Section 14A. Consulates perform the Act's acts abroad: registration of births for descent, registration of declarations of renunciation made abroad, and the annual registrations by which a citizen by registration or naturalisation resident abroad maintains the connection that answers the seven year deprivation ground.

Finality varies by provision, and the differences are practical law. The Section 9(2) determination is conclusive within its field, so that other authorities, passport officers and tribunals included, must accept the Central Government's answer to whether a foreign citizenship was voluntarily acquired, and the remedy against a wrong determination is constitutional review of that determination, not a collateral finding elsewhere. A deprivation order follows the committee procedure and is likewise subject to judicial review on the grounds public law supplies: the sufficiency of the statutory ground, the observance of the procedure the section prescribes, and the public good condition. Registration and naturalisation refusals are administrative decisions examinable in revision under Section 15 within its terms and by the constitutional courts on ordinary principles. The certificate under Section 13 is evidence of the status it certifies, strong in the person's favour in later proceedings, and its existence explains why advisers in doubtful cases move early for a determination rather than accumulating transactions on an untested status.

Delegation and rule making complete the picture. Section 16 permits the Central Government to delegate its functions under the Act, other than the excepted powers, to officers and authorities subordinate to it, which is how a statute that names the Central Government at every turn is in fact administered through State Governments, district collectors and consular officers. Section 18's rule making power has produced the Citizenship Rules, and it is in the rules, not the Act, that the applicant meets the forms, the fees, the registers of declarations and certificates, and the inquiries by which residence and character are verified. The two instruments must be read together in any real case: the Act states the right and the condition, the rules state the door and the queue. A reform of practice under this statute is therefore usually a reform of the rules and the machinery, and the long intervals between an amendment's enactment and its working effect, which the Assam implementation record displays at the largest scale, are intervals consumed by the machinery the rules create. The pillar closes its exposition there, because that is where the statute ends: in offices, registers and files, deciding the questions its sections frame, one family archive at a time.

## Marriage and citizenship: what the bond changes and what it does not

No misconception in this field is more persistent than the belief that marriage to an Indian citizen makes a person an Indian citizen. The Act contains no such rule, and the history of its silence is instructive. Under the law India inherited, a woman's citizenship had historically followed her husband's in systems of coverture that the twentieth century discarded, and the Constitution and the Act of 1955 made a deliberate break: citizenship under the Act is personal, acquired and lost by the individual's own route and acts, and marriage neither confers it on a foreign spouse nor strips it from a citizen spouse who marries a foreigner. What marriage does is open a category. The foreign spouse enters Section 5's list and may apply for registration on completing seven years of ordinary residence in India, taking the oath and satisfying the authority like every other registrant. A citizen who marries abroad and settles abroad keeps Indian citizenship until the citizen's own act, renunciation or voluntary acquisition of the foreign citizenship, brings Sections 8 or 9 into operation, and the foreign spouse's nationality is legally irrelevant to that position.

The consequences at the edges reward statement. The breakdown of a marriage does not undo a registration already granted: a spouse registered as a citizen holds the status personally, and divorce is not among Section 10's grounds, although a registration procured by a marriage that was a fraud on the Act, a marriage of convenience entered to obtain the status by false representation, is exposed under the fraud ground like any other procured grant. The children of the marriage take their positions from the birth and descent rules applied to their own births, not from the fact of the marriage, so the child born in India to the citizen parent and the foreign spouse is a citizen by birth under the regime of the child's date, comfortably in the third regime because one parent is a citizen and the other, if lawfully resident, is not an illegal migrant. The foreign spouse's own parents and family acquire nothing by the marriage. And the spouse who never registers, living decades in India on the strength of the marriage and a residence permit under the foreigners legislation, remains a foreigner in law throughout, a position whose consequences appear on the spouse's death or the citizen's death, when property, guardianship and residence questions are suddenly governed by the status that was never regularised. The pillar records the point because the files reach advisers at exactly that moment of bereavement, when the cure that seven years and an application would have supplied is no longer available in the same form.

One further distinction governs the use of every document in this field, and it is the distinction between the proof of a fact and the proof of a status. A birth certificate proves that a child was born at a place on a date to named parents; it does not state, and no registrar certifies, the citizenship consequence that Section 3 attaches to those facts, which must be worked out by applying the regime of the date to the parents' positions. A parent's passport proves that the State issued a travel document to a person treated as a citizen at its date; the citizenship it evidences is the parent's, and its bearing on the child's claim is one step removed, through the parent's status at the earlier moment of the child's birth. Files fail when documents are offered as though they proved the status directly, and succeed when each document is assigned to the element it actually proves, place, date, identity, parentage, a parent's status, an entry's legality, and the elements are assembled in the order the governing section demands. That discipline of assembly, obvious once stated, is the practical craft the Act imposes on everyone who works under it, and it is the craft on which the outcomes described throughout this pillar have turned.

## The paper the Act issues: certificates and their evidentiary value

The Act speaks of citizenship as a status, and then, characteristically, surrounds the status with paper whose legal weight varies by instrument, and an adviser must know which paper proves what. The certificate of registration under Section 5 and the certificate of naturalisation under Section 6 are constitutive: they are the grant itself in documentary form, and the status runs from them. The certificate under Section 13, issued where citizenship is in doubt, is declaratory and evidentiary: the Central Government certifies that the person is a citizen, the status having existed independently, and the certificate is received in later proceedings as strong proof of what it records. The passport, the electoral roll entry and the cardholder registration are none of these things: each is issued on an assessment of citizenship for another statute's purposes, each is evidence an inquiry may weigh, and none is conclusive of the status, which is why a passport holder can still face a Section 9 reference and a voter can still face a tribunal. Registers complete the system: the rules maintain registers of declarations of renunciation, of certificates granted and of consular registrations of births, and it is against those registers, decades later, that the derivative questions this pillar has traced, the resumption of a child's citizenship, the date of a parent's renunciation, the fact of a consular registration, are answered. The statute's paper is therefore not bureaucracy around the law. It is the law's memory, and the cases the Act decides hardest are the cases where the memory has a gap.

## Reading the Act as a whole: what the pillar has established

The reading can be gathered. The Citizenship Act 1955, Act 57 of 1955, is a short statute of five routes, three modes of loss, one State-specific provision and one diaspora scheme, built on a constitutional commission that fixed the founding population and remitted everything after to Parliament. Its birth route began as a rule of soil and became, in two amendments, a rule of parentage tested at a date. Its descent route began through the father, widened to either parent, and acquired a consular time limit that fails claims in silence. Its discretionary routes demand categories, years and an oath, and leave their holders exposed to a deprivation power from which the automatic routes are immune. Its Assam provision converted an accord into citizenship classes and survived a Constitution Bench by four to one, with the dissent's time-based objection left standing in the reports for a future bench. Its cardholder scheme gives the diaspora durability without membership, and its cancellation power, widened in 2019, has drawn the comment the wide ground invites. Over the whole stands the movement the series thesis names: the decisive facts have migrated from the applicant to the applicant's parents, from the present to a past moment, and from the register the State keeps to the archive the family kept, and the law of Indian citizenship is, in operation, the law of proving those migrated facts.

For the three readers the brief named, the payloads are these. The applicant and adviser take the routes and their requirements, the table that compresses them, and the warning that the route must be identified before the documents are gathered, because each route makes different facts decisive. The person establishing status by birth takes the three regimes and the primacy of the date of birth, the single fact from which the whole inquiry unfolds. The aspirant and researcher take the statute's architecture and the 2024 judgment, the majority's deference to a dated political settlement and the dissent's proposition that a classification can decay into unconstitutionality with time, which together frame the questions the field will argue for a generation. None of these payloads depends on the amendment controversy of 2019, which has its own article; all of them depend on the enacted text of 1955 and on the changes, flagged where they bite, that the decades made to it.

## Conclusion: a statute of dates, parents and proof

The Citizenship Act 1955 repays the reading this pillar has given it because its apparent simplicity dissolves, on inspection, into three disciplines that govern every case under it. The first is the discipline of dates. Citizenship by birth has three regimes and the date of birth selects among them; descent has its own turning points in 1992 and 2004; the Assam provision is built from two dates and a decade; naturalisation is an arithmetic of years and months; deprivation and cancellation carry their five year and seven year periods. A practitioner who has the dates wrong has the case wrong, whatever else is right. The second discipline is that of the parent. At point after point the Act decides the applicant's status by the status of another person, a parent at a birth, a spouse across a marriage, a cardholder parent behind a child, and the law of citizenship in India is, to a degree its popular accounts miss, a law of the previous generation. The third discipline is proof. Statuses that arise by law must still be evidenced to be enjoyed; statuses granted on application must be proved into existence; and statuses under attack are defended with documents. The Act's text is short. The files it generates are long, and they are long because the text asks, of ordinary families, questions about dates, parents and papers that only a well kept record can answer. That, in the end, is what Act 57 of 1955 is about.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How can a person acquire Indian citizenship?

Indian citizenship is acquired through the five routes stated in Sections 3 to 7 of the Citizenship Act 1955. A person may be a citizen by birth in India under Section 3, by descent from a citizen parent for a birth outside India under Section 4, by registration under Section 5 within one of the categories that section names, by naturalisation under Section 6 on satisfying the qualifications in the Third Schedule, or by incorporation of territory under Section 7 where territory becomes part of India and a notified order specifies the persons covered. Birth and descent operate by force of law where their conditions are met. Registration and naturalisation are grants made on application by the authority the Act and the Citizenship Rules designate.

### Q: How did citizenship by birth change over time?

Citizenship by birth under Section 3 has passed through three regimes fixed by date of birth. A person born in India on or after 26 January 1950 and before 1 July 1987 is a citizen by birth whatever the nationality of the parents. A person born on or after 1 July 1987 and before 3 December 2004 is a citizen by birth only if either parent was a citizen of India at the time of the birth. A person born on or after 3 December 2004 is a citizen by birth only if both parents were citizens at the time of the birth, or one parent was a citizen and the other was not an illegal migrant at that time. The amendment of 1986 made the first change and the amendment of 2003 made the second.

### Q: What are the conditions for naturalisation?

Naturalisation under Section 6 requires the applicant to satisfy the qualifications in the Third Schedule to the Act. The applicant must not be an illegal migrant, must have renounced or undertake to renounce any other citizenship in accordance with the Schedule, and must have resided in India or been in the service of a Government in India throughout the twelve months immediately preceding the application. During the fourteen years preceding those twelve months the applicant must have resided in India or been in such service for periods amounting in the aggregate to not less than eleven years. The applicant must be of good character, have an adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution, and intend to reside in India or to enter the service the Schedule names. An oath of allegiance completes the grant.

### Q: Who is an illegal migrant under the Act?

An illegal migrant, under Section 2(1)(b) of the Act, is a foreigner who has entered India without a valid passport or other travel documents, or who has entered India with a valid passport or other travel documents but remains in India beyond the period permitted by those documents or by any rule or order made under the foreigners legislation. The definition was inserted by the amendment of 2003. Its consequences run through the Act: an illegal migrant cannot be registered as a citizen under Section 5, cannot be granted a certificate of naturalisation under Section 6, and, since the third birth regime began on 3 December 2004, the child of a citizen and an illegal migrant born in India is not a citizen by birth under Section 3.

### Q: How can Indian citizenship be lost?

Indian citizenship can be lost in the three ways stated in Sections 8, 9 and 10 of the Act. Under Section 8, a citizen of full age and capacity may renounce citizenship by a declaration made in the prescribed manner, and ceases to be a citizen when the declaration is registered. Under Section 9, a citizen who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country, by naturalisation, registration or otherwise, ceases automatically to be a citizen of India on that acquisition, because India does not permit dual citizenship. Under Section 10, the Central Government may deprive a person of citizenship by order, but only where the person is a citizen by registration or by naturalisation, and only on the grounds that section states, which include fraud in obtaining the grant, disloyalty towards the Constitution and a sentence of imprisonment of not less than two years within five years of the grant.

### Q: How can an OCI card be cancelled?

The registration of an Overseas Citizen of India cardholder may be cancelled by the Central Government under Section 7D of the Act. The grounds are that the registration was obtained by fraud, false representation or concealment of a material fact; that the cardholder has shown disaffection towards the Constitution of India; that the cardholder has unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy during a war in which India is engaged; that the cardholder has been sentenced to imprisonment for a term of not less than two years within five years after the registration was issued; that cancellation is necessary in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations with a foreign country or the interests of the general public; and that the cardholder has violated a provision of the Act or of another law specified by the Central Government. The cardholder must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard before cancellation.

### Q: What does Section 6A provide for Assam?

Section 6A, inserted by the amendment of 1985 following the Assam Accord, makes special provision for persons of Indian origin who came to Assam from the territory that became Bangladesh. A person who came before 1 January 1966 and has been ordinarily resident in Assam since entry is deemed to be a citizen of India as from 1 January 1966. A person who came on or after 1 January 1966 and before 25 March 1971, and has been ordinarily resident in Assam since, must be detected as a foreigner and must register, and becomes a citizen when ten years have expired from the date of detection, during which period the person's name is not included in any electoral roll. A person who came on or after 25 March 1971 receives nothing under the section and is to be detected and dealt with as a foreigner under the foreigners legislation.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about Section 6A?

A Constitution Bench of five judges decided the validity of Section 6A on 17 October 2024 and upheld the provision by a majority of four to one. The majority held that Parliament had legislative competence to enact the provision under its power over citizenship, that the guarantee of equality in Article 14 permits reasonable classification, and that the historical circumstances of Assam together with the Assam Accord, a political solution of which the section was the legislative solution, supplied a legitimate basis for the classification. The majority also held that the cut-off dates of 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971 were not arbitrary. Justice J. B. Pardiwala dissented and would have declared the provision prospectively invalid, reasoning in part that it afforded insufficient protection to the indigenous population of Assam and had continued without a temporal limit.

### Q: What is the difference between citizenship by registration and citizenship by naturalisation?

Registration under Section 5 is open only to the categories the section names: persons of Indian origin with the stated residence, spouses of citizens with seven years of ordinary residence in India, minor children of citizens, children of registered citizens, persons whose parents were citizens of independent India, and Overseas Citizen of India cardholders of five years with twelve months of residence. Naturalisation under Section 6 is open to a person who belongs to no such category, on the strength of the Third Schedule qualifications alone. The deciding authorities and the residence computations differ: registration turns on category and its stated period, while naturalisation turns on twelve years of residence in the pattern the Schedule prescribes, together with character, language and intention. Both are grants on application, and both leave the holder within the reach of deprivation under Section 10.

### Q: How long must a foreign spouse live in India before applying for registration as a citizen?

A person married to a citizen of India may apply for registration under Section 5 after being ordinarily resident in India for seven years before making the application. Marriage alone confers no citizenship and no shorter period: it places the spouse within a category eligible to apply, and the seven years of ordinary residence in India complete the eligibility. Residence outside India during the marriage does not count towards the period, however long the marriage has lasted. The spouse must also take the oath of allegiance, and the grant remains a decision of the prescribed authority on the application and the reports it calls for. A spouse who is an illegal migrant within Section 2(1)(b) is outside the category altogether.

### Q: What happens to a minor child when a parent renounces Indian citizenship?

Where a citizen renounces Indian citizenship by a registered declaration under Section 8, every minor child of that person also ceases to be a citizen of India. The loss is derivative: it follows the parent's act and does not depend on any act or wish of the child. The Act gives the child a safeguard in return. A person who ceased to be a citizen in this way as a minor may, within one year after attaining full age, make a declaration of the intention to resume Indian citizenship, and resumes the status in accordance with the section. The renouncing parent's spouse is not affected by the declaration and loses citizenship only by the spouse's own declaration or by another provision of the Act.

### Q: Who decides whether a person has voluntarily acquired a foreign citizenship under Section 9?

Section 9(2) of the Act commits the question to the Central Government. Where a question arises whether, when or how any person has acquired the citizenship of another country, that question is determined by the Central Government, and the determination is conclusive for the purposes of Section 9. No other authority, including a passport authority or a tribunal dealing with another question, decides the matter collaterally. The inquiry proceeds on the material of the foreign acquisition, such as the certificate of naturalisation and the oath taken abroad, and the person concerned is heard before the determination is made. Termination itself, where the acquisition is established, operates automatically under Section 9(1) from the date of the foreign acquisition.

### Q: Can a citizen by birth be deprived of citizenship under Section 10?

No. The deprivation power in Section 10 is available only against a person who is a citizen of India by registration or by naturalisation. It does not reach a citizen by birth under Section 3, a citizen by descent under Section 4, or a citizen by incorporation of territory under Section 7. A citizen by birth who commits the gravest offence faces the criminal law, and a citizen by birth who voluntarily acquires a foreign citizenship loses Indian citizenship by the automatic operation of Section 9, but no order of the Central Government can deprive such a citizen of the status. This grading of security, under which the granted statuses carry their grounds of recall and the statuses arising by law do not, is a deliberate feature of the Act's scheme of loss.

### Q: Which entitlements does Section 7B confer on an OCI cardholder?

Under Section 7B, a cardholder is entitled to the rights the Central Government specifies by notification, and the notifications have conferred a multiple entry, multipurpose lifelong visa facility, exemption from the registration and reporting requirements that bind other foreigners, and parity with non-resident Indians in economic, financial and educational fields. Parity is expressly withheld in respect of the acquisition of agricultural or plantation properties. The card confers no citizenship and none of its incidents: no vote, no eligibility for public employment, and no right to hold constitutional offices such as member of Parliament or of a State legislature, or judge of the Supreme Court or a High Court. The cardholder remains a citizen of another country, holding a durable bundle of facilities under Sections 7A to 7D of the Act.

### Q: What does Section 14A provide about registration of citizens and identity cards?

Section 14A, inserted by the amendment of 2003, provides that the Central Government may compulsorily register every citizen of India and issue a national identity card to that citizen. It designates the Registrar General of India as the National Registration Authority for the purposes of the provision. The section is enabling in character: it empowers the creation of a register of citizens and the issue of cards under it. It does not itself confer citizenship on any person, and the card it contemplates is an instrument recording a status that arises under the acquisition provisions of the Act, Sections 3 to 7. Its significance is structural, marking the point at which the statute moved from declaring statuses on application and by operation of law towards enumerating the citizenry as a whole.

### Q: Within what period must a birth abroad be registered at an Indian consulate for citizenship by descent?

For a person born outside India on or after 3 December 2004, citizenship by descent under Section 4 requires that the birth be registered at an Indian consulate within one year of the date of birth. The Central Government may allow registration after that period where it is satisfied that the registration could not be made within the year. The parents must also declare that the child does not hold a passport of another country. If the birth is not registered within the period and no extension is allowed, the child does not acquire citizenship by descent, and the omission cannot be cured by any later registration. The family must then look to another route, such as registration under Section 5 while the child is a minor child of citizens.

### Q: For what service may the conditions for naturalisation be waived?

The Third Schedule to the Act empowers the Central Government to waive all or any of the qualifications for naturalisation where, in its opinion, the applicant has rendered distinguished service to the cause of science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace or human progress generally. The waiver operates on the Schedule's conditions, which include the residence pattern, and it is framed around fields of human achievement rather than wealth, investment or influence. It does not waive the threshold requirement of Section 6 itself that the applicant not be an illegal migrant, because that bar stands in the section and not in the Schedule. The power is exercised sparingly, and an applicant who relies on eminence must still be brought within Section 6 by the Central Government's satisfaction on the material placed before it.

### Q: What happens under Section 6A to persons who entered Assam on or after 25 March 1971?

Section 6A confers nothing on a person who came to Assam from the specified territory on or after 25 March 1971. Such a person is not deemed a citizen, is not entitled to register under the section, and acquires no status by residence, however long, under its terms. The person is to be detected as a foreigner and dealt with under the foreigners legislation, including the Foreigners Act 1946 and the tribunal machinery that operates with it, which may lead to expulsion. The majority of the Constitution Bench that upheld Section 6A on 17 October 2024 recorded this position expressly: the provision's benefit is confined to the classes defined by the earlier dates, and its silence for the later class is part of the settlement the Accord made and Parliament enacted.

### Q: How many years of residence in total does the Third Schedule require for naturalisation?

The Third Schedule requires twelve years of presence in a defined pattern. The applicant must have resided in India, or been in the service of a Government in India, throughout the period of twelve months immediately preceding the date of the application, and that block must be complete. In addition, during the fourteen years immediately preceding that twelve-month period, the applicant must have resided in India or been in such service for periods amounting in the aggregate to not less than eleven years. The eleven years need not be continuous, so absences within the fourteen years do not defeat the claim if the aggregate is met, but no shortfall in the final twelve months can be made good from the earlier period.

### Q: What did the dissenting judge hold in the Section 6A case?

Justice J. B. Pardiwala, dissenting in the Constitution Bench decision of 17 October 2024, would have declared Section 6A prospectively invalid, that is inoperative from the date of the judgment, leaving statuses settled under it undisturbed. He reasoned that the provision departs from a nationally uniform framework of citizenship, that the absence of any temporal limit on its operation renders it susceptible to arbitrary implementation, and that it fails to afford stronger protection to the indigenous population of Assam. He also held that a provision which satisfies the test of reasonable classification when it is enacted may become unconstitutional with the passage of time, as the circumstances that justified the classification fall away. The dissent was therefore advanced from the direction of the State's indigenous population, not from the direction of the migrants the provision benefited.
