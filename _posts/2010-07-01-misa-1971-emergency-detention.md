Few statutes in the Republic's record carry a heavier charge in political argument than the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, and few are described with less precision. To one side of the argument, MISA 1971 is the legal face of the Emergency, the instrument by which a government imprisoned its opponents without charge, without disclosure and without any court able to intervene. To the other side, it was a detention law of a familiar Indian type, enacted four years before the Emergency for reasons unconnected with it, equipped with the safeguards the Constitution itself prescribes for preventive detention, and abused, if it was abused, by the men and women who operated it rather than by anything in its text. Both accounts contain something the record supports, and both, stated without qualification, misdescribe the statute. The disagreement worth having is narrower and harder than either slogan. It is about the distance between the Act Parliament passed in 1971 and the Act that was actually in force between June 1975 and March 1977, and about which legal steps, each identifiable and each reversible, travelled that distance.

That distance is the subject here. The method follows the order this framework requires. The statute is set out first as a text, stripped of characterisation, so that a reader can see what Parliament enacted in 1971 and what was later inserted into it. The case for the law is then stated at its strongest, in the terms its defenders used and still use, before the case against it is stated at its strongest in the terms of the official inquiry that examined its working. The factual disputes, which are real and which explain why reference works contradict one another on basic points such as the year of repeal, are separated from the value disputes, which no accumulation of evidence can settle. A verdict is offered at the end on what is settled, and it rests on a single organising claim that the whole record supports. A detention statute becomes an instrument of mass detention when four things happen in sequence: the grounds of detention are withheld from the detenu, the disclosure of those grounds is barred by law, the statute is placed beyond challenge for violating fundamental rights, and the habeas corpus remedy is suspended in practice. Call it the four-step closure. Each step on its own leaves a safeguard standing somewhere else in the system. All four together leave none. MISA is the only Indian detention statute in which all four steps were completed, and that, rather than anything in the character of the 1971 text, is what separates it from the preventive detention laws that came before it and after it.

## What the Maintenance of Internal Security Act Actually Said

Begin with the text, because public argument about MISA habitually begins somewhere else. The Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971 is Act 26 of 1971. It received the assent of President V. V. Giri on 2 July 1971 and it replaced an ordinance, the Maintenance of Internal Security Ordinance, 1971, which the President had promulgated on 7 May 1971. Its long title states its purpose in the spare language of its genre: an Act to provide for detention in certain cases for the purpose of maintenance of internal security and matters connected therewith. Nothing in the long title, the preamble or the operative sections mentions an emergency, a political opposition, or any political party. The Act was a general preventive detention law for the whole of India, of the kind the Constitution contemplates in Article 22 and in the legislative entries that distribute the detention power between the Union and the states.

Section 3 conferred the detention power. The Central Government or a State Government could direct that a person be detained if satisfied that detention was necessary to prevent that person acting in a manner prejudicial to the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, or the security of India, or prejudicial to the security of the State or the maintenance of public order, or prejudicial to the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. A parallel power covered foreigners whose continued presence was to be regulated or whose expulsion was being arranged. The power could also be exercised by District Magistrates, by Additional District Magistrates specially empowered, and by Commissioners of Police in the cities named in the section, subject to a discipline that matters for everything that followed. An officer who made an order had to report it forthwith to the State Government with the grounds, and the order lapsed within a short fixed period unless the State Government approved it. A State Government that made or approved an order had to report it to the Central Government within seven days, again with the grounds. The statute, that is, built a reporting chain from the district to the state capital to New Delhi, and the chain ran on grounds stated in writing.

### Who could sign a MISA detention order?

A detention order under Section 3 could be made by the Central Government or a State Government, and also by a District Magistrate, an Additional District Magistrate specially empowered by the State Government, or a Commissioner of Police in the cities the section named, with each officer's order requiring prompt report to, and approval by, the State Government.

That delegation is worth pausing on, because it explains both the reach of the Act and one of the recurring findings about its abuse. A power that only a cabinet could exercise would be a power used sparingly and visibly. A power that a district officer could exercise, subject to later approval by his own government, is a power that can be used in large numbers, at speed, and at a level of the administration where the distinction between a security judgment and a local political instruction is hardest to police from outside. The statute did not hide this. Delegation to district officers had been a feature of Indian preventive detention since the predecessor statute of 1950, and Parliament in 1971 retained it knowingly, on the stated logic that the threats the Act addressed, sabotage, smuggling, communal violence, disorder around essential supplies, presented themselves first to district administrations. Whether that logic was sound is part of the value dispute examined later. As a matter of text, the design choice is plain.

Against the power, the 1971 Act set a safeguard architecture that tracked Article 22 of the Constitution with some care. Section 8 required the authority making an order to communicate the grounds of detention to the detenu as soon as may be, ordinarily not later than five days from the date of detention and, in exceptional circumstances recorded in writing, not later than fifteen days. The same section required the authority to afford the detenu the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate Government. Section 8 also contained, in its second sub-section, the seed of everything that followed: the authority was not required to disclose facts which it considered to be against the public interest to disclose. A distinction was therefore written into the original Act between the grounds, which had to be communicated, and the underlying facts, some of which could lawfully be held back. In 1971 that distinction left the detenu knowing the case against him in outline and able to answer it. What the Emergency amendments did, as a later section shows, was to abolish the communication of the grounds themselves for the class of detenus who mattered most, which collapsed the distinction entirely.

Sections 9 to 12 built the Advisory Board mechanism that Article 22(4) of the Constitution requires for detention beyond three months. Boards were to consist of three persons who were, had been, or were qualified to be appointed as judges of a High Court. The appropriate Government had to place every detention case before a Board within thirty days, with the grounds and any representation the detenu had made. The Board reported within ten weeks, and its opinion divided the world in two: if the Board found sufficient cause, the Government could confirm the order and continue the detention; if the Board found no sufficient cause, the Government was bound to revoke the order and release the person forthwith. Two limits on this safeguard deserve honest statement, because the strongest version of the case against the original Act depends on them. The detenu had no right to appear before the Board through a legal practitioner, and the Board's proceedings and report, apart from its opinion, were confidential. Review under the 1971 Act was therefore review by a judicially composed body working on papers the detenu could not fully test, conducted without counsel. It was a real check, and the record of the predecessor statute shows Boards ordering releases, but it was a check conducted largely in the dark. Section 13 fixed the outer limit of detention under a confirmed order at twelve months. Section 14 permitted revocation at any time and regulated repeat orders. Taken as a whole, the 1971 scheme was a detention law with a stated maximum, a duty to disclose grounds, a right of representation, and an external reviewing body with power to compel release. A reader who knows only the Act's later reputation will not recognise that description, which is precisely why the description has to come first.

The context of enactment completes the textual picture. The Preventive Detention Act, 1950, the first central detention statute of the Republic, had been allowed to lapse at the end of 1969 after repeated extensions, and its passage and working are traced in the account of [the Preventive Detention Act 1950 and the debates that produced it](/2010/06/15/preventive-detention-act-1950-explained/). For roughly seventeen months India had no central preventive detention statute. The ordinance of May 1971 and the Act that followed it were products of a specific and deteriorating security situation: the crisis in East Pakistan, a refugee movement into India on a very large scale, an approaching war that arrived in December 1971, and the Naxalite insurgency within the country, which in those years involved armed violence in West Bengal and elsewhere. The government of Indira Gandhi presented the measure as a security instrument for a state facing infiltration, sabotage and internal armed challenge, and Parliament enacted it in that setting. None of this makes the Act benign, and none of it was the whole truth about its later uses, which began well before 1975 in proceedings against smugglers, hoarders and political activists of the far left. It does establish the fact on which the rest of the analysis depends. MISA was not written for the Emergency. It was written four years earlier, for a different crisis, in a form that resembled the detention statutes the Republic had operated since 1950, and the Emergency is something that happened to it.


Two further provisions of the original Act frame everything that followed, and both repay attention before the argument begins. Section 15 gave the appropriate Government power to release a detenu temporarily, on conditions and on bond, and to cancel the release at will, the statutory root of the parole decisions that punctuate the Commission's record: temporary release in the scheme was a matter of executive grace administered by the detaining Government itself, not an entitlement a court supervised, so that even the statute's humanity ran through the detainer's hands. Section 16 barred suits and prosecutions for anything done or intended to be done in good faith under the Act, a standard official immunity clause whose presence in the original text is a reminder that the 1971 Parliament anticipated hard use of the power and chose to protect its operators in advance. Neither provision made the Act what it became. Each shows the draftsman's awareness, present from the first day, that a detention statute is operated by officials who will err and worse, and that the Act's real discipline was therefore located where this account has located it, in disclosure, representation and the Board, the provisions that forced the detaining system to show its work to somebody outside itself. When those provisions were disapplied, Sections 15 and 16 remained in force untouched, and the combination that resulted, grace administered by the detainer and immunity for the detainer, with scrutiny abolished, is as compact a description of the Emergency text as the four-step closure itself.

## The Case for the Law, Stated at Its Strongest

The defence of MISA, in its serious form, is not a defence of everything done in its name between 1975 and 1977. It is a set of propositions about preventive detention itself, about the constitutional settlement that authorises it, and about the situation of the Indian state in the first half of the 1970s, and it deserves to be met on that ground rather than on the ground of its weakest popular version.

The first proposition is constitutional. Preventive detention in India is not a colonial survival that slipped past the framers or a power seized by governments against the text. It is provided for in the Constitution itself, in Article 22, which both confers safeguards and expressly withholds some of them from persons detained under a preventive detention law, and it is distributed by the Seventh Schedule between Parliament and the state legislatures as an ordinary head of legislative power. The Constituent Assembly adopted those provisions after argument, with misgivings recorded on the floor, because the majority of its members believed a state facing communal violence, insurgency and external threat needed a power to detain on satisfaction rather than on proof. A critic who calls MISA unconstitutional in conception therefore has to argue not with the government of 1971 but with the settlement of 1950, and the serious critics, to their credit, usually do argue exactly that. The government's answer, in 1971 and again in 1975, was that it was exercising a power the Constitution granted in terms, through a statute enacted by Parliament, with the Advisory Board machinery the Constitution prescribes. Formally, that answer was correct, and its formal correctness is part of what made the later history possible.

The second proposition concerns the threat environment, and it is stronger for the early 1970s than retrospective writing usually allows. The state that enacted MISA confronted a war within the year, millions of refugees, an armed Maoist insurgency that assassinated policemen and landlords, organised smuggling networks that the government regarded as an economic security threat, and, by 1974 and 1975, a railway strike that stopped the national network, large scale industrial unrest, and a political movement whose stated objective was the removal of the elected government by extra parliamentary pressure. Indira Gandhi's public defence of the Emergency, and of the detentions that accompanied it, was that the alternative to decisive action was the disintegration of democratic government itself, that forces opposed to democracy were exploiting its freedoms to destroy it, and that a government which refused to defend the Republic's institutions in the name of liberty would lose both the institutions and the liberty. That argument was made at the time by the Prime Minister and by the Congress leadership, and versions of it have been repeated since by historians and commentators sympathetic to her assessment of 1975. It cannot be refuted by pointing to the abuses, because it does not deny that firm action has costs; it asserts that inaction had greater ones. Meeting it requires engaging the counterfactual, what an India without the detentions in the winter of 1975 would have looked like, and honest critics concede that the counterfactual is not self evidently peaceful.

The third proposition is about the design of detention law everywhere. Preventive detention exists, its defenders argue, because the criminal process answers a different question. A prosecution asks whether a person committed a defined act in the past and proves it to a standard; it is slow by design, it requires witnesses willing to testify, and it collapses when the threat is a plan rather than a completed act and when witnesses are intimidated. Every democracy that has faced sustained political violence has maintained some preventive instrument, and India's version, the defence continues, was in its 1971 form more constrained than many: grounds in writing within days, a representation the government had to consider, a Board of High Court calibre with power to order release, and a twelve month outer limit. On this view the correct object of criticism is not the existence of such a statute but the removal of its constraints, and the correct lesson is about amendment discipline rather than about detention itself. That is a serious argument, and a later section of this account adopts a version of it.

The fourth proposition is drawn from the detentions data itself, and it is the one least often heard. The Emergency detentions under MISA were not confined to the political class. The states classified MISA detenus into categories, and alongside the prominent political prisoners there were large numbers of persons detained as smugglers, hoarders, black market operators and ordinary offenders against public order, categories the government presented as proof that the Act was being used against the economic offences and disorder that its text named. The Shah Commission, as will be seen, found that the decisions to arrest and release were in many cases governed by political considerations, and that finding is part of the record. But the composition of the detained population is also part of the record, and it complicates any simple picture in which the Act was used for one purpose only. A defence that rests on composition alone proves too little, since a law can be aimed at smugglers in one district and at an opposition organiser in the next, and both uses sit in the same statistics. The point is narrower: the text of the Act named real harms, some of those harms were real in 1975, and the debate about MISA cannot be conducted honestly by pretending otherwise.

## How the Emergency Rewrote MISA Between 1975 and 1976

The Emergency was proclaimed by President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed on 25 June 1975, on the ground of internal disturbance, and the detentions of opposition leaders, journalists, trade unionists and activists began in the early hours that followed. What matters for a legislative account is not the proclamation itself but the sequence of legal instruments that then rebuilt MISA from the inside. There were five principal steps, and their order is the argument.

The first step was taken within days. The Maintenance of Internal Security (Amendment) Act, 1975, Act 39 of 1975, effective from 29 June 1975, inserted Section 16A into the Act under the heading of special provisions for dealing with emergency. Section 16A applied to detentions under the proclamations of 3 December 1971 and 25 June 1975 and it worked by disapplication. In the case of a detenu in respect of whom the Government made the declaration the section contemplated, namely that the detention was necessary for dealing effectively with the emergency, Sections 8 to 12 of the Act were declared not to apply. Those five sections were the safeguard architecture described above: the duty to communicate grounds, the opportunity to make a representation, the constitution and procedure of the Advisory Boards, and the duty to release when a Board found no sufficient cause. In their place Section 16A substituted review by the detaining Government itself, which was to reconsider at intervals not exceeding four months whether detention remained necessary, and which was expressly permitted, by sub-section (5), to conduct that review on the basis of information in its possession without communicating or disclosing any of it to the person concerned and without affording him any opportunity of making a representation. The detenu's twelve month maximum under Section 13 survived in form, but stripped of the Board confirmation that had given it content. The constitutional significance of this step is easy to miss because it was done by ordinary amendment. Article 22(4) requires Advisory Board review for detention beyond three months save where Parliament provides otherwise in the cases Article 22(7) permits, and the Emergency amendments were constructed to sit inside the space the Constitution left. The closure was achieved, at this first step, not by breaking the constitutional text but by using its permissions to their limit.

The second step barred the door that Section 16A had closed. Sub-sections (8) and (9) of Section 16A, added in the months that followed, first by ordinance in the autumn of 1975 and then by the amending Act of 1976 with retrospective effect, provided that the grounds of a detention covered by a declaration, and all information and materials on which those grounds and declarations were based, were to be treated as confidential, deemed to refer to matters of State and deemed against the public interest to disclose. No one was to communicate or disclose them, and the detenu was declared entitled to neither their communication nor the production of any document containing them. The distinction that the original Section 8(2) had drawn, between grounds that must be shared and facts that might be withheld, was inverted. The whole file became confidential as a matter of law, and disclosure became not a matter of official discretion but a statutory prohibition.

The third step moved the protection from the statute book to the Constitution. The Constitution (Thirty ninth Amendment) Act, 1975, by its Section 5, inserted a block of new entries into the Ninth Schedule, and among them, as entry 92, stood the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971, identified by its citation as Central Act 26 of 1971. The legal consequence flowed from Article 31B: an Act specified in the Ninth Schedule was not to be deemed void, or ever to have become void, on the ground that it was inconsistent with the fundamental rights in Part III, notwithstanding any judgment to the contrary. The insertion was not, in itself, a novelty; the Ninth Schedule had been built for land reform legislation and enlarged many times since 1951, and its interaction with the basic structure doctrine is traced in the account of [Kesavananda Bharati and the basic structure doctrine](/2010/02/01/basic-structure-doctrine-explained/). But its application to a detention statute in the middle of a detention drive changed what a court could do with a challenge to the Act itself. A detenu who argued that Section 16A violated Article 22 now faced the answer that the Act containing Section 16A had been placed by constitutional amendment beyond Part III challenge as that protection was then understood. The amending power had been used to immunise the product of the amending power's own emergency legislation, and to do so while many of the legislators who might have resisted the amendment were themselves in detention under the statute being immunised, a circumstance the critics of the period have never tired of pointing out and the defenders of the period have never satisfactorily answered.

The fourth step concerned duration. The amending legislation of 1976 adjusted Section 16A's period of operation, substituting a period of twenty four months from 25 June 1975 for the shorter period first enacted, and a companion amendment Act of the same year, Act 14 of 1976, recast parts of Section 16A retrospectively to 29 June 1975, regularising declarations, approvals and reviews that had been made under the earlier text. Retrospectivity of this kind has a quiet function in a detention regime: it removes the argument, available in any individual case, that a particular order failed for want of compliance with the text in force when it was made, because the text is then altered to match what was done.

The fifth step was not legislative at all, and it is treated in its own section below, because the brief sequence matters. Alongside the amendments, the Presidential order under Article 359 suspended the right to move any court for the enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22, and when detenus nevertheless petitioned the High Courts, the question of the remedy reached the Supreme Court. The four legislative and constitutional steps had removed the grounds, the disclosure, the Board and the challenge to the Act. The judicial step removed the forum. Set out in sequence, the transformation looks like this.

| Legal step | Instrument | What it changed | Safeguard removed |
|---|---|---|---|
| The 1971 baseline | Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971 (Act 26 of 1971) | Created the detention power with grounds, representation, Advisory Board review and a twelve month maximum | None; the full safeguard scheme stood |
| Grounds withheld | Section 16A, inserted by the Amendment Act 39 of 1975 | Sections 8 to 12 disapplied for declared detenus; government review substituted for Board review | Communication of grounds, representation, Advisory Board scrutiny |
| Disclosure barred | Section 16A, sub sections (8) and (9), added 1975 to 1976 | Grounds and all underlying material made confidential by law and deemed against the public interest to disclose | Any legal route to seeing the case against the detenu |
| The Act immunised | Constitution (Thirty ninth Amendment) Act, 1975, entry 92 of the Ninth Schedule | Placed MISA beyond challenge for inconsistency with fundamental rights under Article 31B | Constitutional challenge to the statute itself |
| Duration secured | Amendment Acts of 1976, including Act 78 of 1976 | Extended the Section 16A regime to twenty four months from 25 June 1975 and recast its machinery retrospectively | The discipline of a short, renewable emergency text |
| The remedy closed | ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976) | Held that no person had standing to move a court for habeas corpus while the Article 359 order operated | The habeas corpus remedy against unlawful detention |

### What is the four-step closure, and why does each step matter on its own?

The four-step closure is the sequence by which a detention statute loses every external check: grounds are withheld, disclosure is barred by law, the statute is immunised against fundamental rights challenge, and the habeas corpus remedy is closed, each step survivable alone because another safeguard remains standing elsewhere.

Understanding the sequence changes how the individual steps are judged. Withholding grounds is a known feature of security detention in many systems, and where a court or a board can still examine the file, the detenu's ignorance is partially compensated by someone else's knowledge. Barring disclosure is more serious, and yet where a reviewing body retains compulsory powers the bar binds the detenu rather than the reviewer. Immunising the statute removes the constitutional argument, and yet so long as statutory review survives, orders can still fall for non compliance with the Act itself. Suspending the remedy is the gravest step, and yet a remedy suspended for a short, defined crisis against a statute that still contains its safeguards is a different thing from the same suspension after those safeguards have been removed. The Emergency amendments took the steps in the order that maximised their combined effect, and they took them against a statute whose own text had, in 1971, contained each of the safeguards being removed. This is why the series thesis, that everything which made MISA notorious happened after enactment, is not a debating point but a description of the legislative record. The wider constitutional entrenchment of the same period, the attempt to place the Emergency settlement beyond the reach of courts and future Parliaments alike, belongs to [the 42nd Amendment and the Emergency charter it created](/2010/02/15/42nd-amendment-act-1976-explained/), and is linked rather than retold here; MISA's hardening was the detention arm of that larger project, and the two movements reinforced one another at every stage.


## MISA Before the Emergency: The Working Statute from 1971 to 1975

Between its enactment and the proclamation of June 1975, MISA lived the ordinary life of a security statute, and that life is evidence in the controversy, because it shows what the unamended text did and did not do when operated by the same political order that later amended it. The Act's first field of operation was the war of December 1971. The proclamation of emergency issued on 3 December 1971, during the war with Pakistan, remained formally in force for years afterwards, a fact of some importance for the legal history, because Section 16A as later drafted was keyed to that proclamation as well as to the proclamation of 1975, and because the existence of a continuing war emergency supplied part of the drafting bridge over which the 1975 regime walked. Under the 1971 proclamation the Act operated alongside the Defence of India Act, 1971, whose scheme modified several of MISA's provisions for the duration of its own force, including extensions of detention in defined classes of case. Foreigners stood in the hardest position under the original Act: Section 17 permitted a foreigner in specified classes, entry with arms, unauthorised presence in border and notified areas, suspected espionage under the Official Secrets Act, to be detained without Advisory Board opinion for periods beyond three months up to two years, a reminder that the universal language of the safeguard scheme always had a starker annex for those outside the political community.

In the same years the Act was used, as its text invited, against the organised smuggling and hoarding networks that the government of the day treated as economic warfare, and against cadres of the Naxalite movement in West Bengal and elsewhere, in proceedings that generated their own litigation and their own grievances well before the word Emergency attached to the statute. Two features of this working period matter for the verdict. The first is that the safeguard machinery functioned visibly: grounds were communicated and litigated, representations were made and occasionally succeeded, Advisory Boards sat and in some cases reported against detention, and the High Courts entertained challenges to orders on the classic grounds of non application of mind, irrelevant grounds and mala fides, setting orders aside where the detaining authority's satisfaction could not bear inspection. The jurisprudence of the early MISA years is a jurisprudence of grounds, of the single decisive ground, of the withheld document, and it exists because the statute gave courts and Boards material to work on. The second feature is that the same years display, in embryo, the habits the Commission later documented at scale: grounds drawn in formulae, satisfaction recorded in the language of the section rather than in the facts of the case, and a readiness in district administrations to reach for detention where the criminal process, with its witnesses and its standards, would have been slower and less certain. The embryo matters because it disciplines the argument in both directions. Against the defence, it shows that the operational vices were not invented in 1975. Against the critics, it shows that vices which a functioning safeguard system repeatedly exposed and sometimes corrected became, after 1975, vices that no institution was positioned to see, and the difference between those two conditions is the whole subject of the four-step closure.

The working statute also developed a relationship with its companion economic statute that the Emergency then fused. COFEPOSA, enacted in 1974, took the smuggling and foreign exchange class of detention out of MISA's general scheme into a dedicated Act with its own Advisory Board arrangements, and the two statutes ran in parallel through the Emergency, with the classification of detenus into political and economic streams administered largely through the choice of statute under which an order was drawn. That administrative choice carried consequences the detenus understood precisely: the class into which an order fell influenced the conditions of confinement, the treatment of the family, and the prospects of review, and it was a choice made by the detaining authority on its own file. A legislative history that treats MISA as a single self contained instrument misses this ecology. Detention in the Emergency was a system of statutes, proclamations, rules and classification practices, and MISA's place in it, the hardest core, defined by the most complete removal of safeguards, is a comparative judgment that the rest of this account has tried to make exact.

## The States and the Districts: How the Machine Actually Ran

MISA was a central Act administered substantially by state governments and their district officers, and its federal operation shaped its character in ways a Delhi centred account understates. The detention power under Section 3 ran at three levels: the Central Government, the State Governments, and the officers, District Magistrates, specially empowered Additional District Magistrates and the named Commissioners of Police, to whom the section delegated it subject to state approval. In practice the great majority of orders originated at state and district level, which meant that the Emergency's detention map was not uniform. States administered by the ruling party at the Centre applied the Act with the grain of the central direction; the classifications of detenus, the conditions of their confinement, the liberality or stinginess of parole, and the tempo of review varied with the state administration, and the Commission's volumes record that variation state by state. The reporting chain the statute required, district to state within days, state to Centre within seven days with grounds, was designed as a discipline on this dispersion, a means by which a wrongful district order could be caught by a reviewing government before it hardened into a long detention. Under Section 16A the chain changed function. The declaration machinery required state approval and central intimation, but the material moving upward was no longer grounds communicated to a detenu and tested by his answer; it was the detaining system's own account of itself, and the four monthly reconsideration the section substituted was conducted inside the same administrative family that had made the order. Federal dispersion, which in the 1971 design had been tempered by disclosure and Board review, in the amended design multiplied the sites at which an unreviewable discretion could be exercised while thinning the means by which any one exercise of it could be examined.

The district level deserves a paragraph of its own, because the Commission's most damning operational findings sit there. A District Magistrate's satisfaction, in the statutory scheme, is a judgment about a named person on material referable to that person. The findings record, instead, satisfaction manufactured in bulk: lists of names transmitted downward with instructions, orders prepared by subordinate staff for signature, grounds copied across files, and, in instances the Commission documented, orders dated to precede events they purported to answer, the backdating that the brief for this article singles out and that the Commission substantiated from the files. Each of those practices was unlawful under the unamended Act's own terms, and each would have been vulnerable, before 1975, to a court asking for the file. After the confidentiality clause, the file was a state secret as a matter of law, and the question could not effectively be asked. The machine, in other words, did not break the law at the district level and then hide the breakage; the law was rewritten at the centre so that the breakage at the district level ceased to be examinable. That inversion, from concealment of illegality to legalisation of unexaminability, is the most precise single sentence that can be written about the Emergency amendments, and it is a sentence about drafting rather than about tyranny, which is why it can be verified from the statute book and why it should anchor the teaching of this period.

The state level also supplied the period's clearest proof that the detention regime was a political instrument and was understood as one by its operators. The government of Tamil Nadu, led by the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, which had opposed the Emergency's course, was dismissed in January 1976, and detentions of its leading figures followed under the President's rule that replaced it, a sequence in which the removal of an elected state government and the detention of its leadership appear as two movements of a single policy rather than as a constitutional event and a security response that happened to coincide. Other state administrations required no such handling, because their political direction already ran with the Centre's, and the contrast between the two classes of state is the federal evidence for the Commission's central finding. A security law administered by states applies a national text through local politics; where the local politics was the ruling party's own, detention lists and party purposes merged without friction, and where it was not, the Constitution's emergency and President's rule provisions were used first to change the administration and then to operate the statute. The lesson generalises beyond the period. Any detention regime whose orders originate at district and state level will reproduce, in its pattern of use, the political map of the governments operating it, and safeguards that assume a neutral administrator will fail exactly where they are most needed. The 1971 Act's safeguards were addressed to that reality imperfectly but genuinely, through disclosure and an external Board. The amended Act withdrew the address and left the reality.

## MISA in Operation: The Detentions and the Record

A statute hardened in the manner described above will be operated in the manner its hardening permits, and MISA was. The detentions began with the proclamation itself. In the first hours and days, the police arrested the leadership of the opposition parties, the organisers of the movement led by Jayaprakash Narayan, trade union leaders, journalists, student activists and members of organisations the government had decided to suppress, and the arrests continued throughout the Emergency as new categories of person came within the view of district administrations. The names at the head of the detained population are a roll of the Indian politics of the period: Jayaprakash Narayan himself, Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, L. K. Advani, George Fernandes, who evaded arrest for a period and was held later, and younger figures such as Chandrashekhar and Mohan Dharia, who had broken with the ruling party from inside it and were detained for the break. Detention on this scale at the political summit was accompanied, in far greater numbers, by detention at the base: party workers, union organisers, teachers, lawyers who appeared for detenus, and the office holders of the organisations the government treated as fronts. The Shah Commission record, examined in the next section, documents orders made against persons whose offence, on the face of the file, was association, speech or presence.

The legal form of these detentions concealed a distinction that confusion about the period habitually destroys, and precision here is a service the legislative record can perform. MISA was not the only detention instrument of the Emergency. The Defence of India Act and the Defence of India Rules made under it, referred to in the records as the DIR and in some accounts by the fused acronym DISIR, supplied a parallel detention and control regime, and a second statute, the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act, 1974, known as COFEPOSA, supplied detention powers aimed at smuggling and foreign exchange offences. Persons were held under each of these instruments, and the same person could be pursued under more than one. The distinction matters for the numbers, for the safeguards, and for the argument. MISA detenus after the 1975 amendments stood in the weakest legal position of any class, because Section 16A had removed their grounds, their representation and their Board. When a retrospective account attributes every Emergency detention to MISA, it overstates the reach of one statute and understates the breadth of the apparatus, which is the opposite of the truth the record supports: the apparatus was wider than MISA, and MISA was its hardest core.

The scale of the whole operation can be stated with more confidence than popular writing usually manages, because the figures most often quoted descend from a single official source. The count that the Shah Commission's work put into circulation, and that later accounts citing the Commission reproduce, is 110,806 persons detained during the Emergency. Of these, 34,988 were detained under MISA and 75,818 under the Defence of India Rules, the two figures summing to the total. The figures require three qualifications, which are developed in the section on factual disputes. They count detentions under the two principal instruments and not every arrest, every COFEPOSA detention or every short police custody of the period. They are aggregates compiled from state returns of uneven quality. And they include, as the classification system described earlier shows, persons detained for economic offences and ordinary public order grounds alongside the political detenus who dominate the memory of the period. Subject to those qualifications, the order of magnitude is not in genuine dispute. Something over one hundred thousand detentions were carried out under the two instruments, roughly a third of them under MISA, and the MISA third was the part of the detained population that the law had stripped of every ordinary means of finding out why.

Detention under the hardened MISA had a texture that the bare figures do not convey, and the Commission record supplies it. A detenu covered by a Section 16A declaration received an order and, in substance, nothing else: no grounds to answer, no file to inspect, no Board to persuade, and a reviewing authority that was the detaining Government itself, reconsidering his case at intervals on material he could not see. Family visits, parole and correspondence were governed by the conditions the appropriate Government specified under the Act's detention conditions power, and the record of the period contains repeated findings of parole refused on occasions, such as a death in the family, where no security rationale was even asserted. Orders were in many instances not individualised in any meaningful sense: the Commission documented orders prepared in batches, orders signed against names supplied on lists, orders in which the grounds, where any were recorded for the file, were cyclostyled formulae applied identically to persons of entirely different profiles. The point is not merely administrative sloveness. A safeguard system is a set of occasions on which a second mind examines a first mind's decision, and the amendments had abolished the occasions. What remained was a single mind, the detaining authority's, examining itself on its own material, and the operational record is what that arrangement predictably produced.

### What happened to MISA detenus when the Emergency ended?

When the Emergency was revoked on 21 March 1977, after the election that the government of Indira Gandhi lost, the detentions collapsed as a system rather than ending case by case: orders were revoked in large numbers, the remaining detenus were released as the Janata government took office, and the freed leadership formed the new administration's core.

That ending casts a particular light backwards. The releases were not the product of the statute's own machinery working at last; no Board found the grounds insufficient, no court ordered the doors opened. The detention regime ended because the electorate ended the government that operated it. Defenders of the period treat this as the system's ultimate safeguard functioning as designed: the Emergency was submitted to the voters at the first election, the voters delivered a verdict, and the verdict was obeyed. Critics answer that a safeguard which operates only after one hundred thousand detentions, and only because the detaining government chose to call the election it then lost, is not a safeguard of the detenu's rights at all but a description of their absence. Both readings are taken up in the section on value disputes. The operational fact is simpler and is common ground: between June 1975 and March 1977 the only institution that reviewed MISA detentions with any independence was the electorate, and it reviewed them all at once, at the end.

## The Case Against the Law, Stated at Its Strongest

The case against MISA does not rest on rhetoric, and its strongest form is documentary. It rests principally on the report of the Shah Commission of Inquiry, the body appointed by the Janata government in 1977 under Justice J. C. Shah, a former Chief Justice of India, to inquire into the excesses, malpractices and misuse of authority of the Emergency period. The Commission published its findings in three volumes running to 525 pages, through interim reports and a final report in 1978, and its chapter on the detention laws remains the single most authoritative account of how MISA worked in practice, because it was compiled from the Government's own files, from the testimony of the officers who operated the system, and from the records of the detaining authorities themselves.

The Commission's findings on the circumstances of the Emergency itself framed its findings on detention. It concluded that there was no economic crisis and no crisis of law and order of a character that justified the proclamation, and that the decision to impose the Emergency was taken by the Prime Minister alone, without consulting her Cabinet colleagues. On detention specifically, its conclusion was unqualified: the provisions of the Maintenance of Internal Security Act and of the Defence of India Rules were not followed in their operation but were abused in order to damage political opponents. In its interim report the Commission stated the mechanism plainly, recording that the decisions to arrest and to release particular persons were taken entirely on political considerations intended to be favourable to the ruling party, and that employing the police to the advantage of one party is a sure source of subverting the rule of law. A finding in those terms, from a commission working on the official record, removes the ground from under the most common defence of the detentions, which is that a sound law was operated conscientiously against genuine threats and marred only by excesses at the margin. On the Commission's evidence the political direction of arrests was not the margin. It was a documented feature of the system's ordinary operation, extending to orders made, backdated and multiplied to meet the requirements of the moment, to detenus held after the ostensible ground had evaporated, and to the use of detention against journalists, lawyers and organisers whose detention served no purpose the Act's text would recognise.

The strongest form of the case against the statute, as distinct from the case against its operators, builds on that record in three moves. The first move is about Section 16A. A detention law that withholds grounds does not merely inconvenience the detenu; it abolishes the activity that every other safeguard exists to support. A representation answers grounds. A Board tests grounds. A court examines whether grounds are relevant, sufficient and honestly held. Remove the grounds and each surviving institution is reduced to examining a conclusion. The Emergency amendments therefore did not weaken the safeguard system at its edges; they removed its object, and they did so by design, in terms, with the confidentiality clause of sub-section (9) standing as proof that the removal was considered rather than inadvertent. The second move is about the Ninth Schedule insertion. Constitutional immunisation of a detention statute, enacted while opposition legislators were detained under that statute, is the point at which the ordinary language of legislative process stops describing what happened. A Parliament from which the statute's principal victims were absent by the statute's own operation amended the Constitution to place the statute beyond the victims' legal reach. Defenders respond, correctly as far as it goes, that the Thirty ninth Amendment was enacted by the majorities the Constitution requires. The critics' answer, made by constitutional commentators of the standing of H. M. Seervai in his great commentary on the Constitution, is that formal validity is the beginning of the inquiry into Emergency legislation and not its end, because the question the period poses is precisely what a determined majority can lawfully do to the conditions of its own continuance. The third move is about consequences for the text itself. A statute book is a set of standing instructions to future governments. MISA demonstrated, and its demonstration is available to any later reader of the record, that an ordinary detention Act can be converted into an unreviewable one in a single parliamentary session, using only instruments the Constitution supplies. The critics' deepest objection to MISA is therefore not to its 1971 text, which many of them would replace with a constrained detention power of some kind, but to its proof of concept.

The reception of the Shah Commission report completes the case against, because it shows what happened to accountability when the political wheel turned again. The Commission's work was not implemented by any systematic prosecution or compensation scheme. When Indira Gandhi returned to power in 1980, her government repudiated the Commission's findings, treated the report as a partisan document, and allowed it to fall out of official circulation; copies became genuinely difficult to obtain for years, and the report survived in public memory largely because Era Sezhiyan, a parliamentarian of the period, republished its text in a volume that kept it in circulation outside official channels. A state that commissions an inquiry, receives findings of abuse documented from its own files, and then makes those findings hard to read has answered the question the inquiry posed by the only means left to it. That afterlife is part of the legislative record of MISA, because it bears directly on the argument, considered below, that the political system corrects itself. The correction of 1977 was real. So was the un-correction of 1980, and a verdict on the period that stops at the first of those dates has not finished reading the record.


## Conditions, Families and the Press

The lived record of MISA detention sits in the Shah Commission's volumes in the form of prison evidence, and it corrects two sentimental errors at once. The first error is the assumption that detention was a uniform experience of the prominent. The classification system divided the detained population by the administration's own estimate of importance: in states such as Andhra Pradesh the record shows detenus sorted into classes, with the leading political figures in the highest class receiving better accommodation, food and access, lesser political workers in a middle class, and those detained for economic offences and ordinary grounds in the lowest, held in conditions approximating those of the ordinary prison population. Privilege inside a detention system is itself a finding: it shows an administration grading its prisoners by political salience, which is a confession about the criterion the system was actually applying. The second error is the assumption that detention's burden fell only on the detained. The Commission documented the refusal of parole on family occasions, the interruption of livelihoods for detenus who were the earning members of households, and the use of detention's threat against family members and associates of persons the administration wished to silence or to flush out. George Fernandes's case supplied the period's emblem of the second practice: a national leader driven underground, pursued for months, and finally held while proceedings in the Baroda dynamite case were built around the circle that had sheltered him. A detention order, in a system where the order cannot be examined, radiates outward from its named subject to everyone whose association with him becomes dangerous, and that radiation, the silencing of people who were never detained at all, is a real part of the statute's operation that no count of detenus captures.

The press stood in a particular relation to the detention regime, as victim, as target and as record keeper. Journalists were detained under MISA, and the detention of Kuldip Nayar, among the best known cases, reached the Delhi High Court, which ordered his release in proceedings whose judgment arrived after his detention had been revoked, an episode remembered because it showed a High Court insisting on its habeas jurisdiction in the Emergency's early months. Censorship did the larger part of the work against the press as an institution, and censorship was imposed under the Defence of India Rules rather than under MISA, a distinction this account has insisted on for the detention figures and which applies here with equal force: the apparatus was a system, and assigning every element of it to the most famous statute flatters that statute's importance while obscuring how the elements supported one another. Detention removed the persons, censorship removed the reporting of the removals, and the confidentiality clauses removed the legal record by which the removals could later be audited. When the audit finally came, the Shah Commission had to reconstruct the detention system substantially from the administration's internal files, because the public record of the period had been managed as carefully as the detentions themselves. That reconstruction is the reason the Commission's volumes, rather than the newspapers of 1975 and 1976, are the foundation of every serious account since, and the reason their preservation, against the official neglect described earlier, is a historiographical fact and not merely a political one.

## The Advisory Board Question

At the centre of the original Act's design, and at the centre of its Emergency transformation, sits an institution that general readers rarely examine: the Advisory Board. Its design explains both the value of what was removed in 1975 and the limits of what had existed before. A Board under Sections 9 to 11 was a body of three persons of High Court calibre, appointed by the very Government whose orders it reviewed, sitting without the detenu's counsel, working on the grounds and the representation and on any further material it chose to call for, its proceedings confidential, its report divided between a confidential narrative and a stated opinion on whether sufficient cause for detention existed. Where the Board found no sufficient cause, the Government had no discretion: revocation and release followed as obligations. The design is a compromise characteristic of the Article 22 settlement, review by judges in everything but name, procedure and publicity. Its strength was personnel and consequence: the reviewer was not the detainer's colleague but a person of judicial standing, and the reviewer's negative opinion bound. Its weakness was opacity: because the proceedings were confidential and unreasoned in public, a Board system could decay into confirmation without any visible sign, and the detenu who was confirmed learned nothing except that he remained detained.

Section 16A's treatment of the Board is the analytical crux of the amendments, because the section did not reform the compromise, attack its opacity, strengthen its personnel or publish its reasons. It simply disapplied Sections 9 to 12 along with the rest of the safeguard block for the declared class, and substituted reconsideration by the appropriate Government, the detainer reviewing itself. The substitution exposes the theory on which the whole closure rested. Review was not abolished in form; a review existed, at stated intervals of not more than four months, conducted on the file. What was abolished was the independence of the reviewer. Every safeguard system can be described as a set of answers to three questions: who reviews, on what material, with what consequence. The 1971 Act answered: a judicially composed Board, on grounds the detenu has seen and answered, with release the mandatory consequence of a negative opinion. The amended Act answered: the Government itself, on material the detenu cannot see, with continuation the default consequence of its own satisfaction. A reformer who believed the Board system defective had available every intermediate answer, security cleared counsel, reasoned opinions in redacted form, a judicial chairman with a casting voice, statutory time limits on confirmation. None was chosen. The choice actually made, review by the reviewed, is the choice that converts a safeguard into a ritual, and the post Emergency settlement's restoration of the Board architecture in successor statutes, rather than its replacement by anything more ambitious, shows how completely the argument of the period was framed by the 1971 design: the Republic, having seen the compromise destroyed by disapplication, rebuilt the compromise and relied on the difficulty of disapplying it again.

## The Judicial Position

The courts occupy an ambiguous place in the MISA story, and precision about the sequence matters more than a general verdict on judicial courage or its absence. The first judicial word on the Emergency detentions was not the Supreme Court's. It belonged to the High Courts, and it went against the Government. Detenus petitioned under Article 226 for habeas corpus, arguing in substance that even with the enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22 suspended by the Presidential order under Article 359, a detention could still be challenged on the grounds that it did not comply with the statute under which it purported to be made, or that it was mala fide, or that it had been ordered by an authority the Act did not empower. Nine High Courts accepted the maintainability of that argument. Their reasoning drew a line that has structured Indian detention law ever since the distinction was first drawn under the predecessor statute: the suspension of a fundamental right's enforcement is not the suspension of the statute, and a detenu who says that the executive has broken its own law is not, without more, asking the court to enforce the suspended right. The picture of a judiciary uniformly compliant with the Emergency does not survive contact with that record. Nine constitutional courts, staffed by judges who knew the temper of the government they were ruling against, held the petitions maintainable and in a number of cases ordered relief.

The Government appealed, and the appeals were consolidated in the case that gives the habeas corpus litigation its permanent name. On the remedy sentence the whole of this controversy turns, and it is stated here without reargument, because the decision, its reasoning and its afterlife are owned elsewhere in this series: the Supreme Court, by a majority of four to one in ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla, delivered on 28 April 1976, held that while the Article 359 order operated, no person had any standing to move a court for a writ of habeas corpus to challenge a detention, even on the ground that the order was not in compliance with the Act or was mala fide, and [the full account of that decision and of Justice Khanna's lone dissent](/2010/07/15/adm-jabalpur-habeas-corpus-case/) traces how the majority reached a conclusion the nine High Courts had rejected. For the purposes of the MISA account, the decision's function in the four-step closure is what matters. Steps one to four had been taken by amendment and by constitutional amendment. Step five, the closing of the forum, was taken by judgment, and it was taken against the considered view of nine High Courts, which is why the standard description of the case as the judiciary's darkest hour, the description associated with commentators from Seervai to V. M. Tarkunde, who characterised the majority judgments in terms of institutional self destruction, has the hold it does. The same record, read whole, also shows the judiciary as the only institution of the state that produced, at the High Court level, a reasoned refusal. Both facts belong in the verdict, and a treatment that keeps only one of them is advocacy rather than history.

Two later judicial facts complete the position without reopening the 1976 argument. The first is that the habeas corpus decision has been expressly overruled by a nine judge bench of the Supreme Court in the privacy judgment in K. S. Puttaswamy v Union of India, so that its authority as law is extinguished and its place in the books is as a warning. The second is constitutional rather than judicial: the Forty fourth Amendment, considered below, made the enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 non suspendable even during an emergency, removing the textual foundation on which the 1976 majority had built. The remedy that failed in 1976 was thus restored by both routes the system possesses, by judgment and by amendment, and each restoration is an institutional admission about what had occurred. An account of MISA that ends in 1977 misses the fact that the legal order spent the following years formally repudiating the decision that had completed the closure, which is itself evidence about how the closure is to be judged.

### Why did nine High Courts side with the detenus?

The nine High Courts held that the suspension of fundamental rights enforcement did not suspend the detention statute itself, so a detenu could still ask a court to test whether his order complied with the Act, was made by an authorised officer, or was mala fide, a distinction between enforcing a suspended right and enforcing the executive's own law.

## The Factual Disputes, and What the Record Supports

Reference works and reputable accounts contradict one another on at least four factual questions about MISA, and a reader is owed an explanation of each contradiction rather than a silent choice among them. The contradictions are instructive, because three of the four arise from the same cause: the habit of compressing a sequence into a slogan.

The first dispute is the date of the Act. A large body of popular writing describes MISA as an Emergency law, groups it with the instruments of 1975, or implies that it was enacted to detain the opposition in the Emergency. The record does not support this. The Act is Act 26 of 1971, assented to on 2 July 1971, preceded by an ordinance of 7 May 1971, and debated and enacted in the setting of the East Pakistan crisis and the Naxalite insurgency. The error matters because it reverses the causal story. If MISA were an Emergency creation, the lesson of the period would be that emergencies generate bad statutes, a lesson every government can accept in the abstract. The true sequence, an ordinary statute hardened after enactment, yields the harder lesson that the danger to a safeguard system lies in the amendment process applied to laws already on the book, which is a lesson about ordinary legislative vigilance rather than about exceptional moments.

The second dispute is the number of detentions, and it is the dispute on which the most confident nonsense is written. Figures in circulation range from round claims of one hundred thousand, through the 110,806 of the Commission derived count, to claims of a MISA detained population far above the MISA specific figure. The divergence has identifiable causes. Some accounts count arrests, which include short custody and ordinary criminal process, while the Commission derived figures count detentions under named instruments. Some combine MISA and the Defence of India Rules without saying so, and some attribute the combined total to MISA alone, roughly tripling the statute's own figure of 34,988. Some count only the prominent political detenus, producing figures in the hundreds that describe a real class and misdescribe the whole. And some figures descend from state returns of differing completeness, since the aggregate was assembled from the records of the detaining administrations themselves. The defensible statement is the layered one given earlier: 110,806 detentions under the two principal instruments in the count the Shah Commission's work circulated, of which 34,988 were under MISA and 75,818 under the Defence of India Rules, subject to the limits of the underlying returns. A researcher who wants to test those layers against the primary record works from the Commission's three volumes and the state wise lists preserved with them, material that survives in document and spreadsheet copies of uneven quality; files of that kind can be opened and read privately in the browser with [the ReportMedic office file viewer for spreadsheets, documents and presentations](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which renders them on the reader's own device without uploading the file anywhere. The methodological point does not depend on the tool: in detention statistics, the instrument, the class and the source must always be named together, or the number is decoration.

The third dispute is the date and manner of the Act's end, on which sources divide between 1977 and 1978 and between repeal and lapse. This dispute dissolves once the sequence is separated. The political decision to end MISA belonged to 1977: the Janata government took office in March of that year committed to dismantling the Emergency's legal apparatus, detenus were released, and the Act ceased to operate as a going concern. The legal extinction of the Act belonged to 1978. MISA did not lapse, because unlike its predecessor of 1950 it carried no sunset clause whose expiry could end it. It was repealed, by the Maintenance of Internal Security (Repeal) Act, 1978, Act 27 of 1978, the repeal recorded against the parent Act as effective on 3 August 1978. Accounts that date the repeal to 1977 are dating the decision; accounts that date it to 1978 are dating the statute; accounts that describe a lapse are importing the biography of the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 into the biography of its successor. The question the seeded FAQ in this article asks, who repealed MISA and when, therefore has a two part answer in the text and a one part answer in the FAQ, where the repealing Act and its year are the legally operative facts.

The fourth dispute concerns the Shah Commission itself: what it was, and what authority its findings carry. It is sometimes dismissed in political argument as a victor's tribunal whose conclusions can be discounted accordingly, and sometimes invoked as though it were a court whose findings bind. It was neither. It was a commission of inquiry appointed under the Commissions of Inquiry Act by the government that succeeded the Emergency government, chaired by a former Chief Justice, working substantially on the records of the detaining state itself, and its findings are findings of fact and opinion by an inquiry, not convictions. Their authority rests on the quality and provenance of the evidence it printed, which is why its documentary method matters: where the Commission quoted the file, the file can be checked. Its detentions chapter has worn well under that test, and no rival official account of the MISA detentions has ever been produced to set against it, a silence that is itself part of the evidentiary position.

## Parliament and the Missing Opposition

Every instrument in the four-step closure except the Jabalpur judgment passed through Parliament, and the condition of Parliament in those months is a fact of the legislative record that neither side of the controversy may skip. The Lok Sabha that enacted the Emergency amendments was the House elected in 1971, its term extended during the Emergency beyond the five years the Constitution originally contemplated, the extension itself effected by constitutional amendment in the same period. A substantial part of its opposition membership was in detention under the statute the House was amending. Debates on the detention amendments were therefore conducted in a chamber from which the amendments' principal objects were absent in prison, under censorship that restricted what the country could read of the proceedings, and under party discipline in a ruling party whose internal dissent had itself been answered, in prominent cases, with detention. The majorities were real majorities of the members voting, and the forms of the Constitution were observed: bills introduced, clauses considered, amendments of the Constitution carried by the special majorities Article 368 requires, assent given. It is exactly this completeness of form that makes the period the hardest case in Indian legislative studies. Nothing in the record permits the comfortable conclusion that the forms were bypassed. The forms were used, by a chamber whose composition the executive had altered by detention, to remove the safeguards of the detained. Defenders of the period answer that attendance and voting were lawful, that members detained were detained under a valid law for reasons the state asserted, and that a Parliament is not required to be a sympathetic audience for the measures a government brings. Critics answer that a special majority manufactured in part by imprisonment is a fact about the amendment, not a debating point about it, and that the Thirty ninth Amendment's immunisation of MISA, carried while MISA held the opposition, is the point where Indian constitutionalism came closest to a self sealing system: the detaining power detains the legislators, the remaining legislators immunise the detaining power, and the courts, in the final step, decline the remedy. The verdict section of this account treats that sequence as the strongest single exhibit for the statutory failure thesis, because every link in it is documented in the Gazette and in the Commission's record, and none of it depends on attributing motives that the documents do not state.

The same parliamentary record contains the counterweight, and fairness requires it in the same section. The Parliament of 1977 and 1978, elected after the Emergency, used the identical forms for the opposite work: it repealed MISA outright, it enacted the Forty fourth Amendment with its restoration of safeguards, and it appointed, through the executive it sustained, the Commission whose report supplies the evidence on which this entire account rests. The institution that had sealed the system unsealed it, and it did so within eighteen months of the election that changed its composition. A theory of the period that treats Parliament merely as the executive's instrument cannot explain the speed and completeness of the reversal, any more than a theory that treats Parliament as liberty's reliable guardian can explain 1975. The record supports the narrower and more useful proposition with which this account is concerned throughout: the forms are neutral, the texts are what endure, and a legislature's virtue in a crisis is measured by the amendments it refuses when refusal is costly. Measured by that standard the Parliaments of the period divide exactly as the country divided, and the statute book preserves both halves of the division in the same bound volumes, the hardening Acts of 1975 and 1976 facing the repealing Act of 1978 across a gap of three years.

## The Constitutional Architecture Around the Statute

MISA's hardening was executed inside a constitutional emergency architecture whose moving parts a reader must hold separately, because public argument habitually fuses them into a single darkness. Three distinct mechanisms operated. The proclamation under Article 352 created the Emergency itself and with it the Union's enlarged legislative and executive reach. The Presidential order under Article 359 suspended the enforcement of specified fundamental rights, Articles 14, 21 and 22 among them, which is a suspension of the remedy in the courts rather than a repeal of the rights, a distinction on which the nine High Courts built and the Supreme Court majority declined to build. Article 358's automatic suspension of Article 19 during an emergency proclaimed on grounds of war or external aggression belonged, in its 1975 application, to the continuing 1971 proclamation rather than to the internal disturbance proclamation, a technicality that mattered to the pleading of the period and that illustrates how much of the Emergency's law ran on the interaction of two proclamations issued four years apart for different stated grounds. Around these mechanisms Parliament worked the amendment power: the Thirty eighth Amendment insulating proclamations from judicial review, the Thirty ninth placing MISA and other statutes in the Ninth Schedule and the Prime Minister's election beyond the ordinary courts, and the Forty second carrying the general entrenchment of the settlement. Section 16A's drafting belongs visibly to this architecture: its own text is keyed not to the existence of disorder but to the operation of the two named proclamations, and its period clause, as amended, simply adopts the proclamations' lifespan with a twenty four month outer bound. The statute and the constitutional instruments were designed as one machine, and they stopped as one machine when the second proclamation was revoked in March 1977, leaving only the repeal and the Schedule omission to be tidied in 1978. This interlocking is why the controversy about MISA can never be only about a detention Act. The Act was the point at which the emergency architecture touched the largest number of individual lives, and its record is the most concrete evidence of what the architecture was for.

## The Value Disputes That Evidence Cannot Settle

Underneath the factual disputes sit three disagreements that are not about what happened, and an honest treatment names them as value disputes and declines to dress a preference as a finding.

The first is the standing dispute about preventive detention itself. One position, held by civil liberties lawyers and by a tradition running through the Radical Humanist circle around V. M. Tarkunde to the citizens' liberties organisations that documented the Emergency, is that detention without trial is a constitutional anomaly that no safeguard architecture redeems, because a power to imprison on satisfaction will always expand to fill the politics of the moment, and the correct response to the MISA record is abolition of the category. The opposing position, held by successive governments of every party and by the framers who wrote Article 22, is that a state which cannot detain preventively cannot discharge its first duty, that the category is constitutionally sanctioned precisely because the framers weighed the objection and answered it, and that the correct response to abuse is the repair of safeguards rather than the surrender of the power. The MISA record informs this dispute without settling it, because both sides can read the same record as confirmation: the abolitionist points to the speed of the closure, the retentionist to the fact that the closure required five deliberate steps, each of which a vigilant legislature or court could have refused.

The second dispute is about where the failure of 1975 to 1977 is located. The political failure thesis, argued seriously by defenders of the period and by some historians who have no sympathy with it, holds that the statute book was not the problem: the same texts, operated by a government observing the conventions of restraint and facing a Parliament and press able to resist, would have produced a severe but lawful security regime, and what failed was the political system, its dominant party, its subservient administration and its intimidated public sphere. The statutory failure thesis, argued by the critics whose case is set out above, holds that this lets the law off too easily: a detention statute whose safeguards can all be removed by the majority of the day, in a session from which the opposition is absent in prison, is a standing invitation, and a legal order that relies on the restraint of those it empowers has not understood its own function. The verdict offered at the end of this account sides, on this point, with a version of the statutory thesis, for a reason the record supplies. The political failure thesis explains 1975. It does not explain why the instruments of 1975 remained available in text until 1978, or why the corrective amendment of 1978 left one of its own safeguards uncommenced, patterns this series traces across many statutes under the name of the notification gap. Systems that depend entirely on virtue fail in the same places repeatedly; systems that attend to their texts at least make failure require effort.

The third dispute is about the electorate's verdict of 1977 and what it proves. For the defence of the period, the election is the central exculpatory fact: democracy was interrupted by its own constitutional procedures and restored by its own central ritual, and a system capable of that self correction has resources that a purely legal analysis understates. For the critics, the election proves almost the opposite, because the restoration was contingent on a single decision, the decision to call the election at all, taken by the person who had most to lose from it, on a misreading of her own intelligence reports. A safeguard that depends on the incumbent's miscalculation is not a safeguard. This dispute is genuinely irresolvable by evidence, because it turns on how much contingency a constitutional order may honourably contain, and readers will divide on it according to their sense of what institutions are for. It is named here as a value dispute so that neither side's version is smuggled into the factual sections above, where it does not belong.


## The Comparative Angle: MISA Among Detention Laws

MISA sits in a line, and its position in the line is the comparative point. Behind it stood the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, the statute whose lapse it filled, constrained by constant parliamentary renewal and remembered chiefly for the litigation it generated over the disclosure of grounds. Beside it during the Emergency stood COFEPOSA, the 1974 statute aimed at smuggling and foreign exchange offences, which shared the Emergency experience of hardening amendments but was built for a narrower class and survived the period to remain in force long afterwards, a contrast worth dwelling on: the economic detention statute outlived the Republic's reckoning with the Emergency almost untouched, while the general internal security statute was repealed outright. Ahead of it stood its successor. When Parliament returned to general preventive detention legislation after the Janata interlude, it enacted a new statute rather than reviving the repealed one, and [the National Security Act 1980 and the framework it built](/2010/08/01/national-security-act-1980-explained/) occupies the ground MISA vacated, with the Article 22 safeguard architecture restored to the foreground of its design. The choice of a fresh statute over a revival matters. Repeal followed by new enactment is the legislative form of a judgment: the Janata Parliament did not merely switch MISA off, it struck it from the book, and the Parliament of 1980, legislating for the same security problems, wrote on a clean page rather than reusing the condemned text.

The argument that later detention laws are functionally equivalent to MISA, a staple of political debate whenever a preventive detention or security statute is tightened, deserves the serious two sided treatment the framework requires rather than agreement or dismissal. In favour of the equivalence, the continuity is real. The constitutional foundation is unchanged: Article 22 still authorises preventive detention and still withholds the ordinary arrest protections from detenus. The safeguard architecture of every successor statute is the architecture MISA had in 1971, grounds, representation, Advisory Board, maximum period, because that architecture is what the Constitution prescribes. And the recurring operational complaints about later detention laws, formulaic grounds, mechanical Board confirmations, detention used where prosecution was available, echo the Commission's findings about the 1950s and 1970s closely enough to establish that the failure mode is systemic rather than personal to one statute. Against the equivalence, the differences are structural and they are exactly the four steps. No successor statute has been amended to disapply its own disclosure and Board provisions wholesale for a declared class of detenus. No successor statute has been inserted into the Ninth Schedule to immunise it against fundamental rights challenge, and since the basic structure jurisprudence matured, such an insertion would face the review the courts developed for laws placed in the Schedule after April 1973. And the habeas corpus route stands open, with Articles 20 and 21 placed beyond suspension. The equivalence argument is therefore true about the foundation and false about the superstructure. Preventive detention in India retains a family resemblance across its statutes, because the Constitution designs the family. MISA in its Emergency form was the member of the family in which every protection the design allowed to be removed was removed, and resemblance is not identity.

A second comparison, usually neglected, is with the detention practice the Republic inherited and rejected. The colonial state detained under the Defence of India Rules of the Second World War and under the public safety legislation of the provinces, on executive satisfaction, without the constitutional superstructure of Article 22, Advisory Boards composed of High Court calibre members, or a Supreme Court with writ jurisdiction over the detaining power. The distance between that inheritance and the 1971 Act is the measure of what the constitutional settlement added. The distance between the 1971 Act and its own 1976 form is the measure of how quickly the addition can be subtracted while every formal step remains lawful. Comparative reading thus cuts in both directions at once: against complacency, because the subtraction happened; against despair, because the settlement's machinery, once the political moment passed, was the machinery by which the subtraction was reversed.

A longer comparison reaches behind the Republic to the statute whose name performed, for an earlier generation, the work MISA's name performs now. The Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919, remembered by its committee chairman's name as the Rowlatt Act, authorised detention and trial procedures outside the ordinary courts for revolutionary offences, and it provoked the first all India political movement of the Gandhian era before being repealed within three years without having been worked as its authors intended. The parallels instruct by their limits. The Rowlatt Act was a colonial statute imposed against the near unanimous opposition of the Indian members of the legislature that passed it, and its life was short because the political order that enacted it could neither use it at scale nor survive the agitation against it. MISA was a statute of the Republic, enacted by an elected Parliament, sustained for seven years, and hardened by the elected legislature itself at the moment of its widest use. Its repeal, likewise, came not from an imperial withdrawal but from an electoral verdict and a successor Parliament. The comparison strips away the last comforting misreading of the MISA story, the suggestion that detention without trial is a foreign substance the Republic ingested and expelled. On the record, the Republic manufactured the substance domestically, refined it domestically, and, to its credit, also manufactured the refusal of it domestically, in the High Courts that held for the detenus, in the Commission that documented the abuse, and in the electorate that ended the regime. Both the capacity for closure and the capacity for correction sit inside the same constitutional tradition, which is why the tradition's texts, rather than its heroes or its villains, are the reliable objects of study.

The comparative frame also explains a silence in the Indian record that foreign readers notice. Jurisdictions that have passed through a detention crisis of this order commonly produce, afterwards, a jurisprudence of reparation: judgments awarding compensation for unlawful detention, official apologies, lustration of the officials responsible. India produced the Commission and the repeal, and then, in the main, stopped. No general compensation scheme for Emergency detenus was enacted by the Union, no prosecutions of consequence followed the Commission's findings, and the officials named in its volumes largely resumed their careers. What recognition came arrived late and from the states rather than the Centre: in the decades after the Emergency, several state governments instituted honour pensions for persons detained under MISA and the Defence of India Rules during the Emergency, Madhya Pradesh's Loktantra Senani pension for the fighters for democracy being the best known of the schemes, a monthly payment administered by the state as a mark of honour rather than as damages assessed for a wrong. The distinction matters to the families who receive such pensions and to the argument about the period. A pension honours the detenu's resistance; it does not record the state's liability. The Republic's settlement with its Emergency detenus was therefore honorific where it was not silent, and a researcher who asks the compensation question, as the FAQ below does, must be answered in those terms rather than with a scheme that never existed.

## The End of MISA: Release, Repeal and Removal

The statutory death of MISA took two instruments and a constitutional amendment, and setting them out in order closes the factual record. The first was political and has been described: the revocation of the Emergency on 21 March 1977 and the release of the detenus, which emptied the Act of its population. The second was the Janata government's repeal. The Maintenance of Internal Security (Repeal) Act, 1978, Act 27 of 1978, repealed the parent Act outright, the repeal taking effect on 3 August 1978, and with it fell the entire amended structure, Section 16A included. No saving for a successor regime was attempted and no part of the text was carried forward into another statute by reference; the repeal was of the whole Act, which is why accounts of the Act's survival in some transformed sense are mistaken. The third instrument removed the constitutional residue. The Constitution (Forty fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 omitted entry 92 from the Ninth Schedule, so that the schedule ceased to carry a statute that no longer existed, and the same amendment rebuilt several of the constitutional positions the Emergency had occupied, among them the non suspendability of Articles 20 and 21 discussed above.

### What did the Forty fourth Amendment do to MISA's Ninth Schedule entry?

The Forty fourth Amendment Act, 1978 omitted entry 92 from the Ninth Schedule, deleting the Maintenance of Internal Security Act from the list of statutes that Article 31B protected against fundamental rights challenge, a tidying of the constitutional text that accompanied the repeal of the Act itself and the wider reversal of the Emergency settlement.

Why repeal outright, rather than retain a repaired Act, is a question the FAQ below takes up in terms of the Janata choice; in legislative terms the answer visible in the record is that repair would have required Parliament to rewrite nearly every operative provision the Emergency had touched, against the fresh memory of what each provision had done, and that the political mandate of 1977 was for repudiation rather than renovation. There is also a drafting reality. A statute that has been amended into a different instrument, with disapplications, deeming provisions and retrospective recastings layered across its sections, is a poor vehicle for a new settlement even when the will to settle exists. The clean page of 1980 was made possible by the clean repeal of 1978, and the sequence, repeal first and re enactment later under a different name and politics, is the form the Indian Parliament has generally used when a statute's text has become inseparable from its disgrace.

### Why does MISA still appear in political argument long after its repeal?

MISA survives in political argument because the name became an epithet for detention without trial at its widest use, and an epithet needs no statutory life: the word is deployed against later detention laws and governments as a compressed verdict, which is why the legal content of the actual statute keeps being displaced by shorthand.

That afterlife has a cost, and it falls on accuracy. When MISA functions as a synonym for oppression in general, its specific history, the respectable 1971 text, the five identifiable steps, the nine High Courts, the Commission's figures, disappears into the synonym, and the lessons the specific history teaches become unavailable. The purpose of an account organised around the four-step closure is to make the epithet unnecessary: a reader equipped with the sequence can say exactly what would have to happen to a later statute before the comparison with MISA was earned, and can say it in a form that a defender of that statute must answer step by step rather than dismiss as rhetoric.

## Anatomy of an Order: One Detention Under Two Regimes

The difference between the 1971 statute and its Emergency form is most exactly seen by following a single hypothetical detention through both regimes, using only the procedures the texts prescribe. Take a district organiser of an opposition party, arrested in his home town on the satisfaction of the District Magistrate that his activities are prejudicial to the maintenance of public order. Nothing in the example is exotic; the Commission's files contain the pattern in thousands of instances, and the example's ordinariness is its point.

Under the Act as enacted in 1971, the order is only the first document in a sequence that generates paper at every step, and each sheet of paper is a point at which the detention can fail. The District Magistrate must report the order forthwith to the State Government with the grounds, and the order dies within the statutory period unless the State Government approves it, a decision a second authority must actively take. Within five days, or exceptionally within fifteen for reasons recorded in writing, the detenu must be told the grounds, and from the moment of that communication he holds the raw material of his own defence: he can make a representation to the Government pointing to mistaken identity, to the staleness of the material, to the absence of any act answering the section's description. Within thirty days the Government must place the grounds and the representation before an Advisory Board of High Court calibre, which reports within ten weeks, and if the Board finds no sufficient cause the Government must release him forthwith, whether it wishes to or not. If the Board finds sufficient cause and the order is confirmed, the detention still carries its own expiry: twelve months from the date of detention, at the end of which the authority of the order is spent. At every stage a court remains available in the background, testing the order against the statute on the classic grounds, irrelevant material, non application of mind, mala fides, and the detenu's lawyers, who may not appear before the Board, may appear before the court. The system is closed, paper based and far from generous, and detenus under it spent long months in prison on thin material. But it is a system with joints, and pressure applied at a joint, a defective ground, a late communication, a divided Board, could and did release men.

Under the Act as it stood in, say, January 1976, the same arrest produces a different documentary world. The District Magistrate's order, once the declaration machinery of Section 16A is engaged and the State Government has declared the detention necessary for dealing effectively with the emergency, carries the detenu into the disapplied zone. There are no grounds to communicate, because Section 8 does not apply, and the material on the file is confidential by force of sub-section (9), deemed a matter of State whose disclosure the law itself forbids. There is no representation to make, because the right to make one fell with the same sections, and the four monthly reconsideration that replaces the Board is conducted by the Government on its own material, expressly without hearing the detenu or showing him anything. There is no Board, because Sections 9 to 12 do not apply either. There is no constitutional challenge to the section under which all this is done, because the Act stands in the Ninth Schedule. And there is, after April 1976, no habeas corpus petition that a court may entertain, because the Supreme Court has held that the detenu has no standing to move one while the Article 359 order operates. The documentary sequence that in 1971 generated five successive occasions of scrutiny generates, in 1976, a single self confirming file. The man's release, when it comes in March 1977, comes from outside the legal process altogether, from an election. Worked through at the level of one file, the four-step closure stops being a metaphor about a period and becomes a description of a procedure, and the description is checkable against the amended text by any reader with the Act in hand, which is the standard this series sets for an analytical claim.

The example also shows where responsibility for the transformation sits, and it does not sit in one place. The district officer who signed the order acted under a power Parliament had given him in 1971 and never withdrew. The declaration that carried the detenu into the disapplied zone was made under a section Parliament inserted in 1975. The confidentiality that sealed the file was enacted in the same amending sequence. The constitutional immunity was conferred by a constitutional amendment carried by special majorities. The closed forum was the work of a court. A narrative that assigns the whole transformation to a single villain, or to a single institution, fails the file level test, because the file shows five hands at five moments, each able to have acted otherwise, none able to plead that the step it took was compelled by the step before. That distribution of responsibility is the deepest reason the period resists summary, and the strongest reason for teaching it through its texts rather than through its reputations.

## How the Record Is Read: Three Working Uses

An account built on sources of very different kinds has to say how each kind is to be handled, because the MISA literature mixes them without warning, and the mix is the source of most of its errors. Three classes of reader work on this record professionally, and each has a discipline the others can borrow.

The historian's materials are the statute book, the Commission's volumes, the parliamentary record and the memoir literature, in descending order of trust for questions of law and in a more complicated order for questions of motive. The statute book settles what the law was on any date, and it settles it completely, provided the reader works from the amended text rather than from a summary: the difference between MISA in 1973 and MISA in 1976 is invisible in most summaries and is the whole story in the Gazette texts. The Commission's volumes settle most of what can be settled about operation, subject to the discount proper to an inquiry appointed by the victors, a discount the Commission's own documentary method largely earns back. The memoirs, of ministers, officials, detenus and judges, are evidence of what their authors wished believed and, handled with that caution, evidence of a great deal else: the detenu memoirs of the period, written by men and women who emerged to govern the country, fixed the public memory of detention long before the official record was readable, and a historian who ignores them cannot explain why the epithet outlived the Act. The parliamentary record is the thinnest source of the four for the Emergency years themselves, for reasons the detentions explain, and correspondingly rich for 1971, 1977 and 1978, the years in which the chamber argued about detention with its full membership present or freshly released.

The journalist's problem is different: compression under deadline, against a readership for whom MISA is a word in a headline. The record suggests three working rules. Date the Act correctly, because the Emergency law error reverses the story's meaning, as the section on factual disputes showed. Name the instrument in every detention claim, MISA, the Defence of India Rules or COFEPOSA, because the instruments carried different safeguards and different populations, and the blended figure misleads in both directions. And treat every precise figure as a claim about a source: the Commission derived count can be cited as such in a clause, and the citation, rather than false precision, is what separates reporting on this record from the commemorative writing that fills the anniversary pages each June with numbers nobody can trace. A retrospective that observes those three rules will be more accurate than most of the shelf, because most of the shelf observes none of them.

The researcher's use is the comparative one, and it is where the four-step closure earns its keep as an analytical instrument rather than a label. The closure converts a historical episode into a test that can be run on any detention statute, Indian or foreign, of any period: are grounds communicated, can disclosure be compelled anywhere in the system, is the statute itself open to constitutional challenge, and does a court remedy lie against a non compliant order. A statute that passes all four limbs is a constrained detention law, however severe its use in a given case. A statute that fails one limb has a located weakness, and the failure predicts where abuse, if it comes, will hide: behind the withheld ground, inside the confidential file, beyond the immunised text, or outside the closed forum. Run against MISA, the test gives the cleanest result in the Indian record, four failures in sequence between 1975 and 1976, and run against the successor statutes it gives the mixed results the comparative section described, which is what a test is for. The model also disciplines the rhetoric of equivalence in both directions, since a statute failing no limb cannot be called MISA without emptying the word, and a statute failing two limbs cannot be defended by pointing to the two it passes.

## What Is Settled, and What Remains Contested

The framework for controversy and critique ends by separating the settled from the contested, and the separation, on the MISA record, is unusually clean.

Settled, as matters of fact established by the legislative and official record: the Act was enacted in 1971 as Act 26 of 1971 for the security conditions of that year, in a form containing the standard Article 22 safeguards of grounds within days, representation, Advisory Board review with power to compel release, and a twelve month maximum. Between June 1975 and the middle of 1976 those safeguards were removed for declared detenus by Section 16A and its confidentiality sub sections, the Act was placed in the Ninth Schedule as entry 92 by the Thirty ninth Amendment, and the habeas corpus remedy was closed by the Supreme Court's majority in the Jabalpur case against the view of nine High Courts. Detentions under MISA and the Defence of India Rules together numbered 110,806 on the count the Shah Commission's work circulated, 34,988 of them under MISA. The Commission found that the detention provisions were abused to damage political opponents and that arrest and release decisions were governed by political considerations. The Emergency ended by electoral verdict in March 1977, the Act was repealed by Act 27 of 1978, and entry 92 was omitted from the Ninth Schedule by the Forty fourth Amendment.

Contested, as matters of fact on which a careful reader must hold a range rather than a point: the completeness of the state returns beneath the detention aggregates; the precise distribution of the MISA detained population between its political, economic and public order classes, which the classification records describe unevenly; and the weight to be given to particular episodes within the Commission's narrative where testimony conflicted. None of these uncertainties touches the settled core above, and any treatment that uses them to cast doubt on the core has confused the margin with the record.

Contested, as matters of value on which the record cannot adjudicate: whether preventive detention should exist in a constitutional democracy at all; whether the failure of the Emergency years is charged to the statute book or to the political order that operated it; and whether the election of 1977 vindicates the system's safeguards or exposes their contingency. On the second of these, this account has stated its own position, that the statutory thesis explains more of the pattern than the political thesis, and the position is offered as a verdict reached on the evidence of the sequence, not as a finding of fact.

The transferable lesson is the durable residue. Legislatures guard liberty less often by refusing detention powers than by refusing the steps that hollow them: the amendment that disapplies a statute's own safeguards, the confidentiality clause that converts a file into a secret, the schedule that lifts a statute out of constitutional reach, and the acquiescence that lets a remedy lapse unexercised. Each of those steps, in the MISA record, arrived wearing ordinary legislative dress, moved by ministers, debated in a thinned chamber, printed in the Gazette. The four-step closure is worth naming because what has a name can be recognised while it is still a sequence of small, lawful, reversible acts, rather than afterwards, when it has become a period with a name of its own. MISA was an ordinary detention law for four years and a byword for two generations. The distance between those two conditions was travelled in about twelve months, in the open, by amendment. That is the controversy, and on the evidence, it is also the conclusion.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was MISA and why was it enacted?

MISA was the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971, Act 26 of 1971, a central preventive detention statute enacted by Parliament and assented to on 2 July 1971, replacing an ordinance of 7 May 1971. It empowered the Central and State Governments, and specified district officers, to detain a person on satisfaction that detention was necessary to prevent conduct prejudicial to the defence, security or public order of India, or to the maintenance of essential supplies and services. It was enacted in the setting of the East Pakistan crisis, a refugee influx, approaching war and the Naxalite insurgency, as the successor to the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, which had lapsed at the end of 1969.

### Q: How was MISA used during the Emergency?

During the Emergency proclaimed on 25 June 1975, MISA became the principal instrument for detaining the political opposition, journalists, trade unionists, student activists and others the administration treated as threats, alongside large numbers detained for economic offences and public order grounds. Its use was transformed by amendment: Section 16A disapplied the provisions requiring grounds to be communicated, representations to be heard and Advisory Boards to review, and made the detention file confidential by law. Detainees were therefore held without being told the case against them and without any external review, in many cases for the duration of the Emergency. The Shah Commission later found that arrest and release decisions under the Act were governed in many instances by political considerations.

### Q: Why was MISA placed in the Ninth Schedule?

The Constitution (Thirty ninth Amendment) Act, 1975 inserted the Maintenance of Internal Security Act into the Ninth Schedule as entry 92. Under Article 31B, a statute listed in that Schedule could not be struck down for inconsistency with the fundamental rights in Part III of the Constitution. The Government of the day placed MISA there in August 1975, weeks into the Emergency, so that the Act, including the Emergency amendments then being made to it, would stand beyond challenge on fundamental rights grounds while mass detentions were under way. The entry was omitted by the Forty fourth Amendment Act, 1978, after the Act itself had been repealed.

### Q: How widely was MISA applied against political opponents?

On the count the Shah Commission's work circulated, 34,988 persons were detained under MISA during the Emergency, out of 110,806 detentions under MISA and the Defence of India Rules together. The political class of detenus reached from Jayaprakash Narayan, Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee and L. K. Advani down through party workers, union organisers, journalists, students and lawyers. States classified detenus by category, with prominent leaders held separately from those detained for economic offences. The Commission found that decisions to arrest and release were in many cases taken on political considerations favourable to the ruling party, which is the official record's answer to how political the application was.

### Q: What was Section 16A of MISA?

Section 16A, headed special provisions for dealing with emergency, was inserted into the Maintenance of Internal Security Act by the Amendment Act 39 of 1975, effective from 29 June 1975. Where the Government declared a detention necessary for dealing effectively with the emergency, Section 16A disapplied Sections 8 to 12 of the Act, removing the duties to communicate grounds, to receive the detenu's representation and to place the case before an Advisory Board. It substituted reconsideration by the detaining Government itself at intervals of not more than four months, conducted without disclosing material to the detenu, and its later sub sections made the grounds and all underlying material confidential by law and barred their disclosure to anyone.

### Q: Who repealed MISA and when?

MISA was repealed by the Janata government through the Maintenance of Internal Security (Repeal) Act, 1978, Act 27 of 1978, the repeal taking effect on 3 August 1978. The political decision belonged to 1977, when the Janata Party won the general election held after the Emergency and took office committed to dismantling the Emergency's legal apparatus, releasing the detenus and revoking the orders. The statute itself, which unlike its 1950 predecessor carried no sunset clause, remained on the book until Parliament repealed it the following year. Accounts that date the repeal to 1977 are dating the election and the decision; the repealing Act is an Act of 1978.

### Q: What did the Shah Commission find about Emergency detentions?

The Shah Commission of Inquiry, appointed by the Janata government in 1977 under Justice J. C. Shah, found from the Government's own records that the provisions of the Maintenance of Internal Security Act and the Defence of India Rules were not followed in operation but were abused in order to damage political opponents. It recorded that decisions to arrest and to release particular persons were taken entirely on political considerations intended to favour the ruling party, and it documented detention orders made in batches, on transmitted lists, with formulaic grounds, in some instances backdated. It also found that the Emergency itself had been decided by the Prime Minister alone, without Cabinet consultation and without a law and order crisis to justify it.

### Q: Is there any law like MISA in force today?

No statute in force reproduces MISA in its Emergency form, because the defining feature of that form was the combination of features no successor combines. Preventive detention statutes exist, the National Security Act, 1980 chief among them, together with state public safety and goonda Acts and the economic detention statute COFEPOSA. Those laws retain the Article 22 architecture of communicated grounds, representation and Advisory Board review, under which orders are regularly challenged and quashed in the High Courts. None has been amended to disapply its own safeguards for a declared class of detenu, none stands in the Ninth Schedule, and Articles 20 and 21 can no longer be suspended in an emergency. The resemblance is in the foundation, not in the closure.

### Q: Was MISA passed for the Emergency, or did it already exist before 1975?

MISA already existed for four years before the Emergency. It is Act 26 of 1971, assented to on 2 July 1971, and it replaced an ordinance promulgated on 7 May 1971 in the setting of the East Pakistan crisis and the Naxalite insurgency. It was enacted as the successor to the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, which had lapsed at the end of 1969. Describing MISA as an Emergency law is the commonest factual error in writing about the period. The Emergency did not create the Act; it amended it, inserting Section 16A in 1975 and placing it in the Ninth Schedule, and that sequence, an ordinary statute hardened after enactment, is the centre of its history.

### Q: Did MISA allow a person to be detained without any trial?

Yes, because that is what preventive detention means in Indian law. A MISA detention order was not a charge, required no prosecution, no evidence tested in court and no conviction; it rested on the detaining authority's satisfaction that the person, if left free, would act in a manner prejudicial to the grounds the Act named. The detenu was not an accused person awaiting trial but a person imprisoned to forestall anticipated conduct. Under the original Act the absence of a trial was balanced by communicated grounds, a representation and Advisory Board review. During the Emergency, Section 16A removed those balances for declared detenus, leaving detention without trial and also without disclosure or external review.

### Q: Why do different sources give different numbers for Emergency detentions?

The figures differ because the sources count different things. The count the Shah Commission's work circulated is 110,806 detentions in total, of which 34,988 were under MISA and 75,818 under the Defence of India Rules. Accounts that attribute the combined total to MISA alone roughly triple the statute's figure. Accounts that count arrests rather than detentions, or that add detentions under COFEPOSA, produce larger totals. Accounts that count only prominent political prisoners produce very small ones. The underlying aggregate was also assembled from state returns of uneven quality. A figure for this period is usable only when it names the instrument counted, the class counted and the source it descends from.

### Q: Did a MISA detenu have the right to make a representation against the detention order during the Emergency?

Under the Act as enacted, yes: Section 8 required the detaining authority to afford the detenu the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate Government. For the class of detenus covered by a declaration under Section 16A, that right was removed during the Emergency, because Section 16A disapplied Section 8 altogether and expressly permitted the Government to conduct its periodic reconsideration of a detention without affording the detenu any opportunity of making a representation. The detenu under a declaration therefore had no statutory channel through which to answer a case he had also not been shown, which is the precise combination that distinguishes the Emergency regime from ordinary preventive detention.

### Q: Did the Advisory Board review MISA detentions during the Emergency?

For ordinary detentions under the Act, the Advisory Board machinery of Sections 9 to 12 remained in the text. For detenus covered by a declaration under Section 16A, who formed the politically significant class, Advisory Board review was abolished in practice, because Section 16A declared that Sections 8 to 12 should not apply to them. In place of the Board, a body of persons of High Court calibre whose negative opinion obliged the Government to release, the amended Act substituted reconsideration by the appropriate Government itself at intervals not exceeding four months. The distinction between review by an external body with power to compel release and reconsideration by the detainer is the institutional heart of the Emergency amendments.

### Q: Which prominent opposition leaders were detained under MISA?

The leadership of the opposition movement was detained in the first hours and days of the Emergency and at intervals afterwards. Those held under MISA and the parallel detention instruments included Jayaprakash Narayan, the movement's leader; Morarji Desai, who later became Prime Minister; Atal Bihari Vajpayee and L. K. Advani of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh; George Fernandes, who evaded arrest for a period before being held; and figures who had broken with the ruling party from inside it, including Chandrashekhar and Mohan Dharia. Below that level the detained population ran into the tens of thousands: state and district party workers, trade unionists, student activists, journalists and lawyers who represented detenus.

### Q: How was MISA different from the Defence of India Rules under which people were also detained?

MISA was a permanent parliamentary statute of general application, in force from 1971, authorising preventive detention on the satisfaction of the Central Government, State Governments and specified officers, with its own safeguard code. The Defence of India Rules were subordinate legislation made under the Defence of India Act for the purposes of a proclaimed emergency, and they supplied a parallel and wider apparatus of controls, including censorship and detention, for the duration of that emergency. In the Commission derived count, 75,818 persons were detained under the Rules against 34,988 under MISA. Confusing the two inflates MISA's numbers and obscures the fact that MISA detenus covered by Section 16A stood in the weakest legal position of any detained class.

### Q: How did the government of the day defend the Emergency amendments to MISA?

The defence, as made by the Prime Minister, Indira Gandhi, and the Congress leadership, was that the country faced organised disorder aimed at displacing an elected government by extra parliamentary means, that the constitutional emergency provisions existed for exactly such a situation, and that detention powers with their ordinary safeguards would have been unusable against the networks involved, since grounds disclosed could expose sources and representations and Board hearings would have turned detention into litigation the government would fight case by case while the disorder continued. The amendments were presented as temporary, confined to the emergency's duration, and subject to periodic government review. Critics answered that each element of that defence described the safeguards themselves as the problem to be removed.

### Q: What safeguards did the MISA that Parliament passed in 1971 contain?

The Act of 1971 carried the safeguard architecture of Article 22 in statutory form. Section 8 required the grounds of detention to be communicated to the detenu ordinarily within five days and in no case later than fifteen days, with facts against the public interest to disclose excepted, and required that he be given the earliest opportunity to make a representation. Sections 9 to 12 required every case to go within thirty days before an Advisory Board of persons of High Court calibre, which reported within ten weeks and whose finding of no sufficient cause obliged the Government to release. Section 13 capped detention under a confirmed order at twelve months. The Emergency amendments are intelligible only against that baseline.

### Q: Why did the Janata government repeal MISA outright instead of keeping an amended version?

Repeal expressed the verdict the Janata government had been elected to deliver. The Emergency amendments had not added a separable chapter to the Act; they had disapplied its operative safeguards, recast its machinery retrospectively and carried its name into the Ninth Schedule, so that repair would have meant rewriting nearly every provision that mattered against the fresh evidence of the Shah Commission's inquiry. Repealing the whole Act also carried the symbolic clarity the government wanted: the statute under which the opposition had been imprisoned would cease to exist rather than be renovated by its former detenus. When Parliament legislated again on preventive detention in 1980, it enacted a new statute, the National Security Act, on a clean page.

### Q: How long could a person remain detained under MISA during the Emergency?

The original Act capped detention under a confirmed order at twelve months under Section 13. During the Emergency that cap lost its safeguard content for declared detenus, because Section 16A removed the Advisory Board confirmation to which the cap was keyed and substituted the Government's own periodic reconsideration, while the Section 16A regime itself was extended to run for twenty four months from 25 June 1975. In practice, detenus held in the first days of the Emergency who were not released earlier remained in detention until the general releases that followed the Emergency's revocation on 21 March 1977, a period approaching twenty one months, without any external body having examined their cases.

### Q: Did Emergency detenus detained under MISA receive any compensation or official recognition afterwards?

No general compensation scheme for Emergency detenus was enacted by the Union, and no damages were assessed for the detentions as a class; the Shah Commission inquired and reported, but its findings led neither to a compensation code nor to prosecutions of consequence. Recognition, where it came, arrived later and from the states. Several state governments instituted honour pensions for persons detained under MISA and the Defence of India Rules during the Emergency, of which Madhya Pradesh's Loktantra Senani pension for those who had fought for democracy is the best known. Such a pension honours the detenu's resistance as a public service; it is not an assessment of the state's liability for the detention itself.
