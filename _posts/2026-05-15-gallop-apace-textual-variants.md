The Gallop apace variants are the second great single-speech case in the early texts of Romeo and Juliet, and the harder of the two. Juliet's wedding-night soliloquy, the speech that opens at "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1), survives in three early witnesses that do not tell the same story about it. The first quarto of 1597 prints four lines of the speech and then brings the Nurse in. The second quarto of 1599 prints the whole design, thirty-three lines of invocation, impatience and prophecy, and inside its sixth line it prints the most argued-over word in the play: "That runawayes eyes may wincke" (3.2.6). The First Folio of 1623, set from a later quarto in the same line of descent, repeats the word and changes almost nothing else in the speech except one verb form. Every modern reader therefore meets the soliloquy through an editor's decisions, and the decisions are not decoration. Point the quarto's word one way and a fleeing sun shuts its eyes so that Romeo can come unseen; point it another way and the streets of Verona must empty of wanderers first. Replace the word, as some nineteenth-century editors did, and the line is about rumour, or surprise, or anything except what the quartos print.

![Juliet's Gallop apace soliloquy and its variants across the early texts - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-40.webp)

The verdict of this study can be stated at the outset, because everything that follows is its evidence. The quarto word is sound and should stand. No emendation proposed in three centuries of trying has earned its place in the text: each buys a smoother sense by abandoning the letters the witnesses agree on, and the letters, once the sentence is read with its own first four lines, yield a sense that needs no rescue. The word should be pointed as a singular possessive, "runaway's", and the runaway is the sun, Phoebus, the fugitive driver whose steeds Juliet has just ordered home to their lodging. His eyes wink shut, in the period sense of wink as sleep, as his chariot goes down; that closing of the day's one great eye is the condition Juliet demands before Romeo can leap to her arms untalked of and unseen. The Folio's single substantive gift to the speech, "grown" for the second quarto's "grow" at 3.2.15, should be accepted, as most editors accept it, because there the transmitted reading limps and the Folio's form completes an idiom. Everything else in the speech descends from the second quarto, and the first quarto's four-line stump, precious as evidence about performance, has no authority over the wording of a speech it barely prints. A companion study on this site reads the same soliloquy as an utterance of desire, [Juliet's 'Gallop Apace' Speech: Desire in Verse](/2022/07/15/juliet-gallop-apace-soliloquy-desire/); the present study reads it as a document, a thing handed down by printers, and asks what exactly has been handed down.

## The Verdict in Brief: Why the Quarto Word Should Stand

Three questions decide the fate of the crux, and each has a short answer that the long sections below defend. First, is the transmitted word nonsense? A reading may be emended when it cannot be construed, and only then; conjecture is the surgery of last resort, not a styling choice. "Runawayes" can be construed. The word "runaway" was live in Shakespeare's own vocabulary for a thing that flees: Lorenzo in The Merchant of Venice warns that "the close night doth play the runaway" (2.6.40), night itself cast as a fugitive that will not wait. A substantive that the playwright uses elsewhere as a figure for flight is not a compositor's gibberish, and a possessive built on it is regular English of the period. Second, whose eyes must close for the sentence to work? The clause gives the purpose of Night's curtain: the eyes are to wink so that Romeo can come "untalked of and unseen" (3.2.7). The eyes belong to whatever watches the world by day, and the speech has spent its first four lines dismissing exactly one watcher, the sun, whose horses are being driven to their lodging in the west. Third, what do the witnesses permit? The second quarto and the Folio agree on the word; the third and fourth quartos, reprints in the same line, agree as well. The first quarto, the only witness that might have offered an independent check, does not print the line at all. Against that unanimity an emender must show not that another word reads better, but that the agreed word cannot be what the author wrote. Nobody has shown it. The parade of replacements, rumourers and unawares and rude day's and Nature's eye, is a history of ingenuity failing the same examination: each conjecture explains the sentence by ceasing to resemble the evidence.

The editorial principle at stake generalises beyond one line, and the brief for this study names it as the complication to be faced rather than avoided. Editors decide between rival readings by weighing authority against sense, document against judgment, and the whole craft lies in knowing which consideration governs which kind of variant. Where the witnesses divide, authority arbitrates; where they agree, only demonstrated impossibility licenses departure; where the copy-text limps in grammar or idiom and a dependent witness offers the completed form, judgment may accept the gift without surrendering the copy-text's general authority. The speech supplies a clean test of each rule. At the crux the witnesses agree and the sense is recoverable, so the word stands. At "grow" and "grown" the copy-text's form is the weaker idiom and the Folio, though a derivative book with no independent authority for this play, offers the form the sentence wants, so the gift is taken and recorded. At the opening quatrain the witnesses genuinely divide, mansion against lodging, send against bring, and there authority decides for the second quarto, whose text of the play rests on copy close to the author's papers, against a first quarto whose version of the scene is a compressed performance-shaped remnant. One speech, three kinds of variant, three different operations of judgment: that is why this soliloquy, and not some larger canvas, is the right size of case for watching editors work.

A word about method before the evidence is opened, since the comparison this study's title promises is of an unusual shape. Most textual comparison in this play is horizontal: two full texts of a scene laid side by side, reading against reading, as the Queen Mab case allows. The Gallop apace comparison is vertical. One witness gives the whole speech, another gives its first four lines and its staging, and the third gives the whole speech at one remove of copying. Comparing a text with a stump is not a poorer version of comparing two texts; it is a different science, in which the short witness is interrogated not for its readings but for its behaviour, what it keeps, what it flattens, where it stops. The behaviour, faithfully read, certifies the long witness's character: a text whose opening a performance tradition could remember only in flattened form, while the full pattern survived in print, is a text that lived two lives, and the editor's task is to keep the lives distinct. Every section below observes that distinction. The first quarto is believed about the stage and disbelieved about the letter; the second is believed about the letter and interrogated, at one syllable, about its completeness; the Folio is believed about neither until its one gift is weighed. Readers who keep those standings in mind will find the verdicts at each crux less like preferences and more like what they are, the output of a consistent weighing of unlike evidence.

## One Speech, Three Witnesses: What the Early Texts Print

The documentary facts are stark, and they should be set down before any interpretation softens them. The second quarto, printed in 1599 and advertised on its title page as "newly corrected, augmented, and amended", gives Juliet the full soliloquy: four lines driving the sun's horses home, the appeal to Night to spread her close curtain, the falconry of the hooded blood, the great central invocation "Come, night; come, Romeo; come, thou day in night" (3.2.17), the fantasy of Romeo cut out in little stars (3.2.21 to 25), and the closing simile of the child with new robes who may not wear them (3.2.28 to 31), before the Nurse's entrance breaks the reverie and Juliet turns with "O, here comes my nurse" (3.2.31). The Folio of 1623 gives the same speech at the same length, since its text of this play descends from the quarto line, through the third quarto of 1609 and the fourth of 1622, with the Folio's compositors adding their own accidentals and one substantive variant of consequence. The first quarto of 1597 gives four lines.

The two middle witnesses deserve a sentence of their own, because editors cite them in the speech's apparatus and readers deserve to know what their agreement is worth. The third quarto of 1609 is a page-for-page reprint of the second, issued from the rights that had passed to John Smethwick in the transfers of 1607, and it transmits the soliloquy without substantive change, crux included. The fourth quarto of 1622, likewise Smethwick's, reprints the third with a corrector's hand at work in places, and it was a copy of this book, annotated or not, that the Folio's compositors set from for the play. For this speech the chain means that Q3, Q4 and F1 form a single stem of descent from Q2, three books and one authority. Their unanimous "runawayes" is therefore one testimony repeated, not three testimonies agreeing, and the apparatus that lists them in a row flatters the evidence. The point generalises across the play's textual criticism and disciplines the present study's verdict: the crux rests on Q2 alone, and Q2's credit must carry it, as a bank's single signature must carry a note. That the credit holds is the finding the following sections test from every side. "Gallop apace you fierie footed steedes", "To Phoebus mansion, such a Waggoner", "As Phaeton, would quickly bring you thether", "And send in cloudie night immediately": so the speech runs in the earliest printed text, and at that point the quarto's direction intervenes, "Enter Nurse wringing her hands, with the ladder of cordes in her lap", and the scene is off into the catastrophe of the news. Twenty-nine lines of the fullest soliloquy Juliet ever speaks, including every line posterity quotes from it, are simply not there.

### How many lines does the second quarto give the soliloquy?

The second quarto gives the soliloquy thirty-three lines, from "Gallop apace" (3.2.1) to the child-and-robes simile and the turn to the entering Nurse, a span modern editions number 3.2.1 to 33. Against that span the first quarto's four lines stand in a ratio of roughly one to eight, the sharpest single-speech compression in the play's early texts.

The asymmetry matters because it reverses the usual relation between the two quartos. Across the play as a whole, the first quarto runs to roughly two-thirds of the second quarto's length, compressing everywhere but preserving the skeleton of every scene; a reader collating the two texts scene by scene, as the companion study of [Q1 vs Q2: What the Differences Reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/) does, grows used to a Q1 that paraphrases, transposes and trims by lines at a time. In this scene the compression is of another order. The soliloquy is not trimmed but amputated, reduced to its launching quatrain, and the cut falls exactly where the speech turns from public myth, the sun's horses and Phaeton's recklessness, to the private matter of the wedding night: the curtain, the hooded blood, the match played for a pair of stainless maidenhoods, the body imagined as a bought but unpossessed mansion. Whatever agency shortened the speech, an abridging company, a reporting actor's memory, a scribe writing for the road, the cut has a shape, and the shape is decorum as much as economy. The four lines kept are the four lines a spectator could repeat in a tavern. The twenty-nine lines dropped are the speech.

Two consequences follow for the textual study. The first is that the crux at 3.2.6 is a single-lineage crux. For the Queen Mab speech, the parallel case in this series, both quartos print the set piece and their disagreement is itself evidence; here the second quarto's word stands alone, supported only by its own descendants, and the editor's usual recourse, asking the independent witness, is unavailable. The second consequence is that the first quarto's evidence about this scene is evidence about staging, not wording. Its stage direction, fuller and more theatrical than the second quarto's spare "Enter Nurse with cords", shows the Nurse entering with the rope ladder visible in her lap, the stage property that embodies the wedding night's machinery, and its Juliet passes from invocation to catastrophe in four lines, a timing that tells a director how fast the scene can be made to turn. What Q1 cannot do is vote on readings it does not contain. The editor of the speech is therefore thrown back on the second quarto's authority and on the discipline of conjecture, which is where the discipline is most severely tested.

## The Four Lines the First Quarto Keeps, Word by Word

Because the first quarto keeps so little, what it keeps can be examined entire, and its four lines disagree with the second quarto at three of their four line-endings and in the grain of nearly every phrase. The disagreements are the classic profile of the 1597 text: not the divergences of an author revising, which tend to concentrate in conception, but the divergences of a text transmitted through performance and memory, which scatter across wording while the sense and the metrical frame hold firm. Set the two openings side by side and the pattern instructs.

The first line is common property: "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" stands in both quartos with only spelling between them, Q1's "fierie footed steedes" against Q2's modernised descendants. The divergence begins at the destination. Q2 sends the horses "Towards Phoebus' lodging", the sun's home at the western edge of the world, the stable where the day's team is unharnessed. Q1 reads "To Phoebus mansion", a grander and vaguer noun, the kind of substitution a memory makes when it keeps the god and the upward register but loses the precise domestic word. "Lodging" is the better reading on every ground an editor can invoke: it is the harder and more specific word, it belongs to the speech's governing contrast between the sun's public house and Juliet's private chamber, and it prepares the later figure of the "mansion of a love" (3.2.26) by setting a lesser lodging against the greater purchase. A revising author might trade lodging for mansion; a reporting memory trades the marked word for the unmarked one as a matter of course. The direction of error runs one way, and it runs from Q2 toward Q1.

The third line divides the witnesses over Phaeton's driving. Q2: "As Phaeton would whip you to the west", a line with a whip-crack in it, the boy driver lashing the team toward the one point of the compass the sentence needs. Q1: "As Phaeton, would quickly bring you thether", which keeps the god and the haste but replaces the instrument and the destination with a neutral verb and a pointing adverb. The Q1 line is intelligible and even metrical, which is precisely what makes it diagnostic: it is the line as it survives in a head that remembers the myth and the rhythm but has let the exact words go. "Whip" and "west" alliterate across the line break with "wagoner" above them, a woven sound-pattern of the kind this speech sustains everywhere; Q1's line has no such weave. Editors print Q2, and no serious edition has taken "thether" into the text, but the variant earns its place in the apparatus because it shows what kind of witness Q1 is at the very moment its testimony runs out.

The fourth line completes the quatrain with the smallest and most eloquent variant of the three. Q2: "And bring in cloudy night immediately." Q1: "And send in cloudie night immediately." One verb differs. "Bring" belongs to the speech's system of summons and arrival, the same verb that will govern "come, night; come, Romeo" and the Nurse who "brings news" (3.2.33); Juliet commands the horses to fetch night in, as a servant brings in candles. "Send" is the verb of dispatch at a distance, flatter and more distant, and it breaks the chain by which the whole soliloquy imagines night as something carried into the room. Again the variant is Q1's characteristic move, the near-synonym substituted under pressure of performance or recollection, and again the editor follows Q2 without a tremor. But the accumulation instructs: three substantive variants in four lines, all of one type, all degradations of a more patterned original. Whoever stands behind Q1 had the speech by its sense and its music, not by its letter. That is a finding about this scene that the whole-text theories of Q1's origin, memorial reconstruction in the line of H. R. Hoppe's 1948 monograph on the bad quarto of this play, theatrical abridgement in Kathleen Irace's account of the short quartos as adapted performance texts, must each digest, and it is why the four lines, for all their poverty, are evidence of the first rank about the speech's life in the theatre. They are no evidence at all about the wording of the twenty-nine lines they omit, and the editor who quotes them in the crux's apparatus, as some older discussions did by implication, is citing a witness who was not in the room for that line.

A last observation on the quatrain's variants concerns the direction of their sense, since editors are sometimes accused of preferring Q2 out of loyalty to its general credit rather than from the evidence of the passage. Here the passage supplies the proof internally. Q2's destination, "Phoebus' lodging", is picked up twenty-four lines later by the "mansion of a love" (3.2.26), the sun's modest lodging answered by the bride's grand purchase, an architecture of housed and housing figures that spans the speech and binds its ends. Q1's "mansion", imported into line 2, spends the figure early and leaves line 26's mansion unparalleled and flat. A reading is not original because it is prettier; it is original because the work's own pattern spends it twice. The quatrain's variants thus carry a miniature of the whole case for Q2's copy: the fuller text is the patterned text, the pattern is checkable by any reader with both books open, and the shorter witness's divergences fall, at every point, on the pattern's breaking side.

One further point belongs to this section because it concerns what Q1's stump does to the play rather than to the apparatus. In Q1's timing, Juliet has barely invoked the sunset before the Nurse is wringing her hands in the doorway, and the audience's knowledge, that the wedding day has already turned to murder and exile in the street below, collides with a desire that has had four lines to declare itself. The scene plays as irony with the volume turned up and the psychology turned down. In Q2's timing the desire is given its full aria, and the catastrophe, when it enters, annihilates something the audience has been made to inhabit for thirty-three lines. Textual critics sometimes write as if the choice between the quartos were a choice between documents; it is also a choice between two plays, one swift and emblematic, one dilated and devastating, and the editor who prints Q2's speech is choosing the second play on documentary grounds that happen also to be dramatic ones.

## The Twenty-Nine Lines Only One Lineage Carries

Before the crux takes the stage, the omitted twenty-nine lines deserve their own examination, because their textual condition is the quiet marvel of the case. Everything in the soliloquy that the world remembers, beyond the opening summons, exists in exactly one line of transmission. The appeal to the "love-performing night" (3.2.5), the crux itself, the falconry of the hooded and bating blood (3.2.14), the triple summons "Come, night; come, Romeo; come, thou day in night" (3.2.17), the vision of Romeo cut out in little stars until "all the world will be in love with night / And pay no worship to the garish sun" (3.2.22 to 25), the bought and unpossessed "mansion of a love" (3.2.26), the impatient child with new robes (3.2.28 to 31): every one of these stands in the second quarto and its descendants and nowhere else. Had the 1599 book perished as completely as most Elizabethan playbooks perished, the soliloquy would be the first quarto's four lines and a title in a stationer's ledger. The speech's survival is a single thread, and the thread held.

### Which famous lines of the speech exist only in the second quarto's line of transmission?

All of them beyond the opening quatrain: the curtain appeal and its crux, the falconry of the "unmanned blood", the "day in night" invocation, Romeo "cut him out in little stars", the "mansion of a love", the child with new robes. The first quarto prints none; each survives because the 1599 quarto and its reprints carried it down.

Within those twenty-nine lines the transmission is, the crux apart, unusually clean, and the cleanliness itself is a datum. A reported or memorially degraded text betrays itself by scattering small errors, the flattened synonyms and transposed phrases that mark the first quarto's quatrain; the second quarto's long speech shows no such scatter. Its words sit in their patterned places, the falconry terms exact, "unmanned" for the hawk not yet flown at game and "bating" for its fluttering impatience in the hood, terms a reporter garbles and a compositor setting from good copy preserves. Its figures interlock across the whole span: the sun's lodging of line 2 answers to the mansion of line 26, housed gods and housed love; the curtain of line 5 answers to the black mantle of line 15, Night's two garments; the wink of line 6 answers to the garish sun of line 25, the eye shut and the worship withdrawn. A speech so woven is either the author's draft fair in the copy or the most improbable feat of reconstruction in the period's theatre. Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto argues, for the short text as a whole, that its maker knew the play from the stage; nothing in the long speech's weave suggests that the second quarto's maker knew it from anywhere but the page. The bibliographical consensus the delivered studies in this series record, that Q2 was set from copy close to the author's papers, what the Greg tradition calls foul papers, rests on exactly such internal evidence play-wide; this speech is one of its strongest single exhibits.

Two smaller features of the twenty-nine lines reward the textual eye. The first is the reading at 3.2.21, "when he shall die", where the pronoun matters beyond its size: Juliet imagines Romeo dead and stellified, not herself, and the quarto reading keeps the fantasy's direction outward, toward the lover transformed, where the sentiment of some later reprints and stage texts, which give "when I shall die", turns it inward toward the speaker's apotheosis. The early witnesses agree on "he", and the agreement binds; the variant is a warning that not all corruption is early, and that the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries added their own layer of drift on top of the seventeenth century's. The second feature is the speech's close. Q2 runs the child-and-robes simile straight into the Nurse's entrance with Juliet's hail, "O, here comes my nurse, / And she brings news; and every tongue that speaks / But Romeo's name speaks heavenly eloquence" (3.2.31 to 33), lines that belong to the soliloquy's movement while opening the door to the scene's second half. The Folio keeps them; the first quarto, having no soliloquy to close, begins its scene's second half at the door itself. An editor numbering the speech must decide where reverie ends and address begins, and the standard disposition, soliloquy to line 33 and dialogue from the Nurse's first words, is an editorial convenience laid over a Q2 text that flows uninterrupted. Even the speech's boundaries, it turns out, are partly the editor's work, which is the mildest and most universal form of the hand this series keeps uncovering.

## The Line the First Quarto Never Prints: Anatomy of the Crux

The crux sits at 3.2.6, inside the appeal to Night that follows the dismissal of the sun: "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night, / That runawayes eyes may wincke, and Romeo / Leap to these arms, untalked of and unseen" (3.2.5 to 7), as the line stands in the second quarto's spelling. Three features of the transmitted form deserve emphasis before the centuries of commentary are allowed to bury them. The word carries no apostrophe, because the early printers did not mark possessives in the modern way; the choice between "runaway's" and "runaways'" is therefore not a choice between two early readings but a choice between two parsings of one early reading, and an editor makes it by judgment, not by collation. The spelling itself wanders across the early editions, runnawayes and run-awayes and run-awaies in the quartos and the Folio, as the textual scholar W. J. Rolfe noted in his edition's summary of the case, without the word's identity ever changing. And the clause is a purpose clause: Night is to spread her curtain so that these eyes may wink and Romeo may leap. The grammar makes the eyes an obstacle to be neutralised, eyes whose openness would talk of the lovers and see them, which is why the very next words are "untalked of and unseen". Any parsing of the crux must satisfy that grammar: the eyes belong to a watcher, or to watchers, whose vigilance the curtain defeats.

### What word stands at the crux in every early text that prints the line?

One word stands there everywhere: runaway, spelled runawayes or run-awayes, in the second quarto of 1599, in its reprints of 1609 and 1622, and in the First Folio. No early witness prints any other substantive at the crux. The whole dispute concerns what that agreed word means and how an editor should point it.

The history of the dispute is a caution against supposing that difficulty in a line implies corruption in its text. The older commentators, as S. W. Singer observed in his celebrated 1853 paper on the passage in Notes and Queries, did not attempt to change the word; they sought to explain it. William Warburton, editing in the generation after Alexander Pope, identified the runaway as Phoebus, the sun fleeing westward. George Steevens argued at length that the runaway is Night herself. Francis Douce proposed Juliet, the runaway from her father's house in spirit. The Reverend John Halpin, in an essay Dowden's edition describes as learned and ingenious, argued for Cupid, the runaway god whose eyes, closed in sleep, would leave the lovers unguarded by desire's own sentry. R. G. White, who signed himself Shakespeare's scholar and titled his early paper "Who was Juliet's Runaway?", resisted Warburton's identification for years and came round to it in the end. John Payne Collier remarked that more suggestions had been made for the word than there are letters in it. By the time H. H. Furness compiled the New Variorum edition of the play, the condensed summary of comment on this single word filled, on Rolfe's count, twenty-eight octavo pages of fine print, and Dowden's appendix raises the figure to thirty closely printed pages. No other word in the play, arguably no other word in Shakespeare, carries so much commentary per letter.

Edward Dowden, whose Arden edition of the play closed the nineteenth century's work on the line, added the two observations that still frame the sober end of the debate. The first concerns the verb: "wink" in the period commonly bears the sense of shutting the eyes in sleep, not the modern flicker, so the clause wishes the watcher asleep, not blinking. The second concerns punctuation: Dowden proposed, without altering the received text in his own edition, that the sentence might be pointed so that Juliet's thought runs from the curtain straight to the consequence, the sun's eyes closing as the price of Romeo's leap, and he glossed the runaway accordingly as the sun itself, paraphrasing the clause "yonder sun may sleep". Dowden accepted Theobald's pointing, "That th' runaway's eyes may wink", into his text, the inserted article making explicit what the quarto's bare possessive leaves compressed. Between them, Dowden's two moves, gloss the verb by its period sense, identify the runaway by the speech's own opening movement, mark the method this study follows: construe before cutting, and let the speech arbitrate its own metaphors.

The spelling cloud around the word, finally, is worth dispersing, since it has fed a minor mythology of its own. Rolfe's summary lists the early editions' forms as "runnawayes", "run-awayes", "run-awaies" and "run-aways", and readers meeting the word in those dresses sometimes suppose that the variation itself signals corruption, as if four spellings meant four words. It signals nothing of the kind. Spelling in the period's printing houses was compositorial weather, varying by workman, by type supply and by the hour of the night, and a substantive that survives in one form through four such climates of accident is unusually stable, not unusually suspect. The Folio's hyphen, in particular, has done quiet mischief, tempting readers to hear "run-awayes" as a compound adjective, the eyes of a runaway thing, where the quarto's unhyphenated run of letters leaves the word's grammar to be settled by the sentence. Strip the accidentals away, as any modernised text must, and the witnesses' agreement is total: one substantive, one verb, one purpose clause. The crux was never in the letters. It was in the centuries that read them.

## The Word Itself: "Runaway" in the Period's English

Before the candidates are judged, the word deserves its own hearing, because half the crux's literature proceeds as if "runaway" were a freak requiring rescue, when its behaviour in the period's English is regular and its figurative range is the point. The substantive names, in the usage of the time, one who runs away: a fugitive from justice, a servant or apprentice fled from a master, a soldier deserted from his colours, and by an easy transfer a horse that bolts with its rider helpless. Every one of those senses is a sense of custody escaped, of a charge breaking bounds and making for the horizon, and the word's energy lies in the escape, not in any trade or station of the escaper. That energy is what Juliet's sentence needs. Her speech is a machinery of bounds and their breaking: the sun confined to his lodging, the blood hooded like a hawk, the match played within the rules of a game, the mansion bought but not yet possessed. A runaway, a thing that has broken its custody and is fleeing across the sky, belongs to that machinery as no rumourer or abstraction can, and the possessive built on the word asks only whose flight it is.

Against that background the plural parsing's lexical wound can be stated with precision. Hunter's and Delius's wanderers require "runaway" to mean a person who runs about, a roisterer or night-walker abroad for pleasure, and that is not an extension of the word's field but a contradiction of it: the runaway of the period's usage flees from something, singly and with intent, and is defined by the custody escaped. Hales's admission of 1892, that the required sense is a hapax in Shakespeare and in all English literature, is therefore not a pedantic footnote but the exact measure of the parsing's cost. The singular parsing's cost is nil by comparison: it asks the word to do, for the sun, what the word does everywhere, name a fugitive in flight, and it asks "wink" to do what the period let it do, close in sleep. A crux in which one side must invent a sense and the other need only apply two attested ones is not, in the adjudicative sense, a crux at all; it is a preference that hardened into a controversy by being repeated in good editions. The history sections of this study explain the hardening. The lexical facts resist it.

## Who Is the Runaway? The Identifications Judged

Strip the problem to its elements and it is a riddle in casting. Somebody's eyes must wink shut before Romeo can enter unobserved. The candidates proposed across three centuries are the sun, Night, the moon, Phaeton, Cupid, Romeo, Juliet, and, in the plural parsing, the unnamed wanderers of the Verona streets. Adjudicating among them is not a matter of taste; each candidate can be tested against the sentence, the speech and the playwright's habits, and most fail at least one test outright.

The sun, Warburton's candidate and Dowden's, passes every test. The speech opens as a dismissal of the sun: his steeds are to gallop to their lodging, his reckless understudy invoked to whip them faster, his light replaced by "cloudy night immediately" (3.2.4). The appeal to Night's curtain is the second movement of the same operation, and its purpose clause naturally names the casualty of the first: the sun's eyes, the one great eye of the day, closing as his chariot reaches the lodging. The identification also respects the verb. A god's eye can wink in the sleeping sense; the sun at setting is the cosmos's daily act of going to sleep, and Juliet's wish is that on this day it happen early. Finally, the identification preserves the speech's economy of persons. Juliet addresses two powers in turn, the sun's team and Night herself; the runaway belongs to the first address, the curtain to the second, and the sentence stitches the two together as cause and condition. Nothing needs to be imported. On the evidence of the speech alone, before any parallel is called, the runaway is Phoebus in flight to his lodging, and the reading has the further virtue of being the one the eighteenth century reached first and the nineteenth century's best editor, after every alternative had been aired, returned to.

### Why can the runaway not be Night herself?

Night cannot be the runaway because Night is the sentence's addressee: Juliet asks Night to spread her curtain so that the eyes may wink, and a power is not asked to veil her own eyes as a favour to herself. The eyes must belong to another watcher, and the speech's only other watcher is the departing sun.

Steevens's Night, argued with all that great annotator's learning, founders on that grammar, and founders again on sense: Night's business in the speech is to be the lovers' accomplice, the "love-performing" power whose darkness is their element; her eyes winking shut would be the accomplice falling asleep on duty. The moon, proposed in passing by commentators Dowden lists, fails on absence: the moon is nowhere in the speech, and a crux is not solved by importing a body the passage never mentions. Phaeton fails on grammar and myth together; the boy is the imagined driver, not a watcher, and his eyes, in the myth Juliet is invoking, are about to close in death by falling, which would darken the line with an omen the sentence does not want. Romeo fails on the purpose clause, since his eyes are the ones meant to see by lover's light a moment later: "Lovers can see to do their amorous rites / By their own beauties" (3.2.8 to 9). Juliet, Douce's candidate, fails on the plain choreography of the speech, in which Juliet is the speaker at her window, not a fugitive crossing a stage. Halpin's Cupid is the most beautiful failure. The runaway god of love, whose mother in the old stories cannot keep him home, gives the line a mythological perfume, and Halpin built his case with learning; but Cupid's eyes closed would not unwatch the lovers, since Cupid is the power that watches lovers into being, and nothing in the speech summons him. The case rests on the word's fitness for a fugitive god rather than on the sentence's need for a sleeper.

The remaining candidates can be judged by a single criterion, importation: a solution that must fetch its referent from outside the speech has the burden of explaining why the speech hid its own key. The moon fails the criterion in its purest form. Nothing in the thirty-three lines names or figures the moon; Juliet's night is pointedly moonless, a darkness of curtain and mantle, and to make the moon the runaway is to stock the speech with a body its author kept out of it. Phaeton fails a subtler version of the same test. He is present in the speech, but present in one role only, the imagined whip-hand of line 3, a counterfactual driver ("such a wagoner / As Phaeton would whip you") whose function is to hurry the real driver home. To promote him to the watcher of line 6 is to double his employment within three lines and to make his eyes, of all eyes, the ones whose closing lets Romeo come, when the myth Juliet invokes ends with those eyes closing in a fall that kills him; the omen is precisely what her sentence is not courting. Romeo and Juliet fail the choreography test already noted, but they fail importation too, in mirror image: they are too present, the speech's subject and speaker, and the purpose clause needs a third party, the watcher whose exclusion defines the lovers' privacy. The roll of failures has a shape. Every rejected candidate is fetched from the speech's outskirts, its mythology, its personnel, its unmentioned sky, while the accepted candidate stands in the sentence's own first clause-cluster, driving the horses of lines 1 to 4. That distribution is what a correct identification looks like in a compressed poetic text, and it is why the sun's claim has outlasted abler advocacy than its rivals ever found.

The plural parsing, runaways' eyes, the eyes of persons running about the streets at night, was given its classic form by Joseph Hunter and adopted by Nicolaus Delius, the great German editor, and by Schmidt and Daniel after him; Rolfe's edition declares itself "quite well satisfied" with it. Its appeal is the appeal of the ordinary: Juliet wants no witnesses, and the witnesses a wedding night fears are people. Its weakness is lexical, and Professor J. W. Hales, in Longman's Magazine in February 1892, conceded the point with a candour that still stings: "runaway" in the required sense, a night-wanderer or roisterer abroad after dark, is a hapax legomenon, a word attested in that meaning nowhere else in Shakespeare and nowhere else in English literature. A parsing that requires an otherwise unknown sense of a common word asks more of the reader than a parsing that requires a common word to name the god the speech has just dismissed. The plural also flattens the sentence's architecture, exchanging the cosmic watcher, whose closing eye is the sunset Juliet has been commanding for four lines, for a crowd of anonymous street traffic that the speech never assembles. And it sits awkwardly with the purpose clause's pairing, "untalked of and unseen": unnamed wanderers might see, but the paired fear of being talked of belongs to a world of named witnesses, to the household and the city, concentrated for the speech's purposes in the one eye that sees everything by day. The verdict stands with Warburton, Theobald, White and Dowden: singular, and the sun.

Two supports from outside the line deserve their weight before the emendations are heard. Thomas Keightley, defending the authentic text in the Notes and Queries correspondence of October 1853 against the season's conjectures, pointed to Lorenzo's line in The Merchant of Venice, "For the close night doth play the runaway" (2.6.40), where the substantive names a fugitive who will not be stayed, and argued that a poet who could cast night itself as the runaway could cast the sun as one with at least equal right. The parallel does not identify Juliet's runaway; it does something more useful, proving that "runaway" as a figure for a fleeing cosmic power is Shakespeare's own coin, not a misprint's accident. The second support is negative and therefore strong. In four hundred years no document, no manuscript fragment, no annotated early copy, no contemporary allusion has surfaced offering any other word at this place. The emendations to be examined next are all, without exception, inventions of the study, proposed against a unanimous transmission. That does not make them wrong; conjecture has rescued other lines in other plays. It fixes the burden they must carry, and the burden, as the roll of them shows, is one that ingenuity alone has never been able to lift.

## The Emendations: Rumourers, Unawares, Nature's Eye

The conjectures divide into those that repunctuate the quarto word and those that replace it, and the division is the measure of their merit. Theobald's "th' runaway's" belongs to the first class and is barely an emendation at all: it expands the possessive by supplying the article that prudish grammar wants, on the argument, refined by the commentator Allen whom Dowden cites, that a spoken "the" could be absorbed by the final letter of "that" in the copy the compositor set. Dowden printed Theobald's pointing, and the reading survives wherever an edition prints the line with the article, a small monument to the principle that the least intervention that construes the text is the best. Every replacement conjecture belongs to the second class, and each must answer two questions: how did the author's word become the quarto's word in the printing house, and why is the replacement's sense not merely possible but necessary. The roll of failures is distinguished.

Benjamin Heath, in his Revisal of Shakespeare's text in 1765, read "Rumour's eyes may wink", making the watcher Rumour, whose eyes the curtain closes so that the lovers may be "untalked of" as well as unseen. The sense is exquisite, the neatest fit to the paired nouns of any conjecture ever offered, and it founders on the letters: as Singer himself reported in the act of superseding it, Heath's word "differs widely from run-aways in the trace of the letters". S. W. Singer, in the 1853 paper that reopened the whole question, went further along the same road and proposed "rumourers' eyes may wink", arguing that the unfamiliar word, a plural for the carriers of gossip, might in an indistinct hand have been misread by the printer as "runawayes". Singer's conjecture has the merit of explaining the error it posits and the support of the "untalked of" that follows; it remains a word Shakespeare is not known to use, introduced to gloss a word he is known to use, and the correspondence columns of Notes and Queries spent the autumn of 1853 showing that the trade was unnecessary. A correspondent signing R. H. C. proposed "rude day's eyes", the day's rude glare; W. R. Arrowsmith, surrendering to Singer's ingenuity in print, still held to the authentic text, and Keightley's defence of the quarto reading, armed with the Merchant of Venice parallel, closed the season with the honours even. The market had spoken: the conjectures were brilliant, and the text stayed where it was.

Zachary Jackson's "unawares" travelled furthest, from conjecture into print. The word gives a limping sense, that surprise's eyes may wink, and its resemblance to the quarto's letters is, as Singer concedes, slight and nothing more. Yet Charles Knight admitted it into the text of his edition, and John Payne Collier admitted it into his, so that for a generation of mid-century readers the line in their Shakespeare was a line no early book contains. Alexander Dyce's judgment on the pair of them is the period's own verdict and has never been improved: he pronounced the emendation "villainous". Dyce's severity matters because Dyce was no enemy of conjecture; his own contribution to this crux was the despairing suggestion that the last syllable of the quarto word ought to be "days", coupled with the admission that he could not determine what word originally preceded it. When the most surgical editor of the age offers a syllable and a shrug, the honest state of the question is on record: the transmitted word resists replacement because nothing that resembles it improves it, and nothing that improves it resembles it.

### What did Dyce call Jackson's "unawares"?

Dyce called it "villainous". The word judged not only Jackson's conjecture but the editorial act of printing it: Knight and Collier had admitted "unawares" into their texts, displacing a reading every early witness supports, on no stronger ground than a slight similarity of letters.

The most literary of the conjectures came from outside the editorial succession altogether. The essay "Juliet's Runaway, Once More" builds its case on Marlowe: Shakespeare, its author argues, had the great soliloquy of Doctor Faustus by heart when he wrote Juliet's invocation, since her demand for haste is the precise converse of Faustus's cry for delay, the "lente, lente currite, noctis equi" reversed into a gallop. In Faustus, Marlowe's magician apostrophises "Fair Nature's eye", the sun, and bids it rise again; the essayist concludes that Juliet's line as Shakespeare wrote it carried the same phrase, and that the quarto's word conceals "Nature's eye": "That Nature's eye may wink, and Romeo / Leap to these arms". The conjecture has genuine criticism in it, the Faustus parallel is real and the converse structure of the two speeches is one of the surest things known about the soliloquy's making, and the proposed sense, Nature's eye, the sun, closing, is exactly the sense this study defends. It fails as emendation for the usual reason: no plausible path runs from "Nature's" in a manuscript to "runawayes" on the printed page, and an emendation that cannot narrate its own corruption is a gloss in disguise. Its value is diagnostic. A critic arguing from the speech's genesis and a bibliographer arguing from its grammar reach the same sleeper by different roads; where the roads converge, on the sun's eye closing so that Romeo may come, the editor may treat the identification as settled even while the word that carries it stays, as it should, the quarto's own.

The autumn of 1853 deserves a final word as the crux's documentary set piece, because it shows a learned culture processing a doubtful word in public and reaching the verdict this study reaches. Singer opened the season in the issue of 2 July with his "rumourers", in a paper whose first move was to quote the Folio text entire and to concede that the older commentators had explained rather than altered the word. The correspondence that followed ran the full gamut the question allows. R. H. C.'s "rude day's" was praised in terms that now read as period politeness, as a conjecture with which the poet "might undoubtedly have written" the line, and Arrowsmith, surrendering to Singer with grace, recorded the surrender and kept his allegiance in a single sentence: he would "still adhere to the authentic text", glossing the darkness Juliet invokes as one under cover of which even the fugitive from justice might snatch a wink of sleep, a gloss that makes the runaway a figure for every hunted thing the night shelters. Keightley's October letter then supplied the defence's documentary anchor, the Merchant of Venice parallel, and posed the question the season had been circling: not what word might improve the line, but whether Shakespeare wrote the passage as the Folio gives it. By the season's end the conjectures had been admired, catalogued and set aside, and the text stood where the printers left it. The episode is the burden of proof working in real time, and its outcome, admiration without adoption, is the template every later discussion of the crux has followed, Dowden's appendix and the modern textual note included.

## Grow or Grown: The Folio's One Correction

Nine lines below the crux, the witnesses divide over a syllable, and the division teaches the opposite lesson from the crux itself. Juliet asks Night to hood her unmanned blood, "bating in my cheeks, / With thy black mantle; till strange love, grown bold, / Think true love acted simple modesty" (3.2.14 to 16). The second quarto reads "grow bold"; the Folio reads "grown bold". Modern practice splits along a line that reveals each edition's theory of the Folio. Editors who treat the Folio's text of this play as wholly derivative, a reprint of a reprint with compositorial noise, nonetheless print "grown" in large numbers, because the sentence wants it: "till strange love, grown bold" makes the participle carry the process, love emboldened by degrees under the mantle, where "grow bold" leaves the verb suspended between infinitive and imperative moods and the clause limping. The Folger Shakespeare, the most conservative widely used modern text, keeps the quarto's "grow", a deliberate refusal to let a derivative witness correct its copy-text even where the correction beckons. Both choices are defensible, and the difference between them is the difference between editing by rule and editing by judgment within rule, a distinction the next section takes up.

### Does the Folio correct any other word in the speech?

Substantively, no. Across the thirty-three lines the Folio agrees with the second quarto in every word but this one, its remaining differences being accidentals of spelling and punctuation from its compositors. The speech is a clean specimen of the Folio's descent: a late reprint of the quarto line, able to tidy an idiom, unable to offer an independent text.

The Folio's workmanship on the play, as Charlton Hinman's study of the volume's printing established and the delivered Folio study in this series sets out, was divided between compositors of unequal care, the workmen Hinman lettered B and E, whose stints can still be traced page by page. Nothing in that division rescues "grown" into authority, since a compositor's or corrector's improvement is an accident of the printing house either way; but it explains the kind of witness the Folio is at this line, a book in which intelligent hands were physically present at the type, capable of normalising a phrase toward the idiom as they set it. The variant is best understood as the printing house thinking, and an editor who adopts it adopts a thought, not a testimony, recording the adoption so that the distinction survives.

The variant's importance is out of proportion to its size because it isolates the exact point at which editorial theory must become editorial practice. The Folio of this play was set from the fourth quarto of 1622, itself a corrected reprint of the third quarto of 1609, itself a reprint of the second quarto of 1599; the line of descent is a chain of copies, and no link in it touches the author's papers. On a strict theory of authority, therefore, "grown" has no more right to the text than any Folio compositor's slip. Yet the editors who print it are not being careless; they are applying the older and deeper rule that where the copy-text's reading is grammatically or idiomatically defective, an editor may adopt any reading, from any witness however derivative, that repairs the defect, provided the repair is recorded. "Grow" is not nonsense, and the Folger's retention of it is the strongest proof; but it is the weaker form of a phrase the language elsewhere completes, and the Folio's "grown" may preserve, through the quarto chain, a sophistication introduced by a corrector who had the idiom in his ear, if not the manuscript before his eyes. This study accepts "grown", with the majority, and records the acceptance as the speech's one licensed departure from its copy-text. Set beside the crux, the pair of decisions shows the method whole: at 3.2.6 the witnesses agree and the sense construes, so nothing moves; at 3.2.15 the copy-text limps and a dependent witness completes the idiom, so one syllable moves, in the apparatus's full light. The editor's hand, which the series thesis for these textual studies keeps in view, is visible in both places, and in both places it is the discipline of the hand, not its absence, that the evidence justifies.

## Small Change: The Minor Variants of the Long Speech

A census of the long speech's substantive variants, early witness against early witness, is short enough to complete, and completing it reframes the crux. Between the second quarto and the Folio, across thirty-three lines, exactly one substantive divides the books, the participle at 3.2.15. Between the second quarto and the first, across the four lines both print, three substantives divide them, all in the quatrain. Within the twenty-nine lines only the Q2 line carries, the early books offer no substantive division at all. The arithmetic deserves emphasis because popular accounts of Shakespearean textuality suggest a fog of variants in which any word might be another's guess. This speech shows the reality: a transmission stable to within one syllable, carrying one word whose meaning, not whose form, posterity disputed. The editor's problems here are not the problems of a corrupt text. They are the problems of a sound text read by disputatious centuries.

The later centuries supplied the variants the early ones withheld, and the chief of them is worth its paragraph. At 3.2.21 the quartos and the Folio read "when he shall die": Juliet, imagining Romeo dead, bids Night take him and cut him out in little stars. A long line of later reprints and stage texts gives "when I shall die", transferring the stellification to Juliet herself. The alteration flatters a sentiment, the heroine envisaging her own apotheosis, and it reverses the passage's actual generosity, in which the imagined glory is all the lover's and the speaker asks only to have given him away to the sky. Editors restore "he" without debate, since every early witness carries it, but the variant's career teaches the standing lesson of this study from the other end: corruption is not a seventeenth-century monopoly, print goes on drifting wherever a phrase can be made smoother or more sentimental, and the apparatus that records "I" in later texts performs for the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the same office the crux literature performs for the sixteenth. The speech's text was settled early; what later ages unsettled, they unsettled by taste, and taste is the one editor whose work cannot be collated away, only noticed.

One further piece of small change belongs to the record, since school and stage texts trade in it constantly: the pointing of the close. The quarto runs the final simile and the hail to the Nurse in one breath, and editions divide over whether Juliet's "O, here comes my nurse" (3.2.31) closes the soliloquy or opens the dialogue, a division with no words at stake and real consequences for how the speech is excerpted in anthologies, where it commonly ends at "may not wear them" with the simile's pathos intact and the Nurse unentered. The anthology speech, the audition speech, the speech of the quotation books, is thus an editorial artefact twice over, once in its wording and once in its frame, and the frame was chosen by the tradition of excerpting rather than by any witness. None of this impeaches the text. It completes the census: one crux of meaning, one syllable of idiom, one pronoun of later sentiment, one frame of convention, and against that small change, thirty-three lines in which the early books speak, where they speak at all, with a single voice.

## Accidentals: Spelling, Pointing and the Dress of the Speech

Between the substantives that editors fight over stands the quieter half of the text, its accidentals, and this speech's accidentals carry one lesson the crux debate usually obscures. The second quarto dresses the lines in the spelling of its printing house: "wincke" for wink, "learne" for learn, "unmannd" for unmanned, long s and the period's free traffic between u and v throughout. No modern edition keeps that dress, and the standard practice of modernising spelling is itself an editorial theory in action: accidentals are referred to the copy-text only where an editor labours to reproduce them, while the reading texts that fill classrooms silently translate the speech into present-day orthography. The translation is nearly free of cost at most points, since "wincke" and "wink" are one word in two coats. At the crux it is not free. The quarto's "runawayes" is, considered as an accidental, a bare plural-or-possessive shape, and the modern editor's apostrophe, the most consequential accidental in the play, is a decision taken in the course of dressing the text. The crux is therefore the place where the two halves of Greg's division meet: a substantive everyone agrees on, wearing an accidental that decides its meaning. Editors who announce that they merely follow the copy-text for accidentals confess, at this line, that the copy-text has nothing to say; the apostrophe is judgment all the way down, and the honest apparatus says so.

Pointing tells the same story at lower stakes. The early texts punctuate by pause and breath more than by syntax, and the crux sentence in the quarto dress runs its clauses together with commas where a modern text plants colons and semicolons: "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night, / That runawayes eyes may wincke, and Romeo / Leape to these armes, untalkt of and vnseene". Dowden's proposed repunctuation, examined earlier as the lightest of interventions, works on this layer of the text, and its lesson extends across the speech. The triple "Come, night; come, Romeo; come, thou day in night" (3.2.17) wears semicolons in most modern editions, a stately pointing that slows the summons into ceremony; the quarto's lighter stops let the commands tumble. Neither pointing is the author's beyond dispute, since pointing in foul-papers copy was itself largely the scribe's or compositor's, and the reader who feels the speech's pace quicken or settle between editions is feeling an accidental at work. Lineation, the third accidental, is mercifully stable here: unlike the Queen Mab speech, part of which the second quarto sets as prose and leaves editors to relineate, this soliloquy stands in the quarto as verse throughout, its lines disposed as the modern texts dispose them, and the compositor's verse frame has never needed rescue. A speech can be textually fortunate in its frame and unfortunate in a single word; this one is the type specimen.

One more accidental deserves record because it touches the series' standing question of how editions present the same words as different speeches. The Folio capitalises and italicises its way through the lines with a solemnity the quarto lacks, and its compositors' habits, studied across the whole volume in the delivered Folio study in this series, leave the speech's substantives untouched while retouching its surface at every line. A reader collating the Folio page against a modern text learns the Folio's true value for this play at a glance: it is a superb witness to how the seventeenth century dressed Shakespeare, and, the single participle at 3.2.15 excepted, a silent one about what Shakespeare wrote. That silence is not a defect. It is the Folio being what it is, the collected monument of 1623, and the error lies only in asking it questions its descent forbids it to answer.

## How Editors Decide: Copy-Text, Accidentals and the Burden of Proof

The complication the brief for this study insists on, how editors actually choose between rival readings, can now be answered from the cases rather than from theory alone, though the theory names the moves. Since W. W. Greg formulated the rationale of copy-text in the middle of the twentieth century, editors of Shakespeare have distinguished between substantives, the words themselves, and accidentals, the spelling, punctuation and pointing in which the words are dressed. For accidentals the editor follows a single chosen copy-text, for this play the second quarto, whose forms stand nearest the author's own habits of dress. For substantives the editor judges each variant on its merits, because substantive variants are where authorship lives and where transmission errs. The distinction explains at a stroke why the crux is argued in the language of sense while its apostrophe is settled in the language of grammar: the word runawayes is a substantive and is judged by whether it can be the author's word; the apostrophe that parses it is an accidental, supplied by the editor under the copy-text's licence, and the Folio's pointing, being later and derivative, has no vote.

Greg's framework also disciplines conjecture, and the crux is the framework's hardest local test. A conjecture, in the disciplined sense, is a substantive with no documentary support at all, admitted only when the transmitted substantives cannot be construed as the author's sense. The burden therefore has two parts: the emender must first convict the transmitted reading, showing not that it is difficult but that it is impossible, and must then acquit the replacement, showing a credible mechanism by which the author's word became the printer's word. The crux defeats every challenger at the first part. "Runawayes" construes, as the identifications section showed, once "wink" is read in its period sense and the sun is allowed to be the fugitive of his own chariot; and a reading that construes cannot be convicted. Singer's "rumourers" and Jackson's "unawares" fail the second part as well, since neither can travel from a plausible manuscript form to the quarto's letters without a journey no compositor's error-pattern supports, which is why Dyce's generation, willing as it was to conjecture, kept them in the notes and out of the text, Knight and Collier's lapses standing as the exception that proved the rule's necessity. Paul Werstine's later scepticism about the narratives editors build on such foundations, his warning that the stories told to justify texts can harden into scenery, applies with force here: the tale of the misreading compositor, invented to carry "rumourers", is scenery of exactly that kind, a story whose only evidence is the conjecture it was built to support.

One traditional maxim of the craft can be tested against the speech before the positive account is given: prefer the harder reading, the lectio difficilior, on the ground that transmission degrades the specific into the generic far oftener than it invents the specific out of the generic. The maxim decides the quatrain at a glance. "Lodging" is harder than "mansion", "whip you to the west" harder than "quickly bring you thether", "bring in" harder in context than "send in"; at each point the second quarto holds the difficult form and the first the facility, and the maxim and the stemma vote together. At the crux the maxim protects the transmitted word from a different direction: "runawayes" is by far the hardest reading ever proposed for the line, harder than rumourers, harder than unawares, and its difficulty is the difficulty of a compressed possessive in a passage of high metaphor, not the difficulty of nonsense. The maxim has limits an honest editor keeps in view, since it can be made to canonise mere error wherever error is odd, and the delivered debate over the first quarto's status, in which Paul Werstine figures as the standing sceptic of comfortable narratives, warns against letting any single rule harden into scenery. Used as a presumption and not a proof, the preference for the harder reading is one more instrument pointing, at every contested place in this speech, toward the second quarto's page.

The positive side of the method is worth stating as plainly, since a study of variants can leave the impression that editors merely refuse. Editors choose the second quarto as copy-text for the speech because its text of the play, in Brian Gibbons's Arden account and in the bibliographical consensus the delivered studies in this series record, descends from copy close to the author's draft, its spellings and its unresolved features bearing the marks of foul papers rather than of the playhouse. They accept the Folio's "grown" because judgment about a limping idiom outranks pedigree. They print the first quarto's stage business in their notes and sometimes in their texts because for staging the 1597 book, whatever its origin, is a witness of the first performances, a point Lukas Erne's edition of Q1 pressed upon the profession. They leave "runawayes" standing because the documentary case is unanimous and the semantic case is sufficient. Each decision is of a different logical kind, and the mark of the competent edition is that its apparatus lets a reader see which kind has been made where. The mark of the incompetent one, Knight's and Collier's mid-century texts among them, is the silent substitution, the conjecture wearing the text's clothes with no apparatus to confess it. Readers who never open an apparatus are nonetheless reading its consequences in every line, which is why the next section, on what the standard editions actually put before the common reader, is a practical and not a merely bibliographical question.

## The Modern Settlement: What the Standard Editions Print

Open the editions a reader is likeliest to hold and the crux stands in two forms, both of them the quarto's word, differing only in the apostrophe. The Arden line, Brian Gibbons's second-series edition and René Weis's third-series edition after it, prints "runaway's", the singular possessive, and defends the sun in the note. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition prints the singular as well, as does the Norton text, so that the classroom mainstream reads the line as the day's eye closing. The Folger Shakespeare, edited by Barbara Mowat and Paul Werstine, prints "runaways'", the plural, and thereby keeps faith with Hunter and Delius through Rolfe: the eyes are the eyes of night-wanderers, and the note confesses the word's singularity by recording the alternatives. Between the singular and the plural there is no difference of document, only of judgment about which watcher Juliet fears, and the profession's centre of gravity sits with the singular because the speech's own first movement supplies its watcher and the plural must invent its crowd. No standard edition prints a replacement word. "Rumourers" and "unawares" survive only in textual notes and in the history of editing, which is where conjectures that fail the burden of proof belong; the variorum tradition that preserves them, crowned by Furness's thirty pages on this word, performs the opposite service from Knight's and Collier's, keeping the guesses visible precisely so that the text need not carry them.

The companion case in this series, [The Queen Mab Speech Across the Quartos](/2026/05/08/queen-mab-speech-across-quartos/), shows the same settlement from the other side: there the two quartos print rival forms of a famous speech and the editor arbitrates between witnesses; here one witness holds the line and the editor arbitrates between interpretations. The pair of studies together cover the editor's whole trade. It is worth adding that the speech's text, crux apart, is among the most stable in the play: a reader moving between Arden, Oxford, Norton and Folger will find the thirty-three lines identical in every substantive but the one syllable at "grow" and "grown", a stability that throws the crux into its true relief. The line is famous not because the text is chaotic but because it is the single place in a serene transmission where a word's meaning, not its documentary form, remained unsettled long enough for three centuries of ingenuity to accumulate around it.

Two modern presentations of the speech deserve separate mention because they refuse the settlement's terms in instructive ways. Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto prints the 1597 text as a document in its own right, and in its pages the soliloquy stands at four lines, the crux absent, the scene's timing exposed: a reader who wants to know what the early stage may have made of the speech reads the speech there, in its stump, with the Nurse's hands wringing in the doorway. Parallel-text presentations, of the kind Jay L. Halio prepared for this play's two-text problem, set Q1 and Q2 in facing columns and let the eye do the collating; for this speech the format is devastating and clarifying at once, a full column against four lines, the visual form of the stemma. Neither presentation competes with the critical edition's settled text. Each serves the reader the settled text cannot serve, the one who needs the evidence unarrayed by verdict, and their existence beside the Arden and Folger volumes marks the maturity of the modern settlement: the text is fixed, the witnesses are available, and the argument is preserved entire for whoever wishes to re-run it.

### What did Dowden propose changing without changing a word?

Dowden proposed changing the pointing of the sentence at 3.2.5 to 7: keeping every quarto word, he repointed the clause so that the runaway sun's sleep and Romeo's leap stand as twin consequences of Night's curtain, and he offered the paraphrase "yonder sun may sleep" for the sense released. The proposal models the lightest editorial touch.

## The Crux on Stage: What Performance Can and Cannot Hear

A natural objection shadows every page of the crux literature, and a study built to outlast its rivals should face it: no audience has ever heard an apostrophe. The actor playing Juliet speaks "runaway's" and "runaways'" with the same breath, and the crux that filled thirty variorum pages vanishes in the theatre into a single uninflected sound. The objection is sound as far as it goes, and it locates the crux precisely: this is a reader's crux, an event in the history of the printed page, and its arena is the study and the classroom rather than the stage. But three qualifications keep the theatre from dismissing the textual work altogether. First, performance inherits the edition's other decisions in audible form: the "grown" or "grow" at 3.2.15 changes the rhythm of a spoken clause, and the choice between the full thirty-three lines and any cut toward the first quarto's four is the largest interpretive act a production performs on the scene, made nightly by directors who may never have seen the 1597 book. The Q1 text's modern afterlife, in editions that print it as a performance document and in the scholarly argument, associated with Lukas Erne, that it records the play as played, means the stage and the study now trade evidence in both directions.

Second, the crux disciplines the actor's understanding even where it cannot reach the audience's ear. A Juliet who knows the eyes are the sun's plays the line as the completion of her dismissal of the day, her voice travelling back to the steeds and the lodging; a Juliet taught the plural plays it as a glance over the shoulder at a watching city. The two performances differ in gesture and temperature while differing in no audible phoneme, and promptbooks and rehearsal notes, the working documents of the theatre, are full of such sub-audible decisions transmitted from editions. Third, the objection proves too much if pressed: by its logic every unpronounced feature of a text, its spelling, its pointing, its paragraphing of prose, would lie outside criticism, and with them most of what editing is. The pointing of the crux is unpronounceable and consequential, as the pointing of "Come, night; come, Romeo" is unpronounceable and sets the speech's tempo in every reader's inner ear. The theatre's verdict on the crux is therefore not that the question is unreal, but that it belongs to the branch of the art the theatre shares with the library, the preparation of the words before they are spoken. Editors settle the text; actors settle its delivery; the crux lives in the first settlement and fructifies in the second, which is the most that any textual question can ask.

## What the Variants Do to the Speech

A textual study owes its reader a final accounting in the currency of the play itself: what do these decisions change for anyone who reads, watches or performs the soliloquy? Three consequences stand out. The first is temporal. The four-line Q1 version and the thirty-three-line Q2 version distribute the scene's weight differently, as the earlier sections showed, and every production silently chooses between them whenever it cuts the speech, as productions often do, toward the quatrain's brevity. The editor who prints the full text and the director who trims it are collaborators in a single act of judgment, and the apparatus is where their collaboration becomes honest.

The second consequence is interpretive, and it is the crux's real stake. Point the word as the sun's and the speech's first movement closes like a hand: the horses housed, the great eye shut, the curtain drawn, the lover admitted, a cosmos reordered in five lines by a girl's imperative. Point it as the wanderers' and the speech looks outward to the city, to Verona's streets and their traffic, and the wedding night becomes a matter of public vigilance evaded rather than of day itself dismissed. The difference is not pedantry; it is the difference between a soliloquy whose antagonist is time and one whose antagonist is society, and productions inherit the choice whether or not they know an editor made it. This study has argued for the cosmos against the crowd, on the grammar of the sentence and the testimony of the speech's own opening; but the argument's existence is the point the series thesis insists on, that the settled line on the page conceals an editor's hand, and that restoring the crux restores the reader's right to judge the hand's work.

The third consequence is musical, and it belongs to the variants of the quatrain. Q2's opening weaves its sounds, wagoner, whip, west, in a pattern that enacts the gallop it commands; Q1's parallel lines keep the sense and lose the weave. A reading of the speech's verse music, such as the companion study ['Gallop Apace': Reading the Verse Music](/2025/03/14/gallop-apace-verse-music-prosody/) undertakes, is therefore necessarily a reading of Q2's speech, and its findings about tempo and stress describe a text that one of the two early witnesses does not contain. Textual criticism is sometimes accused of pedantry about documents while the poem waits elsewhere. The accusation reverses the truth for this speech: the poem's most celebrated effects, the woven opening, the falconry, the stars, exist in the form readers love because a particular document carried them and a line of editors, from the Folio's correctors to Weis and Levenson, chose that document and defended its words against three centuries of alternatives. The variants are not obstacles between the reader and the speech. They are the speech's biography, and the biography explains the speech.

The classroom is where the biography does its most useful work, and a final consequence follows there. The crux is, pedagogically, the most efficient exhibit in the play: a single line in which a student can watch every editorial operation at once, collation against the stump of Q1, the stemma that discounts Q3, Q4 and F1 to one testimony, the burden of proof falling on Singer and Jackson, the apostrophe confessing itself an editor's act. School editions that gloss the line with a bare equivalent, runaway's, the sun's, perform a kindness and a small theft, giving the answer while hiding the question that makes the answer worth having. The better school and university texts print the word with its note, and the note is the lesson: that a classic text is not a found object but a maintained one, kept in repair by identifiable hands working under statable rules. A student who has followed this one word from the 1599 printing house through Warburton, Heath, Singer, Dyce, Dowden and Furness to Weis and the Folger has, without noticing the journey, learned the whole method of textual criticism on a single page of evidence. That is the deepest sense in which the crux earns its thirty variorum pages. The word is a school in itself, and its curriculum is the discipline this series exists to describe.

## The Defensible Text: A Closing Verdict

The findings can be gathered into the short form an editor's preface would use. Copy-text: the second quarto of 1599, throughout. The crux at 3.2.6: the copy-text's word, runawayes, retained without emendation, pointed as a singular possessive, the runaway identified as Phoebus the fleeing sun, with Theobald's optional article and Dowden's repunctuation recorded as interpretations, and the plural pointing of Hunter, Delius and the Folger recorded as the standing alternative. The conjectures of Heath, Singer, Jackson and the Faustus essayist: rejected, each for failure to convict the transmitted reading or to narrate its corruption, and preserved in the apparatus as the history of the line's fascination. The Folio at 3.2.15: its "grown" accepted against the copy-text's "grow", as the single licensed departure, the Folger's contrary retention noted. The first quarto: authority for staging, none for wording, its four lines and its great stage direction cited wherever the scene's performance is in question. Under that settlement the speech reads as the mainstream of modern editions presents it, and the presentation is not an accident of habit but the outcome of argued decisions at every contested point. A word that has filled thirty variorum pages can rest there: not solved beyond all dispute, for the dispute is part of its meaning now, but settled beyond the reach of any replacement yet proposed, the quarto's own word, doing its work in the dark it describes.

The settlement is falsifiable, and stating its falsifiers is the honest close. A single annotated copy of the second quarto, corrected in a contemporary hand at 3.2.6, would outweigh every argument in this study. A manuscript leaf of the speech in any hand of the period would reopen the identifications at once. A newly noticed use of "runaway" for a night-wanderer in a text of the 1590s would repair the plural parsing's lexical wound and force the pointing question back into genuine doubt. None of these has appeared in four centuries of searching, in an age that has recovered stranger documents from stranger places, and the verdict's confidence is calibrated to that long silence: the word stands, the sun sleeps, and the apparatus keeps the door through which better evidence, if it exists, will one day walk. Until it appears, the editor's duty is the one this study has performed throughout: print the quarto's word, point it with open eyes, and leave the whole evidence where the next reader can re-run the inquiry without taking any predecessor's word for anything at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do the second quarto and the Folio differ at the crux line?

In nothing but dress. The second quarto of 1599 prints "That runawayes eyes may wincke" and the First Folio of 1623 prints the same words with its own spelling and hyphenation, "run-awayes" in the transcriptions Rolfe collected from the early editions. The Folio's text of the play descends from the quarto line through the third and fourth quartos, so its agreement is inherited rather than independent; but the practical consequence is the same. No early book offers a rival word at 3.2.6, and every disagreement about the line, from Warburton to the modern editions, is a disagreement over the meaning and pointing of a word all the witnesses share.

### Q: Why can the first quarto not settle the runaway crux?

Because it never prints the line. The first quarto of 1597 gives Juliet only the opening four lines of the soliloquy, "Gallop apace" through "cloudie night immediately", and then brings in the Nurse with the ladder of cords. The crux stands at the sixth line of the full speech, inside the twenty-nine lines the short text omits. An editor facing a doubtful word normally asks whether the independent witness agrees; here the independent witness is silent, not from corruption but from absence. The crux must therefore be settled from the second quarto's authority and the internal evidence of the speech, which is why it has attracted conjecture instead of collation.

### Q: Which emendation did Knight and Collier print in their texts?

Jackson's "unawares". Zachary Jackson conjectured that the quarto's "runawayes" concealed "unawares", giving the clause the sense that surprise itself might sleep while Romeo came unseen. Charles Knight admitted the word into the text of his edition of the play, and John Payne Collier admitted it into his, so for a generation of nineteenth-century readers the printed line contained a word no early witness supports. Alexander Dyce pronounced the emendation "villainous", and the severity has stuck to it in the histories of editing. Modern editions have returned the word to the notes, where it survives as the standing example of a conjecture whose resemblance to the transmitted letters is too slight to license its sense.

### Q: What did Singer propose in place of the quarto's word?

"Rumourers". In his 1853 paper on the passage in Notes and Queries, S. W. Singer proposed that Juliet asks Night to close the eyes of rumourers, the carriers of gossip, so that Romeo may leap to her arms "untalked of and unseen". Singer argued that the unfamiliar word, written indistinctly in the copy, could have been misread by the printer as "runawayes", and he built on Benjamin Heath's earlier singular conjecture, "Rumour's". The proposal fits the paired nouns of the following line better than any rival, and the autumn correspondence of 1853 treated it with respect before declining it: a word Shakespeare is not known to use, however elegant, cannot displace a word every witness prints.

### Q: What does "wink" mean in "eyes may wink"?

It means close in sleep. In the English of the play's period, as Dowden's edition insists in its appendix on this line, "wink" commonly denotes shutting the eyes with the intention of keeping them shut, the sense the word carries in the phrase winking at a fault and in the sleep of sentries and gods. Juliet is not asking for a blink or a flicker; she is asking for the watcher of the world to be asleep, so that the night's business can proceed unobserved. The period sense is one of the crux's quiet solutions: half the line's difficulty dissolves once the verb is given back its sixteenth-century weight, and the sun's eye closing at his lodging needs no emendation to be intelligible.

### Q: Which commentator made Cupid the runaway?

The Reverend John Halpin, in an essay that Dowden's appendix calls learned and ingenious. Halpin argued that the fugitive whose eyes must close is Cupid, the runaway god of love, a child forever escaping his mother's governance in the mythography of the period; with Cupid asleep, the lovers would be free of desire's own surveillance and urgency alike. The case is the most beautiful of the identifications and among the weakest, since the speech never summons Cupid and since his vigilance is not what bars Romeo's way. Halpin's Cupid survives in the literature of the crux as proof that an identification can be charming, documented and wrong, a category the line has filled more richly than any other in the play.

### Q: How does the Merchant of Venice parallel bear on the crux?

It proves the word is Shakespeare's. In The Merchant of Venice, Lorenzo hurrying his companions warns that "the close night doth play the runaway" (2.6.40), using "the runaway" as a figure for a fleeing power that will not wait to be caught. Thomas Keightley deployed the parallel in the Notes and Queries correspondence of October 1853, defending the quarto reading of Juliet's line against that season's conjectures: a playwright who could call night a runaway could call the sun one, and a word with a home in the canon is not a misprint awaiting rescue. The parallel does not by itself identify Juliet's runaway, but it removes the premise of every emendation, the premise that the transmitted word is beneath the author.

### Q: What is at stake in pointing the word "runaway's" or "runaways'"?

The identity of the speech's antagonist. The early printers supplied no apostrophes, so the editor's pointing chooses between a singular fugitive and a plural crowd. Point it "runaway's" and the eyes are the sun's: the speech's first movement, the dismissal of the day, completes itself, and Juliet's opponent is time and light themselves. Point it "runaways'" and the eyes belong to night-wanderers in Verona's streets, and her opponent is society, the watching city. Productions, illustrations and school editions inherit the choice invisibly. The Arden and Oxford texts point the singular; the Folger Shakespeare points the plural, following Hunter and Delius. The disagreement is the largest interpretive fork in the speech that no difference of document causes.

### Q: Which pointing does the Folger Shakespeare print at the crux?

The plural: "runaways'". The Folger text, edited by Barbara Mowat and Paul Werstine from the second quarto as copy-text, reads "That runaways' eyes may wink", understanding the eyes as those of persons abroad in the streets at night whose watching the curtain must defeat. The choice descends from Joseph Hunter's explanation, adopted by Delius and declared satisfying by Rolfe, and the Folger's note records the singular alternative and the word's strange fame. The same edition keeps the second quarto's "grow" where the Folio reads "grown" at 3.2.15, a pair of decisions that marks the Folger as the most conservative of the standard modern texts at this speech: where judgment is required, it stays nearest the copy-text's letter.

### Q: How much print did the New Variorum give this single word?

Some thirty pages. H. H. Furness, compiling the New Variorum edition of Romeo and Juliet, devoted the most conspicuous part of his appendix to a synopsis of the guesses about "runawayes"; Rolfe's edition counts the condensed summary at twenty-eight octavo pages of fine print, and Dowden's appendix raises it to thirty closely printed pages. The word had already drawn R. G. White's essay "Who was Juliet's Runaway?", Collier's remark that the suggestions outnumbered the word's letters, and an autumn of correspondence in Notes and Queries in 1853. No other substantive in the play has a literature of this scale, and the scale is itself evidence editors weigh: a reading that has resisted three centuries of motivated replacement has passed a test no conjecture can sit for it.

### Q: What changes in the opening quatrain between the two quartos?

Three of its four lines. The first quarto sends the horses "To Phoebus mansion" where the second has "Towards Phoebus' lodging"; it has Phaeton "quickly bring you thether" where the second has him "whip you to the west"; and it reads "send in cloudie night" where the second reads "bring in cloudy night". Each variant replaces a specific, patterned word with a flatter near-synonym, the signature of a text transmitted through performance or memory rather than through the author's papers. The first line alone, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds", is common to both. Editors print the second quarto's quatrain entire, and cite the first quarto's forms as the clearest small specimen in the play of how its wording degrades in reported transmission.

### Q: Why does the first quarto bring the Nurse in so early?

Because its text of the scene is built for speed, whether the shortening came from a company abridging the play for performance, from the memory of a reporter who retained the famous opening and lost the rest, or from both causes in succession, as the rival accounts of H. R. Hoppe and Kathleen Irace divide the explanation. The stage direction it gives the entrance, the Nurse wringing her hands with the ladder of cords in her lap, is a spectator's or a player's detail, and the compression throws the scene's irony forward: desire has four lines before catastrophe answers it. What the timing cannot be used for is the wording of the omitted lines, which the edition of the speech takes from the second quarto alone.

### Q: What does the second quarto read where many editions print "grown bold"?

"Grow bold". At 3.2.15 the copy-text reads "till strange love grow bold", and the First Folio reads "grown bold". The majority of modern editions take the Folio's participle, judging the quarto's form a limping idiom that a corrector in the quarto chain or the Folio's printing house completed; the Folger Shakespeare retains "grow", declining to let a derivative witness correct its copy-text. The variant is the speech's one clean case of a substantive division between the two great early authorities, and its smallness is instructive: set against the crux, where the witnesses agree and only the meaning is disputed, "grow" and "grown" show a dispute of the opposite kind, two documents, two forms, and a choice that grammar and idiom must arbitrate.

### Q: What did Theobald actually change in printing "th' runaway's"?

He added a word that is not a word of substance: the article "the", compressed as "th'". Lewis Theobald, following a suggestion of Warburton's, printed "That th' runaway's eyes may wink", making explicit the definite fugitive the clause implies, and the commentator Allen later supplied the mechanism, proposing that a spoken or written "the" could have been absorbed by the final letter of "that" in transmission. Dowden accepted Theobald's pointing into his text. The intervention is the minimum that grammar can ask, and it divides editors still: editions that print the line without the article keep the copy-text's exact count of words, while Theobald's form survives as a reminder that pointing and supplying are judgments, renewed by every editor who sets the line.

### Q: Why do editors accept the Folio's "grown" yet keep the quarto's "runawayes"?

Because the two cases fail opposite tests. At the crux the copy-text's word can be construed, as the sun's eyes closing in the period sense of "wink", and a reading that construes may not be displaced by a derivative witness or by conjecture. At "grow bold" the copy-text's form is the weaker idiom, the sentence limps, and the Folio's "grown" completes the phrase an editor can show the language wanting. Greg's rationale of copy-text licenses both decisions: substantives are judged on their merits case by case, authority yields to demonstrated defect, and defect must be demonstrated, not asserted. The pair of lines, nine lines apart in one speech, is the neatest classroom demonstration in the play of the difference between a crux of meaning and a crux of transmission.

### Q: What should a reader do when two editions print the crux differently?

Read the note before choosing a side. The difference between "runaway's" and "runaways'" is a difference of pointing supplied by editors, since the early texts print no apostrophe, and any responsible edition records the alternative in its textual note at 3.2.6. A reader who finds the plural in the Folger and the singular in the Arden is not facing a corruption but a judgment, and the notes lay out the evidence: the speech's opening movement for the sun, the fear of witnesses for the crowd. Choosing between them is a legitimate act of interpretation, provided it is done knowingly. What no reader should do is treat either pointing as the quarto's own, or suppose that the word itself, which no edition changes, is in doubt.

### Q: Has the crux ever been settled to general agreement?

In the text, yes; in the commentary, never quite. Every standard modern edition prints the quarto's word, and the age of replacement conjectures closed when Knight's and Collier's "unawares" was withdrawn from the texts that had carried it. On that level the crux is settled: the word is "runaway", and the only live question is its pointing. Beneath that settlement the identifications continue to divide readers, the sun against the night-wanderers, because the evidence underdetermines the choice and each parsing flatters a different reading of the speech. The distinction matters for how the line is taught: the documentary question has an answer, the interpretive question has a majority, and an honest edition keeps the two from being confused.

### Q: What happens to the scene's timing in the first quarto's short version?

The scene accelerates into emblem. Juliet invokes the sunset, and within four lines the Nurse is in the doorway wringing her hands, the rope ladder in her lap, and the news that will unmake the wedding day begins its equivocal approach. The audience's knowledge of the street fight does the work the soliloquy does in the full text: irony replaces inhabitation. The bride's desire is stated rather than dwelt in, and the catastrophe falls on a wish barely uttered instead of on an aria the house has been made to share for thirty-three lines. Editors and directors who cut the speech toward the quarto's proportions are repeating, usually without knowing it, the first quarto's own solution to the scene, which is one reason the short text remains a living document for the stage.

### Q: Why did the nineteenth century spend so much print on one word?

Because the word sat at the crossing of the century's two great Shakespeare industries, editing and appreciation, and resisted both. The variorum tradition, crowned by Furness, made completeness a duty: every conjecture ever printed had to be collected, weighed and shelved, and this line had attracted conjectures since Heath's "Rumour's" in 1765. At the same time the line stood in the most admired speech of the most admired heroine, so that its difficulty felt like a lock on a treasure room; White's "Who was Juliet's Runaway?" and Collier's quip about the letters are the sounds of an age that believed the right word, once found, would release the speech's whole meaning. The print survives as a monument to that belief, and as a warning the textual notes of modern editions quietly repeat: the word was never lost, only doubted.
