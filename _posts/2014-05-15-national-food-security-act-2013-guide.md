The National Food Security Act, 2013 is the statute that converted India's food welfare schemes into legal entitlements, and the conversion is the whole story. Before it, a family that was refused grain at a ration shop had been denied a benefit that a government had chosen to give and could choose to withdraw. After it, the same family is denied something the law says it is owed, and the law itself supplies the remedy: a cash allowance for the grain that did not arrive, a named officer who must hear the complaint, and a commission that can penalise the official who ignores that officer. Whether those remedies work as designed is a separate question, and it belongs to the record of implementation. What the statute did on its face is not in doubt. It took the targeted public distribution system, the anganwadi meal, the school midday meal and a maternity benefit, and rewrote them as rights held by identified persons against the state.

![The National Food Security Act 2013 and the entitlements it created, showing what a household is owed and the remedy when supply fails - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The Act does this through one design choice that determines almost everything else about it, and a reader who grasps that choice understands the statute. Coverage is fixed as a percentage of the population. Section 3(2) extends the grain entitlement to up to seventy-five per cent of the rural population and up to fifty per cent of the urban population, and section 9 has the Central Government determine, state by state, how many persons that percentage yields. Eligibility is therefore not a finding that a household is poor. It is a finding that a household falls inside a numerical allocation that its state received. The Act creates a genuine legal entitlement, enforceable in ways this article sets out in detail, but the holder of the entitlement is selected administratively, by a list drawn up inside a quota. That is the namable claim of the statute, and every operational controversy it has generated, about exclusion, about the frozen census, about the deserving and the undeserving, is a working out of that single decision.

## What existed before, and why a scheme was not enough

Public distribution of grain in India is older than the republic. Rationing began as a wartime measure and continued after independence as a general system of government grain shops in towns and cities, expanding in the nineteen sixties as domestic production faltered and imports rose. The scarcity of the middle nineteen sixties, when India imported grain on a vast scale under the United States PL-480 programme and lived, in the phrase of the time, from ship to mouth, fixed the permanent shape of the system: the state would buy grain from its own farmers at announced prices in surplus regions, hold it centrally, and sell it cheaply everywhere. The Green Revolution that followed was built on that promise of purchase, which is why procurement and distribution have been two ends of one machine ever since, and why the statute of 2013, which legislates for the distribution end, cannot be understood without the procurement end it presupposes. For most of that history the public distribution system was broadly universal: any household could hold a ration card and buy a quota of grain, sugar and kerosene at controlled prices. The system was expensive and leaky, and its benefits reached the well connected as readily as the hungry, but it did not require the state to decide, household by household, who was poor enough to eat at a subsidised price.

The universal system rested on an institution built in the nineteen sixties. The Food Corporation of India, set up in 1965 under the Food Corporations Act, 1964, became the procurement and storage arm of the Union: it bought grain from farmers at the prices the government announced, held the buffer stock, and moved grain from surplus states to deficit states, while the distribution arm ran through the state civil supplies departments and the licensed fair price shops. The two arms met in the central pool, the stock from which allocations to the states were made, and it is that pool, with that history, that section 2(2) of the Act defines and section 22 obliges the Centre to fill. The statutory scheme of 2013 did not design a supply chain. It inherited one, complete with its procurement geography, its storage losses and its political economy, and wrote entitlements against the grain that chain already moved. Much of what is puzzling in the Act, the protection of historical offtake in Schedule IV, the Centre's duty to transport to state depots, the state's duty from the depot to the shop door, is the inherited chain described as a set of legal duties, link by link.

That changed in 1997, when the system was retargeted. The targeted public distribution system divided households into those below the poverty line and those above it, concentrated the deepest subsidy on the below poverty line group, and in December 2000 added the Antyodaya Anna Yojana for the poorest households of all, a scheme whose name and launch date the Act itself records in the explanation to section 3. Targeting was meant to cut the food subsidy bill and aim it at need. What it required, in exchange, was an accurate official answer to a question Indian administration has never answered well: which households are poor.

The answer was produced by periodic below poverty line censuses conducted by the states against criteria set by the Centre, and it was wrong in both directions at scale. Households with means appeared on the lists and drew subsidised grain they did not need, which is an inclusion error and a fiscal cost. Households in genuine destitution were left off, which is an exclusion error, and for the household concerned it is the difference between eating from the ration shop and not eating from it. Bogus and duplicate ration cards multiplied in many states. Because the lists were the gateway to grain, they became objects of local patronage and local capture. The fiscal critique of the old universal system, that it subsidised the non-poor, was answered by a targeted system whose identification machinery could not reliably find the poor.

The machinery deserves a closer look, because the Act's design is unintelligible without it. The below poverty line censuses proceeded by scoring: field staff visited households and awarded marks against socio economic indicators set for the purpose, and a household's aggregate score, cut off at a line fixed by the number of poor the state was permitted to recognise, decided its status. A scored census of that kind has three failure modes that no amount of care in the field removes. It measures what is visible to an enumerator on a single visit, which is a poor proxy for the income of a household in the informal economy. It is conducted at intervals of years, while poverty in an agrarian economy is a condition households fall into and climb out of with seasons, illness and harvests. And its cut off is set by a quota derived from a poverty estimate, so that the census is asked to find a predetermined number of poor households rather than the number that exists. The measurement of poverty itself was contested at the expert level throughout the period: the committee chaired by Dr N. C. Saxena, appointed to advise on the methodology for identifying the poor, recommended in 2009 that a far larger share of the rural population be recognised, and that identification proceed by automatic exclusion of the visibly well off and automatic inclusion of the visibly destitute rather than by scored ranking of everyone in between. That recommendation, made four years before the Act, is the intellectual bridge between the targeted system and the percentage design that replaced it.

It was into this gap between promise and list that the courts stepped, a full decade before Parliament legislated.

The litigation did not proceed in a vacuum, and its social machinery is part of the Act's prehistory. Behind the petition stood a countrywide network of organisations, academics, journalists and activists that came to be known as the Right to Food Campaign, which supplied the Court with field evidence, pressed state governments between hearings, and converted interim orders into local monitoring of schools, anganwadis and ration shops. The Court's Commissioners institutionalised that monitoring from above. Legislation drafted a decade later, in a Parliament where the campaign's demands formed the background of the debate and its experts had advised the National Advisory Council on the draft Bill, inherited both the jurisprudence and the network: the social audits, vigilance committees and grievance officers of the Act are the statutory form of a monitoring practice that civil society and the Court's Commissioners had improvised together. The case itself remained alive on the Supreme Court's board after the Act, its later orders turning to the implementation of the statute it had provoked, which makes the litigation that rare thing in Indian public law, a proceeding that wrote its own successor and then stayed to supervise it.

## The court before the statute

In 2001, drought in Rajasthan produced starvation deaths in districts where the Food Corporation of India held large stocks of grain in storage. The People's Union for Civil Liberties petitioned the Supreme Court, in Writ Petition (Civil) No. 196 of 2001, arguing that the right to life under Article 21 of the Constitution includes the right to food, and that a state which holds food while people starve for want of it violates that right. The Court did not write a new programme. It did something more legally interesting: it took the food schemes the governments already ran and converted them, by interim order, into enforceable entitlements.

The order of 28 November 2001 is the foundation document. It addressed eight schemes, among them the public distribution system, the Antyodaya Anna Yojana, the midday meal scheme, the integrated child development services, the Annapurna scheme for destitute senior citizens, the old age pension scheme and the maternity benefit scheme, and it directed governments to implement them as obligations rather than as discretionary welfare. For the midday meal it went further and directed that the dry grain ration then given in many states be replaced by a cooked meal served in every government and government assisted primary school. The Court kept the petition alive under a continuing mandamus, appointed its own Commissioners, Dr N. C. Saxena and Shri S. R. Sankaran, to monitor compliance and report, and returned to the case over the following years with further directions on identification of the poorest households, on the licensing and conduct of fair price shop dealers, and on the universalisation of child nutrition services.

The constitutional route the Court took matters for understanding what kind of right the Act later codified. Article 21 guarantees that no person shall be deprived of life except according to procedure established by law, and the Court's jurisprudence had long read life to mean more than animal existence. Article 47, among the Directive Principles of State Policy, records that the state shall regard the raising of the level of nutrition and the standard of living of its people as among its primary duties. Directive Principles are not enforceable by any court, and a statute was not required to make nutrition justiciable. What the litigation did was to use the enforceable right to life to discipline the executive's own schemes: the government that announced a scheme for feeding children or the destitute could be held to the scheme's terms as a matter of fundamental right. The interim orders worked at the level of the shop and the school, not only at the level of principle. The order of 28 November 2001 itself directed that fair price shop licences be cancelled where dealers kept shops closed during stipulated hours, failed to supply below poverty line families at the fixed rates, retained the cards of households, made false entries in cards, or diverted grain to the open market. A court that writes licence conditions for ration shops is not declaring an abstraction. It is administering a food system, and it continued to administer one, through its Commissioners' reports and periodic compliance hearings, until Parliament built a statutory administration of its own.

The eight schemes the order of 28 November 2001 converted are worth naming, because the Act of 2013 can be read as their codification. They were the targeted public distribution system itself; the Antyodaya Anna Yojana for the poorest households; the mid day meal scheme; the Integrated Child Development Services; the Annapurna scheme of free grain for destitute elderly persons; the national old age pension scheme; the national maternity benefit scheme; and the national family benefit scheme. Map that list onto the statute and the lineage is unmistakable: the distribution system and Antyodaya became section 3, the mid day meal and child development services became sections 5 and 6, the maternity benefit scheme became section 4's cash entitlement, and the conversion technique itself, scheme benefits declared to be legal entitlements and enforced as such, became the Act's governing idea. Parliament in 2013 did not borrow the Court's conclusion. It enacted the Court's method, and extended it to a scale of coverage no interim order had attempted.

The significance for the statute is direct. When Parliament finally legislated in 2013, it was not inventing a right to food and imposing it on a blank administrative field. It was codifying, extending and systematising entitlements that the Supreme Court had been enforcing, scheme by scheme and order by order, for twelve years. The Act's structure follows the litigation's map. Grain through the distribution system, meals for young children, a cooked meal at school, support for pregnant women and nursing mothers: the four corners of Chapter II of the Act are the four corners of the Court's interim orders. A statute that began as a court's answer to starvation deaths carries that origin in its design, most visibly in its preference for wide coverage over precise targeting, because the litigation had taught everyone involved what identification failure costs the household that is failed.

## From campaign promise to enacted text

The political route ran alongside the judicial one. The Right to Food Campaign, a network of organisations and individuals that formed around the litigation, pressed for legislation that would convert the Court's orders into a comprehensive statute. In October 2010 the National Advisory Council produced a draft National Food Security Bill proposing legal entitlements covering about three quarters of the population. An expert committee chaired by Dr C. Rangarajan, appointed by the Prime Minister, examined that proposal in January 2011 and recommended a smaller coverage and a phased approach, on the ground that procurement and fiscal capacity would not sustain the wider promise. The Bill introduced in the Lok Sabha on 22 December 2011 by the Minister for Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution, Shri K. V. Thomas, steered between the two: it proposed covering seventy-five per cent of the rural and fifty per cent of the urban population, but divided the covered population into a priority category receiving seven kilograms of grain per person per month at the lowest prices and a general category receiving a smaller quantity at a higher price.

The Standing Committee on Food, Consumer Affairs and Public Distribution, which reported on 17 January 2013, dismantled that two tier structure. It recommended a single included category with a uniform entitlement of five kilograms per person per month at subsidised prices, the collapse of the priority and general distinction within the covered population, and caution on replacing grain with cash transfers before banking infrastructure could support them. It also recommended that the Centre define the criteria by which households would be excluded, in consultation with the states, rather than leaving the whole of identification to inference, and it proposed that commodities beyond grain, pulses and sugar among them, be supplied in addition. The enacted Act accepted the unified category and the uniform quantity, left identification to state guidelines without a central exclusion code, and did not take up the wider commodity basket, which survives only as the diversification direction in section 12(2)(f) and the aspirations of Schedule III. The committee stage is therefore where the Act acquired its final shape: one covered class, one quantity, grain only. The government accepted the substance of that redesign. The Bill that returned to Parliament in 2013 carried one covered class, the priority households, at five kilograms per person, with the Antyodaya households preserved inside it at thirty-five kilograms per household.

The executive then jumped the legislative queue. With Parliament not in session and the session's end approaching, the National Food Security Ordinance, 2013 was promulgated on 5 July 2013, bringing the entitlements into force by executive legislation. A Bill to replace the ordinance was introduced on 7 August 2013. The Lok Sabha passed it on 26 August 2013 and the Rajya Sabha on 2 September 2013. Presidential assent followed on 10 September 2013, and the Act, numbered 20 of 2013, repealed the ordinance while saving everything done under it. Section 1(3) declares that the Act shall be deemed to have come into force on 5 July 2013, the ordinance date, so the legal entitlement runs without a gap from the ordinance to the statute.

### When did the Act begin to operate, and what did it replace?

It began on 5 July 2013, the date of the National Food Security Ordinance, 2013, and section 1(3) deems the Act itself to have commenced on that date. The Act repealed the ordinance under section 45 and saved all action taken under it, so entitlements, identifications and directions made in the ordinance period continued as acts under the statute.

The route matters for one reason beyond chronology. An entitlement that began by ordinance and was then enacted by Parliament, in a text shaped substantially by a standing committee and descended from twelve years of court orders, is not a routine welfare announcement dressed in statutory language. Its drafters wrote it to be claimed. The chapters on the allowance and on grievance redressal, which have no equivalent in most Indian welfare legislation, are the proof.

One constitutional point completes the passage history, because it explains the Act's otherwise puzzling shape, a central statute that operates entirely through state machinery. Parliament's competence to legislate on food distribution rests on the Concurrent List, whose Entry 33 covers trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, foodstuffs. Food supply is therefore a field on which both Parliament and the state legislatures may legislate, and the states had built the administrative system, the civil supplies departments, the shop licensing under orders made under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, and the local knowledge of households, over decades. A central Act in a concurrent field can set the entitlements, the ceilings, the prices and the grievance structure for the whole country, as this one does, while leaving the operation to the machinery the states already possess, and section 32's preservation of state schemes above the floor is the textual trace of that constitutional settlement. The Act is best read as a federation bargain reduced to a statute: the Union supplies the grain, the money and the legal form of the right; the states supply the lists, the shops and the officers; and each side's failures are actionable, if at all, in the machinery the bargain created.

## The architecture of the National Food Security Act

The Act has forty-five sections arranged in thirteen chapters, and four schedules. Its organisation is itself an argument about what a food law must contain, so the map is worth reading before the provisions. Chapter I holds the short title, extent and commencement and a long definitions section. Chapter II, the provisions for food security, contains the entitlements: grain under the distribution system in section 3, nutritional support to pregnant women and lactating mothers in section 4, nutritional support to children in section 5, prevention and management of child malnutrition in section 6, and the implementation of the supporting schemes in section 7. Chapter III is a single section, section 8, creating the right to a food security allowance. Chapter IV governs identification: the determination of coverage in section 9, state identification of households in section 10, and publication of the lists in section 11. Chapter V, section 12, mandates reforms in the distribution system. Chapter VI, section 13, is the women empowerment chapter, a single provision on the head of the household. Chapter VII builds the grievance machinery: an internal mechanism in section 14, a District Grievance Redressal Officer in section 15, and a State Food Commission in section 16, with ancillary provisions running to section 21. Chapters VIII, IX and X distribute obligations among the Central Government, the state governments and local authorities. Chapter XI covers transparency and accountability, Chapter XII the provisions for advancing food security beyond the entitlements themselves, and Chapter XIII the miscellaneous provisions, including penalties in section 33 and the repeal of the ordinance in section 45.

The schedules carry operational content that other statutes would put in rules. Schedule I fixes the subsidised prices. Schedule II fixes nutritional standards, in calories and protein, for each category of meal. Schedule III lists the objectives for advancing food and nutritional security that governments are to progressively realise under section 31. Schedule IV records the state wise allocation of foodgrains protected by the second proviso to section 3(1). Section 37 empowers the Central Government to amend any of the schedules by notification, laid before Parliament, which means the prices, the nutritional standards and the protected allocations can all be changed by the executive without a fresh Act, a delegation whose significance appears when the price question is examined below.

Schedule III deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the only part of the Act that describes the food system the drafters ultimately wanted, as opposed to the one they could legislate into claims. Read with section 31, which obliges the Centre, the states and local authorities to take specified measures progressively to advance food and nutritional security, the Schedule sets objectives across the whole chain: revitalising agriculture, including its diversification toward animal husbandry and fisheries; ensuring access to inputs, credit and insurance for producers; protecting the livelihood security of small and marginal farmers; securing access to food for people in remote, hilly and tribal areas; providing safe drinking water, sanitation and health care alongside nutrition; spreading nutrition and health education; and addressing malnutrition, including its micronutrient dimension. None of this is enforceable, and the Schedule does not pretend otherwise; its legal character is stated in section 31's language of progressive realisation. Its function in the statute is different. It records, inside the Act itself, the distance between the grain entitlement the law could make justiciable and the food security its own title promises, and it gives courts, commissions and campaigners a text of the legislature's own admissions to quote back at the executive. Aspirational schedules are often dismissed as decoration. In a statute that grew out of litigation conducted by quotation, they are ammunition laid down in advance.

Two features of the definitions repay attention. Section 2(6) defines food security itself, narrowly and operationally, as the supply of the entitled quantity of foodgrains and meal specified under Chapter II. The Act does not promise the absence of hunger in the abstract. It promises specified quantities, of specified things, to specified persons, and defines its own central concept as the delivery of exactly those quantities. Section 2(5) defines foodgrains as rice, wheat or coarse grains, or any combination of them, conforming to quality norms the Central Government determines by order. The entitlement is a grain entitlement. Pulses, edible oils and other foods enter the Act only at its margins, in Schedule III's aspirations and in section 12's direction that the commodities distributed be diversified over a period of time. A reader who expects a nutrition statute will find a grain statute with a nutrition chapter attached, and the distinction explains several of the criticisms examined later in this article.

Two further definitions shape the statute's daily operation. Section 2(16) defines the ration card as a document issued under an order or authority of the state government for the purchase of essential commodities from fair price shops under the system, which locates the card's legal origin in state orders rather than in the Act itself: the Act commands the entitlement, the state issues the instrument. Section 2(9) defines meal as hot cooked or pre-cooked and heated before service meal, or take home ration, as may be prescribed by the Central Government, a definition written against the substitution of packaged commodities for cooked food in the feeding programmes. And one absence in section 2 is as significant as any presence in it. The Act nowhere defines household, the unit to which ration cards are issued, by which the Antyodaya entitlement is measured, and whose head section 13 renames. Household composition, the treatment of joint families, of single member households, of the destitute person without a family, is left to the state orders and guidelines under which cards are made. The statute's central unit is an administrative artefact, defined in practice by the list making that section 10 authorises, and a remarkable amount of the litigation and grievance the Act generates is, at bottom, argument about a word Parliament chose not to define.

## The core entitlement: grain, measured per person

Section 3 is the Act's centre of gravity, and it is drafted as a right, not as a scheme description. Every person belonging to a priority household, identified under section 10, shall be entitled to receive five kilograms of foodgrains per person per month at the subsidised prices specified in Schedule I from the state government under the targeted public distribution system. The unit of the entitlement is the person, not the household, which is a deliberate repudiation of the older practice under which a ration card carried a fixed household quota regardless of family size. A household of six is owed thirty kilograms. A household of two is owed ten.

The first proviso to section 3(1) preserves the Antyodaya Anna Yojana inside the new structure. Households covered by that scheme are entitled to thirty-five kilograms of foodgrains per household per month at the same Schedule I prices, to the extent the Central Government specifies for each state under the scheme. The two units therefore run in parallel: the per person measure for the general covered population, and the per household measure for the poorest group, whose entitlement the drafters carried across from the pre-existing scheme rather than recalculating it. The explanation to the section records that the Antyodaya Anna Yojana is the scheme launched by the Central Government on 25 December 2000, as modified from time to time, which anchors the carried over category in a named, dated predecessor rather than leaving it to inference.

The second proviso performs a different kind of protection, this one for states rather than households. If the annual allocation of foodgrains to a state under the Act is less than the average annual offtake of foodgrains over the preceding three years under the normal targeted public distribution system, the offtake is protected, at prices the Central Government determines, and the state is allocated foodgrains as specified in Schedule IV. The proviso answered a concrete fear expressed by states with historically high lifting, several of which ran distribution systems broader than the new percentages would fund: that conversion to the Act would cut their grain. Schedule IV's table of state wise allocations, totalling a little under fifty-five million tonnes in the table as enacted, is the written form of that guarantee.

Schedule I itself contains a clause that most summaries omit, and it changes the character of the famous prices. Eligible households are entitled to foodgrains at a subsidised price not exceeding three rupees per kilogram for rice, two rupees per kilogram for wheat and one rupee per kilogram for coarse grains, for a period of three years from the commencement of the Act, and thereafter at such price as the Central Government may fix from time to time, not exceeding the minimum support price for wheat and coarse grains and the derived minimum support price for rice. The prices of three, two and one were therefore never enacted as permanent. They were enacted as a three year opening position, after which the price becomes an executive decision disciplined only by a ceiling set at the support price. What the Centre did with that power is examined in the section on the price that ceased to be charged. The statutory position, stated plainly, is that Parliament fixed the opening prices and delegated the future ones.

Two features of the entitlement's arithmetic reward attention, because both are invisible in summary accounts. The first is a qualification inside the Antyodaya proviso that summaries drop: the thirty-five kilogram entitlement runs to the extent specified by the Central Government for each state in the Antyodaya scheme, which means the poorest category's numbers are fixed by the scheme the proviso incorporates, and the state wise extent of Antyodaya coverage is an executive specification under a statutory umbrella. The second is an anomaly produced by the two units running side by side. Because the priority entitlement is per person and the Antyodaya entitlement is per household, the two measures cross as household size grows. A priority household of eight is owed forty kilograms a month. An Antyodaya household of eight is owed thirty-five. The poorest category, measured per head, receives less grain per person than the general covered category once the household is large enough, and the disadvantage grows with every additional member, falling hardest on exactly the large, destitute households the Antyodaya scheme exists to single out. No drafter intended that result; it is the arithmetic residue of carrying a household based scheme entitlement, fixed in the year 2000 for the household sizes then assumed, into a statute whose general measure is per person. It stands in the text as a warning about the whole enterprise of statutory entitlement design: the unit in which a right is measured is a distributive decision, and an unexamined unit will distribute in ways nobody chose.

The entitlement is also a quality entitlement, in a limited but real sense. Section 2(5) ties the definition of foodgrains to conformity with quality norms the Central Government determines by order, so grain that fails the prescribed norms is not, in strictness, the foodgrains section 3 promises. The grievance system takes the point up from the beneficiary's side: the Food Security Allowance Rules, 2015 assign complaints about the quality of foodgrains to the District Grievance Redressal Officer, to be dealt with within a period not exceeding fifteen days from the complaint. The quality limb then collides with rule 9 of the same Rules, under which the allowance is not payable to a person who refuses the entitlement on the ground of quality, a pairing that leaves the beneficiary who is offered rotten grain with a complaint route for the grain and no money remedy for the month, and which critics of the Rules have identified as the point where the allowance's design protects the system against the claimant rather than the claimant against the system.

Section 3(3) adds a small flexibility: a state government may provide wheat flour in lieu of the entitled quantity of foodgrains, in accordance with guidelines the Central Government specifies. The provision recognises that in several regions the delivered commodity is flour rather than grain, and it keeps the substitution inside the entitlement rather than outside it.

### Where does the grain in a ration shop come from?

It comes from the central pool, the stock procured by the Central Government and the state governments through minimum support price operations and maintained for allocations under the distribution system. Section 22 obliges the Centre to allocate the required quantity from that pool to each state and to transport it to designated depots in the state.

The supply chain behind the entitlement is therefore a procurement chain. Section 2(2) defines the central pool by reference to procurement at the minimum support price, and section 22(4) requires the Central Government to procure foodgrains for the pool through its own agencies and the state governments and theirs, to allocate the grain to states, to provide for its transportation to designated depots, and to create and maintain scientific storage. The quantity each state receives is set by section 22(2) according to the number of persons belonging to eligible households identified in that state, which ties the physical supply of grain to the identification exercise examined below. Where the grain originates, in which states it is procured, at what support price and with what consequences for cropping patterns, is the subject of [the legal framework governing minimum support price and procurement](/2014/07/01/msp-procurement-legal-framework-india/), which owns that analysis; the point for this statute is that the entitlement in section 3 is only as real as the pool behind section 22, and the Act says so in its own structure by placing the procurement and allocation duties in a chapter of obligations rather than in a preamble of hopes.

## Coverage: the percentage that decides who holds the right

Why the two figures stand where they do is a question the passage history answers. The National Advisory Council's draft of 2010 proposed coverage of the order of seventy-five per cent of the whole population. The expert committee chaired by Dr C. Rangarajan, which reported in January 2011 on the Council's draft, recommended a materially smaller coverage tied more closely to measured poverty. The enacted blend, seventy-five per cent of the rural population and fifty per cent of the urban, sits between those positions and produces its celebrated fraction by arithmetic rather than by announcement: because roughly sixty-nine per cent of India's population was rural in the census of 2011, covering three quarters of the rural population and half of the urban yields coverage of about two thirds of the whole. The asymmetry between the two percentages carries the design's reasoning about where identification fails worst. Measured poverty incidence was higher in the countryside, and rural households were the population the below poverty line censuses had most visibly failed. The urban share is lower not because urban hunger is rarer in kind but because the urban poor are harder to enumerate at all, the migrant worker without a settled address or documents being the limiting case of every list based system, and a higher urban percentage would have multiplied paper entitlements the machinery could not attach to findable households. Even at fifty per cent, the urban list is where the Act's identification problem concentrates, which is why the grievances of the urban excluded recur through the machinery's work.

Section 3(2) provides that the entitlements at subsidised prices shall extend up to seventy-five per cent of the rural population and up to fifty per cent of the urban population. Two words in that sentence carry the design. The first is the pair of percentages, which convert the abstract idea of covering roughly two thirds of the country into two administrable numbers, one for the village and one for the town, reflecting the judgment that rural poverty is deeper and rural markets thinner than urban ones. The second is the phrase up to. The Act fixes a ceiling, not a floor, for each state: a state is authorised to cover as much as the stated share of its population, and the Central Government determines, under section 9, the percentage coverage for each state within that ceiling and the total number of persons to be covered, calculated on the basis of the population estimates as per the census of which the relevant figures have been published.

Applied across the country on the census figures available at enactment, the percentages produced intended coverage of about 81.35 crore persons, the figure the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution has used in describing the Act's reach and which the Cabinet adopted in its decision of 29 November 2023 on free foodgrains. Roughly two thirds of the population of India was thereby made the holder of a legal right to buy grain at three rupees, two rupees or one rupee a kilogram, a scale of statutory entitlement without close parallel in Indian legislation.

The design was chosen against an alternative that the drafting history shows was considered and rejected at every stage. The National Advisory Council draft, the Rangarajan Committee's response, the introduced Bill and the Standing Committee's report all argue, in different vocabularies, about the same fork: identify the poor by a test and entitle them, or draw a wide numerical boundary and entitle everyone inside it. The test based route had been tried since 1997 and had produced the exclusion and inclusion errors described earlier. The percentage route does not pretend to find the poor. It fixes a share of the population large enough that most of the poor are arithmetically certain to be inside it, accepts that many non-poor households will be inside it too, and moves the identification question from whether a household is poor to the narrower question of which households fill the state's allocation. Section 10 gives that narrower question to the states.

The cost of the design is written into section 9's census anchor. The total number of persons to be covered in a state is calculated on population estimates from the census of which the relevant figures have been published. A decennial census is therefore the denominator of the entitlement, and until a later census's figures are published and the determination revised, population growth, urbanisation and migration change the real share of the living population that the fixed number represents. The covered number stays still while the population moves. Every year that passes without a fresh determination widens the gap between the percentage on paper and the percentage in fact, and the households squeezed out by that arithmetic are disproportionately the young, the newly formed household and the family that has moved for work. This is not an implementation failure that better administration can cure. It is the foreseeable consequence of the section 9 mechanism, and it is the strongest structural criticism of the Act that does not depend on disputing the value of the entitlement itself.

Within the national ceilings, the determination under section 9 did not give every state the same percentages. The state wise coverage fixed by the Central Government varied with the measured incidence of poverty, so that poorer states received rural coverage shares well above the all India average the two figures suggest, and better off states received shares well below it. The percentages in section 3(2) therefore describe the envelope of the national settlement rather than the entitlement pattern of any particular state, and two households in identical poverty on opposite sides of a state border can stand in different relations to the Act because the quotas above them differ. That consequence is inherent in a design that federalises identification while nationalising the ceiling, and it is compounded for the states protected by the second proviso to section 3(1), whose allocations under Schedule IV reflect historical offtake rather than the new percentages, so that the map of who is fed by the Act is a palimpsest of the old system's geography written under the new system's numbers. The Act nationalised the right. It did not, and by its structure could not, nationalise the probability that any given poor household holds it.

The percentages also freeze into an absolute number, and the number is worth stating because official statements repeat it so often that its origin is forgotten. Applying the state wise percentages to the census of 2011 produced a covered population of about 81.35 crore persons, the figure the Union Cabinet's free foodgrain decision of November 2023 still used a decade later to describe the Act's beneficiaries. The entitlement population of India under this statute is therefore a computed quantity from the demography of 2011, not a count of any later year. Every birth since the determination adds a person the ceiling does not see, every state's share of the national total is a share of a 2011 map, and the system's administrators work with beneficiary numbers that are exact, auditable and steadily more historical. A right defined by quota is only as current as the census beneath the quota, and this Act's quota rests on a census that the section 9 mechanism will replace only when a later census's figures are published and a fresh determination is made.

## Identification: the list that turns a percentage into a ration card

Between the percentage and the person stands section 10. The state government shall, within the number of persons determined under section 9 for the rural and urban areas, identify the households to be covered under the Antyodaya Anna Yojana, in accordance with the guidelines applicable to that scheme, and identify the remaining households as priority households, in accordance with such guidelines as the state government may specify. The first proviso obliged states to complete the identification as soon as possible and in any event within a period not exceeding three hundred and sixty-five days after the commencement of the Act. The second proviso protected the transition: until identification was complete, the state continued to receive its allocation under the existing distribution system, so no household was to lose grain because its state was slow to draw the new lists. Section 10(2) requires the list to be updated, within the same numerical ceiling, in accordance with the guidelines. Section 11 requires the list of identified eligible households to be placed in the public domain and displayed prominently.

Three consequences follow from this allocation of functions, and together they answer the question applicants actually ask, which is why a neighbour holds a card and they do not. First, there is no national eligibility test in the Act. Parliament deliberately did not write income criteria, asset criteria or exclusion criteria into the statute; the criteria are state guidelines, made under a power the Act grants, and they differ from state to state. Many states built their lists on the Socio Economic and Caste Census of 2011 and on deprivation and exclusion criteria derived from it, others on state surveys and existing lists, and the resulting patchwork is a feature of the design, not an accident of it. Second, the ceiling binds without exception. A state that has filled its section 9 number cannot add a deserving household without removing another or exceeding its allocation; the competition for the entitlement is zero sum inside the quota, which is why identification disputes are so bitter. Third, the transparency duties are the citizen's handle on the process. A list that must be published and prominently displayed can be checked, and a name wrongly absent from a published list is a grievance with a document at its centre, which is precisely the kind of grievance the machinery of Chapter VII was built to receive.

The list is not a one time artefact, and the duties around it are continuing ones. Section 10(2) requires updating within the ceiling, which in practice means a permanent local politics of addition and deletion: births, deaths, marriages, migration and the formation of new households press names onto the list, while the ceiling presses with equal force in the opposite direction, and every addition inside a full quota is, arithmetically, someone else's deletion. States built verification practices around the statutory display duty in section 11, reading draft lists in village and ward forums and inviting objections before finalisation, so that the published list functions as both a record and an invitation to correct it. The grievance this generates is distinctive: it is rarely a complaint that the law was broken, and almost always a complaint that the list is wrong, which is why the Act's complaint machinery is designed to receive documented disputes about names rather than only disputes about grain.

The Act came into force state by state as identification and systems were completed, and the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution recorded that by 1 November 2016 all thirty-six states and union territories had implemented the Act and were receiving allocations under it, with Kerala and Tamil Nadu the last to come on board. Set that date against the three hundred and sixty-five days the proviso to section 10(1) allowed for identification, and the first lesson of the Act's administration appears: the identification of some eighty-one crore people's households took the system more than three years, not one, and the households ate through the interval only because the second proviso kept the old allocations flowing until the new lists were ready. The transition provisos of this Act, usually skipped by summary accounts, are among its most consequential provisions. A statute that had switched entitlements on a date, lists or no lists, would have manufactured the largest exclusion event in the history of the distribution system. This one was drafted, at each joint, to default to continuity. Kerala and Tamil Nadu had both run broad, near universal distribution systems of their own and joined on terms that protected their existing allocations, an illustration of the second proviso to section 3(1) doing the work it was drafted to do.

## Nutrition beyond grain: children, mothers and the meal

Chapter II does not end with section 3, and the remainder of the chapter is what entitles the Act to the words nutritional security in its preamble. The grain entitlement addresses calories in the household. Sections 4, 5 and 6 address the persons inside the household whose nutritional needs a shared grain quota historically failed to reach: the pregnant woman, the nursing mother, the child under six, the schoolchild and the malnourished child.

Section 5(1)(a) entitles every child in the age group of six months to six years to an age appropriate meal, free of charge, through the local anganwadi, so as to meet the nutritional standards specified in Schedule II, with a proviso that for children below the age of six months exclusive breastfeeding shall be promoted. Section 5(1)(b) entitles every child up to class VIII, or within the age group of six to fourteen years, whichever is applicable, to one midday meal, free of charge, every day except on school holidays, in all schools run by local bodies, government schools and government aided schools, again meeting the Schedule II standards. Section 5(2) requires every such school and anganwadi to have facilities for cooking meals, drinking water and sanitation, while permitting centralised kitchens in urban areas where required, under central guidelines. Section 6 obliges the state government, through the local anganwadi, to identify children who suffer from malnutrition and provide them meals, free of charge, meeting the higher standards Schedule II prescribes for them.

Schedule II gives these promises numbers. For children from six months to three years, the take home ration must supply five hundred calories and twelve to fifteen grams of protein. For children from three to six years, a morning snack and hot cooked meal must supply the same five hundred calories and twelve to fifteen grams of protein. For malnourished children from six months to six years, the take home ration must supply eight hundred calories and twenty to twenty-five grams of protein. For the school meal, the standard is four hundred and fifty calories and twelve grams of protein in the lower primary classes and seven hundred calories and twenty grams of protein in the upper primary classes. For pregnant women and lactating mothers, the take home ration must supply six hundred calories and eighteen to twenty grams of protein. These figures matter legally and not merely nutritionally. A meal that falls below the Schedule II standard is not a partial performance of the entitlement; it is a non-supply of the meal the Act specifies, and it is the standard against which a complaint under Chapter VII, or a claim arising from the midday meal rules, is measured.

A drafting distinction inside Chapter II deserves exact statement, because it governs how hard each entitlement is. Section 3 confers the grain entitlement directly, in the language of right, with no intervening condition. Section 4 opens with the words subject to such schemes as may be framed by the Central Government, and section 7 provides that the state governments shall implement schemes covering the entitlements under sections 4, 5 and 6 in accordance with guidelines, including cost sharing between the Centre and the states, as may be prescribed by the Central Government. The nutrition entitlements are therefore scheme mediated in a way the grain entitlement is not. They are statutory entitlements, and the Supreme Court's orders in the right to food case had treated their scheme predecessors as enforceable for a decade before the Act, but the Act routes them through schemes framed by the executive and funded by a cost sharing arrangement the executive prescribes. When a meal fails, the legal route runs through the scheme and the grievance machinery rather than through the clean entitlement and allowance structure of sections 3 and 8. The difference between a right that operates directly and a right that operates through a scheme is the difference between the Act's strongest chapter and its most qualified one, and it was a deliberate drafting choice, visible on the face of the text.

The financing of the nutrition chapter shows the same structure from the money side. Section 22(3) requires the Central Government to provide foodgrains for the entitlements under sections 4, 5 and 6 to the state governments at the prices specified in Schedule I for eligible households, so the grain inside the anganwadi meal and the school meal moves through the same subsidised channel as the ration shop grain, and the cooking, transport and personnel costs are carried by the schemes under the cost sharing the Central Government prescribes under section 7. The Mid-Day Meal Rules, 2015, framed under the Act, build a small accountability code around the school meal: samples of the cooked food are collected at least once a month from randomly selected schools or centralised kitchens and sent for examination to accredited laboratories, and where the meal is not provided on school days continuously for three days or for at least five days in a month, the state government must fix responsibility on the person or agency at fault. The school meal is thus the most densely governed of the Chapter II entitlements, a position it owes to its history: of all the schemes the Supreme Court's orders touched, the cooked midday meal was the one whose daily performance could be verified by any parent at any school gate, and the statutory scheme inherited that visibility.

## The maternity benefit: six thousand rupees and the scheme that pays five

Section 4 entitles every pregnant woman and lactating mother to two things. The first, under clause (a), is a meal, free of charge, during pregnancy and for six months after childbirth, through the local anganwadi, meeting the Schedule II standards. The second, under clause (b), is a maternity benefit of not less than six thousand rupees, in such instalments as may be prescribed by the Central Government. A proviso excludes from the cash benefit women in regular employment with the Central Government, a state government or a public sector undertaking, and those in receipt of similar benefits under any other law. The exclusion is coherent on its own terms: the benefit exists to compensate wage loss and support nutrition for women whose work carries no maternity protection, and a woman already covered by the maternity benefit legislation for organised employment, the subject of [the amendment that extended paid maternity leave](/2016/01/15/maternity-benefit-act-amendment-2017/), is not the gap the section aims at.

The figure of six thousand rupees was not invented in 2013. The Indira Gandhi Matritva Sahyog Yojana, the conditional maternity benefit scheme run on a pilot basis in fifty-three districts from 2010, paid six thousand rupees, and the Act generalised that amount into a universal statutory entitlement expressed as a floor: not less than rupees six thousand. The entitlement is, on the text, universal among pregnant women and lactating mothers outside the proviso's exclusion. It is not confined to a first child, it is not confined to women below a poverty line, and its conditions are only those the Central Government prescribes for the instalments.

The scheme through which the benefit has been delivered since 2017, the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana administered by the Ministry of Women and Child Development, pays five thousand rupees, for the first living child, in instalments tied to registration of the pregnancy, antenatal care and registration of the child's birth, with a further benefit for a second child where that child is a girl. The Government's stated reconciliation of the five thousand with the statute's six is that a mother who delivers in a health facility receives in addition a cash incentive under the Janani Suraksha Yojana, and the two together reach approximately the statutory figure. The Right to Food Campaign and other critics of the scheme answer, in terms that track the statutory text closely, that section 4 fixes a floor of not less than six thousand rupees payable as maternity benefit, for every pregnancy outside the proviso, and that a scheme which pays five thousand, for a first child only, against documentary conditions many poor women cannot easily meet, delivers less than the section promises to fewer women than the section covers. Both positions should be stated as their holders state them, because the dispute is genuinely about what the words not less than require of a scheme framed under them. The textual point itself is not contestable: the scheme is narrower than the section, and the section is the law.

The drafting route of the maternity benefit explains part of the distance between section and scheme. The instalments of the benefit are prescribed by the Central Government, and section 39(2)(a) places the maternity benefit scheme, including its cost sharing between the Centre and the states, among the subjects of central rule making. The entitlement is therefore real but routed: its amount is fixed by the statute as a floor, while its conditions, its instalment structure and its beneficiary restrictions are set in the scheme the floor was supposed to discipline. A scheme that restricts the benefit by birth order does not amend the section; it administers it narrowly, and the remedy for the gap lies in the political and judicial forums rather than in the grievance machinery of Chapter VII, whose officers police distribution and delivery, not scheme design. That allocation of the argument, to legislatures and courts rather than to the district officer, is itself a fact about how this statute distributes power among its own institutions.

The pattern recurs with the allowance examined below. The Act's most original provisions are not the grain quantities, which largely continued what the distribution system already did for the covered population. They are the maternity benefit and the food security allowance, the two places where the statute creates a money claim against the state for an individual, and they are the two provisions whose delivery has lagged furthest behind their text.

## When the grain does not come: the food security allowance

Section 8 is the shortest operative provision in the Act and the one that most distinguishes it from a scheme. In case of non-supply of the entitled quantities of foodgrains or meals to entitled persons under Chapter II, such persons shall be entitled to receive such food security allowance from the concerned state government, to be paid to each person within such time and manner as may be prescribed by the Central Government. The right is automatic in structure: it does not depend on proving fault, on the dealer's excuse, or on a discretionary sanction. Non-supply of the entitled quantity is itself the trigger, and the debtor is the state government. Section 24(4) repeats the obligation from the state's side, making the state government responsible for payment of the allowance in case of non-supply.

The Central Government prescribed the time, manner and quantum in the Food Security Allowance Rules, 2015, made in January 2015. The computation in rule 6 prices the failure rather than merely replacing the grain: the allowance is the quantity not supplied multiplied by the difference between one and a quarter times the minimum support price of the relevant foodgrain for that marketing season and the price specified in Schedule I. The beneficiary is therefore compensated at a rate above the support price, not at the subsidised price he would have paid, which converts the allowance from a refund into something closer to damages for the state's default. Rule 7 requires the nodal officer to ensure payment by the end of the third week of the month following the month in which the non-supply occurred. Rule 8 routes payment through bank accounts, permitting a first payment in cash where the entitled person has no account and requiring an account to be opened within a month. Rule 5 requires month end verification of the status of distribution at each fair price shop, with the reasons for non-distribution placed in the public domain. The machinery, on paper, is complete: detection by verification, computation by formula, payment by a deadline, all without the beneficiary having to prove anything beyond the recorded fact of non-supply.

Rule 9 then discloses the design's dependence on the beneficiary's own conduct. The allowance is not payable to an entitled person who does not visit the fair price shop to claim his entitlement during the month, subject to an exemption the nodal officer may record in writing, and it is not payable to a person who refuses to claim the entitlement on the ground of the quality of the foodgrains or on other grounds. The first limb is understandable as an anti fraud measure and operates harshly against exactly the persons the Act most concerns itself with, the migrant worker absent from the village, the elderly person unable to travel to the shop, the woman whose mobility is constrained, since absence from the shop in the month of non-supply extinguishes the money claim for that month. The school meal has its own parallel allowance: the Mid-Day Meal Rules, 2015 require the state government, where the meal is not provided on a school day for want of grain, cooking cost, fuel or the cook's absence, to pay a food security allowance by the fifteenth of the succeeding month consisting of the quantity of foodgrains per the child's entitlement and the cooking cost prevailing in the state, with responsibility to be fixed where meals fail for three days continuously or five days in a month.

The Rules also build the reporting chain on which the allowance depends, and its design shows who is expected to set the remedy in motion. A nodal officer verifies the status of distribution at each fair price shop at the month's end and places the details, including the reasons for any non-distribution, in the public domain. Where foodgrains have not been supplied because of a wilful lapse by the fair price shop dealer, the nodal officer must register a complaint with the District Grievance Redressal Officer forthwith. The nodal officer then submits a monthly summary report to the District Magistrate, or another authorised officer, by the fifteenth day of the month following distribution, recording the cases of non-supply, the reasons and the remedial measures taken. Detection, payment and discipline are thus all designed to begin inside the administration, on the strength of its own records, without any application by the household. That is the allowance's strength as a text and its weakness as a practice: a remedy that requires the defaulting system to report its own default will fire exactly as often as the reporting is honest, and the beneficiary's own complaint, through the machinery described next, remains the external check on which the whole structure quietly relies.

The allowance also inverts the usual direction of Indian welfare rule making, in a way that repays notice. Section 8 leaves the time and manner of payment to be prescribed by the Central Government, and the Food Security Allowance Rules, 2015 are accordingly central rules that prescribe the duties of state functionaries: the nodal officers who verify, the officers who report to the District Magistrate, the timeline by which the state must pay. The obligation to pay is the state's, the money comes from the state's budget under section 24(4), but the procedure that disciplines the payment was written in New Delhi under section 39(2)(c). The design is a deliberate use of the Centre's one reliable lever over a state operated system, the power to write the forms, the deadlines and the reporting chain, and it illustrates the technique of the whole Act: where the Centre cannot run the shop, it standardises the paperwork of the shop, and trusts the paperwork to discipline the running. The account of the allowance given here is legal information about the statutory scheme, written for study; it is not advice on any individual claim, and a household pursuing one must work from the Rules and the records of its own case.

Why, then, is the allowance so rarely the subject of an actual payment that its existence surprises most beneficiaries. Three structural reasons emerge from the text itself. The allowance depends on the state's own verification machinery recording the non-supply, and the recording officer answers to the same administration that must pay. The individual amount, computed on a month's grain for one household, is small against the cost in time and travel of pursuing it. And the claim requires the beneficiary to know of a remedy that neither the ration card, nor the shop's display boards, nor the standard publicity of the distribution system describes. A right that must be known to be claimed, whose detection machinery belongs to the debtor, and whose value is small per claim, will be underclaimed as a matter of arithmetic rather than of anyone's bad faith. The general position described in this article is information about the law and not advice on an individual case, and a household pursuing a complaint should expect that dates, records and the shop's own registers will decide it, which is why keeping them matters.

## The woman's name on the card

Chapter VI consists of section 13 alone, and its placement as a chapter of the Act, titled women empowerment, signals that the drafters regarded it as a reform and not a clerical rule. Section 13(1) provides that the eldest woman who is not less than eighteen years of age, in every eligible household, shall be head of the household for the purpose of issue of ration cards. Section 13(2) handles the transitional case: where a household has no woman of eighteen years or above but has a female member below that age, the eldest male member shall be the head of the household for the purpose of issue of the ration card, and the female member, on attaining eighteen, shall become the head in his place.

The provision operates on the document that organises the household's relationship with the food system. The ration card names the household, lists its members and records its entitlements, and the person named as head is the person the system recognises: the person to whom notices go, whose identity anchors the card's authentication, and whose name stands for the household in every register the transparency provisions expose. Indian administrative practice had defaulted to the eldest man. Section 13 reverses the default as a matter of law, in every eligible household, without exception for region or community. Its practical reasoning, as its defenders in the drafting debates stated it, is that the household member most likely to convert a grain entitlement into food for the household is the woman who manages its kitchen, that naming her head aligns the legal form with the nutritional reality, and that the card in her name gives her a state issued identity document and a measure of standing in a system she previously entered only as someone's dependent. Its critics at the point of issue are rarely critics of the principle; the resistance the provision meets is administrative and social at once, in households where the card has always carried the man's name, in offices where reissuing crores of cards takes years, and in the gap between the name on the card and the person who in fact travels to the shop, authenticates and carries the grain home. The section changes who the law sees when it looks at a household. What households and offices do with that change is the implementation record, which is a different article's subject.

Two limits of the provision should be stated exactly, because the provision is often credited with more than its text performs. The first is in the section itself: the woman is head of the household for the purpose of the issue of a ration card, and the headship the Act confers is a headship within the food system, carrying no consequence for property, succession or any other legal relation. The second follows from section 13(2): the design is a default with a bridge, not an absolute rule, since the eldest male member holds the headship until a female member of the household attains eighteen. What the section does within its limits is nonetheless concrete. The named head is the person the digitised beneficiary record treats as the household's anchor, the name in which the card is issued and against which the household's transactions are recorded, and under the Cash Transfer of Food Subsidy Rules, 2015 it is the head of the eligible household into whose bank account a cash subsidy, where that route is prescribed, is paid. A provision that began as a statement about dignity ends, in the system's plumbing, as a rule about whose name the money and the grain follow.

## The machinery of complaint

A statute that creates entitlements against the state must answer the question of where a denied entitlement is taken, and Chapter VII answers it in three tiers. The first tier, under section 14, is internal: every state government shall put in place an internal grievance redressal mechanism, which may include call centres, help lines, designation of nodal officers, or such other mechanism as may be prescribed. The second tier, under section 15, is a named officer. The state government shall appoint or designate, for each district, a District Grievance Redressal Officer for expeditious and effective redressal of grievances in matters relating to distribution of entitled foodgrains or meals, and to enforce the entitlements under the Act. The officer hears complaints regarding non-distribution of entitled foodgrains or meals and matters relating to them, and takes necessary action for redressal in such manner and within such time as the state prescribes. Under section 15(6), a complainant dissatisfied with the redressal, and equally an officer or authority against whom an order has passed, may appeal to the State Food Commission. The third tier is that Commission.

Section 16 requires every state government, by notification, to constitute a State Food Commission to monitor and review the implementation of the Act. Its composition is specified with a care that reveals its intended character. It consists of a Chairperson, five other members, and a Member-Secretary who must be an officer of the state government not below the rank of Joint Secretary. At least two of the persons holding those offices must be women, and one must belong to the Scheduled Castes and one to the Scheduled Tribes. Members are appointed from among persons with service or eminence in fields the section lists, agriculture, civil supplies, nutrition, health, law, human rights, social service, management, food policy and public administration among them, or with a proven record of work on the food and nutrition rights of the poor. The term is up to five years, with reappointment permitted, and no one may hold office after attaining sixty-five years. Section 18 permits a state to designate an existing statutory commission or body to function as its State Food Commission, and section 19 permits two or more states, with the Central Government's approval, to share a Joint State Food Commission, two provisions that soften the institutional demand on smaller states.

The functions in section 16(6) make the Commission a monitor, an inquirer and an appellate body at once. It must monitor and evaluate the implementation of the Act in the state. It may, on its own motion or on receipt of a complaint, inquire into violations of the entitlements provided under Chapter II. It advises the state government and its agencies on effective implementation. It hears appeals against the orders of the District Grievance Redressal Officer. And it prepares annual reports, which the state government must lay before the state legislature. For its inquiries it holds, under section 20, all the powers of a civil court trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: summoning and enforcing attendance and examining on oath, discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records, and issuing commissions for examination of witnesses, together with a power to forward a case to a Magistrate having jurisdiction.

The teeth of the machinery are deliberately small, and their smallness is part of the design's honest description. Section 33 provides that any public servant or authority found guilty by the State Commission, at the time of deciding a complaint or appeal, of failing to provide the relief recommended by the District Grievance Redressal Officer without reasonable cause, or of wilfully ignoring such recommendation, shall be liable to a penalty not exceeding five thousand rupees, after a reasonable opportunity of being heard. Section 34 provides for adjudication of that penalty by a member of the Commission authorised as an adjudicating officer. Observe the structure: the District Grievance Redressal Officer recommends relief, the Commission's penalty attaches not to the original denial of grain but to the failure to act on the recommendation, and the maximum penalty is five thousand rupees. The machinery is built for correction rather than punishment, for getting the household its grain rather than for fining the functionary, and a reader assessing it should hold both truths together: it gives every denied household a forum, a record and an appeal, which the scheme era did not; and its coercive edge, at the end of that road, is a capped penalty small enough to be absorbed as a cost of indifference by the office that incurs it.

Two softnesses in the text should be recorded, because both were choices. The District Grievance Redressal Officer's qualifications and powers are left by sections 15(2) and 15(3) to be prescribed by the state government, as are the manner and time for hearing complaints, so the officer who is the citizen's first forum varies in authority from state to state by design. And the officer is appointed or designated by the same state government whose distribution machinery he must police, commonly by designating an existing district officer with other duties. The Commission's staff, salaries and expenses likewise come from the state government under sections 16(7) and 17. None of this makes the machinery useless; commissions with civil court powers and a duty to report to the legislature are not nothing. It does mean the Act's enforcement chapter depends, at every joint, on the cooperation of the governments it polices, which is the recurring condition of the entire statute.

### What can the District Grievance Redressal Officer actually order?

The officer inquires into complaints about the distribution of entitled foodgrains and meals and directs relief, which can include delivery of what was withheld and payment of the food security allowance. An official who, without reasonable cause, fails to provide the relief the officer recommends faces a penalty of up to five thousand rupees under section 33.

Two ancillary provisions widen the machinery's reach in ways that are easy to miss. Section 43 permits the services of the District Grievance Redressal Officers and the State Food Commissions to be utilised in the implementation of other schemes and programmes of the Centre and the states, as the state government prescribes, which makes the food grievance apparatus a reusable piece of welfare infrastructure rather than a single statute enclave, and which also dilutes, in proportion to the other work loaded onto it, the attention the food entitlements receive. And the Commission's annual report, laid before the state legislature under section 16(6)(f), is the one place in the Act where enforcement is converted into a public, political record: a commission that cannot compel a budget can still compel a debate, and the drafters' decision to route the Commission's findings to the legislature rather than only to the government it monitors is the enforcement chapter's clearest statement of its own theory, that publicity is the sanction of first resort in a system whose formal penalties are capped at five thousand rupees.

Behind the table stands a habit worth more than any single provision in it. Every remedy in the table runs on records: the published list, the shop's register, the verification report, the dated complaint. A household that keeps its ration card, its receipts and copies of its complaints together holds the evidence each remedy requires, and documents kept in a simple spreadsheet or presentation file can be reviewed anywhere with [an online viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) when the software that created them is not available. The Act rewards the documented grievance and strands the undocumented one, which is an argument for treating the paperwork of an entitlement as part of the entitlement.

## Transparency written into the statute

Chapter XI assumes that the cheapest enforcement officer is the public. Section 27 provides that all records relating to the targeted public distribution system shall be placed in the public domain and kept open for inspection by the public, in the manner the state government prescribes. Section 28(1) requires every local authority, or other authorised body, to conduct or cause to be conducted periodic social audits on the functioning of fair price shops, the distribution system and other welfare schemes, to publicise the findings and to take necessary action; section 28(2) empowers the Central Government, where it considers it necessary, to conduct social audits through independent agencies with experience of them. Section 2(20) gives social audit a statutory definition, the process in which people collectively monitor and evaluate the planning and implementation of a programme or scheme, which makes the audit a legal institution and not a slogan.

Section 29 completes the structure by requiring every state government to set up vigilance committees, as specified in the Public Distribution System (Control) Order, 2001, at the state, district, block and fair price shop levels, with due representation to local authorities, the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, women and destitute persons or persons with disability. The committees regularly supervise the implementation of all schemes under the Act, and must inform the District Grievance Redressal Officer in writing of any violation of the Act and of any malpractice or misappropriation of funds they find. The design is a chain of observation: the committee at the shop watches the shop, the written report moves the observation into the grievance machinery, the officer acts on a record rather than a rumour, and the Commission's annual report carries the pattern to the legislature. Chapter X's duties for local authorities, in sections 25 and 26, place responsibility for proper implementation on panchayats and municipalities in their own areas, and section 30 directs the Centre and the states, in implementing the Act, to give special focus to the needs of vulnerable groups in remote, hilly and tribal areas and other areas difficult of access. Read together, these provisions describe the drafters' theory of enforcement: not a food police, but a density of witnesses with statutory standing, feeding a small formal machinery.

## The reforms the Act ordered in its own system

Section 12 obliges the Central and state governments to endeavour to progressively undertake reforms in the targeted public distribution system, and section 12(2) lists what the reforms shall include. Doorstep delivery of foodgrains to the outlets of the system, so that the shop receives its stock rather than collecting it and the point of leakage moves upstream where it can be watched. Application of information and communication technology, including end to end computerisation, to ensure transparent recording of transactions at all levels and to prevent diversion. Leveraging Aadhaar for unique identification, with biometric information of entitled beneficiaries, for proper targeting of benefits. Full transparency of records. Preference to public institutions and public bodies such as panchayats, self help groups and cooperatives in the licensing of fair price shops, and management of fair price shops by women or their collectives. Diversification of the commodities distributed, over a period of time. Support to local public distribution models and grain banks. And the introduction of schemes such as cash transfer and food coupons, to ensure the foodgrain entitlements of Chapter II, in such area and manner as the Central Government may prescribe.

The list is a negotiated settlement between two views of the system's disease, and its internal tensions are visible. The computerisation, Aadhaar and transparency items treat the disease as diversion: grain stolen between the godown and the household, ghost cards drawing real grain. The doorstep delivery and licensing preference items treat it as structure: a private dealer with a thin margin and a local monopoly has incentives no inspection regime fully corrects, so change the dealer and shorten the chain. The cash transfer item treats the disease as the commodity chain itself, and it sits in visible tension with the Standing Committee's caution, recorded in its report of January 2013, that cash transfers should not be introduced before banking infrastructure could carry them; the enacted text keeps the option and leaves its geography and manner to central prescription, where it has remained an option rather than the system's direction. The fair price shop around which all of this turns is itself a creature of the older control regime: section 2(4) defines it as a shop licensed to distribute essential commodities by an order issued under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, the statute whose control orders built the licensing architecture on which the entire distribution system still stands and whose provisions are set out in [the complete guide to the Essential Commodities Act](/2014/03/01/essential-commodities-act-1955-guide/). The food security entitlement of 2013 is delivered, shop by shop, through a licensing skeleton built by the control legislation of 1955, and the reforms chapter is the record of Parliament's attempt to renew the skeleton without replacing it.

The cash transfer option received its statutory machinery two years later, and the form it took confirms how cautiously the option has been held. The Cash Transfer of Food Subsidy Rules, 2015, framed under the Act, provide for the subsidy to be paid in cash into the bank account of the head of the eligible household in areas the Central Government specifies, in place of grain through the shop, leaving the household to buy its food in the open market. The arguments rehearsed around those Rules are the targeting argument in miniature. For cash: it ends the physical chain in which grain leaks, it respects the household's choice of food, and it converts a logistics empire into a payment. Against cash: it exposes the poorest household to open market price movements with a transfer fixed by formula, it depends on banking access and on the account holder's control of the money within the household, and it dissolves the fair price shop network whose survival the rest of the Act assumes. The Standing Committee's caution of January 2013 has, in substance, prevailed over the reform list's boldest item: the entitlement continues to move as grain, and the cash route survives as a prescribed possibility at the margin of a system built for commodities.

## Two governments, one obligation

The Act is a central statute that only state machinery can operate, and Chapters VIII and IX draft that interdependence as a set of reciprocal duties. The Central Government must allocate the required quantity of foodgrains from the central pool to each state, as per the entitlements under section 3 and at the prices specified in Schedule I, must allocate in accordance with the number of identified persons in the state, and must provide the grain for the Chapter II nutrition entitlements at the same Schedule I prices. It must procure the grain, transport it to its designated depots in each state, assist the state with the expenditure on intra state movement, handling and the margins paid to fair price shop dealers according to prescribed norms, for which the Food Security (Assistance to State Governments) Rules, 2015 supply the framework, and create and maintain scientific storage. Section 23 adds the shortfall rule: where supply from the central pool to a state runs short, the Central Government shall provide funds to the extent of the short supply so the state can meet its Chapter II obligations, a duty the Provisioning of Funds to State Governments for Short Supply of Foodgrains Rules, 2014 were framed to operate, converting a failure of grain into a transfer of money with which the state may procure what the pool could not send. The shortfall rule matters beyond its occasions of use, because it completes the Act's theory of the Centre's obligation: the Centre's duty is not exhausted by sending what the pool contains, and a Centre that has allocated a quantity it cannot physically supply remains liable for the value of the deficiency. Allocation, in this statute, is a promise, and section 23 is the promise's price. The state government, for its part, must take delivery at the designated depots, organise intra state allocation for delivery at the doorstep of each fair price shop, ensure actual delivery to the entitled persons at the Schedule I prices, build scientific storage at state, district and block levels, strengthen its food and civil supplies corporations, maintain institutionalised licensing of fair price shops, and pay the food security allowance when delivery fails.

The same distribution of labour runs through the rule making power in section 39, and the map of who makes which rules is a serviceable summary of the Act's federal design. The Central Government's rules cover the maternity benefit scheme and its cost sharing, the manner and amount of the food security allowance, schemes of cash transfer, food coupons or other grants in lieu of entitlements, the time and manner of the suo motu disclosure duties, the identification guidelines for Antyodaya households under the scheme, and the norms for assistance with intra state costs. The state governments' rules and guidelines cover the identification of priority households, the qualifications, manner of appointment and procedure of the District Grievance Redressal Officers, the constitution and working of the State Food Commissions, the district level internal grievance mechanisms, and the manner of conducting social audits. A question about the Act that cannot be answered from its sections is very often answerable from this map: the statute distributes not only duties but the pen, and the level of government that holds the pen for a function is the level whose politics will decide how that function is performed.

The miscellaneous chapter completes the federal machinery with three provisions a reader should know exist even if they are rarely litigated. Section 35 permits the Central Government and the state governments to delegate their powers and functions under the Act within their respective spheres, the routine provision that lets a statute addressed to governments be worked by directorates and district officers. Section 40 is the state governments' general rule making power, the mirror of section 39, and it is under section 40 and the identification power in section 10 that the operative eligibility guidelines of each state are made. Section 42 arms the Central Government with the standard power to remove difficulties in giving effect to the Act during its opening years, the transitional valve by which a statute that had to be commenced through an ordinance, against a system already running, could be adjusted to the machinery it found. Together with section 41's continuation of existing schemes and orders and section 45's repeal of the Ordinance with savings, these provisions are the Act's shock absorbers: unglamorous, seldom cited, and the reason the statute could take over a feeding system of eighty-one crore people without a day's interruption of supply.

Around these duties the Act places its supremacy and direction clauses. Section 36 gives the Act and the schemes under it overriding effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law. Section 38 empowers the Central Government to give directions to state governments for the effective implementation of the Act, with which the states shall comply. Section 37, noted earlier, lets the Centre amend the schedules by notification. Section 41 carries forward the schemes, guidelines, orders and mechanisms existing at commencement until they are replaced under the Act, so the system never stood still waiting for new rules. And section 32 preserves the space above the statutory floor: nothing in the Act precludes the Centre or a state from continuing or formulating other food based welfare schemes, and a state may, from its own resources, provide benefits higher than the Act provides.

### Can a state give more than the Act provides?

Yes. Section 32(2) expressly preserves a state government's power to continue or formulate food or nutrition based plans or schemes providing benefits higher than the Act itself, from its own resources, and section 32(1) preserves other food based welfare schemes. The Act is a floor for entitlements, not a ceiling on what a state may fund.

The state space above the floor has been used in both directions of the coverage argument. Chhattisgarh enacted its own Food and Nutrition Security Act in December 2012, before the central Act, covering a substantially larger share of its population and distributing commodities beyond grain, including pulses and iodised salt, from state resources. States with a tradition of near universal distribution treated the central percentages as a funding baseline rather than a description of their systems. The floor and ceiling distinction matters to the targeting debate examined below: the Act fixes what the Union will fund as an entitlement, and states that judge the floor too low for their politics or their poverty remain free to buy the difference themselves.

### What happens to the entitlement when a flood or drought disrupts supply?

Section 44 suspends the claim itself: neither government is liable for a claim by an entitled person where war, flood, drought, fire, cyclone or earthquake affects the regular supply of foodgrains or meals, and the Central Government may declare whether such a situation has arisen. The entitlement is a right against a functioning supply chain, not insurance against its destruction.

The force majeure clause is the Act's bleakest piece of drafting honesty, and it repays a moment's attention. The statute was born of a drought in which people starved beside full godowns, a failure of distribution, not of supply, and against that failure the entire apparatus of entitlement, allowance and grievance is directed. Against the opposite failure, a genuine collapse of supply caused by the listed calamities, the Act disclaims liability in advance. A drought that empties the pool is the one contingency in which the entitled person has no claim, which is a legally coherent position, since a right to receive grain presupposes grain to receive, and an uncomfortable one, since the calamity that most reliably produces hunger is the calamity the clause names first among equals after war. What the clause does not suspend is the obligation's political and constitutional remainder: the Article 21 jurisprudence out of which the Act grew recognised no force majeure defence to starvation, and a government that pleaded section 44 while its people starved would be answering the statute's question in a forum that decided the matter the other way in 2001.

## Who pays: the subsidy the statute presupposes

The quantities and prices in Chapter II are only half the entitlement; the other half is a financing structure the Act assumes rather than describes, and a reader who wants to understand the statute's politics must reconstruct it. The issue price charged to the beneficiary, three rupees, two rupees and one rupee, is a small fraction of what the grain in the central pool costs the state to acquire and hold. Procurement takes place at the minimum support price, and to that price must be added the costs of storage, movement, interest and handling before a kilogram reaches a fair price shop. The difference between the full economic cost and the issue price is the food subsidy, and under the Act's allocation of duties it falls on the Centre for the grain in the pool: section 22 charges the Centre with allocation, procurement, transport to state depots and assistance with onward costs, and section 23 requires the Centre to provide funds to the state governments to the extent of any short supply of foodgrains against allocation. The state governments carry the intra state movement, storage, shop licensing and delivery duties of section 24, assisted by central support for intra state transport, handling and dealer margins under the Food Security (Assistance to State Governments) Rules, 2015, which prescribe the norms and the pattern of sharing for that assistance.

The size of the commitment was computed before the Act existed, and the computation is part of the statute's history. The report of the Standing Committee on the Bill recorded the official estimate that, had the legislation been in operation in 2011-12, the food subsidy would have stood at Rs 98,842 crore, rising to Rs 1,12,205 crore in 2012-13, and it noted the far smaller additional requirement computed for the second of those years, Rs 2,409 crore over the subsidy the existing system already carried, because the targeted system being replaced was itself heavily subsidised. Both figures need to be carried together to be understood. The Act's gross subsidy is among the largest line items in the Union budget; its marginal cost over the predecessor system, at inception, was comparatively modest, because most of the covered population was already receiving subsidised grain and the Act's true additions were the widened coverage, the lowered prices and the new money entitlements. Estimates of the Act's cost that quote only the gross figure describe the food economy of India; estimates that quote only the marginal figure describe the Bill. The fiscal argument that surrounded the statute, examined below, is in large part an argument about which of those two numbers answers the question being asked.

The financing structure also explains a distributive feature that the entitlement chapters do not mention. Because the subsidy is the difference between economic cost and issue price, every increase in procurement prices flows automatically into the subsidy bill without any further decision by Parliament, and every expansion of coverage multiplies it. The statute therefore created an open ended fiscal commitment whose growth is driven by the support price politics examined in the companion statute guide, while its own text fixed only the beneficiary's side of the price. A legislature that wished to cap the commitment would have had to write the cap into the Act, in the coverage ceilings or the schedule amendment power, and the ceilings of section 3(2) are the only fiscal brake the text contains. Everything else in the financing of the entitlement is administration.

One element of that administration deserves isolation, because it is where the financing structure touches the entitlement's daily reality. The last link of the chain, the fair price shop, is run in most states by a dealer working on a margin fixed by government, out of which the shop's rent, labour, transport from the godown and losses must all be met. Section 22(4) obliges the Centre to assist the states with the margins paid to fair price shop dealers according to prescribed norms, and the Assistance Rules of 2015 carry that support into the state budgets, an acknowledgement written into the Act's financing that the dealer's economics are a component of the entitlement rather than an overhead upon it. The reasoning is the system's oldest lesson. A margin set below the cost of honest operation does not save the exchequer money; it prices the difference into diversion, short weighing and the closed shop, the exact failures the grievance machinery then processes at public expense. The Act's treatment of dealer margins as a subsidised input, like the grain itself, is one of its quietest and most practical judgments, and it completes the financing picture: the Union subsidises the grain, assists the movement and the margin, funds the shortfall in money, and leaves the states to fund the apparatus of delivery within which all of it either reaches the household or does not.

## The price that ceased to be charged

Schedule I, as enacted, fixed the subsidised prices for three years and then handed the price to the Central Government, capped at the minimum support price. The three years ran out in July 2016. The Central Government did not revise the prices upward, and the prices of three rupees, two rupees and one rupee continued to be charged for the entitled grain for the remainder of the decade, an executive choice made within the Schedule's own terms, since the Schedule sets a ceiling and a cap, not a mandatory price path. Then the scheme layering that section 32 permits changed the position twice over. In March 2020, in response to the pandemic, the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana began supplying additional free foodgrain to the Act's beneficiaries over and above the statutory entitlement, a scheme operating alongside the Act rather than under its entitlement chapter. And the Cabinet decided in December 2022 that the statutory entitlement itself would be supplied free of cost for a year from 1 January 2023, a decision the Cabinet then extended: by its decision of 29 November 2023, the Central Government undertook to provide free foodgrains to about 81.35 crore beneficiaries for five years from 1 January 2024, at an estimated cost of about Rs 11.80 lakh crore over the five years, as the Press Information Bureau recorded the decision.

The legal anatomy of that position deserves precision, because commentary routinely misstates it. The Schedule I prices were not repealed, and Schedule I was not amended under section 37 to substitute zero. The prices stand in the statute as the prices at which the entitlement is defined, and the state governments' duty under section 24 is still expressed as delivery at the prices specified in Schedule I. What changed is that the Central Government, which bears the cost of the grain and fixes the effective issue price within the Schedule's ceiling, set the price the beneficiary pays at nothing. The entitlement therefore runs at a zero price by executive decision under a schedule that permits it, and it could be repriced by executive decision within the same schedule, up to the minimum support price cap, without Parliament being asked. The Act's own drafting made the price the most executive controlled element of the entire entitlement, which is why the question whether beneficiaries pay the prices in the Act, examined in the implementation literature, has a statutory answer and a practical answer that point in opposite directions. The statutory answer is that the prices of three, two and one remain the legal specification of the entitlement. The practical answer is that they are not collected. Both answers are true, and the distance between them is a measure of how much of this Act's real content is settled in the space the text leaves to the executive.

One consequence of the zero price deserves recording, because it reverses a mechanism the original design relied on. A subsidised price, however small, rations offtake: a household that must pay something takes its entitlement when it needs it and leaves grain unlifted when it does not, and the system's offtake figures under the priced regime understated the number of households actually drawing. At a price of zero the signal disappears, offtake moves toward the full entitlement, and the Ministry's own review of the free grain period recorded about eighty crore persons receiving foodgrains free of cost against the intended coverage of about 81.35 crore, delivered through a network of more than five lakh fair price shops. The fiscal commitment and the coverage claim therefore rise together, which is the honest arithmetic of free distribution: it reaches more of the entitled population precisely by spending more on each month of it. Whether that trade is sound policy is the targeting argument taken up next; that it is the trade being made is fixed by the arithmetic of the entitlement itself.

## The argument about targeting, taken seriously

The standing controversy over the Act is whether covering two thirds of the population is defensible public finance or an expensive refusal to aim. The stance of this article is that both cases must be heard in their strongest form, attributed to the people who actually make them, before any verdict is earned.

The fiscal case was stated most authoritatively by the High Level Committee on restructuring the Food Corporation of India, chaired by Shri Shanta Kumar and reporting in January 2015. The Committee recommended that the Act's coverage be reduced from sixty-seven per cent of the population to about forty per cent, that the per person entitlement for those retained be raised from five kilograms to seven, that the retained poorest be protected at the lowest prices while others paid a price linked to the support price, and that cash transfers replace grain distribution progressively, beginning in cities of more than a million people. The argument is not that the poor should get less. It is that a subsidy spread across two thirds of the population is spread too thin to transform the diet of the poorest, that the fiscal cost of procuring, storing, moving and distributing grain for eight hundred million people crowds out the agricultural investment that would raise their incomes, and that a smaller, deeper entitlement would feed the genuinely hungry better per rupee. Ashok Gulati, who chaired the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices while the Bill was debated and later sat on the Shanta Kumar Committee, had made the cost side of the argument in a Commission paper of May 2013 with Surbhi Jain, estimating the full cost of the Bill's commitments well above the official figures once procurement, storage and distribution were honestly counted, and pressing the claim that the money would do more for food security invested in agriculture than spent on moving subsidised grain.

The rights case answers that the fiscal arithmetic assumes the state can find the forty per cent, and that this is the assumption the evidence destroys. Jean Drèze and Reetika Khera, the economists most closely associated with the Right to Food Campaign's research programme, built their case on field measurement of the targeted system: their survey of the distribution system across nine states in 2011 found sample households receiving about eighty-five per cent of their official grain quota, evidence that a reformed distribution system could deliver; and their work on identification documented the scale on which poverty line lists excluded households in visible destitution, the very error the percentage design exists to prevent. The asymmetry argument completes the case. An inclusion error costs the exchequer the price of grain a non-poor household did not need. An exclusion error costs a poor household its food. A state that has repeatedly demonstrated it cannot identify the poor accurately, and whose failures fall on the excluded rather than the exchequer, is rationally entitled to prefer the error it can afford. On this view the width of the coverage is not the Act's indulgence. It is the Act's premium on an insurance policy against its own administrative incapacity, and states such as Tamil Nadu, which ran near universal systems, demonstrated that breadth and low leakage are compatible when the system is not asked to perform the impossible task of sorting the poor at the ration shop door.

The rights case also draws on the reform experience of the states in the decade before the Act, and the connection between that experience and the statute's text is direct. The research programme associated with Drèze and Khera documented a turnaround in several states that had rebuilt their distribution systems from within: Chhattisgarh, which moved fair price shops from private dealers to community institutions, delivered grain to the shop's door, computerised its records and widened coverage toward the universal; Odisha and others that undertook similar repairs; and Tamil Nadu, whose universal system had never been dismantled. In those states, offtake rose and measured diversion fell, on the strength of administrative reforms rather than narrower targeting. Section 12's reform list reads, item for item, like a codification of that state experience: doorstep delivery, community and women's management of shops, computerisation, transparency of records, wider and simpler coverage. The Act did not invent its reform programme in New Delhi. It generalised the practice of the states that had demonstrated the targeted system's failures were failures of administration rather than of generosity, and it wrote their remedy into national law while making the remedy's hardest element, the identification of the poorest forty per cent, unnecessary by covering two thirds instead. That is the strongest form of the rights case: not that breadth is costless, but that the alternative the fiscal case proposes had already been tried, at scale, for sixteen years, and had produced the exclusion the litigation of 2001 was filed to protest.

The proposal for narrower coverage did not die with the committee stage. A discussion paper issued by NITI Aayog in 2021 proposed reducing the coverage of the Act to sixty per cent of the rural population and forty per cent of the urban population, reviving the fiscal case in institutional form, and the proposal was not adopted. The episode matters to the reading of the statute because it demonstrates where the coverage question lives: not in amendment of section 3(2), which no government has introduced, but in the periodic restatement of the fiscal case by official bodies, answered each time by the political cost of withdrawing a legal entitlement from named households on published lists. An entitlement whose trimming requires a government to strike names off ration cards its own machinery issued is a right; an entitlement that exists only as a percentage in a framework document is a scheme. The distance between those two propositions is the Act's whole achievement and its whole fiscal problem stated in one sentence.

The verdict this article defends follows the structure of the statute. On the question the two sides actually dispute, whether breadth or narrowness is the better response to demonstrated identification failure, the evidence supports breadth: exclusion errors under targeted identification were large, persistent and borne by those least able to absorb them, and no identification technology deployed since has repealed the problem. The fiscal case proves something real but different: that the entitlement as designed is expensive in ways its sponsors understated, and that its procurement and storage tail imposes economic costs a cash system would avoid. Both things can be true because they answer different questions. The Act's genuine defect lies where neither side's standard argument points: in section 9's frozen denominator, which converts a defensible breadth at enactment into a shrinking share of a growing population, manufacturing exclusion by arithmetic in every year without a fresh census determination. Breadth was the right answer to the identification problem. Freezing the breadth to a census is the decision that quietly takes the answer back.

## What the Act does not do

A pillar article owes the reader the negative map as clearly as the positive one. The Act does not guarantee a minimum support price to any farmer; support prices remain an executive announcement, and the procurement that fills the central pool is an obligation of the Centre, not a right of the seller. It does not entitle any household to pulses, edible oil, sugar or any commodity beyond rice, wheat and coarse grains, the diversification of section 12(2)(f) being a direction for reform over time and not an entitlement, and Schedule III's wider nutritional ambitions being framed as objectives to be progressively realised rather than claims to be enforced. It does not create a damages remedy for hunger suffered during a denial; the money remedies are the allowance, computed by formula, and the capped penalty of section 33. It does not prescribe a national eligibility criterion, a national list, or a national appellate authority, and its State Food Commission has no counterpart at the Centre with power over states. It does not displace the constitutional route: the writ jurisdiction under which the right to food was first enforced remains available for the failures the statutory machinery cannot reach, and the Act's entitlements stand alongside, not above, the Article 21 jurisprudence that preceded them. And it does not finish its own project: the maternity benefit arrives narrowed by its delivery scheme, the allowance arrives dependent on the debtor's own verification, and the commissions arrive dependent on state budgets and state appointments. Within the wider family of Indian welfare statutes, where most schemes still rest on executive discretion, the Act remains the extreme case of the opposite technique, and [the complete guide to India's welfare laws](/2017/07/01/welfare-laws-india-complete-guide/) sets it among the statutes it must be compared with.

The negative map has one further entry that surprises most readers. The Act contains almost no criminal law. The diversion of grain, the offence around which the old system's enforcement revolved, is not an offence under this Act at all; it remains punishable, where it is punishable, under the orders made under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 and the licensing conditions those orders impose. Within the four corners of the food security statute, the only penal provision is section 33's capped penalty for ignoring a grievance officer's recommended relief. The choice was structural. A statute whose theory is that entitlement plus transparency plus a money remedy will discipline a distribution system has no need of a food police, and its drafters, who had watched the Essential Commodities Act's prosecution machinery operate for decades without curing diversion, legislated their scepticism. Whether the scepticism was wisdom or abdication is the question the implementation record answers, and it is deliberately left to that record. What can be said from the text alone is that the Act's enforcement bet is placed entirely on the claimant: on the household that knows its quantity, keeps its records, files its complaint and claims its allowance. Every provision examined in this article either informs that household, arms it, or pays it. None of them polices anyone else on its behalf.

For the reader who works with this statute, in an examination hall, a district office or a household argument at a ration shop, the propositions worth holding exactly are few and they are all in the text. The Act is Act 20 of 2013, assented to on 10 September 2013 and deemed in force from 5 July 2013. The entitlement is five kilograms per person per month for priority households and thirty-five kilograms per household for Antyodaya households, at the Schedule I prices of three rupees, two rupees and one rupee, which are prices in Schedule I and not Schedule II, the second schedule being the nutritional standards for meals. Coverage is up to seventy-five per cent rural and fifty per cent urban, determined state wise on the published census. The remedy for non-delivery is the food security allowance under section 8, computed under the Rules of 2015, and the forum is the District Grievance Redressal Officer with an appeal to the State Food Commission. The eldest woman of eighteen or above is the head of the household for the ration card. A reader who holds those propositions, with their section numbers, holds the statute's skeleton; everything else in this article is the flesh of argument, history and criticism arranged around them.

The Act's own incompleteness should not be mistaken for failure of its central act. Before September 2013, the food schemes of the Indian state were promises. After it, some eighty-one crore people held a document, and behind the document a text, that said what they were owed, in kilograms and rupees, and named the officer to complain to and the commission to appeal to when the promise was broken. The evidence of how that text has performed, state by state and year by year, is the subject of [the implementation record of the Act and the public distribution system](/2014/06/01/nfsa-pds-implementation-record/), which owns the delivery evidence this pillar has deliberately not taken. The statute's own verdict can be stated in one sentence, and it is the claim with which this article began: the National Food Security Act converted a scheme into a right, and then made the holder of the right a matter of administrative allocation, so that the law of food in India is a law of entitlements whose first question is never what a household needs, but whether its name is on the list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the National Food Security Act guarantee?

It guarantees specified quantities of food to specified people, as legal entitlements rather than welfare benefits. Persons in priority households are entitled to five kilograms of foodgrains per person per month at the prices in Schedule I, and Antyodaya households to thirty-five kilograms per household per month. Children are entitled to meals through the anganwadi and to a free midday meal at school, meeting the nutritional standards in Schedule II. Pregnant women and lactating mothers are entitled to a meal and to a maternity benefit of not less than six thousand rupees. Where the entitled quantity is not supplied, section 8 guarantees a food security allowance from the state government.

### Q: Which households are covered by the National Food Security Act?

The Act covers eligible households, meaning the priority households and the households under the Antyodaya Anna Yojana, within a numerical ceiling. Section 3(2) extends coverage to up to seventy-five per cent of the rural population and up to fifty per cent of the urban population, and section 9 has the Central Government determine each state's coverage and the total number of persons to be covered, calculated on published census figures. Within that number, state governments identify the households under section 10. Coverage is therefore determined by a household's presence on its state's list of eligible households, not by a national income test written into the Act.

### Q: How much foodgrain does a household get and at what price?

A person belonging to a priority household is entitled to five kilograms of foodgrains per person per month, so the household's quantity rises with its size. A household covered by the Antyodaya Anna Yojana is entitled to thirty-five kilograms per household per month. The prices are those in Schedule I: not exceeding three rupees per kilogram for rice, two rupees for wheat and one rupee for coarse grains. Schedule I fixed those prices for the first three years and thereafter left the price to the Central Government, capped at the minimum support price. Under the free foodgrain decision, the entitled grain has been supplied free of cost for five years from 1 January 2024.

### Q: What maternity benefit does the food security law provide?

Section 4 entitles every pregnant woman and lactating mother to a meal, free of charge, through the local anganwadi during pregnancy and for six months after childbirth, meeting the nutritional standards in Schedule II, and to a maternity benefit of not less than six thousand rupees, paid in instalments the Central Government prescribes. Women in regular employment with the Central Government, a state government or a public sector undertaking, and women already receiving similar benefits under another law, are excluded from the cash benefit. The benefit has been delivered through the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana, which pays five thousand rupees for a first living child, with a further amount available under the Janani Suraksha Yojana for institutional delivery.

### Q: When is a food security allowance payable?

The allowance is payable whenever an entitled person is not supplied the entitled quantity of foodgrains or meals under Chapter II of the Act. Section 8 makes non-supply itself the trigger, without any need to prove fault, and the state government is the debtor. Under the Food Security Allowance Rules, 2015, the amount is the quantity not supplied multiplied by the difference between one and a quarter times the minimum support price and the Schedule I price, and the nodal officer must ensure payment by the end of the third week of the month following the month of non-supply. It is not payable where the person did not visit the fair price shop to claim the entitlement, or refused it on grounds of quality.

### Q: Why does a ration card treat the eldest woman as head of household?

Section 13 provides that the eldest woman who is not less than eighteen years of age, in every eligible household, shall be head of the household for the purpose of issue of ration cards. The provision aligns the legal document with the household's nutritional reality: the woman who manages the kitchen is made the person the food system recognises, receives notices for and records as the household's anchor. Where a household has no woman of eighteen or above but has a girl below that age, the eldest male member is head until she attains eighteen, when she becomes head in his place. The chapter containing the provision is titled women empowerment.

### Q: How do you complain if you do not get your ration?

The Act builds a three tier route. The first tier is the state's internal grievance redressal mechanism under section 14, which may include call centres, help lines and nodal officers. The second is the District Grievance Redressal Officer appointed for each district under section 15, who hears complaints about non-distribution of entitled foodgrains or meals and takes action for their redressal; vigilance committees and social audits also feed violations to this officer in writing. The third tier is the State Food Commission, which hears appeals against the officer's orders under section 15(6) and may also inquire into violations of Chapter II entitlements on its own motion or on complaint.

### Q: Does the mid day meal create a legally enforceable claim?

Yes. Section 5(1)(b) entitles every child up to class VIII, or within the age group of six to fourteen years, to one midday meal, free of charge, every day except school holidays, in schools run by local bodies, government schools and government aided schools, meeting the nutritional standards in Schedule II. The entitlement predates the Act in enforceable form: the Supreme Court's order of 28 November 2001 in the right to food case directed cooked midday meals in government and government assisted primary schools. The Mid-Day Meal Rules, 2015 add a money remedy, requiring a food security allowance of the grain quantity and the cooking cost where the meal is not provided on a school day.

### Q: What is the difference between a priority household and an Antyodaya household?

Both are eligible households under the Act, but the entitlement is measured differently. A priority household's entitlement is per person: five kilograms of foodgrains per person per month, so a larger household receives more. An Antyodaya household's entitlement is per household: thirty-five kilograms per month regardless of the household's size. The Antyodaya category is carried into the Act from the Antyodaya Anna Yojana, the scheme launched on 25 December 2000 for the poorest households, and section 3's first proviso preserves its measure and its prices. States identify Antyodaya households under the guidelines of that scheme, and the remaining eligible households as priority households under state guidelines framed under section 10.

### Q: Who fixes the number of people each state may cover under the Act?

The Central Government does, under section 9. It determines the percentage coverage under the distribution system for the rural and urban areas of each state, within the ceilings in section 3(2) of up to seventy-five per cent rural and up to fifty per cent urban, and the total number of persons to be covered is calculated on the basis of population estimates as per the census of which the relevant figures have been published. The state government's role begins after that number is fixed: within it, the state identifies which households hold the entitlement. The division is the Act's central federal bargain, a central quota and a state list.

### Q: How does a household get its name on the list of eligible households?

Through identification by its state government under section 10. The state must, within the number of persons determined for it under section 9, identify the households covered by the Antyodaya Anna Yojana under that scheme's guidelines and the remaining households as priority households under guidelines the state itself specifies. The Act writes no national income or asset test; states built their lists on their own criteria, many drawing on the Socio Economic and Caste Census of 2011. The list must be placed in the public domain and displayed prominently under section 11, and section 10(2) requires it to be updated within the same numerical ceiling.

### Q: What is a child entitled to receive through the anganwadi under the Act?

Under section 5(1)(a), every child from six months to six years of age is entitled to an age appropriate meal, free of charge, through the local anganwadi, meeting the nutritional standards in Schedule II: five hundred calories and twelve to fifteen grams of protein for children from six months to three years and from three to six years. For a child below six months, the Act directs that exclusive breastfeeding be promoted instead. Under section 6, a child identified through the anganwadi as suffering from malnutrition is entitled to meals meeting the higher standard of eight hundred calories and twenty to twenty-five grams of protein. Section 5(2) also requires the anganwadi to have facilities for cooking, drinking water and sanitation.

### Q: What can a household do if its name is wrongly left off the list of eligible households?

The Act gives the household three practical levers. The list of eligible households must be placed in the public domain and displayed prominently under section 11, so an omission can be identified and pointed to as a documented error rather than a rumour. Section 10(2) obliges the state government to update the list within the number of persons determined under section 9, so correction is a standing statutory process and not a favour. And the grievance machinery applies: a complaint may be taken through the internal mechanism under section 14 to the District Grievance Redressal Officer, whose remit covers matters relating to the distribution of entitled foodgrains and the enforcement of entitlements under the Act.

### Q: What must happen when the central pool falls short of what a state needs?

Section 23 answers the question in money rather than in grain. If there is a short supply of foodgrains from the central pool to a state, the Central Government shall provide funds to the extent of the short supply to the state government, in the manner prescribed, so that the state can meet its obligations under Chapter II. The design recognises that the state's duties to entitled persons cannot be suspended by a failure in the Centre's procurement or movement of grain. It is distinct from the force majeure position in section 44, under which no claim lies where war, flood, drought, fire, cyclone or earthquake affects the regular supply itself.

### Q: What is a State Food Commission, who sits on it and what is it empowered to do?

Section 16 requires every state government to constitute a State Food Commission to monitor and review the implementation of the Act. It consists of a Chairperson, five other members and a Member-Secretary who must be a state government officer not below the rank of Joint Secretary; at least two of those office holders must be women, and one must belong to the Scheduled Castes and one to the Scheduled Tribes. Members serve terms of up to five years and may not hold office after sixty-five. The Commission monitors and evaluates implementation, inquires into violations of Chapter II entitlements on its own motion or on complaint, advises the state government, hears appeals against the District Grievance Redressal Officer, and reports annually to the state legislature.

### Q: Which reforms did the Act require in the public distribution system?

Section 12 requires the Central and state governments to endeavour to progressively undertake reforms including doorstep delivery of foodgrains to the outlets of the system; application of information and communication technology, including end to end computerisation, to record transactions transparently and prevent diversion; leveraging Aadhaar for unique identification of beneficiaries; full transparency of records; preference to public institutions, panchayats, self help groups and cooperatives in licensing fair price shops, and management of shops by women or their collectives; diversification of the commodities distributed over time; support to local distribution models and grain banks; and schemes such as cash transfer or food coupons to deliver the Chapter II entitlements, in areas and in a manner the Central Government prescribes.

### Q: Does the Act guarantee only foodgrains, or nutrition more broadly?

Both, but with different legal strength. The foodgrain entitlement in section 3 is direct and quantified: five kilograms per person per month for priority households, thirty-five kilograms per household for Antyodaya households, at Schedule I prices. The nutrition guarantees are broader in reach, covering meals for children through the anganwadi and at school, meals for malnourished children, and meals and a maternity benefit for pregnant women and lactating mothers, all measured against the calorie and protein standards in Schedule II. But sections 4 to 6 operate through schemes framed by the Central Government and implemented under section 7 with prescribed cost sharing, while section 3 operates directly. Schedule III adds objectives for advancing food and nutritional security that governments must strive to progressively realise, without making them enforceable claims.

### Q: What relief can a District Grievance Redressal Officer direct?

The officer, appointed or designated for each district under section 15, hears complaints regarding non-distribution of entitled foodgrains or meals, and matters relating to them, and takes necessary action for their redressal in the manner and within the time the state government prescribes. The officer's central instrument is the recommendation of relief, and the Act gives that recommendation a sanction: under section 33, a public servant or authority found by the State Food Commission to have failed, without reasonable cause, to provide the relief the officer recommended, or to have wilfully ignored the recommendation, is liable to a penalty not exceeding five thousand rupees. A complainant dissatisfied with the redressal may appeal to the State Food Commission under section 15(6).

### Q: How did the Supreme Court's right to food case prepare the ground for this Act?

The Act codified what the Court had already begun to enforce. In People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 196 of 2001, brought after starvation deaths in drought affected Rajasthan, the Supreme Court read the right to food into Article 21 and, by its interim order of 28 November 2001, converted eight government food schemes, including the public distribution system, the Antyodaya Anna Yojana, the midday meal scheme and the child development services, into legal entitlements, directing cooked midday meals in government and government assisted primary schools. The Court kept the case under continuing mandamus for years, acting through appointed Commissioners. When Parliament legislated in 2013, the entitlements in Chapter II followed the map the Court's orders had drawn.

### Q: Why does the Act cover a fixed share of the population instead of testing the income of each household?

Because the identification of poverty household by household had failed at scale under the targeted system that ran from 1997, producing large errors in both directions: households with means on the lists, and destitute households off them. A percentage of the population, set high enough that most of the poor are arithmetically inside it, removes the need for the ration shop system to decide who is poor and confines identification to filling a state quota under section 10. The cost is accepted inclusion of many non-poor households, which critics such as the Shanta Kumar Committee of 2015 answered by proposing coverage near forty per cent. Defenders, including Jean Drèze and Reetika Khera, answer that a wrongly excluded poor household pays for targeting errors with hunger, while a wrongly included household costs only the price of its grain.
