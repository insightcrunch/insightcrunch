The death-feigning potion that Friar Laurence hands to Juliet in Act 4 is usually introduced as a clever piece of plotting, a desperate cleric's bright idea, and that introduction is wrong in a way that distorts everything downstream. The vial holds the oldest machinery in the whole tragedy. Long before any Veronese lover drank, heroines of Greek romance were swallowing drugs that stopped breath and pulse convincingly enough to earn them a funeral and a tomb; Italian tale-tellers were dosing husbands and brides with powders measured in days; tellers of wonder tales were laying apparently dead girls in glass coffins to wait out their enchantments. The playwright invented neither the drug nor its schedule nor its terrors. What the play received, through a documented chain of Italian, French, and English books, was a device already ancient, already engineered, already freighted with the fear of waking underground. This article surveys that device whole: its taxonomy, its cases across Greek romance, the Italian novella, and the wider tale tradition, the way each telling builds the machine differently, and the reason this one part of the story, alone among its parts, refuses to produce a single inventor.

![A sleeping draught in a glass vial beside a tomb slab, the death-feigning potion of the folktale tradition - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict can be stated at the outset, because the evidence permits a firm one. The feigned-death drug is a motif, not an invention: a travelling piece of narrative equipment, catalogued by folklorists across languages and centuries, which the Verona story absorbed from the common stock of Mediterranean storytelling and then transmitted, in documentary form, from Masuccio to Da Porto to Bandello to Boaistuau to Brooke and Painter and the stage. Two different kinds of history meet in the vial, and confusing them is the standing error the rest of this article works against. The near history is a chain of books, provable by verbal parallels, and the scholarship of Geoffrey Bullough mapped it definitively. The deep history is a diffusion of motif, provable only by recurrence, and no chain of books will ever account for it, because the device appears in texts that cannot have read one another. The play's forty-two hours are the endpoint of both histories at once: a folktale machine, tightened by a dramatist until its tolerances vanished.

## The Potion Has No Inventor: The Verdict of the Motif Map

Every other major element of the tragedy can be assigned a birthplace. The feud has a genealogy in Dante's paired family names. The balcony, the nurse's errands, the wedding-night parting, the tomb-side reconciliation: each enters the written record at an identifiable desk, in an identifiable decade, and source study earns its keep by naming the desk. The drug defeats that method. Set the surviving instances side by side and the pattern that emerges is not a line but a scatter: a Greek prose romance of roughly the second century, a Neapolitan tale collection of 1476, a comic novella in Boccaccio a century earlier still, a German wonder tale recorded in 1812 whose heroine sleeps in glass rather than underground, and behind all of them the strong probability of oral tellings that no library holds. A device found in texts separated by language, religion, genre, and a thousand years, performing the same work in the same sequence, is not awaiting the discovery of its author. It is awaiting recognition as common property.

That recognition changes how the play's most debated scene should be read. Friar Laurence's offer in Act 4, Scene 1 has attracted centuries of moral argument: the plan is called reckless, presumptuous, a cleric meddling in pharmacology beyond his competence. The folktale evidence reframes the charge. Inside the fiction, the friar is not experimenting. He is reaching for a recognised instrument, the way a physician in a Greek romance reaches for it, the way an abbot in Boccaccio reaches for it, with a practitioner's confidence in dose and duration: "Thou shalt continue two and forty hours, / And then awake as from a pleasant sleep" (4.1.105-106). The speech's calm, itemised certainty, its checklist of suspended signs, is the voice of a tradition that has run this procedure many times. The tragedy's horror is not that the device is exotic. The horror is that a device engineered across centuries for rescue is here run with no slack, inside a feud, on a deadline set by a forced wedding, and it fails by hours.

The map that organises this survey, named in the prose of this article as the InsightCrunch motif map, therefore plots two kinds of marker at once. Its solid lines are the documented transmissions, book to book, where verbal evidence proves descent. Its open field is the motif's wider range, where recurrence proves only that the machine was available to any teller who needed it. Keeping those two marks distinct is the whole discipline of the subject, and the sections that follow hold them apart: first the catalogue science that defines a motif, then the cases in the order of their surviving texts, then the engineering comparison, then the quarrel between the folklorists and the source scholars, adjudicated.

## What Is a Folktale Motif, and How Do the Catalogues File a Death-Feigning Drug?

A motif, in the technical sense fixed by Stith Thompson, is the smallest element in a tale that has the power to persist in tradition: a single actor, object, incident, or device that tellers retain while everything around it changes names, cities, and centuries. Thompson's Motif-Index of Folk-Literature, published in six volumes between 1932 and 1936 and revised between 1955 and 1958, files the inherited stock of world storytelling under lettered classes, and its arrangement already teaches the lesson this article needs. The feigned death sits in the K class, the deceptions, where the index records escape by shamming death under K522; the deathlike sleep induced by a drug belongs with the magic objects and sleeps of the D class; the premature burial and the waking in the tomb gather under the motifs of the dead and of ordeals. A single Juliet plot, filed honestly, scatters across several drawers of the cabinet. That scatter is the point. The catalogue does not recognise plots. It recognises parts, and it demonstrates, drawer by drawer, that the parts had independent lives long before any author assembled them into this particular tragedy.

The tale-type index built by Antti Aarne, revised by Thompson in 1961 and again by Hans-Jörg Uther in 2004, supplies the complementary evidence at the level of whole tales. The heroine in the glass coffin, poisoned into a deathlike state by a morsel of apple and restored when the morsel is dislodged, is filed as type ATU 709, the Snow White tale, recorded in its canonical form by Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm in 1812 and distributed, in Thompson's distribution notes, across Europe and beyond. The enchanted sleep of type ATU 410, the sleeping beauty, is a near neighbour and a useful control case: there the sleep is genuine magic, openly known, guarded rather than concealed, and no funeral is staged because no deception is intended. Set beside the Verona device, the control case isolates exactly what makes the potion a distinct machine. The drug's essence is not sleep. The essence is a false certificate of death, issued to deceive specific watchers, inside a specific social emergency, with a timed expiry. Sleep is merely the mechanism the certificate uses.

Folklore method also supplies the caution that the brief for this article demands, and it deserves statement before any case is examined. Recurrence is not descent. When the same device appears in a second-century Greek romance and in a fifteenth-century Neapolitan novella, three explanations compete: the later teller read the earlier text or its descendants; the device travelled orally through tellings no record preserves; or two tellers, facing the same narrative problem, engineered the same solution independently, the explanation folklorists call polygenesis. The narrative problem here is unusually constraining, and its constraint favours recurrence. A plot that must remove a heroine from an unwanted marriage without killing her, hide her from her family, and restore her to a lover later, has very few available mechanisms: flight, disguise, substituted brides, and the feigned death. The drug is what the problem invents when flight is barred and the story requires the family's grief to be genuine. Where documentation exists, descent should be claimed; where it does not, the honest word is motif, and the honest map shows a field, not a line.

A researcher assembling a dossier of this kind, catalogue numbers on one side and play texts on the other, accumulates the sort of private archive that repays keeping in a single searchable store; tools such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) serve exactly that habit of cumulative, cross-referenced note-keeping, in which a motif filed under K522 in one year can be set against a quarto reading collated in another. The dossier matters because the argument of this article is cumulative: no single instance proves the depth of the tradition, and the depth is the finding.

## The Greek Romance Prototype: Anthia's Potion in the Ephesiaca

The earliest surviving full working of the device in European fiction stands in the Ephesiaca of Xenophon of Ephesus, a Greek prose romance usually dated to the second century of the common era, and its construction already contains every moving part the later tradition will use. Anthia, separated from her husband Habrocomes by the romance's machinery of piracy, shipwreck, and sale, faces a forced marriage to Perilaus, a wealthy suitor at Tarsus. She obtains from Euxenus, a physician, a drug she asks for as an escape from the marriage. The physician's preparation performs other than its buyer in the bridal chamber expects poison to perform: it casts her into a sleep indistinguishable from death. She is mourned, entombed with ceremony, and wakes in the tomb in darkness. Robbers, drawn by the grave goods, open the tomb, find a living woman among the treasures, and carry her off to Alexandria to be sold. The device has separated the lovers, suspended the heroine, deceived every watcher, and delivered her alive into the next movement of the plot. The full study of this instance, its manuscript history, and its bearing on the play belongs to the companion treatment of [Xenophon's Ephesiaca and the Sleeping Potion](/2025/11/21/xenophon-ephesiaca-sleeping-potion/); the survey needs the instance at comparative length, as the prototype against which the later engineering is measured.

Measured against the play, the prototype's tolerances are generous, and the generosity is structural. The romance does not price the dose in hours. Anthia's sleep lasts as long as the narrative requires: through the funeral, into the tomb, until the waking can be witnessed by the men who will move her to the next episode. Nobody in the romance is racing a stated deadline, because the romance's separation engine runs on geography rather than on clocks: once awake, Anthia must cross the Mediterranean world, through sale and threatened brothel and the long fidelity plot, before reunion at Ephesus closes the book. The waking, which in Verona is the catastrophe's trigger, is in Ephesus merely a change of captors. The tomb itself is safer. Anthia wakes among grave goods in a tomb built to be robbed, in a genre whose contract guarantees the heroine's preservation, and the robbers who find her have commercial reasons to keep her alive and saleable. The machine, in its Greek form, is a device for continuing a story. Only later, in the Italian chain, does it become a device for ending one.

Two neighbouring Greek cases fix the prototype's boundaries by contrast, and the motif map records both. In Chariton's Callirhoe, older than the Ephesiaca by a century or more, the heroine falls into apparent death with no drug at all: a blow stops her breath, she is entombed as dead, she wakes in the dark among the funeral treasures, and the pirate Theron, robbing the tomb, acquires a living cargo exactly as Xenophon's robbers do. The recurrence of the tomb waking and the robber rescue, with the pharmacy removed, proves that the deep structure is the apparent death and the tomb, and the drug is one technology among several for producing the state. In Achilles Tatius's Leucippe and Clitophon, the apparent death is produced by a third technology, stagecraft: Leucippe's sacrifice by Egyptian bandits is a performed disembowelment, arranged with animal entrails and a trick blade by confederates inside the band, and the hero watches a death that theatre, not pharmacology, has counterfeited. Drug, injury, and stage trick are three casings for one mechanism, and the catalogues file them in different drawers for precisely that reason.

### How does the Greek romance keep its sleeper safe after the waking?

Genre and economics keep her safe, not guards. Anthia wakes in a world contracted to preserve her: the robbers want saleable goods, every captor repeats the calculation that she is worth more alive, and the romance's promised reunion forbids her death. No friend attends the tomb, yet the waking cannot kill her.

Anthia wakes in a narrative world contracted to preserve her: the robbers who open the tomb want goods and find a woman whose living body is itself goods, saleable in Alexandria, and every subsequent captor repeats the calculation that a beautiful captive is worth more breathing. Her chastity and her life are protected by the same market that endangers them, and the romance can therefore afford a waking unwitnessed by any friend. The play strips both protections away. Juliet wakes in a family vault, among ancestral bones, where her living body is worth nothing to anyone present and everything depends on a single friar arriving inside a stated window. The Greek machine tolerates an empty tomb-side; the Veronese machine cannot, and the distance between those two tolerances is the distance between romance and tragedy.

## The Engineering of the Device: Supplier, Dose, Duration, Deception, Waking

Surveyed whole, the motif resolves into five engineering decisions, and every telling in the tradition can be described, without remainder, by the choices it makes at those five points. The supplier decides the device's authority: the drug comes from a physician, an abbot, a friar, a stepmother in disguise, or from no hand at all, and the supplier's profession tells the audience how far the procedure can be trusted. The dose decides its form: powder, draught, distilled liquor, poisoned fruit. The duration decides the plot's clock: an unmeasured night in the romance, three days through the Italian chain, two and forty hours in the play. The deception decides its audience: a bridegroom, a household, a city, a court. The waking decides its price: rescue, sale, grief, madness, or death. Read down the duration column and the tradition's whole drift stands revealed. Greek romance leaves the clock unpriced because its plots run on distance. The Italian novella standardises the dose at three days, a liturgical and narrative span long enough for a funeral, a burial, and a letter's journey, and the standard holds through Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Painter without variation, because the novella plot needs the margin. The play alone prices the device like a contract, and the pricing is the tragedy's signature. Friar Laurence does not say three days. He says: "And in this borrowed likeness of shrunk death / Thou shalt continue two and forty hours, / And then awake as from a pleasant sleep" (4.1.104-106). Forty-two hours is three days with the slack cut away, fitted to a wedding moved forward a day and to a letter that must cross to Mantua and back inside the same span. The motif arrives in the play with its safety margin deliberately machined off, and every subsequent failure, the quarantined letter, the closed church doors, the friar's late arrival, lands inside the gap the machining created.

Read across the supplier row and a second pattern emerges, as telling as the first. The device is almost never supplied by a magician. Its engineers are a physician, an abbot, and a succession of friars: credentialed men, custodians of bodies or of souls, whose authority certifies the counterfeit. The wonder tale is the exception that proves the rule, since the queen's apple works in a world where magic is the local physics, and even there the supplier comes disguised as a pedlar, trading on the trust a seller of goods commands. The novella and romance instances insist on professional suppliers because the device's plausibility within the fiction depends on it: a death certificate that an entire city accepts must be issued, in effect, by the class of men whose business is the boundary between life and death. The play inherits the insistence and sharpens it. Its supplier is both kinds of custodian at once, a friar who is also the play's herbalist, and the concentration of both authorities in one cell is what lets a fourteen-year-old girl stake her life on a vial.

## The Comic Branch: Boccaccio's Ferondo and the Powder of Purgatory

A century before Masuccio, the device receives its most cynical working in the eighth tale of the third day of Boccaccio's Decameron, written around the middle of the fourteenth century, and the case matters to the survey because it proves the machine's neutrality. Ferondo, a rich and jealous peasant, is dosed by an abbot who desires Ferondo's wife: a powder in a drink produces a death so convincing that the man is buried in a tomb as a corpse. By night the abbot and a confederate monk lift the body out and install it in a dark cell, where Ferondo wakes to be told he is in purgatory, is fed, is beaten for his jealousy, and is kept in the performance for months while the abbot fathers a child on the wife. When the abbot restores him, Ferondo returns home persuaded he has risen from the dead, and the wife's pregnancy is credited to the resurrection. Every element of the tragic machine is present and inverted. The supplier is a cleric abusing custodial authority. The deception's audience is the victim himself. The waking is managed by the deceiver, in a counterfeit afterlife built as the tomb's annexe. The cost falls not on the sleeper's life but on his marriage, his dignity, and his understanding of the world.

The Ferondo case disciplines any account of the motif that treats it as inherently a lovers' device. In Boccaccio the drug serves lust and clerical satire; in the Greek romances it serves chastity and fidelity; in the Verona chain it serves marriage against parental command. The constant is not the purpose but the procedure: a certified false death, a tomb, a managed waking. Folklorists call such persistence through contrary uses the mark of a true motif, a part that survives reassembly into alien wholes, and the Ferondo powder is the survey's cleanest proof that the part was common Italian property generations before Da Porto gave it to Giulietta. No descent from Boccaccio's tale to the Verona novelle needs claiming, and none is claimed on the motif map; the value of the case is that it removes any temptation to treat the lovers' powder as a Sienese or Veronese invention. By 1476, when Masuccio's friar supplies Ganozza, Italian prose had been running the device, in comedy and in earnest, for well over a hundred years.

## The Novella Chain: How the Motif Entered the Lovers' Story

What Masuccio Salernitano did, in the thirty-third tale of Il Novellino, printed at Naples in 1476, was not to invent the device but to marry it to the story that would carry it to London. Mariotto of Siena, secretly married to Ganozza, kills a man, flees to Alexandria, and leaves his wife exposed to a forced second marriage; a friar supplies the powder, she is buried as dead, Mariotto returns on garbled news, opens the tomb, and takes her home, where discovery, arrest, and his execution follow, and Ganozza dies of grief. The assembled plot, secret marriage, killing, exile, feigned death, failed information, tomb opening, is recognisably the Verona machine five decades before Da Porto, and the full treatment of Masuccio's powder as narrative fact belongs to the study of that tale in this series; the survey records only the absorption. From this point the motif's history changes character. Before Masuccio, the record shows a field of recurrences. After Masuccio, it shows a chain of books, each demonstrably reading the last, and the device rides the chain as fixed equipment.

Luigi da Porto, writing Giulietta e Romeo in the 1520s and seeing it printed at Venice around 1530, takes the powder from the tradition Masuccio represents and reissues it with the names, the feud, and the city that make the story immortal; the potion as his Frate Lorenzo compounds it is the direct ancestor of the play's vial, and its study belongs to the Da Porto article in this series' own sequence of source studies. Matteo Bandello, printing the tale as the ninth novella of the second part of his Novelle at Lucca in 1554, enlarges the ceremony around the device without altering its specification: the draught, the three days, the failed letter, the tomb. Pierre Boaistuau's French adaptation of 1559 and the two English versions that follow, Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562 and William Painter's prose of 1566, transmit the specification intact across two languages and a religious frontier. The chain matters to the folktale argument in a way that cuts against a lazy version of it. Whatever the device's depth in oral and romance tradition, the play did not drink from the well directly. The playwright's vial arrived by post, in books, with the dose already standardised at three days by tellers who had themselves received it as equipment. The folktale root and the documentary chain are not rival explanations. They are the same object's deep past and near past, and the survey holds both.

Inside the chain, each reteller's handling of the device betrays a judgment about where its interest lies. Masuccio spends his care on the aftermath, the arrest and the execution, as if the powder were merely the plot's key and the law its subject. Da Porto concentrates on the tomb, granting his lovers speech together after the waking, the tradition's most generous management of the device's expiry. Bandello expands the funeral and the report, the public consumption of the false death. Brooke moralises the instrument, framing the whole in a preface that warns against the counsels that supply such remedies, yet keeps the three days and the failure of the letter exactly as received. Only the playwright re-engineers. The compression from three days to forty-two hours, the wedding advanced by a day against the plan, the letter's carrier stopped by a plague search: these are the alterations of a dramatist who understood that the inherited machine was too safe, that a rescue device with three days of slack could not generate the pressure his last act required. The folktale gave the play a machine built for rescue. The play's genius was to run it past its tolerances.

## Waking in the Tomb: The Danger the Play Makes Literal

Every telling of the motif contains a tomb, and every tomb contains the same unspoken clause in the contract: the sleeper must not wake alone, early, or unheard. The tradition manages the clause variously. The Greek romances hand the waking to robbers, whose arrival is guaranteed by the grave goods. The novella chain hands it to the friar's letter, whose failure is the plot's hinge. The wonder tale abolishes the clause altogether by displaying the sleeper in glass, above ground, in a coffin that is a window rather than a vault. The play is the tradition's extremest case because it alone stages the clause from inside the sleeper's imagination before the event, and then fulfils the imagination almost clause for clause. Juliet's soliloquy in Act 4, Scene 3 is the motif reflecting on itself: the first surviving instance in the device's long history in which the woman who will wake in the tomb rehearses the waking in advance, in terror, in detail, and drinks anyway.

The fears she itemises are the tradition's own case history, itemised by its latest heir. She fears the drug is poison, that the supplier has betrayed the certificate: "What if it be a poison, which the friar / Subtly hath ministered to have me dead" (4.3.24-25). She fears waking before the rescuer comes: "What if I wake before the time that Romeo / Come to redeem me? There's a fearful point" (4.3.30-31). She fears the air: the vault's "foul mouth" holds "no healthsome air". She fears madness among the bones, playing with her forefathers' joints, dashing out her brains against the stones. The soliloquy is usually read as a character study, proof of courage or of terror, and it is both; but its deeper interest for the survey is documentary. A device that had been operated for over a thousand years without a recorded word from the sleeper acquires, in the play, the sleeper's own risk assessment, and the assessment is accurate. Every fear she names is a failure mode the tradition had actually exhibited somewhere: suppliers do betray, in Boccaccio; wakings do go unwitnessed, in Siena; tombs do unhinge the living, in the fears the novella tellers gesture at and hurry past. The playwright had read the chain, and the chain's whole archive of danger is compressed into fifty lines of a girl talking herself into drinking.

### What does Juliet fear most about waking in the vault?

Madness, not suffocation, is the fear the speech keeps returning to. Juliet imagines waking before Romeo comes, among the packed bones of her ancestors and Tybalt's festering corpse, until terror unseats her reason and she plays with the dead men's joints and dashes out her brains against the stones.

The speech returns to the fear three times, circling from the drug's honesty to the air's foulness and settling at last on the mind's failure, because a waking that saves the body and loses the reason is the one outcome the plan has no answer for. The friar's schedule promised a rescue; the soliloquy prices the interval before it.

Beneath the soliloquy runs the period's material fear, the premature burial that early modern congregations dreaded with reason: plague pits dug in haste, cataleptic states misread by lay watchers, corpses that revived on the way to the grave and fed a literature of warning. The motif's whole career feeds on that fear and feeds it in return. A story in which a certified corpse wakes underground is the nightmare given plot, and the play's first audiences, for whom burial within a day of death was ordinary practice and medical certification of death was rudimentary, did not need a folklorist to explain why a girl might dash her brains out against a vault rather than wait in the dark. The tradition's later career confirms the diagnosis from the other side. The centuries after the play industrialised the fear: safety coffins with bells and breathing tubes, patented across the nineteenth century; waiting mortuaries in German cities where corpses lay under observation until decay certified what medicine could not; Edgar Allan Poe's tale "The Premature Burial" of 1844, cataloguing cases with a journalist's relish. The motif did not retire when it left the stage. It migrated into technology and terror literature, still doing its old work of asking how certain the living can be that the dead are dead.

## The Friar's Herbs and the Authority of the Supplier

The play certifies its supplier earlier and more carefully than any telling in the chain, and the certification scene is a soliloquy over a basket of herbs. Friar Laurence enters at dawn in Act 2, Scene 3 gathering simples, and states the pharmacology the whole tragedy will run on: "Within the infant rind of this weak flower / Poison hath residence and medicine power" (2.3.23-24). The doctrine is dual-use. The same plant heals in measure and kills in excess; the same earth that is "nature's mother" is her tomb; grace and rude will contend in every compounded thing. When the friar later offers Juliet a "distilled liquor" rather than a powder, the form itself testifies to the workshop the audience has seen: distillation is the herbalist's art, the extraction of a virtue from a rind, and the vial is a product of the garden in Act 2 as surely as the poison Romeo buys in Mantua is a product of the apothecary's poverty in Act 5. The herbal grounding of both drugs, and the garden speech's full doctrine, are treated in this series' study of [the Friar's herbs: poison and cure in Verona](/2022/10/28/friar-laurence-herbs-poison-cure/); the survey notes the structural point. The tradition required a credentialed supplier, and the play, alone among the tellings, shows the credentials being earned on stage, in soliloquy, two acts before the vial changes hands.

The certification also carries the tragedy's blame in embryo, and the motif comparison sharpens where the blame should fall. Boccaccio's abbot is a villain using credentials as cover; Euxenus is a professional serving a client's request; Masuccio's and Da Porto's friars are charitable technicians whose plans fail on logistics. The play's friar is certified, charitable, and exact, and his plan fails anyway, on logistics compounded by plague: the letter to Mantua, carried by Friar John, is stopped because the searchers of the town, "suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Sealed up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2.9-12). No earlier telling had imagined quarantine as the failure mode, because no earlier telling had compressed the schedule until a public-health cordon could eat the margin. The supplier's authority survives the play intact; Laurence's account of the drug in Act 5, Scene 3 is accepted by the Prince as expert testimony, and the man is pardoned where the chain's friars were punished. What fails is not the pharmacy. What fails is the calendar the pharmacy was asked to serve.

## Two Drugs in One Play: The Feigning Draught and the Mantua Poison

The tragedy is unique in the tradition for running the device in both directions at once. The novella chain knows one significant drug, the feigning draught; its poisons are incidental. The play builds its last two acts on a matched pair: a drug that counterfeits death and is administered in love, by a friend, in a cell, free of charge; and a drug that deals death and is administered in despair, by a stranger, in a hovel, for forty ducats. Romeo states the pairing's economics in the apothecary scene: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world, / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1.80-82). The feigning draught fails by arriving at its effect too slowly to save; the poison succeeds with terrible promptness, "drugs are quick" (5.1.85 in most modern editions the phrase closes Romeo's praise of the poison's speed). Between the two vials the play conducts its whole argument about intention and outcome: the benevolent drug kills three people by misadventure, and the murderous drug kills exactly its purchaser, on schedule, as specified. The metaphor system that binds the pair, poison as medicine and medicine as poison across the play's whole image stock, has its own study in this series, [Poison and Medicine as Metaphor](/2024/03/29/poison-and-medicine-metaphor-analysis/), and the survey borrows only the structural observation: the folktale device, imported into the play, is immediately twinned with its dark double, and the doubling is the playwright's, not the tradition's.

The plan scene itself, where the first of the pair is specified, repays reading as an engineering document, and its full analysis stands in the companion study of [Act 4 Scene 1: the Friar's desperate potion plan](/2023/10/20/act-4-scene-1-potion-plan/). The specification is complete in a way no source matches: the route of administration, the onset, the signs abolished one by one, the duration, the funeral arrangements, the waking arrangements, the notification arrangements. Da Porto's Lorenzo offers a powder and a promise; the play's Laurence offers a protocol. The protocol's completeness is itself a tragic device, since it leaves the audience, like Juliet, with nothing unspecified to hope in: every contingency has been named except the one that occurs.

### What does the Friar promise the drug will do to her body?

A total suspension of every sign of life. The Friar promises a cold and drowsy humour through the veins, the pulse stopped, no warmth and no breath, the colour faded from lips and cheeks, the eyes shut like death's own, and every part stiff, stark, and cold in death's borrowed likeness.

The itemisation is the point: a "cold and drowsy humour" running through the veins, the pulse's native progress surceased, no warmth, no breath, the roses in the lips and cheeks faded to paly ashes, the eyes' windows fallen, every part "stiff and stark and cold," appearing like death (4.1.96-103). The promise is a negative inventory, a checklist of every sign by which the living are told from the dead, each one cancelled in turn. Its completeness is the certificate's authority: a household that checks each sign, and finds each one absent, will bury its daughter without a dissenting voice in the room.

### Why does the Friar state an exact number of hours for the sleep?

The figure is exact because the plan must synchronise three separate timetables at once: a wedding fixed for Thursday, a funeral that must follow the drinking, and a letter that must reach Mantua and bring Romeo back inside the same span. A round three days would blur the joins; forty-two hours exposes every one of them.

The exact figure converts a folktale convenience into a dramatic contract. Three days, the tradition's standard allowance, is a span an audience cannot hold in mind as a single tension; two and forty hours is a fuse of measurable length, lit on Wednesday morning against a Thursday wedding, with a letter's round journey to Mantua burning along the same fuse. Stating the number lets every later delay be priced: the wedding advanced a day, the letter stopped overnight, the friar's walk to the vault, each cost is legible against the stated forty-two. The play's clock is the device's oldest feature made newly dangerous, a duration the audience can count while the characters cannot.

## Source, Analogue, or Motif? The Transmission Quarrel Among the Critics

The brief's complication, motif diffusion versus direct source, is not a pedant's distinction; it decides what kind of claim the folktale evidence can carry, and the three authorities the brief names stake out the three possible positions with unusual clarity. Geoffrey Bullough, in the first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957), fixed the documentary settlement that source study still uses. Bullough prints or summarises the chain (Brooke, Painter, and behind them Boaistuau, Bandello, Da Porto) as the play's sources proper, texts the playwright demonstrably worked from, and relegates everything beyond the chain to the category he calls analogues: parallel tellings that illuminate the play's materials without any claim that the playwright read them. The Ephesiaca stands in Bullough's volume as the type of the analogue, and the classification carries a hard bibliographical fact that no enthusiasm for folktale depth can dissolve. Xenophon's romance survived in a single Florentine manuscript and was not printed until 1726, in the Florence edition prepared by Antonio Cocchi, more than a century after the play and two centuries after Da Porto. Whatever reached Verona, it was not that book.

The folklorist's position answers from the other end of the evidence. Stith Thompson's whole science rests on the observation that narrative devices demonstrably travel without books: the motif indices record the feigned death, the deathlike sleep, and the tomb waking across oral traditions collected from tellers who had read nothing, in regions the novella chain never reached. On Thompson's showing, the absence of a printed Ephesiaca before 1726 proves nothing about the device's availability in 1520 or 1595, because devices of this durability move mouth to ear as readily as page to page, and a motif attested in Greek romance, in Italian novella, and in the wonder tale is attested as common property of the storytelling Mediterranean and the Europe beyond it. Where Bullough asks what the playwright read, Thompson asks what the culture held. The disagreement is genuine and cannot be split by courtesy, because the two questions license different sentences. Bullough's method permits "the play's potion descends from Da Porto's powder." Thompson's permits "the play's potion is one flowering of a device its culture had cultivated for a millennium and a half." Only one of those sentences can stand as the headline of an honest account, unless the depths are kept apart.

The editor's position, represented by Brian Gibbons in his Arden edition of 1980, cuts across the quarrel by relocating the interest. For the editor preparing the play for readers and actors, the question is less where the vial came from than what the playwright did to the inherited specification, and Gibbons's introduction dwells on the transformation: the compression of Brooke's months into days, of the three-day sleep into forty-two hours, the wedding advanced, the letter's failure redesigned. On the editorial view, the tradition's role is to supply the control measurement against which the play's alterations register as meaning. A three-day sleep inherited and retained would mean nothing; a three-day sleep inherited and cut announces that the dramatist wanted a machine with no margin, and the announcement is the interpretation.

The adjudication this survey reaches keeps each authority sovereign in its own depth and denies each the others'. At the depth of the book chain, Bullough is simply right, and the folktale framing must not be allowed to blur it: the play's potion descends, documentably, from Da Porto through Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Painter, and any sentence implying that the playwright drew the device from oral tradition or from Greek romance is false to the evidence. At the depth of the device itself, Thompson is right, and the documentary framing must not be allowed to shrink it: the chain's tellers were themselves drawing on a motif with a recorded life of well over a thousand years before Masuccio, distributed far beyond any route the chain travelled, and the chain explains the play's wording without explaining the device's existence. Gibbons's editorial measure then supplies the verdict's third clause: the play's contribution to the motif is neither the drug nor its story but its recalibration, the forty-two hours and the soliloquy, the two alterations that convert an instrument of rescue into an instrument of catastrophe. Kenneth Muir's Shakespeare's Sources (1957), the other great settlement of the same decade, supports the layered verdict from within source study itself, since Muir's distinction between the source a playwright used and the tradition a source itself drew on is exactly the distinction the vial requires. Diffusion above, descent below, transformation at the surface: the motif map's two kinds of marker, and the editor's measure, describe one object at three depths, and the quarrel dissolves when the depths are named.

## Why the Device Endures: Separation, Suspension, and the Reunion It Promises

A motif survives fifteen centuries of retelling by solving a problem that recurs wherever the surrounding societies recur, and the problem the feigning drug solves can be stated in one sentence: a heroine must be removed from a marriage market that owns her, without death, disgrace, flight, or consent from the owners. Every term of the problem is load-bearing. Death is barred because the story wants her back. Disgrace is barred because the story wants her marriageable at the end. Flight is barred, usually, by walls, watchfulness, and the economics of a daughter's value. Consent is barred by definition, since the forced wedding is the problem. The drug answers all four constraints at a stroke. It removes her with the family's own hands, in a funeral they pay for and weep at; it preserves her honour inside the unimpeachable alibi of death; it requires no accomplice beyond one credentialed supplier; and it schedules her return. The device is, in engineering terms, the only solution in its class, and its recurrence across Greek, Italian, French, English, and German tellings tracks the recurrence of the problem: daughters disposed in marriage by fathers, from Ephesus to Siena to Verona to the wonder tale's kingdom, where the queen's apple answers a stepmother's rivalry with the same removal, gentler in management and identical in structure.

The device also flatters the audience's position in a way few mechanisms can match, and the flattery is part of its durability. A feigned death installs the audience as the sole possessor of the truth. Every mourner on stage or page is deceived; the reader never is. The funeral scenes that the device generates are therefore double throughout: grief performed over a living body, eulogies addressed to a sleeper, wedding preparations convertible to burial preparations with the same provisions, as the play's Act 4 turns a feast into a funeral in a single speech of Capulet's. Dramatic irony is often described as the play's method; the motif shows the method to be inherited equipment, perfected by romance tellers who learned that a lament over a heroine known to be alive produces a pleasure available from no other configuration, pity and superiority and suspense compounded. The play's wager is to hold that configuration one act too long, until the irony curdles: the audience that has enjoyed knowing better than the mourners discovers, in the vault, that knowledge without power to intervene is the tragedy's definition of hell, and Romeo dies of the same information failure the audience has been congratulating itself on escaping.

The reunion the device promises is the third term of its appeal, and the tradition splits on whether the promise is kept. Greek romance keeps it: the whole point of Anthia's drug is a marriage preserved through separation to a reunion the genre guarantees. The wonder tale keeps it in the gentlest form, waking to marriage as the story's final chord. The novella chain breaks it, and the breaking is the chain's grim discovery: once the device is placed inside a world of letters, roads, plague, and law, the promise depends on communications the world can interrupt, and the reunion degrades into a meeting over a corpse, then into speeches exchanged over two. Da Porto still lets his lovers speak together in the tomb, the promise half-kept at the price of everything else. The play withdraws even that: Romeo dies believing the certificate genuine, Juliet wakes to a husband already cold, and the reunion the motif had promised for fifteen centuries is staged as its own negation, two people in one vault, awake and dead in the wrong order, minutes apart. The device endures because each age gets the ending its world believes in from the same machine.


## The Supplier's Many Faces: Physician, Abbot, Friar, Stepmother

The taxonomy's first axis deserves a section of its own, because the supplier is where each culture files its trust. Euxenus in the Ephesiaca is a physician, and the choice is documentary realism: in the Greek world the man who compounds drugs is the man who can be asked for one that walks the border of death, and Anthia's transaction has the form of a consultation, a fee, and a professional's discretion. The romance does not moralise the supplier. He is neither villain nor confessor, only a technician whose art the plot rents, and the neutrality is itself information: in the romance's world the device is a service, purchasable, unremarkable, part of what medicine is for. Set beside that neutrality the Italian clerical suppliers and the temperature changes. Boccaccio's abbot is a predator whose office supplies both the powder and the purgatory theatre, and the tale's satire depends on the supplier's cloth: the man certified to judge souls counterfeits a soul's journey. Masuccio's friar and the Lorenzos of Da Porto and Bandello stand at the opposite pole of the same cloth, charitable technicians whose orders make them the natural custodians of a secret marriage and its pharmacological defence. The tradition, in other words, runs an experiment across its instances: the same office, abused in the comic branch, honoured in the tragic chain, and the device itself indicted in neither. What the experiment isolates is the supplier's intention as the one variable the machine cannot control.

The play absorbs both poles and adds a third term. Friar Laurence is the charitable technician of the novella line, certified on stage by the herb soliloquy, and his intention is the plan's most defended element: the Prince's closing judgment accepts his account, and the text gives no speech that convicts him of self-interest. Yet the play also builds into its supplier the abbot's structural sin in innocent form, the enjoyment of the secret. Laurence relishes the stratagem's cleverness, itemises it with a craftsman's pride, and extends his practice beyond the vial to the management of the aftermath, arriving at the vault with "a mattock and a spade" and a crow of iron, still stage-managing. The folktale comparison clarifies the tragedy's allocation of fault better than the moral tradition has done. Across fifteen centuries the device never fails because a supplier compounded it wrongly; the powders work, in Ephesus, in Florence's comic imagination, in Siena, in Verona. Failures cluster in the information system around the device, the letter, the report, the cordon, and in the intentions of men who are not the sleeper. The play is faithful to that distribution to the letter. Its drug performs exactly as specified, on schedule, sign by sign. Everything that kills is adjacent to the machine, not inside it.

The wonder tale's supplier completes the taxonomy by inversion. The queen in the Snow White tale compounds no draught and certifies nothing; she poisons a fruit and lets appetite administer it, and the deathlike state she produces is a weapon aimed at a rival, not a refuge sought by the sleeper. The case matters because it proves the device's chemistry is morally inert even in the tale tradition's own judgment: the identical state, drug-sleep counterfeiting death, is rescue in the heroine's hands and murder in the stepmother's, and the tales keep the two filings separate by the single fact of consent. Juliet's case is the taxonomy's most examined precisely because consent is its entire substance. She asks, she is told the terms, she rehearses the risks alone, she drinks with a toast. The tradition knows no sleeper more consenting, and the play's bleakness is that consent, information, and a competent supplier, all present, still do not suffice against a stopped letter.

## Three Days: The Tradition's Standard Dose and the Play's Recalibration

Duration is the device's most conservative feature, and its conservatism is evidence. From Masuccio through Painter, across a century of retelling in three languages, the dose holds at three days with a fidelity that copyists usually reserve for names and places. The span is overdetermined, chosen by several logics at once. It is a funeral's span: time to certify death, lay out the body, summon kin, dig or open the vault, and bury, with a margin of public mourning that makes the certificate socially irreversible. It is a journey's span: time for news to travel to an exiled husband and for the husband to travel back, the two movements the novella plot must fit inside the sleep. And it carries a resonance no teller needed to state, the three days of entombment that Christian Europe heard in its central story, a span after which rising is the expected miracle. A heroine who wakes on the third day borrows that expectation without claiming it, and the novella tellers, friars' tales among them, left the resonance unspoken and operative. The Greek romances, innocent of the standard, leave the dose unmeasured; the wonder tale, whose coffin is a display case rather than a vault, leaves it open-ended. Only the novella chain, needing funerals and journeys to interlock, machined the dose to three days, and only the play re-machined it.

The recalibration to two and forty hours is the single most consequential alteration the playwright made to any inherited element, and the folktale survey clarifies its mechanics. Forty-two hours is not a dose; it is a schedule. It is computed backward from a Thursday wedding and a Wednesday drinking, fitted to a letter that must reach Mantua, be read, and bring a husband across country roads to a vault on the second night. Where the three-day standard embeds slack at every joint, an extra day for weather, for roads, for a messenger's horse going lame, the forty-two-hour schedule embeds none, and the play then spends its fifth act discovering the joints: Capulet advances the wedding a day and burns the schedule's front margin; the plague searchers seal Friar John's doors and burn its middle; Balthasar, riding with the false report and no letter to correct him, burns the rest. Source study has long noted the compression of the lovers' whole story from Brooke's months to the play's days; the motif survey adds that the compression is at its most violent inside the vial, where the inherited margin of safety, three days against a letter's round trip, is cut by more than a third at exactly the moment the plot doubles the letter's importance. The drug's clock is the tragedy's clock in miniature, and both were set by the same hand, deliberately, past the tolerances the tradition had spent centuries establishing.

## What the Waking Costs: A Comparative Reckoning

The motif map's final column, the cost of the waking, is where the tradition keeps its moral ledger, and the entries vary so widely that the variation itself becomes the survey's most human finding. At the ledger's gentle end stands the wonder tale, where the waking costs nothing at all: the morsel leaves the sleeper's throat, the eyes open, the prince is waiting, and the device discharges its whole debt in a wedding. The gentleness is diagnostic. In the wonder tale the sleeper never consented and never schemed; the state was inflicted, so its lifting is pure restitution, and no price attaches. Greek romance charges more. Anthia's waking buys her nothing but a change of owners: the robbers who lift her from the tomb sell her in Alexandria, and the device that was meant to preserve her marriage delivers her to a slave market, from which the romance needs most of its remaining length to retrieve her. The cost is captivity and years, charged not for the waking but for the world's traffic in beautiful bodies, and the romance's honesty about the charge is part of its realism. The device solves the wedding problem perfectly and solves nothing else; the sea, the market, and the road still wait outside the tomb door.

Boccaccio charges the cost to the sleeper's household rather than the sleeper: Ferondo wakes to beatings in a counterfeit purgatory and returns home to a wife pregnant by his deceiver, his resurrection a public miracle that launders the abbot's adultery. The comic branch's ledger is the tradition's most cynical, since the waking there profits exactly the man who engineered the sleep, and the sleeper's reward for surviving his own funeral is a cuckold's horns sanctified by theology. Masuccio raises the price to a life, and not the sleeper's: Mariotto, who performs the tomb opening that Greek robbers performed for profit, performs it as a husband and pays as a criminal, executed for the violation the plot required of him, while Ganozza, the sleeper, survives her waking only to die of its news. The novella's ledger introduces the pattern the chain will perfect: the cost migrates from the sleeper to the rescuer, and the device's safety record is maintained by billing the man who comes to the tomb.

The play completes the migration and itemises the bill: Paris, dead at the vault door for defending a grave he believed desecrated; Romeo, dead by the efficient poison beside the inefficient draught's sleeper; Juliet, dead by her own hand minutes after the waking the whole machine was built to deliver; and beyond the vault, a settlement between two houses purchased, as the Prince's closing audit states, by the sacrifice of the heirs. The comparative reckoning exposes what the play added to the motif's economics. In every earlier telling, the waking's cost, however steep, is paid and the account closes: Anthia reaches Ephesus, Ferondo reaches home, even Masuccio's Siena buries its dead and ends. In the play the cost is structured as a transfer: the feud, which could not be settled by edicts, marriages, or banishments, is settled by the bill the device presents, and the golden statues are its receipt. The folktale machine was built to let a heroine escape a wedding. Its final form lets two families escape a feud, at retail price, and the survey's darkest finding is that the machine worked: the waking did everything the tradition ever promised, ended the old order and founded a peace, merely billing the lovers for the service.

## The Bride's Instrument: Why the Sleeper Is Almost Always a Woman

One further pattern in the survey demands a verdict, because it governs the device's meaning in the play more than any question of dose or supplier. Across the tradition the sleeper is a woman, and the single male exception, Ferondo, is dosed by another's design and spends his sleep as a dupe. Anthia, Callirhoe, Leucippe, Ganozza, Giulietta in her successive namings, the girl in the glass coffin: the device is the bride's instrument, and the reason is structural rather than decorative. The marriage systems the tales inhabit dispose of daughters and merely inconvenience sons. A son facing an unwanted match can flee, fight, study abroad, enter religion, or wait for an inheritance to change the bargaining table; the tales grant their heroes all of these exits and the heroes use them, Mariotto to Alexandria, Romeo to Mantua. A daughter's exits are the device's whole list: the convent, madness, disgrace, or the feigned death. The drug is what female agency looks like inside a system that has priced every other form of refusal out of reach, a technology for saying no in the only register the household cannot overrule, the register of the corpse.

The play understands this with a precision the sources only imply, and it builds the understanding into the plot's personnel. Juliet's alternatives are canvassed on stage and closed one by one: her mother will not intercede, her father threatens disowning and dragging her to church, the Nurse, her last counsellor, advises bigamy. The cell she visits for confession is the one door the household cannot watch, and the man inside it holds the one instrument the household cannot countermand. Her soliloquy before drinking is the tradition's fullest record of the instrument being chosen with open eyes, and its catalogue of fears doubles as a catalogue of the system's closures: she cannot flee to Mantua, cannot confess the marriage without being locked away, cannot trust even the supplier entirely. Source study sometimes treats the potion as the plot's convenience, the hinge any dramatist would need. The folktale survey corrects the condescension. The device is a social document, the standard equipment of heroines in worlds that left them no other, and its antiquity measures the antiquity of the conditions that required it. When the conditions recur, the device recurs; the glass coffins and the powders are one long archive of daughters declining, in the only available grammar, to be given away.

## The Tomb as Way Station: Burial Customs Inside the Motif

The tomb in these tales is never merely scenery, and the survey closes its case work with the building itself, because the device's plausibility rests on burial customs that the tales exploit with an engineer's eye. The Greek romances entomb their heroines above the danger line of the plot's realism: family tombs built as chambers, furnished, stocked with treasure, and therefore visited, by mourners, by priests, and with dependable regularity by robbers, whose profession is the waking's guarantee. A sealed pit would end the story; the chamber tomb, entered by a door, supplied with goods worth stealing, is a way station by architectural design, and the heroines wake in rooms that traffic passes through. The Italian chain inherits the architecture and localises it: the family vault, the monument in the churchyard where all of a house's ancestors are packed, in the play's phrase, is a chamber of the same kind, visitable, openable with a crow and a mattock, and stocked with the only treasure the feud's tomb robbers seek, the bodies of their enemies and their loves. Juliet's fear of the vault's "foul mouth" and its packed bones is the architecture felt from inside: a family tomb is a crowd, and waking in one is waking in company that cannot help.

The wonder tale's glass coffin is the same architecture sublimed. It answers the identical engineering problem, how to keep a deathlike body accessible to its rescuer, by the opposite method: total display instead of a robbed chamber. Nothing must be opened, nothing dug, no letter must arrive; the coffin stands in the open, the sleeper visible, the waking a matter of a stumble and a jolt when the coffin is moved. Between the robbed tomb and the glass coffin the tradition states its whole theory of the device's danger. The danger was never the sleep. The danger is custody: who holds the body during the interval, who can reach it, who knows. The play's vault is the tradition's worst custody arrangement, a chamber whose keyholders are enemies of the plan, in a churchyard patrolled by a watch, reachable by a husband who has been sent the wrong news and a friar who arrives with tools minutes after the poison. Folklore had been solving the custody problem for a thousand years, with robbers, with glass, with friars stationed at the tomb. The play's catastrophe is, in the survey's final technical judgment, a custody failure, and the motif's long history exists to make that judgment legible: the machine was sound, the dose was standard until it was cut, and the body was left in the one pair of hands, chance's, that the tradition had always taken care to avoid.

## The Failed Letter: The Motif's Second Machine

The survey has treated the drug as the device, but the tradition knows better: from Masuccio onward, the feigning potion travels with a twin, and the twin is a message that must outrun a funeral. The two machines are geared together. The drug creates a secret shared by exactly three people, the sleeper, the supplier, and the absent husband who must be told; the letter is the secret's only road; and the road's failure converts the rescue device into the catastrophe device with no change to the drug at all. Folklore's catalogues file the two under different letters, deception and message, but the novella chain treats them as one mechanism with two parts, and the play inherits the coupling entire. Friar Laurence states the gearing in the plan scene itself: against the waking, "Shall Romeo by my letters know our drift, / And hither shall he come" (4.1.114-115). The vial and the letter leave the cell together, and the tragedy's fifth act is the story of the letter's journey, not the drug's.

Compare the twin's handling down the chain and a grim pattern of refinement appears. Masuccio garbles the news: report of the funeral travels faster than the friar's explanation, and Mariotto returns to Siena in the worst possible state of knowledge, certain of the burial and ignorant of the device, to open the tomb as a mourner and be taken as a criminal. Da Porto perfects the asymmetry by giving the husband a true report of the funeral and no report of the plan, so that the tomb scene's horror is epistemic: Romeo acts rationally on accurate information that happens to be fatally incomplete. Bandello and Boaistuau retain the arrangement, and Brooke versifies it without repair. The play adds the tradition's cruellest upgrade, a second messenger racing the first: Balthasar, who carries the false certificate's news with a servant's fidelity and a horse's speed, against Friar John, who carries the truth and is stopped by plague officers at a sealed door. The falsehood travels by devotion and the truth by post, and the post is quarantined. No folklorist needs to invent a moral for the pattern, because the pattern is the moral the chain spent a century refining: a secret shared by three is a hostage to every road in Italy.

The Greek prototype stands outside the coupling, and its exemption explains its happy ending. Anthia's drug requires no letter because it protects no appointment: nobody must be told, nobody must arrive by an hour, and the waking's only deadline is the robbers' convenience. The device becomes lethal exactly when the novella weds it to a rendezvous, and the rendezvous becomes lethal exactly when the play prices it in hours. There is a lesson here for the source-versus-motif quarrel, and it favours the layered verdict reached earlier. The drug is the motif, ancient and ownerless. The letter coupling is the chain's work, datable, traceable, an innovation of the Italian tellers who needed the device to fail interestingly. The deadline pricing is the playwright's. Three depths, three owners, one machine: the survey's whole method in a single gear train.

## Reading the Play Backwards From the Motif: What the Depth Changes

Restoring the folktale depth alters the play's genre before it alters any detail. The tragedy's second half runs on romance machinery, and the machinery's origin explains the half's peculiar emotional weather, its alternation of lament and logistics, funerals arranged like weddings and weddings collapsing into funerals. Greek romance is the literature of separated spouses preserved through ordeals for reunion, and the play imports the form's central device at the exact midpoint, Act 4, where a tragedy of feud becomes, in mechanism, a romance of separation. The import fails, and the failure is the meaning: Verona is a world in which romance machinery cannot run, because its roads are plagued, its families are armed, its princes have lost control of its streets, and its time has been compressed past the machinery's tolerances. Reading backwards from the motif, the play stops looking like a love story with an unfortunate ending and starts looking like a controlled experiment, an ancient engine of reunion installed in a city engineered to break it, the breakage measured act by act.

The depth also rehabilitates the plan's author in a way the moral tradition resisted. Brooke's preface had framed the story as a warning against "unhonest desire" and the counsellors who abet it, and centuries of commentary inherited the frame, treating the friar's vial as the tragedy's original sin. The survey's evidence reverses the presumption. A device with a fifteen-century service record, operated by physicians and friars across three religions' worth of tellers, standardised in dose, celebrated in reunion endings from Ephesus to the wonder tale, is not a desperate improvisation; it is the period's best practice for an otherwise unsolvable problem, and the friar who reaches for it reaches for the strongest instrument his culture stocks. The tragedy's blame, redistributed by the motif evidence, settles where the play's own closing audit settles it: on the feud that made the instrument necessary, the haste that cut its margin, and the chance that stopped its letter. The Prince pardons the friar because the Prince, like the survey, can see the machine was sound.

For the student of the play, the depth finally converts the potion scenes from plot mechanics into archive. Juliet's soliloquy is a heroine reading her own tradition's case law and drinking anyway. The Capulet lament over the counterfeit corpse is a household performing, in earnest, the grief that romance audiences had watched be performed over Anthia and Callirhoe, with the same double knowledge in the house. The vault scene is the custody failure the tradition had spent a millennium learning to prevent. None of these readings requires the playwright to have opened a folklore collection that did not yet exist. They require only that the device came to him with its history inside it, as devices do, and that a dramatist of his quality could feel the weight of the accumulated instances in the vial he inherited, and priced it, and cut its margin, and let it run.

## The Afterlife: How Later Ages Re-Managed the Waking

The motif did not stop travelling when the play fixed its most famous form, and its afterlife in the play's own descendants offers the survey a control experiment: given the same machine, what did later managers change? The answer, across three centuries, is consistent enough to count as a finding. They changed the waking. David Garrick, whose adaptation held the London stage from 1748, could not bear the play's refusal of the reunion and rewrote the vault so that Juliet wakes while Romeo still lives, granting the lovers the spoken farewell the play withholds. The alteration is usually filed as eighteenth-century sentimentality, but the folktale survey recognises it as something older asserting itself: Garrick restored Da Porto's management, the tradition's own preferred setting, in which the device's promise is half-kept and the lovers meet across the waking. The audience that wept at the farewell was not betraying the story. It was remembering, through whatever channels such memory travels, that the machine was built for reunion and demanding the machine be allowed to work.

Opera repeated the restoration with the full authority of music. In Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi, staged at Venice in 1830, Giulietta wakes in the tomb while Roméo still breathes, and the two sing together before the poison finishes its work. In Gounod's Roméo et Juliette, staged at Paris in 1867, the waking is again timed to the husband's last strength, and the opera closes on a duet of recognition and pardon rather than on the play's sequence of a soliloquy over a corpse and a suicide in the dark. Two national operatic traditions, working independently, made the identical repair, and musicologists who treat the repair as operatic convention miss its depth in the tale tradition: the duet is the Greek ending surfacing through the novella chain's most famous descendant. Anthia's robbers, Da Porto's speaking tomb, Garrick's farewell, and Gounod's duet are one instinct at four dates, the tradition's refusal to let the device end in silence.

The device also emigrated sideways, out of the lovers' story entirely, into the literature of terror and detection that the nineteenth century built on the premature-burial fear. Edgar Allan Poe returned to the counterfeit corpse obsessively: Madeline Usher, entombed in a cataleptic trance that her brother's fear misreads as death, climbs out of the family vault in "The Fall of the House of Usher" (1839) to collect the debt the play's Juliet only feared. Poe's Madeline is the waking with the romance stripped away, no rescuer, no reunion, only the sleeper's return as judgment, and she demonstrates the motif's range from the far side: the same machinery that promised Anthia a husband delivers Roderick Usher a reckoning. The century's spiritualism, its safety coffins, its tales of trance and catalepsy in the periodicals, all draw on the stock the survey has been mapping, and the stock's centre holds. Whether a telling wants reunion, satire, terror, or a feud's settlement, the equipment required is the same equipment Euxenus supplied: a death the certificate believes, a tomb that traffic can reach, and a waking somebody must pay for.

## The Certificate of Death: How the Tales Stage the Proof

A counterfeit death succeeds only if the living certify it, and each telling in the survey stages the certification with a care that rewards attention, because the certifiers are the device's true audience and their competence sets the story's terms. The Greek romances certify by acclamation: the mourning of a city, the funeral games of grief, the tomb furnished as for a princess, a whole society signing the certificate by behaving as its members behave at a death. No physician examines Anthia; none is needed, because the romance's world certifies socially, and the physician who supplied the drug stands inside the consensus he engineered. The wonder tale certifies by failure, the tradition's most delicate stroke: the dwarfs, finding the girl lifeless for the third and final time, cannot bring themselves to bury her, because the body refuses the certificate's final clause, corruption. She lies in the glass coffin undecayed, and the tale states the anomaly outright, that she looked as if she were living. Certification by earth is withheld, and the coffin becomes a pending file, which is why a passing prince can close it.

The play certifies by checklist, and the checklist is the friar's own specification read back by events. He had promised, in Act 4, Scene 1, the cancellation of pulse, warmth, breath, colour, and motion, and Act 4, Scene 5 discovers the body sign by sign through the household's escalating grief: the Nurse's cries, the mother's, Capulet's inventory of his daughter's state, "Hath Death lain with thy wife. There she lies, / Flower as she was, deflowered by him" (4.5.36-37), and the terrible household pivot to burial, "All things that we ordained festival / Turn from their office to black funeral" (4.5.84-85). The scene is the certificate being signed in public, and the play takes care that no qualified examiner dissents: no physician is summoned, no second friar consulted, because the feud's haste and the wedding's wreckage make speed a family virtue. Period medicine had little to add in any case. The tests for death available to a Veronese household, breath on a mirror, pulse at the wrist, warmth at the breast, are precisely the tests the drug was specified to defeat, and the specification's author knew the tests because his profession administered them. The motif's engineering, examined at the point of certification, turns out to be a forgery problem: the drug does not imitate death in the abstract; it imitates death as a particular culture checks for it, sign by sign, and the culture's checklist is the forger's template.

The chain's tellers mostly compress certification into the funeral itself, the public procession standing as proof, and the compression is one more respect in which the play expands what it inherits. By staging the discovery at length, in the bride's chamber, at dawn, among the wedding's musicians and provisions, the play makes certification a scene of dramatic irony at maximum pressure: the audience alone holds the counter-certificate, the friar's promise, and watches the genuine grief of certifiers who have been given no possible way to know better. The folktale's deepest pleasure, knowing more than the mourners, is here made indistinguishable from pain, because the mourners include a father whose tyranny commissioned the device and whose grief is nonetheless real. The certificate is false. The signatures are sincere. That divergence, staged across a dawn chamber in Act 4, is the whole tragedy in one document.
## The Verdict: Depth Without a Single Source

The folktale root of the death-feigning potion is therefore real, and it is not a source. The root system is the motif: a device for counterfeiting death and scheduling a resurrection, attested in Greek romance with three separate technologies, standardised by Italian novella at three days, run in comedy by Boccaccio and in earnest by Masuccio, absorbed into the lovers' story before 1480, and transmitted to the London stage through a chain of named books whose verbal descent no serious scholarship disputes. The play's forty-two hours are the deepest point the root reaches and the point where the root stops explaining: what happens in the vault belongs to the dramatist who cut the margin, wrote the soliloquy, quarantined the letter, and let the sleeper wake minutes late into a room already full of death. Restoring the folktale beneath the potion does not diminish the play's originality. It locates the originality precisely, in the recalibration of an ancient rescue machine until rescue became impossible, and it restores to the friar's vial the weight the cliché of the clever plot device had planed away: fifteen centuries of heroines, sleeping on schedule, in tombs that were supposed to be way stations and became, in one Veronese vault, the destination.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch motif map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch motif map is the survey table in this article that sets the principal tellings of the death-feigning device side by side on five axes: who supplies it, its form and duration, who is deceived, how the waking is managed, and what the waking costs. The pattern it exposes is a split between a stable machine and variable management. From the Ephesiaca of Xenophon of Ephesus through Boccaccio, Masuccio, Da Porto, Bandello, and Brooke to the play, the machine itself barely changes: a certified false death, a tomb, a scheduled waking. What changes, and what decides each ending, is everything around the machine: the margin in the schedule, the reliability of the message that must accompany it, and the custody of the body during the interval. The map therefore uses two kinds of marker. Solid lines record the documented book chain, provable by verbal parallels. The open field records the motif's wider recurrence, where no document connects the tellings and only the device's persistence can be claimed.

### Q: How do folklorists catalogue a motif, and what does a catalogue entry actually record?

Folklorists catalogue a motif by abstracting the smallest element of a tale that persists through retelling and filing it under a standard number in a reference index. Stith Thompson's Motif-Index of Folk-Literature, issued in six volumes between 1932 and 1936 and revised between 1955 and 1958, is the governing catalogue: its lettered classes sort the world's narrative stock, with the K class holding deceptions, where escape by shamming death stands as K522, and the D class holding magic sleeps and the objects that induce them. An entry records the motif's definition, the tales and traditions in which collectors or texts attest it, and cross-references to neighbouring motifs. The tale-type index begun by Antti Aarne, revised by Thompson and then by Hans-Jörg Uther in 2004, performs the same office for whole plots, filing the glass-coffin heroine as ATU 709. A catalogue entry therefore proves distribution, not descent: it shows where a device occurs, across which languages and centuries, and it deliberately leaves open whether the occurrences descend from one another or arose independently.

### Q: Why can no single author be credited with inventing the feigned-death potion?

No single author can be credited because the device is attested, fully engineered, in texts that cannot have influenced one another, across a span of well over a thousand years. The Ephesiaca of Xenophon of Ephesus, a Greek romance of roughly the second century, already contains the physician supplier, the deathlike sleep, the tomb, and the waking. That romance survived in one manuscript and was not printed until 1726, so the Italian tellers who used the device in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Boccaccio in comedy and Masuccio in earnest, cannot have taken it from that book. The wonder tale's poisoned apple and glass coffin extend the distribution further, into oral tradition recorded only in 1812. Where a device appears in sealed compartments of the record, performing identical work in an identical sequence, the economical explanation is a common stock of storytelling on which many tellers drew, reinforced by the likelihood that the underlying problem, removing a heroine from a forced marriage without killing her, engineers the same solution wherever it is posed. Invention requires a first instance that others copied. The record shows a field, not a first instance.

### Q: Which surviving romance gives the device its earliest full working, and what does that priority settle?

The Ephesiaca of Xenophon of Ephesus gives the device its earliest surviving full working: Anthia, facing marriage to Perilaus at Tarsus, obtains a drug from the physician Euxenus, falls into a deathlike sleep, is entombed, wakes in the tomb, and is carried off by robbers who sell her in Alexandria. Chariton's Callirhoe is older still and contains the apparent death and the tomb waking, but no drug; a blow stops the heroine's breath, so the Ephesiaca holds priority for the pharmaceutical form of the device. The priority settles exactly one thing, and it is worth stating narrowly: by roughly the second century, the complete machine, supplier, dose, funeral, tomb, waking, already existed in European fiction, fourteen centuries before the play. It settles nothing about transmission. Because the romance went unprinted until 1726, its priority cannot make it the play's source or the novella chain's ancestor in any documentary sense. It is the survey's benchmark, proof of the device's antiquity and of its independence from any single lineage.

### Q: How does the Greek romance drug differ from the Friar's potion in who controls the waking?

The Greek romance drug leaves the waking uncontrolled, and the romance can afford the looseness; the Friar's potion assigns the waking to a named rescuer on a stated schedule, and the play cannot afford the assignment's failure. In the Ephesiaca, nobody is appointed to attend Anthia's waking. She wakes alone in a tomb stocked with grave goods, and the robbers who open the tomb for plunder become her rescuers by accident and self-interest, carrying her to Alexandria and the plot's next movement. The device works because the genre guarantees traffic past the tomb and a market for the living body found inside it. Friar Laurence's protocol in Act 4, Scene 1 is the opposite construction: the waking is to be witnessed and managed by Romeo, summoned by letter, with the friar himself in attendance, inside a window of two and forty hours. Every element of the management is a person, a road, or a door, and the tragedy consists of the persons, roads, and doors failing in sequence. The romance trusts the world to pass the tomb. The play appoints a rendezvous, and the world declines to keep it.

### Q: Why does the waking rescue the heroine in the Greek romance but destroy the lovers in the play?

The waking rescues in the Greek romance because the romance's world is organised to preserve the heroine, and it destroys in the play because Verona is organised to prevent the information the waking depends on from travelling. Anthia wakes into captivity, but captivity in the Ephesiaca is a preservative: each captor values her alive, her fidelity plot guarantees her survival, and the romance's contract with its readers promises reunion with Habrocomes, which the closing book duly delivers. Juliet wakes into a custody failure. The letter that would have told Romeo of the device was stopped by a plague cordon; the servant Balthasar's false report travelled unopposed; the friar reaches the vault with his crow of iron minutes after the poison has done its work. The difference is not in the drug, which performs to specification in both texts, nor in the sleepers, both of whom consent and endure. It lies in the communications and custody surrounding the waking: guaranteed traffic and a guaranteed genre on one side, a quarantined letter and a feud's armed city on the other.

### Q: How long does the feigned sleep last across the tradition, and what does each length make possible?

The feigned sleep lasts an unmeasured interval in Greek romance, three days through the Italian novella chain and its French and English descendants, and two and forty hours in the play, and each length licenses a different plot. The romance's unmeasured sleep is governed by narrative convenience: Anthia sleeps through her funeral and into the robbers' visit, because the romance's separation engine runs on distance rather than clocks. The three days standardised from Masuccio through Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Painter is a working span, long enough for a funeral, a burial, and a letter's journey to an exiled husband with margin for the road. The play's forty-two hours, fitted backward from a Thursday wedding, strips the margin out: the wedding is advanced a day, the letter is quarantined overnight, and every delay lands inside a schedule with no slack anywhere. Duration is therefore the tradition's most revealing variable. Retellers who kept three days kept a rescue device. The dramatist who cut the dose to forty-two hours converted the same device into a fuse.

### Q: Who supplies the drug in the different tellings, and why is the supplier so rarely a magician?

The drug is supplied by a physician in the Ephesiaca, by an abbot in Boccaccio's comic tale of Ferondo, by friars in Masuccio, Da Porto, Bandello, and the play, and by a disguised queen in the wonder tale; a magician supplies it almost nowhere, because the device requires certification rather than enchantment. A feigned death must convince a household, a city, and a funeral congregation, and conviction flows from the classes a culture trusts at the boundary of life and death: the doctor who compounds, the cleric who shrives and buries. A magician's drug would announce itself as a trick and invite the watchers to test it. A physician's draught or a friar's distilled liquor arrives with professional authority already attached, and the supplier's continuing presence, at the funeral and in the aftermath, vouches for the certificate he has issued. The play concentrates both authorities in one man, a friar who is also the drama's herbalist, introduced in Act 2, Scene 3 expounding the doctrine that poison and medicine share a rind. The tradition's insistence on credentialed suppliers is its realism: forgeries pass when the forger holds the right office.

### Q: In which tellings does the feigned death end in reunion, and what decides between reunion and catastrophe?

The feigned death ends in reunion in the Greek romances and in the wonder tale, and in catastrophe through the Italian novella chain and the play, and the deciding variable is the information system around the device rather than the device itself. Anthia's story closes with her marriage to Habrocomes restored at Ephesus, and the glass coffin's sleeper wakes directly into marriage, because in those worlds the waking needs no appointment: traffic, robbers, or a passing prince can be relied on to find the body, and no false report outruns the truth. Masuccio introduces the fatal coupling, a message that must reach an exiled husband before grief does, and from that tale onward the endings darken in step with the messages' failures: garbled news in Siena, a miscarried letter in Da Porto and his descendants, a quarantined letter and a faster false report in the play. Da Porto still allows his lovers speech together in the tomb, the reunion half-kept. The play withdraws even that mercy. Where the truth travels, the device rescues; where it is stopped, the same device, unaltered, kills.

### Q: Could the potion have reached the play through oral storytelling rather than through books?

The potion reached the play through books, on evidence as firm as source study can supply, and the oral hypothesis is neither needed nor available for the play itself. Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957) prints the chain, Brooke's poem of 1562 and Painter's prose of 1566, behind them Boaistuau, Bandello, and Da Porto, and the verbal and structural parallels between the play and Brooke in particular are dense enough to prove desk work rather than hearsay. The oral channel belongs to the device's deeper history, not to its arrival in London. Before Masuccio married the drug to the lovers' story, the motif demonstrably circulated in forms no library captures, since it appears in the sealed compartments of Greek romance, Italian novella, and the wonder tale with no documentary bridges between them. Folklorists infer oral transmission for that depth because the alternative, repeated independent invention of a complex sequence, strains belief across so many instances. The correct picture is layered: an orally mobile motif, absorbed into a written novella tradition in the fifteenth century, then transmitted to the playwright by the most ordinary of means, printed books on a London desk.

### Q: What separates a source from an analogue in the study of this play?

A source is a text the playwright demonstrably used, and an analogue is a parallel telling that illuminates the material without any claim of use; the separation rests on evidence of access plus correspondence, not on resemblance alone. Brooke's Romeus and Juliet is a source because the poem was printed in London in 1562, was available to a working dramatist, and matches the play in sequence, incident, and at points phrasing closely enough that coincidence cannot explain the fit. The Ephesiaca is an analogue, indeed the type of one in Geoffrey Bullough's first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, because its resemblance to the potion plot is striking while access is impossible to show: the romance survived in a single manuscript and reached print only in 1726. The distinction disciplines argument. Sources explain the play's wording and structure. Analogues explain the climate of story in which those wordings could be understood by an audience, the depth of the stock from which the sources themselves drew. Confusing the categories produces the two standing errors of the subject, claiming that the playwright read books he cannot have opened, and denying that his books stood in a tradition older than printing.

### Q: How does the glass coffin of the tale tradition relate to Juliet's feigned death?

The glass coffin is the feigning device's gentle twin: the same deathlike state and the same suspended heroine, with the custody problem solved by display instead of concealment. In the Grimms' tale of 1812, filed as ATU 709, the heroine lies apparently dead from a poisoned apple, and the dwarfs, finding that she does not decay, place her in a coffin of glass above ground, where she remains visible and reachable until a prince's servants stumble while carrying the coffin and the morsel leaves her throat. Every term answers a term in the Verona machine, inverted. Juliet's body is hidden in a vault whose keyholders oppose the plan; the tale's body is exhibited where any traveller may attend it. Juliet's waking must be scheduled against a dose and a letter; the tale's waking needs no schedule because custody never lapses. The comparison isolates what actually kills in the play. It is not the sleep, which the tale proves survivable indefinitely under good custody. It is the vault, the interval, and the failure of every appointed guardian to be present when the eyes open.

### Q: Is the enchanted sleep of the sleeping-beauty tales the same device as the feigning potion?

The enchanted sleep is a neighbouring device but not the same one, and the tale-type index keeps them apart for a precise reason: the sleeping beauty's sleep deceives nobody. In the tale filed as ATU 410, the heroine's century of sleep is genuine enchantment, openly known, guarded by a court that sleeps with her and a hedge that grows around the castle; there is no funeral, no certificate of death, no watcher to be fooled, and the waking arrives by the curse's own calendar when the prince passes the hedge. The feigning potion is defined by the elements the enchanted sleep lacks. Its sleep is pharmaceutical or fraudulent, its purpose is a false certificate of death issued to specific deceived parties, its setting is a tomb rather than a bedchamber, and its waking is an appointment that human arrangements must keep. The distinction matters for the play because Juliet's terror in Act 4, Scene 3 is precisely the terror of a deception that might be believed too well or discovered too late, a register of fear the sleeping beauty, whose state is public knowledge, never has to enter.

### Q: Why does the device attach itself so persistently to a forced or forbidden wedding?

The device attaches to forced or forbidden weddings because it is the only mechanism that defeats a wedding without crime, flight, or consent, in the marriage systems the tales describe. A daughter ordered to marry a man she refuses, or secretly married to a man her family proscribes, has almost no licit moves: she cannot travel alone, cannot earn a refuge, cannot appeal past her father's authority, and cannot kill the bridegroom her family has chosen. Feigned death answers each constraint. It removes her by her family's own hands, with their money spent on the funeral and their grief certifying the removal; it preserves her honour inside death's unimpeachable alibi; it requires one accomplice, the supplier, rather than an army; and it schedules her return for a moment when the marriage plan has collapsed of its own weight. Anthia faces Perilaus, Ganozza a second match in Siena, Juliet the County Paris, and the wonder tale's heroine a stepmother's rivalry that functions as the same disposal in another key. The persistence of the pairing across fifteen centuries is a social measurement: the device survives exactly as long as daughters are disposed of by contract, and the tales file the protest.

### Q: What does the waking cost the sleeper in the different tellings?

The waking costs the sleeper nothing in the wonder tale, captivity and years in Greek romance, her husband in the novella chain, and her life in the play, and the rising price tracks the tightening of the arrangements around the device. The girl in the glass coffin wakes into marriage, the cost wholly discharged, because her state was inflicted and her custody never lapsed. Anthia wakes into the hands of tomb robbers and a slave market in Alexandria, paying for her preserved marriage with a long servitude the romance needs most of its length to redeem. Ganozza wakes to survival and widowhood: Mariotto, who opened her tomb, is executed for the rescue, and she dies of the news. Juliet pays the ledger's highest entry. She wakes punctually, unharmed by the drug, into a vault where the plan's every support has failed, finds her husband dead by poison beside her, and completes the device's work on herself with his dagger. The pattern across the tellings is exact: the drug never bills the sleeper. The world around the waking does, and its rates rise as the schedules tighten.

### Q: What did the fear of premature burial build, and how does it grow from stories of this kind?

The fear of premature burial built an industry of safeguards: safety coffins fitted with bells, flags, and breathing tubes, patented in numbers across the nineteenth century; waiting mortuaries, notably in German cities, where the dead lay under observation with cords tied to their fingers until decay certified death beyond argument; and a literature of warning and sensation, crowned by Edgar Allan Poe's essay-like tale "The Premature Burial" of 1844, which catalogues revivals with documentary relish. The growth runs in both directions between the stories and the fear. Tales of the feigned death, from the Greek tomb wakings to Juliet's soliloquy with its dread of waking among the bones, gave the fear a plot and a heroine, teaching audiences exactly which signs might be misread and how a certified corpse might fare underground. The fear, fed by plague burial, cataleptic illness, and rudimentary death certification, gave the tales their charge, the reason a girl's fifty lines of hesitation over a vial could hold a theatre silent. Each generation's technology of reassurance, the bell, the mortuary, the stethoscope, is a footnote to the motif.

### Q: Why do folklorists resist drawing a single family tree for a motif found on several continents?

Folklorists resist the single family tree because the evidence for a motif's distribution regularly outruns any possible documentation of descent, and a tree drawn beyond the documents is fiction wearing the dress of method. A family tree asserts that each occurrence copied an ancestor, which requires routes, dates, and carriers; for the feigned death, the record instead offers sealed compartments, a Greek romance unprinted until 1726, Italian novelle of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and oral tales collected in the nineteenth, with gaps no carrier can be shown to have crossed. Two further explanations compete with descent and often fit better. Oral transmission moves devices along routes that leave no paper, so absence of documents cannot prove absence of contact; and polygenesis, independent invention, becomes plausible wherever a narrative problem is so constrained that few solutions exist, as with the daughter who must escape a wedding without dying. The catalogues therefore record distribution and let descent be argued case by case, which is why the honest map of this device shows a documented chain for the years after 1476 and an open field, honestly unconnected, for the fourteen centuries before it.

### Q: What would count as proof of direct borrowing, and does the potion's history supply it?

Proof of direct borrowing requires the conjunction of access and correspondence: evidence that the later teller could have encountered the earlier text, and matches in wording, sequence, or distinctive detail too specific for coincidence or for the common stock to explain. The potion's history supplies that proof at exactly one level and refuses it at the other. Within the novella chain the proof is complete. Da Porto's tale was printed and read by Bandello, whose version Boaistuau adapted, whose French Brooke and Painter Englished, and the parallels of incident and phrasing between Brooke's poem and the play establish the playwright's desk beyond reasonable dispute. Below the chain, the proof fails at access. The Ephesiaca, the device's great ancient instance, existed in a single manuscript until 1726, and no verbal thread ties Boccaccio's powder or Masuccio's to any Greek page. The correct statement of the whole evidence is therefore double: the play borrowed its potion from books, provably, and the books borrowed theirs from a tradition whose borrowings are unprovable in detail because they travelled by mouths and memories rather than by print.

### Q: Why does the device cross languages and religions with so little damage?

The device crosses languages and religions intact because its mechanism is social and pharmaceutical rather than doctrinal: nothing in it depends on a creed, a rite, or a local custom beyond the universals of fathers, weddings, funerals, and tombs. A drug that counterfeits death requires only a culture that compounds drugs, buries its dead with ceremony, and disposes of daughters in marriage, conditions met from pagan Ephesus through Catholic Italy and France to Protestant London, where the chain's own crossing of the Reformation's frontier, a friar's device surviving translation into a Protestant poem and play with its specification unaltered, is the survey's standing exhibit. Translation even improves the device's passport, since each reteller localises the supplier's office, physician, abbot, friar, while leaving the procedure untouched, and procedures travel better than offices. Compare the play's fate-laden machinery, which depends on a specific cosmos, or its wordplay, which dies at every border. The potion is freight, not poetry: standardised, robust, and indifferent to the flag of the ship that carries it, which is the practical definition of a folktale motif at full strength.

### Q: How should an essay argue the potion's folktale depth without inventing a folktale source?

An essay should argue the depth at the level of the motif and the transmission at the level of the books, keeping the two claims in separate sentences with separate evidence. The depth claim rests on distribution: the device is attested in the Ephesiaca, in Chariton's drugless variant, in Boccaccio, and in the wonder tale, across fourteen centuries and sealed compartments of the record, as catalogued in the motif indices of Stith Thompson and the tale-type index revised by Hans-Jörg Uther. State that claim with the catalogues cited and the dates given. The transmission claim rests on documents: the chain from Masuccio and Da Porto through Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Painter, as settled in Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, with the Ephesiaca named an analogue, never a source, since its printing in 1726 postdates the play. The essay's original contribution then falls where the evidence is strongest, on the transformation: the three-day standard cut to forty-two hours, the soliloquy that lets the sleeper audit the tradition's risks, the letter's failure priced against the schedule. Depth above, descent below, transformation at the surface: an argument built in those three storeys cannot be knocked down by either camp.
