The Allahabad judgment of 12 June 1975, delivered by Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court in Election Petition No. 5 of 1971, declared Indira Gandhi's election from Rae Bareli void, held her guilty of two corrupt practices under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, and disqualified her from electoral office for six years. Thirteen days later, on the night of 25 June 1975, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed signed the proclamation of Emergency. The standard telling of these events welds the two dates together as cause and effect: a court unseated a Prime Minister, and the Prime Minister answered by suspending the Constitution's working. The facts in that sequence are accurate, and the reading built on them is wrong. The judgment was the culmination of a constitutional collision course that had been building since 1971 between an executive expanding its amendment power and a judiciary constructing a doctrine to contain that power. It forced a choice Indira Gandhi had been postponing. It did not manufacture the choice, and it did not dictate the answer she gave.

## What the Allahabad High Court Decided on 12 June 1975

Justice Sinha's judgment, read in Courtroom No. 24 of the Allahabad High Court on the morning of 12 June 1975, rested on findings that were narrow in substance. The court held Indira Gandhi guilty of two corrupt practices, each falling under Section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, which forbids a candidate from obtaining the assistance of gazetted government officers for the furtherance of election prospects. The first finding concerned Yashpal Kapoor, a gazetted officer of the Government of India who held the post of Officer on Special Duty in the Prime Minister's Secretariat. Kapoor had campaigned for Indira Gandhi in Rae Bareli in January 1971 before the order accepting his resignation from government service was passed on 25 January 1971, and Justice Sinha held that he remained a government servant in law until that order, whatever retrospective effect the order claimed from 14 January 1971. The second finding concerned the officers of the Uttar Pradesh government in Rae Bareli district: the District Magistrate, the Superintendent of Police, the Executive Engineer of the Public Works Department and an engineer of the electricity department had been drawn into arranging her election meetings of 1 February and 25 February 1971, supplying rostrums, barricades, loudspeakers and the power to run them. State machinery, placed at a candidate's service by officers the state paid, was the assistance Section 123(7) existed to prohibit.

Everything else in Raj Narain's petition failed. Justice Sinha rejected five further grounds of challenge: that Indira Gandhi had procured the assistance of the armed forces for flights by Air Force aircraft and helicopters; that Kapoor and others had distributed clothes and liquor to induce voters; that she and her election agent had appealed to the religious symbol of the cow and calf; that her workers had procured vehicles for the free conveyance of voters to polling stations; and that she had incurred election expenditure beyond the ceiling fixed by Section 77(3) of the Act read with Rule 90 of the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961. The sensational charges, the charges that had filled newspaper columns for four years, were dismissed as not proved. What remained were two findings about process: a private secretary who crossed into campaign work days before his resignation took legal effect, and district officers who built platforms for a candidate's rallies. On those two findings, the court declared the election of the Prime Minister of India void, because Section 100 of the Act left no discretion once a corrupt practice by the candidate stood proved, and it disqualified her for six years from 12 June 1975 under Section 8A.

### How long did Justice Sinha disqualify Indira Gandhi for?

Justice Sinha disqualified Indira Gandhi for six years from 12 June 1975, the maximum period Section 8A of the Representation of the People Act permitted once corrupt practices stood proved. The disqualification barred her from contesting elections and from voting during those six years, and it operated on her personally rather than on the Congress party or its government.

One feature of the judgment is easy to miss and central to everything that followed. Justice Sinha granted an absolute stay of twenty days on the operation of his own order. The Prime Minister was unseated in law, yet the judgment itself gave her a defined window in which to appeal to the Supreme Court before the disqualification bit. The court that delivered the most consequential election judgment in the republic's history built the appeal route into the order, a procedural fact that matters when the judgment is later described as a bolt from a clear sky. Nothing about 12 June 1975 was sudden except the headlines. The petition had been filed in April 1971, within weeks of the election it challenged. Indira Gandhi had entered the witness box in the Allahabad High Court in March 1975 and submitted to cross-examination as the respondent in her own election case, an appearance no sitting Prime Minister had made before. The judgment that closed the case was the terminus of a four-year proceeding conducted in open court, reported at every stage, under a statute enacted in 1951 and applied to candidates at every level of the system.

## Election Petition No. 5 of 1971: The Case Raj Narain Built

Raj Narain, the Samyukta Socialist Party candidate who had lost Rae Bareli to Indira Gandhi in the general election of March 1971, filed Election Petition No. 5 of 1971 in the Allahabad High Court in April 1971. The scale of his defeat frames the petition's audacity. Indira Gandhi had polled 183,309 votes in Rae Bareli against Raj Narain's 71,499, a margin of 111,810 votes recorded in the Supreme Court's own narration of the case in the stay proceedings of June 1975. Election petitions were the ordinary remedy the Representation of the People Act supplied to a defeated candidate, and the Act assigned them to the High Court of the state. Raj Narain's petition alleged a battery of corrupt practices, and his counsel, Shanti Bhushan, constructed the case as a direct assault on the proposition that a Prime Minister's election stood beyond the reach of the standards applied to every other candidate in the country. The petition's drafting made the constitutional stakes plain from the first year: if the charges were proved, Section 100 obliged the court to void the election, and the statute drew no distinction between the election of a Prime Minister and the election of the humblest member of the Lok Sabha.

The trial ground on for four years, and its chronology refutes any picture of a judicial ambush. Raj Narain first had to survive preliminary skirmishing: the Supreme Court ruled on 15 March 1972, in Raj Narain v Indira Nehru Gandhi, on the framing of the issues, and it flagged even then the question that would decide the case three years later, whether a government servant's resignation could be accepted with retrospective effect so as to defeat Section 123(7). Judges were assigned and reassigned; the evidence ran to thousands of pages; and the proceeding kept intersecting with the constitutional litigation of the period, because the parties and the courts were arguing, in parallel, about the same distribution of power. In October 1974 a Supreme Court bench held in Kanwar Lal Gupta v Amar Nath Chawla that expenditure by a political party or supporters with the candidate's consent counted toward the candidate's ceiling. Parliament answered within weeks: the Representation of the People (Amendment) Act of December 1974 rewrote the explanation to Section 77 retrospectively, and Raj Narain challenged that amendment inside the election trial itself. Justice Sinha rejected the challenge to the 1974 Act, a ruling against the petitioner in the middle of his own case, delivered by the judge later accused in opposition mythology of being the executive's victim and in government mythology of being its assassin. The record shows a judge deciding points both ways under intense scrutiny, which is what a trial is.

### Who argued Raj Narain's case before the Allahabad High Court?

Shanti Bhushan, the Allahabad advocate who had been Law Minister in waiting of the opposition's imagination long before he held the office in 1977, argued Raj Narain's case through the four years of the Allahabad trial. His cross-examination of Indira Gandhi in March 1975, and his construction of the Yashpal Kapoor resignation dates, formed the core of the petitioner's case.

The trial's most charged episode came in its final months. Raj Narain sought production of the Blue Book, the official manual of rules for the Prime Minister's security on tour, to test the boundary between the governmental machinery that surrounded a touring Prime Minister and the campaign machinery the Act forbade. The Allahabad High Court's handling of the claim of privilege became a case within the case, and on 24 January 1975 the Supreme Court quashed the High Court's direction for production, settling the privilege question in the government's favour. On 18 March 1975 Indira Gandhi entered the witness box at Allahabad and was cross-examined, the first serving Prime Minister to give evidence in an election trial. When judgment came on 12 June 1975, it came at the end of this fully litigated road: a petitioner who had lost by more than one hundred thousand votes, a respondent who had testified, a statute that admitted no exceptions, and a judge who had ruled against each side on substantial points along the way. The trigger reading of the judgment requires this entire record to be forgotten. The culmination reading begins with it.

## The Statute That Made It Possible: Election Law After 1966

The proceeding that unseated a Prime Minister was possible because of a design decision taken nine years before the petition was filed. Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, as originally worked, election petitions were tried by ad hoc election tribunals appointed by the Election Commission, a system that produced delays, uneven quality and recurring complaints that tribunal members were susceptible to influence. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Act of 1966 transferred the trial of election petitions to the High Courts, where a single judge would hear the petition under Section 86 and decide it under the substantive code of Sections 100 and 101. The reform's stated object was expedition and authority: petitions would be tried by constitutional courts, on the record, with appeal to the Supreme Court under Section 116A. Its unstated consequence was to lodge the most politically explosive jurisdiction in the republic inside the institution the executive could least control. A tribunal could be reconstituted; a High Court judge, holding office under the Constitution's tenure protections, could not be removed for an unwelcome verdict. When Raj Narain filed his petition in April 1971, the case went by the 1966 design to the Allahabad High Court, to be tried by whichever judge the Chief Justice assigned, under a code that had been Parliament's own for twenty years.

The code's internal logic shaped everything that followed. Section 100 listed the grounds on which an election had to be declared void, and the corrupt practice ground was framed in mandatory terms: if the court was of the opinion that a corrupt practice, as defined in Section 123, had been committed by the returned candidate or by another person with the candidate's consent, the election fell. There was no de minimis exception, no weighing of the margin, no discretion to admonish and continue. Section 123(7), the provision under which both proved charges fell, defined as a corrupt practice the obtaining or procuring of assistance, for the furtherance of the candidate's election prospects, from members of specified classes of government servants, gazetted officers among them. The section's rationale was the integrity of the administrative machine: the state's officers were to serve the election as its referees and infrastructure, never as one side's resource. Part VII of the Act then attached the personal consequence: Section 8A disqualified a person found guilty of a corrupt practice for up to six years, as the court determined. The structure was legislative moral philosophy in statutory form, descending from the founding settlement: the republic would police the boundary between the state and the campaign with the heaviest sanction it knew, exclusion from the democratic process itself, and it would police the boundary without regard to the offender's altitude. The 1966 transfer and the 1951 code together composed the machine that received Raj Narain's petition. When the machine produced its verdict on 12 June 1975, the executive's quarrel was, at bottom, with the machine's design, which is why the executive's answer in August 1975 was to redesign the machine retrospectively and to attempt, by Article 329A, to exempt its own elections from the machine altogether.

## The Amendment Cycle of 1971: Parliament Rewrites the Rules

The collision course that ended at Allahabad began with the political settlement of 1971. The general election of March 1971, fought by Indira Gandhi on the Garibi Hatao programme after the Congress split of 1969, returned her party with a two-thirds majority in the Lok Sabha, and the Bangladesh war of December 1971 converted that mandate into something close to national acclamation: [the 1971 election and the Bangladesh war as one unified sequence](/2019/04/12/1971-election-bangladesh-war-india/) supplied the political capital, and the government spent it on constitutional reconstruction. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment, which received the President's assent on 5 November 1971, amended Articles 13 and 368 to declare that Parliament could amend any provision of the Constitution, including the Fundamental Rights, and that a constitutional amendment was not a law within the meaning of Article 13. Its target was the Supreme Court's 1967 judgment in Golak Nath, which had frozen the Fundamental Rights against amendment. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment followed, assented to on 20 April 1972, substituting the word "amount" for "compensation" in Article 31 and inserting Article 31C, which shielded laws giving effect to the directive principles in Articles 39(b) and 39(c) from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment, assented to on 28 December 1971, abolished the privy purses and privileges of the former rulers, deleting Articles 291 and 362 and inserting Article 363A: [the post-1971 amendment cycle](/2019/04/06/privy-purse-abolition-1971-india/) at its most concentrated, three amendments in a single year reaching property, princely privilege and the amendment power itself.

Each amendment was defensible on the policy ground its movers stated, and the pattern they formed was larger than any one of them. The executive had discovered that a disciplined two-thirds majority could rewrite the constitutional text faster than litigation could test the rewriting, and that every judicial doctrine limiting political power could be answered by amending the limit away. The pattern had a founding instance twenty years earlier: [the executive-amendment pattern's founding instance](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/), the First Amendment of June 1951, had answered the Patna High Court and Supreme Court rulings on speech and zamindari abolition by rewriting Articles 19 and 31 and building the Ninth Schedule. What changed in 1971 was tempo and scope. The First Amendment had been a repair job on a new Constitution; the 1971 cycle was a governing method, and it was directed, amendment by amendment, at judgments of the Supreme Court: Golak Nath in 1967, the bank nationalisation judgment of February 1970 (R.C. Cooper v Union of India), and the privy purse judgment of December 1970 (Madhavrao Scindia v Union of India), each of which had struck down a signature measure of Indira Gandhi's government in the space of eleven months. By the end of 1971 the executive's position, enacted three times into the text, was that no judicial decision on property or amendment power was final. The judiciary's answer, when it came in April 1973, was framed for exactly this claim.

## Kesavananda Bharati and the Judiciary's Answer

Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala was decided on 24 April 1973 by a bench of thirteen judges, the largest ever assembled in the Supreme Court, by a majority of seven to six. The bench upheld Parliament's power to amend any part of the Constitution and overruled Golak Nath, conceding the executive the substance of the Twenty-Fourth Amendment. It then attached the condition that has governed Indian constitutional law since: Parliament could not alter the basic structure of the Constitution. Chief Justice S.M. Sikri's enumeration of basic features, and the concurrences of Justices Shelat, Hegde, Grover, Khanna, Reddy and Mukherjea in differing combinations, placed the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, the secular character of the polity, the separation of powers and the federal character beyond the reach of Article 368. The doctrine was, in form, an answer to a question about land reform litigation in Kerala. In substance it was the judiciary's institutional response to the amendment cycle: if every adverse judgment could be amended away, adjudication itself was provisional, and the basic-structure doctrine made one category of judicial decision unamendable. The collision course dates from the juxtaposition of the two propositions enacted within eighteen months of each other: Parliament's claim, in the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, that its amending power met no internal limit; and the court's claim, in Kesavananda Bharati, that the limit existed and that the court would police it. The relationship of that doctrine to [the Kesavananda basic-structure doctrine](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/) in the longer argument over Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is the doctrinal background; the political foreground was the government's reaction to losing control of the doctrine's personnel.

### Which three judges were superseded in April 1973?

Justices J.M. Shelat, K.S. Hegde and A.N. Grover, the three senior-most judges after Chief Justice Sikri, were passed over on 26 April 1973 when Justice A.N. Ray was appointed Chief Justice, two days after the Kesavananda Bharati judgment. All three had decided against the government on basic structure; all three resigned in protest.

The supersession was the second step on the collision course, and in some ways the more revealing one. The Kesavananda Bharati doctrine limited what Parliament could do with the text; the appointment of Justice Ray tested whether the court that guarded the limit would be staffed by judges the executive selected for their amenability. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), treated the supersession as the moment the executive's conflict with the judiciary became open, and the documentary record he assembled of the Law Ministry's soundings about judges' views on the basic structure, conducted before the appointment, supports the reading that commitment to the government's constitutional philosophy had become a criterion for elevation. The three superseded judges resigned, breaking a convention that had held since 1950. The executive had amended the text in 1971; the court had limited the amendment power in 1973; the executive had answered through appointments within forty-eight hours. Every move was legal in form. The cumulative effect was a struggle over which institution would have the last word, conducted through instruments the Constitution itself supplied. That is the definition of the collision course the Allahabad judgment later brought to its forcing point, and it explains why a dispute about a private secretary's resignation date and some rally rostrums could carry constitutional voltage. The courtroom at Allahabad was the one arena in which the struggle arrived as a binary question with a date attached, and the date was 12 June 1975.

## Golak Nath to Kesavananda: The Decade That Set the Course

The collision course had a prehistory, and the prehistory explains why neither side in 1973 could retreat. The Supreme Court had twice upheld Parliament's amending power without internal limit, in Shankari Prasad v Union of India in 1951 and in Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan in 1965. The turn came in Golak Nath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967 by a bench of eleven judges, six to five, under Chief Justice K. Subba Rao: the majority held that Parliament could not amend the Fundamental Rights, treating a constitutional amendment as law within Article 13 and the amending power as ordinary legislative power. Golak Nath froze the settlement of 1951 and 1965 in place and invented prospective overruling for India to soften the freeze. The political meaning was plain to the governments that followed. The land reform and reservation measures that legislatures regarded as the substance of the social revolution promised in the Directive Principles stood exposed to a judicial veto the political branches had never accepted, and the veto's holder was a bench no election could change. The amendment cycle of 1971 was the political system's considered reply to Golak Nath, drafted after two further judgments had shown the doctrine's reach.

Those two judgments, both delivered against Indira Gandhi's government in 1970, fixed the executive's reading of the court as an adversary to be overcome rather than an arbiter to be persuaded. In R.C. Cooper v Union of India, on 10 February 1970, a bench of eleven struck down the nationalisation of fourteen banks by a majority of ten to one, holding that the ordinance and Act violated the compensation guarantee and the freedom of trade; the lone dissenter was Justice A.N. Ray. In H.H. Maharajadhiraja Madhavrao Scindia v Union of India, on 15 December 1970, the court struck down the President's order derecognising the rulers and terminating the privy purses, holding the order an abuse of the constitutional power under Article 366(22). Each judgment was, in isolation, an orthodox exercise of judicial review on texts the government had itself governed under for two decades. In sequence, and within ten months, they persuaded the executive that the court's doctrines were a political programme by other means, and the Twenty-Fourth, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Sixth Amendments were the counter-programme. The sequence matters for the culmination reading because it establishes the pattern of escalation before Kesavananda Bharati was argued: doctrine answered by amendment, amendment answered by wider doctrine. When Granville Austin reconstructed the period in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), he placed the Kesavananda Bharati litigation at the centre of a struggle the executive had been conducting since 1970 by every instrument the Constitution afforded, litigation, amendment and appointment in turn. The Allahabad proceeding was not the start of that struggle. It was the first occasion on which the struggle's outcome attached to the Prime Minister's own seat, and therefore the first occasion on which accommodation, the strategy both sides had used since 1967, ceased to be available.

## The Counter-Doctrine: A Committed Judiciary

The executive did not leave its side of the collision to inference. In the first week of May 1973, defending the supersession in the Lok Sabha, Mohan Kumaramangalam, the Minister of Steel and Mines and the government's most articulate theorist, stated the doctrine on the record: the government, in appointing judges, was entitled and obliged to consider their philosophy, their attitude to the Constitution's social purposes and their conception of the judicial role, and a judiciary that stood outside the nation's transformative project could not claim the appointment system as its property. The Law Minister, H.R. Gokhale, defended the specific appointment on the ground that Justice Ray's record, including his lone dissent in the bank nationalisation case, showed a judge who understood the Constitution's social ends. Opposition members and the bar answered that commitment, demanded as a condition of elevation, dissolved the distinction between a court and a committee, and the controversy fixed the vocabulary in which the struggle was thereafter argued: commitment against independence. The vocabulary matters to the culmination reading because it shows the executive articulating, in Parliament, in May 1973, the theory under which the judiciary was to be brought within the governing project, two years before the Allahabad judgment tested whether the theory governed a courtroom. A government that had announced the doctrine of commitment, superseded three judges in its name, and amended the Constitution three times to reverse judgments had already decided its constitutional question before Raj Narain's petition matured: institutions were instruments, and instruments that resisted were to be refitted. The Emergency's treatment of the courts that resisted after June 1975, the transfer of inconvenient High Court judges in 1976, the supersession of Justice Khanna in January 1977, and the Forty-Second Amendment's attempt to strip the courts of review over amendments, were the doctrine of May 1973 carried to its administrative conclusion. Justice Sinha cannot have been ignorant of the doctrine when he took up the election petition's final arguments, nor of the price judges had lately paid for decisions the government disliked. The judgment he delivered on 12 June 1975 was therefore not merely a ruling on rostrums and resignations. Delivered by a judge working under the announced rule that commitment was the price of a career, it was a refusal, by the institution's own member, to be committed. That refusal, multiplied across the benches that followed, is the human mechanism inside the collision course, and it is why the course runs through courtrooms rather than around them.

## The Collision Course from 1973 to 1975

Between April 1973 and June 1975 the confrontation between the executive and the judiciary spread from constitutional doctrine into the ordinary administration of law, and the election trial at Allahabad ran through the middle of it. The pattern visible in retrospect is of two institutions testing each other at every available point of contact. The judiciary's side of the pattern was not a campaign; Indian courts do not coordinate. It consisted of the accumulated effect of independent rulings that constrained executive action in case after case: the Supreme Court's October 1974 ruling in Kanwar Lal Gupta on election expenditure, which unsettled the accounts of the entire 1971 Parliament and required a retrospective statutory repair; the same court's January 1975 ruling on the Blue Book privilege, which, though it favoured the government, asserted the court's authority to police the boundary between state secrecy and election evidence; and a run of High Court decisions in 1974 and 1975 on preventive detention, press regulation and administrative discretion that the government experienced as a judiciary unwilling to defer. The executive's side of the pattern had its own coherence. The retrospective amendment of the election law in December 1974, passed to neutralise the Kanwar Lal Gupta ruling, repeated the 1971 method inside a pending trial: when a judicial interpretation threatened political interests, the text was rewritten with retrospective effect, and the rewritten text was then pleaded before the very court trying the case.

The political ground was shifting under both institutions at the same time. The Bihar movement led by Jayaprakash Narayan from March 1974, the railway strike of May 1974 led by George Fernandes and suppressed by mass arrests, and the Navnirman agitation that had forced the dissolution of the Gujarat assembly in February 1974 had carried opposition politics out of Parliament and into sustained street mobilisation. Bipan Chandra, Mridula Mukherjee and Aditya Mukherjee, in In the Name of Democracy: JP Movement and the Emergency (2003), documented how the JP movement framed its demand in constitutional language: Indira Gandhi, they recorded the movement arguing, had forfeited the moral title to govern, and the Allahabad judgment was awaited as the legal instrument of that forfeiture. The judgment's timing sharpened everything. On the same 12 June 1975, the Gujarat assembly election results gave the Janata Morcha a majority against Congress, demonstrating at the ballot box that the Prime Minister's post-1971 dominance was spent. A court judgment voiding her election, a state verdict rejecting her party, and a movement demanding her resignation converged within a single news cycle. The convergence was coincidence in its dates and structure in its substance: each development expressed the exhaustion of the 1971 settlement, and together they closed the space in which the judgment could have been treated as one more litigation setback to be appealed in the ordinary way.

## The Opposition's Constitutional Wager: Politics Waits for a Judgment

The collision between executive and judiciary unfolded in front of a political audience that had its own stake in the outcome, and the audience's conduct is part of the evidence for what the judgment was. The opposition of 1974 and 1975, fragmented among the Jana Sangh, the Congress (O), the Socialists, the Bharatiya Lok Dal and the Communists outside the government's orbit, had failed to convert street strength into parliamentary consequence: the Congress majority elected in 1971 sat immovable in the Lok Sabha whatever happened in Gujarat or Bihar. The JP movement, launched from Patna in March 1974 after the Bihar students' agitation and the assembly gherao of 18 March 1974, supplied the street strength in the form of Sampoorna Kranti, total revolution, a programme that was deliberately constitutional in vocabulary and extra-parliamentary in method. Jayaprakash Narayan's argument, repeated through 1974 and into 1975 and documented by Chandra, Mukherjee and Mukherjee in In the Name of Democracy (2003), was that the government had disabled every ordinary instrument of accountability: Parliament was a rubber stamp, the ruling party was a court, the bureaucracy was committed, and the press was being squeezed by advertisement and newsprint controls. What remained, in the movement's analysis, was the judiciary, and specifically the judgment awaited at Allahabad, which the opposition press discussed through the spring of 1975 as the republic's last functioning check.

The wager the opposition placed on the judgment shaped the crisis that followed it. Raj Narain was not merely a defeated candidate; he was a Socialist organiser of Jayaprakash Narayan's circle, and his petition had been followed by the movement for four years as a proxy war conducted by legal means. When the Allahabad court was known to be reserving and then delivering judgment, the opposition's plan for the verdict was already made: moral title, resignation, dissolution. The Gujarat result of 12 June 1975, where the Janata Morcha formed by the opposition parties took the state from Congress, was folded into the same narrative within hours. The opposition therefore treated the judgment from its first hour not as an interlocutory step in election litigation, subject to appeal in the ordinary course, but as a verdict of the nation delivered through its last credible institution. Indira Gandhi's government reciprocated in kind, treating the demand for resignation as a constitutional coup in moral dress and the rally of 25 June 1975, with its appeal to the police and the army, as the proof. Each side, in other words, refused the ordinary appellate frame, and their joint refusal is what transformed thirteen days into a systemic crisis. The culmination reading does not require the judgment to have been political. It requires only that a judgment resolving the decade's institutional argument, delivered into a polity whose organised forces had pre-committed themselves to treating it as final, could not be processed as routine. The forcing quality of 12 June 1975 was supplied by this pre-commitment as much as by the text Justice Sinha read.

## Justice Sinha's Reasoning: Two Findings and Five Rejections

The judgment Justice Sinha delivered was the work of a trial judge reasoning from documents, and its two affirmative findings each turned on a dated piece of paper. The Kapoor finding turned on the chronology of a resignation. Yashpal Kapoor had served Indira Gandhi in the Prime Minister's Secretariat since 1966, holding the gazetted post of Officer on Special Duty. He tendered his resignation on 13 January 1971, in the weeks before the general election, and took up work on Indira Gandhi's Rae Bareli campaign: touring the constituency, addressing workers and organising for the candidate he had served in office. The President accepted his resignation by an order passed on 25 January 1971, expressed to take effect from 14 January 1971, and notified in the Gazette on 6 February 1971. The defence argued that the retrospective effect cured everything: Kapoor was not a government servant on any date after 14 January. Justice Sinha rejected the argument on the ground the Supreme Court itself had indicated in its interlocutory ruling of March 1972, that the question required re-examination precisely because retrospective acceptance could be used to defeat Section 123(7) at will. A resignation, he held, took effect when the competent authority accepted it, on 25 January 1971; until that order was passed, Kapoor's status in law remained that of a government servant, and the assistance he rendered to the campaign before that date, arranging and organising in Rae Bareli as the candidate's election agent in substance, was assistance obtained from a gazetted officer within the meaning of the section. The plea that Kapoor had acted only after tendering his resignation on 13 January, set up late in the written statements, the judgment treated as an afterthought contradicted by the official record. On that reasoning the first corrupt practice stood proved, and with it, under Section 100, the election fell.

The second finding was of the same documentary character. The evidence established that officers of the district administration at Rae Bareli, including the District Magistrate and the Superintendent of Police, and engineers of the Public Works and electricity departments, had supervised the construction of rostrums and barricades and the supply of electric power for the loudspeakers at Indira Gandhi's election meetings on 1 February 1971 and 25 February 1971. The defence characterised these arrangements as the routine security and protocol apparatus that attends a Prime Minister's tour, governed by the Blue Book. Justice Sinha's analysis separated the two kinds of presence: the state could protect a Prime Minister, but a candidate's election meeting, addressed by the candidate as a candidate seeking votes, was an election event, and the officers who built its stage and powered its microphones were assisting the furtherance of her election prospects as surely as if they had distributed her leaflets. The judgment's treatment of the second finding is why the trial's Blue Book battle mattered: the rules of the Prime Minister's security were the executive's claim that the two capacities, head of government and candidate, could not be disentangled. Justice Sinha disentangled them, and held the candidate answerable for the machinery the office had placed at the meeting's disposal.

Against these two findings stand the five rejections, and they are the best internal evidence that the judgment was an act of adjudication rather than of politics. A judge bent on unseating a Prime Minister had richer material available: allegations that Air Force aircraft and helicopters had been procured for campaign travel, that liquor and clothing had flowed to voters through Kapoor's hands, that the cow-and-calf symbol had been deployed as a religious appeal, that voters had been ferried to polling stations in procured vehicles, that crores implied by the petition's arithmetic had been spent beyond the statutory ceiling. Justice Sinha examined each and dismissed each for want of proof, including the expenditure charge, where the Congress party's own organisational spending was held not to be the candidate's unless her consent to it was proved. The cross-appeals that followed map the judgment's impartiality in procedural form: Indira Gandhi appealed against the two findings in Civil Appeal No. 887 of 1975, and Raj Narain appealed against the five rejections in Civil Appeal No. 909 of 1975. Both sides left Allahabad convinced the judge had got the other side's points right and their own wrong. Justice Sinha's personal notes from the trial, preserved in his family's collection and mined by Soli Sorabjee and Arvind P. Datar for Nani Palkhivala: The Courtroom Genius (2012) in reconstructing the legal strategy of the appeal, show the four-year procedural accretion that the post-June narrative flattened into an instant verdict: issue lists reframed by the Supreme Court in 1972, witnesses recalled, a sitting Prime Minister in the box. The judgment was the opposite of an improvisation. It was a sedimented record finally ruled upon, and its sedimented quality is the first reason the trigger metaphor fails: triggers are pulled in a moment; this decision had been loading since April 1971.

## The Evidence Itself: Rostrums, Resignations and the Paper Trail

Because the judgment's reputation travels so far ahead of its contents, the evidence on which it rested deserves reconstruction in its own right. The Kapoor strand began with a sequence of documents no one disputed. Kapoor's letter of resignation was dated 13 January 1971. The government's case, pressed by Indira Gandhi's counsel S.C. Khare, was that the letter ended the matter for election law purposes: a man who had resigned, and whose resignation the President later accepted with effect from 14 January 1971, could not be a government servant when he campaigned in late January and February. The petitioner's evidence showed what Kapoor did in the interval: he travelled to Rae Bareli, met Congress workers, supervised arrangements and conducted himself, in the words of witnesses the court believed, as the candidate's principal organiser, before the acceptance order of 25 January 1971 existed. He was later named formally as her election agent. The legal question, whether retrospective acceptance could rewrite the status of the days before it, had been identified by the Supreme Court in March 1972 as the hinge of the case, with the warning, in the court's own interlocutory reasoning, that the retrospective device might enable any government to defeat Section 123(7) by paperwork. Justice Sinha's resolution of the question followed the logic of the statute rather than the equity of the case: the section prohibited obtaining the assistance of a person holding a gazetted office, Kapoor held the office until the competent authority's order of 25 January, the assistance was obtained in that interval, and the prohibition attached. Notably, the judgment absolved the transaction of any hint of corruption in the moral sense: Kapoor was her devoted aide, and his fault, and hers in law, was sequence, not venality.

The rostrums strand was built from the records of the district itself. Witnesses from the Rae Bareli administration and the Public Works Department described the orders under which the stages for the meetings of 1 February and 25 February 1971 were constructed, the barricading arranged, and the electricity supply for the loudspeakers provided by the state electricity establishment. The defence rested on characterisation: these were the security and protocol arrangements due to a visiting Prime Minister, sanctioned by the Blue Book, the manual whose production the petitioner had sought and the Supreme Court had restricted in January 1975, directing instead that affidavits answer the questions the manual would have answered. Justice Sinha read the affidavits and the oral evidence together and drew the line the Blue Book itself implied: arrangements for the safety and movement of the Prime Minister were the state's duty; arrangements for the conduct of her election meetings, the stage she spoke from as a candidate and the amplification of her appeal for votes, were assistance to her election. The meetings in question were campaign meetings, held in her constituency during the election period, at which she sought votes against named opponents. The officers who erected and powered their stages were gazetted officers of the Uttar Pradesh government. The section's text did the rest. It is worth dwelling on the ordinariness of the proof: muster rolls, tour programmes, departmental orders, the testimony of engineers. The most consequential judgment in the republic's electoral history was not carried by rhetoric about tyranny or destiny. It was carried by the paper trail of a district administration that had done, for the Prime Minister's campaign, what district administrations had grown accustomed to doing for power, and by a statute from 1951 that said they must not.

## Narrow Findings, Broad Constitutional Implications

The strongest counter to the culmination reading deserves its full force. The two corrupt practices Justice Sinha found proved were, in the vocabulary of the period's critics, technical: no bribery was established, no ballot was falsified, no voter was coerced, and the winner's margin of 111,810 votes made the notion that the outcome had been procured by rostrums and a secretary's premature canvassing look disproportionate. Indira Gandhi's defenders argued in June 1975, and sympathetic historians have repeated, that a six-year disqualification for the irregularities of subordinates was a constitutional sledgehammer swung at administrative routine. If the findings were trivial, the argument runs, then the judgment's world-historical consequence was an accident of legalism, and the Emergency that followed was a response to a quirk rather than the working out of a structural collision. The culmination reading, on this objection, overweights two paragraphs of an election judgment.

The objection confuses the narrowness of the findings with the narrowness of the principle they enforced. What Section 123(7) embodied, and what the judgment vindicated, was the constitutional premise that the state's administrative machinery is not the incumbent's campaign machinery, and that this premise binds the Prime Minister with the same force as it binds a first-time candidate in a district town. The identity of the respondent is what converted a routine election judgment into a constitutional event: the petitioner was not alleging that the law had been broken by an ordinary member, and the respondent was not an ordinary member. Every doctrine in contention between 1971 and 1975, the untrammelled amendment power on one side and the basic structure on the other, concerned a single question in different dress: whether the holder of political power in India was subject to legal limits that she could not herself rewrite or staff away. The Allahabad proceeding posed that question in its most concrete available form, stripped of abstraction. Could the Prime Minister be unseated by the election law of the republic, tried in the court the statute named, on the testimony of witnesses anyone could hear? Justice Sinha's answer, that she could and on these facts was, stated the premise of constitutional government against the premise of the amendment cycle. The findings were narrow; the answer was total. That asymmetry is exactly what a culmination looks like: a long structural argument arriving, at last, at a point where it must be answered yes or no, in a form that admitted no further deferral. The disproportionality objection also cuts against its makers: the margin of victory was irrelevant to liability under a statute that voided elections for corrupt practice regardless of margin, and the men who drafted the Representation of the People Act in 1951, legislating in the shadow of [the founding's rights-development tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) between democratic mandate and legal constraint, chose that severity deliberately, because a law that bends for large majorities is not a law of elections at all.

## Article 75(5) and the Question the Judgment Left Open

One feature of the crisis has been underweighted in both readings, and it confirms the culmination analysis from an unexpected direction: the Allahabad judgment did not, of its own force, remove Indira Gandhi from the prime ministership at all. The judgment voided her election to the Lok Sabha and disqualified her from membership. The Constitution, however, does not require the Prime Minister to be a member of Parliament at every moment. Article 75(5) provides that a minister who, for any period of six consecutive months, is not a member of either House ceases to be a minister at the expiration of that period. On the strict construction urged by the government's law officers in June 1975, Indira Gandhi could lawfully have continued as Prime Minister until December 1975 without a seat, and could in principle have sought election from another constituency or entered the Rajya Sabha, subject always to the disqualification's bar on contesting and the appeal's outcome. The Allahabad judgment created a six-month constitutional corridor, not a vacancy. Lawyers in Delhi debated the corridor's dimensions in the newspapers of June 1975, and the debate itself demonstrates the room for manoeuvre that the legal order still afforded the Prime Minister even after its most adverse judgment in a generation.

### Did the Allahabad judgment remove Indira Gandhi as Prime Minister at once?

The judgment did not remove Indira Gandhi as Prime Minister at once. It voided her Lok Sabha election and disqualified her from membership, but Article 75(5) permits a minister six consecutive months in office without a seat in either House. Continuation until December 1975 was constitutionally arguable, so the opposition demanded resignation as a moral act.

The opposition's answer to the corridor was that legality was not the currency in which the judgment dealt: a Prime Minister judicially found guilty of corrupt practices, Jayaprakash Narayan argued, retained the office's furniture and lost its title, and the demand for resignation was framed precisely as a moral demand because the moral register was the only one in which it could be made immediately binding. This framing, strategically understandable, carried a cost the culmination reading should record honestly. By insisting that the judgment's force was moral and immediate rather than legal and appellate, the opposition conceded the executive's premise that the legal process was too slow and too conditional to govern the succession, and the two camps met on the shared ground that law alone would not settle the question. The Emergency was proclaimed by one side of that shared ground. The point matters for adjudication: the crisis of June 1975 was not a contest between law and lawlessness. It was a contest, after the collision of 1971 to 1975, over whether the remaining legal forms, the stay, the appeal, the six months of Article 75(5), would be allowed to run their course or would be short-circuited by the actors who found them inconvenient. Both the rally's ultimatum and the proclamation were short circuits. Only one of them commanded the state.

## Three Legitimate Responses and the Fourth That Was Chosen

Because the choice is the article's crux, the alternatives available to Indira Gandhi in June 1975 must be stated concretely rather than gestured at. The first was acceptance with appeal: resign the prime ministership, allow the Congress Parliamentary Party to elect an interim leader, and prosecute the appeal to the Supreme Court from outside the office, returning vindicated if the appeal succeeded. The judgment's own twenty-day stay made this path orderly rather than chaotic; the machinery existed, and the political cost, though severe, was finite, because an acquittal on appeal would have restored her with the added authority of a leader who had submitted to the law. The second path was legislative and prospective: Parliament could have amended the election law for the future, clarifying the status of officers on the verge of resignation or the boundary between security arrangements and campaign assistance, while leaving the judgment against her personally to be fought on appeal. Such an amendment, prospective in operation, would have been constitutionally unremarkable, an ordinary exercise of the power that had produced the election code in the first place. The third path was simply to let the appeal run: remain in office on the strength of whatever stay the Supreme Court granted, accept the indignity of a conditional order, and govern for the months the Constitution Bench needed. Each path conceded the same premise, that the judgment of 12 June 1975 was a legal fact to be accommodated, appealed or prospectively legislated around. None required surrender of anything except the claim that the office stood above the process that had condemned its holder.

The fourth path was the one taken, and it must be described with the same concreteness. It began inside [Indira's office-rebuilding trajectory](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/): the progressive conversion, since 1969, of the prime ministership from the headship of a cabinet and a party into a personal instrument, staffed by loyalists, defended by a pliant party apparatus, and held increasingly independent of the institutions that were supposed to check it. Viewed against that trajectory, the Emergency proclamation of 25 June 1975 was not an improvisation forced on a reluctant leader by a court's surprise. It was the application, to a judicial challenge, of a governing method already formed: meet institutional resistance by disabling the institution. The amendment cycle had disabled adverse doctrines by rewriting the text. The supersession had disabled an adverse bench by rewriting its composition. The Emergency disabled the adverse judgment by suspending the legal order in which judgments against the executive could be executed, and it did the rest of the office-rebuilding programme's pending work at the same stroke: the opposition leadership was arrested, the press was censored, and the Parliament that remained was free to rewrite both the election law and the Constitution with the courts' prisoner unable to object. The judgment forced the timing. The trajectory supplied the content.

## Twelve Days in Delhi: The Politics Between Judgment and Stay

The interval between the judgment and the stay application, twelve days in which the appeal was prepared and the capital's politics ran at full speed, is usually compressed into a sentence. Its texture matters, because it records the governing party's instinctive answer to an institutional check, an answer rehearsed before the Emergency gave it legal dress. The Congress apparatus mobilised in the capital within hours of the judgment: the party's president, Dev Kant Barooah, who had supplied the era's emblematic slogan at the Guwahati session of the All India Congress Committee in 1974, declaring that India was Indira and Indira was India, organised demonstrations of support at the Prime Minister's residence at 1 Safdarjung Road, where crowds of party workers, bused in from the neighbouring states by the party organisation, performed the loyalty the institutions were withholding. Rallies in support of the Prime Minister were staged through the week, addressed by cabinet ministers who framed the judgment as a conspiracy of the opposition, foreign agencies and a judiciary drawn from a privileged stratum. Sanjay Gandhi, the Prime Minister's younger son, holding no office in party or government, was visible at the centre of the mobilisation, a fact the Shah Commission's later inquiry treated as evidence of the extra-constitutional centre of authority that the office-rebuilding programme had created.

The opposition's twelve days were symmetrical. Its leaders, Morarji Desai among them, demanded resignation as the judgment's automatic consequence and treated the twenty-day stay as an abuse of grace; Desai announced that the opposition would sit in satyagraha at the Prime Minister's residence until she resigned, and the Janata Morcha's Gujarat victory was celebrated in Delhi as the electoral half of a double verdict. Newspapers split on the judgment's meaning along the lines the collision years had laid down: The Indian Express and The Statesman, which had opposed the government's press measures and the supersession, read the judgment as the judiciary's finest hour, while the papers aligned with the government discovered in it proof of judicial overreach and of the need to democratise the bench. What no significant actor did in those twelve days was the thing the legal order prescribed: treat the judgment as a first-instance decision in a pending case, note the appeal, and await the Supreme Court. The executive-in-waiting of the opposition and the executive in office were agreed, for opposite purposes, that the judgment was a political settlement. When Justice Krishna Iyer delivered his conditional stay on 24 June, he was therefore answering not only a stay application but a settled public conviction, on both sides, that the courts had decided who should govern. His order's insistence that the court could not weigh political consequence was the legal order's last attempt to reclaim the question. It survived for a day.

## 24 June 1975: The Conditional Stay and the Closing of the Legal Road

Indira Gandhi's appeal reached the Supreme Court within the twenty-day window Justice Sinha had granted, and on 24 June 1975 the vacation judge, Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer, delivered his order on the stay application, reported as Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, AIR 1975 SC 1590. She had asked for an absolute stay of the Allahabad judgment. Justice Krishna Iyer refused it, and granted instead a conditional stay whose terms defined her legal position with precision: the disqualification was stayed, so that she could continue as Prime Minister and attend and speak in Parliament, but she could not vote in the Lok Sabha and she could draw no salary as a member until the appeal was decided. The order's reasoning, an extended meditation on the judicial function at the intersection of law and politics, held that the court could not weigh the political consequences urged on it and must apply the law's standards to the stay as it would to any litigant's. In form the order was a compromise; in substance it was the third institutional answer in twelve days to the question the collision course had posed. The High Court had said the law unseated her. The Supreme Court had said the law would hold her in a diminished office, neither restored nor removed, while the appeal ran. Between the two orders, no lawful instrument existed that returned her to the undamaged position of 11 June 1975.

### How many days separated the Allahabad judgment from the Emergency proclamation?

Thirteen days separated the Allahabad judgment of 12 June 1975 from the proclamation of Emergency on 25 June 1975. Within them fell the appeal's filing, Justice Krishna Iyer's conditional stay of 24 June, and the Ramlila Maidan rally of 25 June at which Jayaprakash Narayan demanded Indira Gandhi's resignation.

On 25 June 1975 the opposition's convergence reached its peak at the Ramlila Maidan in Delhi, where Jayaprakash Narayan addressed a vast rally, demanded the Prime Minister's resignation as the judgment's moral consequence, and called on the police and armed forces to refuse orders they judged unconstitutional. Raj Narain, the petitioner whose four years of litigation had produced the judgment, announced a satyagraha at the Prime Minister's residence from the following morning. That night, electricity to Delhi's newspaper presses was cut, and President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed signed the proclamation of Emergency under Article 352, citing internal disturbance, on the advice tendered to him without a prior cabinet meeting. The cabinet was informed the next morning. Arrests of opposition leaders under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act began before dawn on 26 June; Raj Narain was among those taken. The sequence of 25 June is the strongest material the trigger reading possesses: judgment, conditional stay, ultimatum rally, proclamation, in thirteen days of tightening crisis. But sequence is not causation of the kind the trigger metaphor asserts. The proclamation's instruments, the grounds prepared in advance, the lists of persons to be detained, the censorship machinery, belonged to a repertoire the government had been assembling through the confrontation years; the Shah Commission, appointed in May 1977 to inquire into the Emergency's excesses, later documented the preparatory consultations that preceded June 1975. A government surprised by a court does not possess detention lists on the night it is surprised. The judgment determined when the fourth path would be taken. The path had been surveyed earlier.

## The Architecture of the Conditional Stay

Justice Krishna Iyer's order of 24 June 1975 repays attention as a legal instrument, because it was drafted for exactly the pressure under which it was delivered and it set the terms on which the crisis's final act would be played. The stay application was governed by Section 116B of the Representation of the People Act, which empowered the Supreme Court, on appeal from a High Court's election judgment, to stay the order's operation on conditions. The appellant sought an absolute stay, restoring the status quo in full: membership, vote, salary, office. The respondent's counsel urged the doctrine of unclean hands and the gravity of a proved corrupt practice. Justice Krishna Iyer's order declined both absolutes. It reasoned that a stay is an equitable and discretionary instrument, that at the stay stage the court cannot prejudge the appeal's merits, and that the balance of convenience had to weigh the fact that the appellant held an office whose vacancy carried consequences for the governance of the country, against the fact that a court of competent jurisdiction had found corrupt practices proved against her. The formula the order adopted, permitting her to discharge the functions of the Prime Minister and to participate in Parliament's proceedings without a vote and without a member's salary, was adapted from the type of conditional stay developed in the election jurisprudence after 1966 for members whose elections stood voided pending appeal. Its ingenuity was institutional: the order kept the government functioning and the appeal alive, and it left the appellant neither vindicated nor deposed, suspended in a legal condition that only the Constitution Bench's eventual judgment could resolve. Legally, the order was complete. Politically, it was unbearable to both camps, and the speed with which it was overtaken, by the rally of the next afternoon and the proclamation of the next night, is the measure of how far the conflict had by then travelled beyond the management of courts.

## The Proclamation's Own Paper Trail

The Emergency proclamation also left a paper trail, and the trail points away from spontaneity. Article 352 empowered the President, if satisfied that a grave emergency existed whereby the security of India was threatened by internal disturbance, to proclaim an emergency by which the federal distribution of power and the Fundamental Rights regime of Article 19 stood suspended. The proclamation of 25 June 1975 was signed by President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed late that night, on the Prime Minister's advice communicated in a letter, before the Council of Ministers had met to consider it; the cabinet was presented with the accomplished fact on the morning of 26 June. The constitutional irregularity of advice tendered without a cabinet decision was not a technicality invented by later critics: it was examined by the Shah Commission in 1977 and 1978, which also recorded the evidence that the instrument had been under contemplation within the Prime Minister's circle for months. Siddhartha Shankar Ray, the Chief Minister of West Bengal and a lawyer close to the Prime Minister, had written to her on 8 January 1975 proposing, in the context of the JP movement, the preparation of a plan of action that contemplated an internal emergency; his evidence and the documentary record before the Commission placed the option inside the government's deliberations almost six months before Justice Sinha delivered judgment. Detention lists under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, a statute tightened by ordinance in the preceding period, were executed within hours of the signature, reaching opposition leaders across the country before dawn, and electricity was disconnected from Delhi's press row in Bahadur Shah Zafar Marg in the small hours of 26 June so that the morning papers could not print. A proclamation improvised in panic does not disconnect the presses before it announces itself. The paper trail of 25 June corroborates, from the executive's own files, what the sequence of 1971 to 1975 establishes from the public record: the fourth path had been mapped before the judgment required anyone to walk it.

No row is intelligible in isolation, and the last row is the least intelligible of all when severed from the ten that precede it. The trigger reading is the last row read alone.

## The Amendment Answer and the Judgment of 7 November 1975

The Emergency Parliament executed the amendment method at a scale the 1971 cycle had only rehearsed. In August 1975 the government carried two instruments. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 1975, Act 40 of 1975, retrospectively rewrote the Representation of the People Act: it restructured the definition of corrupt practice in respect of government servants' assistance, recast the treatment of expenditure incurred by parties and supporters, and altered the law under which the Allahabad trial had been conducted, with effect reaching back to cover the 1971 election. The Constitution (Thirty-Ninth Amendment) Act, 1975, assented to on 10 August 1975, went further. It inserted Article 329A, which removed the elections of the President, the Vice-President, the Prime Minister and the Speaker from the jurisdiction of the courts altogether, assigned disputes over them to a body Parliament would create by law, and, in clause (4), declared that the Allahabad judgment in the pending appeal was void and that Indira Gandhi's election stood valid notwithstanding it. Clause (4) was the amendment method reduced to its essence: not a general rule for the future but a legislative decree deciding a named case, annulling a court's judgment by name and substituting the legislature's own verdict on the facts.

The appeal, heard from August 1975 by a Constitution Bench of the five surviving judges of the Kesavananda Bharati bench, Chief Justice A.N. Ray and Justices H.R. Khanna, K.K. Mathew, M.H. Beg and Y.V. Chandrachud, became the final engagement of the collision course. Its human episode is recorded in Sorabjee and Datar's biography of Nani Palkhivala: Palkhivala, the advocate who had argued Kesavananda Bharati against the government and had been briefed to argue Indira Gandhi's appeal, returned the brief in protest after the Emergency proclamation and censorship, and the appellant's case passed to Ashok Sen and Jagannath Kaushal. Shanti Bhushan argued for Raj Narain, who pressed the cross-appeal from detention. Arguments ran for some thirty days and closed on 9 October 1975. The judgment, delivered on 7 November 1975 and reported as Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, (1975) Supp SCC 1, with five separate opinions, held the field for both sides in a division that defines the culmination reading's verdict. Clause (4) of Article 329A was struck down: Chief Justice Ray found it violated the rule of law, Justice Khanna the norms of free and fair elections, Justice Mathew the principle that election disputes must be resolved by ascertaining adjudicative facts under law, and Justice Chandrachud the separation of powers and Article 14; the basic-structure doctrine, applied for the first time to annul a constitutional provision, held. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act was upheld as ordinary legislation within Parliament's competence, and Indira Gandhi's election, judged under the amended law, was declared valid: on the law as retrospectively rewritten, neither finding of corrupt practice survived. The Allahabad judgment was reversed, and the disqualification fell with it.

The division inside the November judgment is the collision course's true epitaph, and it cuts against both simple readings. Against the trigger reading, the outcome shows the system metabolising the Allahabad judgment through its ordinary appellate process: the appeal the judgment itself had enabled was heard, argued for a month, and decided by the very judges Kesavananda Bharati had made famous, and the executive accepted a ruling that struck down its most brazen instrument, clause (4), in the middle of the Emergency. Against any reading in which the judiciary simply triumphed, the same outcome shows the amendment method succeeding by the side door: the statute was rewritten retrospectively, the rewritten statute acquitted the appellant, and the doctrine that stopped the constitutional amendment did not touch the ordinary law that did the work. Granville Austin's account in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) reads the November judgment as the constitution's immune system operating under occupation, doctrinally intact and practically disarmed. The culmination reading incorporates Austin's judgment and extends it backward: the November verdict was not a new drama but the last move of the sequence that began in November 1971, when the executive announced, by the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, that no judicial word was final, and the judiciary spent four years, at Kesavananda Bharati, at Allahabad and in the November judgment itself, determining which words would nevertheless remain so.

## The Judges of the Collision: Personnel as Doctrine

A collision course conducted through texts was also conducted through persons, and the four judges who successively held the case's fate map its stages. Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha, the trial judge, was a career High Court judge of no public political colour, the third judge to whom the petition had been assigned in its four-year course, and his judgment's even distribution of victories, two charges proved of seven pressed, remains the record's best answer to the charge of judicial conspiracy. His conduct after the judgment is its own small evidence: he neither sought nor received elevation in the years that followed, and the government he had ruled against left him unmolested in office while the Emergency lasted, a forbearance the Shah Commission noted when it examined the treatment of the judiciary. Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer, who granted the conditional stay, was a judge of known sympathy for the government's social programme, appointed to the Supreme Court in 1973; his refusal of an absolute stay, on stated principle, from a bench of one, in the full blaze of executive pressure, is the collision era's clearest demonstration that the judiciary's institutional reflexes survived its staffing. Chief Justice A.N. Ray, whose elevation in April 1973 had begun the appointments struggle, presided over the November 1975 bench and joined in striking down clause (4) of Article 329A on the rule of law ground, against the government that had elevated him and in the middle of its Emergency. Justice H.R. Khanna, the author of the decisive concurrence in Kesavananda Bharati, supplied the free and fair elections ground in November 1975, and paid the personnel price the doctrine exacted in that era: in January 1977 he was superseded for the Chief Justiceship in favour of Justice M.H. Beg, his colleague on the November bench, and resigned, the second supersession of the decade closing the pattern the first had opened.

The pattern of the four judges defeats the simplest version of every partisan reading. The executive's appointments strategy, pursued from April 1973, was supposed to produce a court that would not do what the courts of 1975 in fact did: unseat a Prime Minister at trial, refuse her an absolute stay, and annul the constitutional clause enacted to annul the trial. The judges who performed these acts included the executive's own appointees and elevatees at every step. What the collision produced, instead of obedience, was a judiciary whose members, whatever the route of their arrival, kept answering the institutional question put to them: whether the text contained limits that bound its temporary custodians. Granville Austin recorded the paradox in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) and located its explanation in the professional culture of the Indian bar and bench, a culture the executive could staff but could not rewrite. It is the same culture visible in the lawyers of the case: in Shanti Bhushan conducting a four-year trial against a sitting Prime Minister in her own state's courts, in Nani Palkhivala returning the Prime Minister's brief when censorship made honest advocacy impossible, in the Allahabad bar's protection of its trial from the pressures that surrounded it. The culmination at Allahabad was institutional before it was personal, which is why the persons keep disappointing the narratives written about them.

## Trigger or Culmination: The Adjudication

The two readings can be weighed on the evidence assembled. The trigger reading holds that the Emergency was caused by the Allahabad judgment: a Prime Minister facing disqualification and a diminished office struck at the constitutional order to save herself, and had Justice Sinha decided the five rejected charges as he decided the two proved ones, or had the petition never been filed, the proclamation of 25 June 1975 would not have occurred. The reading has the support of the calendar, of the opposition's own conduct in treating the judgment as the instrument of removal, and of Chandra, Mukherjee and Mukherjee's documentation in In the Name of Democracy (2003) of how the JP movement organised itself around the judgment's anticipated moral effect. It also has the support of Indira Gandhi's own framing, which presented the Emergency as an answer to a crisis of governability crystallised in June 1975. Its weakness is that it cannot explain the repertoire. The detention lists, the censorship orders, the pre-drafted proclamation route through a President approached without a cabinet meeting, the August amendments prepared to annul the judgment by name: these were the artefacts of a method formed across four years of institutional conflict, and a method, once formed, does not require its final occasion to be improvised. The trigger reading explains the date of the Emergency and nothing else about it: not its duration of twenty-one months, not its programme of constitutional reconstruction culminating in the Forty-Second Amendment, not the ease with which the machinery assembled. A cause that explains only timing is not the cause of the thing.

The culmination reading holds that the judgment of 12 June 1975 was the forcing point of a collision course datable from the amendment cycle of 1971: the executive's claim to unlimited amendment power, the judiciary's basic-structure answer, the supersession, the retrospective repairs of adverse rulings, and the election trial in which all of these converged on the person of the Prime Minister. On this reading, the judgment's function was to convert an institutional struggle conducted by indirection into a personal and binary choice that could no longer be deferred, because the law had pronounced on the one office the entire struggle concerned. The choice that followed, Emergency rather than resignation, amendment or patience, was the office-rebuilding programme's consummation, not its reflex. The culmination reading explains what the trigger reading cannot: the prepared machinery, the August amendments' design, the November judgment's divided outcome, and the fact that the constitutional struggle resumed after 1977 along the same doctrinal lines, through Minerva Mills in 1980 striking down the Forty-Second Amendment's attempt to abolish the basic-structure limit itself, as though the collision had merely paused for an election. This article adjudicates for the culmination reading, on the primary evidence of the dated sequence: three amendments, one doctrine, one supersession, one trial, two courts and a proclamation, each step documented in the record of the institution that took it. The Allahabad judgment was the constitutional culmination of that sequence. It forced a choice Indira Gandhi had been postponing, between the legal order that had condemned her and the personal order she had been constructing since 1969. She chose, and the choosing, not the judgment, is where responsibility for the Emergency resides.

## The Judgment in the Long Argument of the Republic

The men and women who framed the Constitution had designed the election petition as a humble instrument, a defeated candidate's remedy, and had lodged it in the High Courts by the Election Commission's machinery and Parliament's statute rather than in any special tribunal the executive might capture. The design choice carried an assumption the founding generation stated in several registers: that democratic legitimacy and legal regularity are not rivals but partners, the mandate being precisely the authority to govern under law and not above it. The amendment struggles of the republic's first decades, over property in the 1950s and over the amending power in the 1960s, had kept that assumption under negotiation without breaking it, because each side, executive and judiciary alike, had continued to argue inside the shared premise that a constitutional text constrained them both. The collision course of 1971 to 1975 was the first sustained attempt by one side to exit the premise: the Twenty-Fourth Amendment's claim to limitless revision, the supersession's claim to a committed bench, and the Thirty-Ninth Amendment's claim to decide a pending case by legislative decree were three forms of a single proposition, that the holder of a parliamentary majority holds the Constitution itself in commission. The Allahabad judgment was the premise's reassertion in its plainest possible form, at the point of maximum resistance: the Prime Minister of India, at the height of her post-1971 authority, stood in the same relation to the election law as the lowliest candidate for a municipal board, and the law, applied by an undistinguished judge in a provincial courtroom, unseated her.

That is why the judgment of 12 June 1975 continues to organise the teaching of Indian constitutional history, and why the two readings of it will not be reconciled by further evidence about June 1975. The trigger reading is a claim about a fortnight. The culmination reading is a claim about the republic: that between 1971 and 1975 the Indian executive attempted to convert a parliamentary majority into constituent sovereignty, that the judiciary resisted with the only weapons it possessed, doctrine and adjudication, and that the collision of those two projects, not the misfortune of a single litigant, is what the Emergency was fought over. The evidence of the dated sequence sustains the second claim, and the sequence's aftermath sustains it further: the doctrine announced in Kesavananda Bharati survived its author's supersession, annulled a clause of the Thirty-Ninth Amendment in November 1975, and annulled the Forty-Second Amendment's abolition of judicial review in Minerva Mills in 1980, after the electorate had itself disposed of the government that proclaimed the Emergency. The judgment at Allahabad stands in the middle of that sequence, neither its origin nor its end, which is the exact position the word culmination describes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Allahabad judgment of 1975?

The Allahabad judgment of 1975 was the decision of Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court, delivered on 12 June 1975 in Election Petition No. 5 of 1971, the petition in which the defeated candidate Raj Narain had challenged Indira Gandhi's election from Rae Bareli in the general election of 1971. The court held her guilty of two corrupt practices under Section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, declared her election void under Section 100, and disqualified her for six years under Section 8A. Five further charges in the petition, including bribery and misuse of Air Force aircraft, were rejected as not proved. The judgment was the first in the republic's history to unseat a serving Prime Minister, and its constitutional force lay in its ordinariness: a single High Court judge applied the election statute of 1951 to the most powerful officeholder in the country in exactly the terms the statute applied to every candidate, after a four-year trial in which the Prime Minister had herself testified.

### Q: Who was Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha?

Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha was the Allahabad High Court judge who tried Election Petition No. 5 of 1971 and delivered the judgment of 12 June 1975 voiding Indira Gandhi's election. He was the third judge to whom the petition, filed in April 1971, had been assigned during its four-year course, and he conducted the trial through its most charged phases, including the litigation over the Blue Book of Prime Ministerial security and Indira Gandhi's appearance in the witness box in March 1975. His judgment found two of the seven charges against the Prime Minister proved and rejected five, a distribution that has anchored his reputation for judicial independence. He had ruled against the petitioner on substantial points during the trial, including the challenge to the retrospective election law amendment of 1974. After June 1975 he neither sought nor received elevation to the Supreme Court, and the government whose head he had unseated left him in office at Allahabad. His trial notes, preserved by his family, document the procedural accumulation behind a judgment later mythologised as a sudden stroke.

### Q: What did Indira Gandhi do that was found illegal?

Justice Sinha found Indira Gandhi guilty of two corrupt practices, each under Section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, which prohibits a candidate from obtaining the assistance of gazetted government officers in furthering election prospects. The first concerned Yashpal Kapoor, an Officer on Special Duty in the Prime Minister's Secretariat, who campaigned and organised for her in Rae Bareli in January 1971 before the order accepting his resignation from government service was passed on 25 January 1971; although the acceptance was expressed to operate from 14 January, the court held that he remained a government servant in law until the order was made, and that his campaign assistance in that interval was assistance obtained from a gazetted officer. The second concerned the officers of the Rae Bareli district administration, including the District Magistrate, the Superintendent of Police and engineers of the Public Works and electricity departments, who arranged rostrums, barricades and power for loudspeakers at her election meetings of 1 February and 25 February 1971. Both findings concerned process rather than money: no bribery was found proved against her.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court overrule the Allahabad judgment?

The Supreme Court reversed the Allahabad judgment, in its decision of 7 November 1975 in Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, reported at (1975) Supp SCC 1, but the reversal is only half of what the five-judge bench did. A Constitution Bench of Chief Justice A.N. Ray and Justices H.R. Khanna, K.K. Mathew, M.H. Beg and Y.V. Chandrachud struck down clause (4) of Article 329A, the provision the Thirty-Ninth Amendment had inserted to declare the Allahabad judgment void and the election valid by legislative decree, holding it an assault on the basic structure of the Constitution. The bench then upheld the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 1975, by which Parliament had retrospectively rewritten the provisions under which she had been convicted, and, applying the amended law, held that neither of Justice Sinha's two findings survived. Her election was accordingly declared valid and the disqualification fell. Raj Narain's cross-appeal against the rejection of his five other charges failed as well. The Allahabad judgment was therefore overruled in result, while the constitutional doctrine its crisis produced was entrenched.

### Q: Why did Indira declare Emergency after the judgment?

The proclamation of Emergency on the night of 25 June 1975 was Indira Gandhi's answer to the position the Allahabad judgment and the Supreme Court's conditional stay of 24 June had placed her in: unseated by the High Court, held in a diminished office without a vote in the Lok Sabha, facing an appeal she might lose, and confronted by an opposition movement under Jayaprakash Narayan demanding her resignation and preparing a satyagraha at her residence. The proclamation under Article 352, signed by President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed on her advice before the cabinet had met, suspended the legal order in which the judgment operated, authorised the detention of the opposition leadership, and freed her government to rewrite the election law and the Constitution. The fuller explanation is structural rather than biographical. The judgment was the culmination of a collision, running since the amendment cycle of 1971, between an executive claiming unlimited power to revise the constitutional text and a judiciary that had answered with the basic-structure doctrine. The Emergency disabled the arena in which she had lost rather than answering the case she had lost.

### Q: What is the Representation of the People Act?

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 is the parliamentary statute, enacted under Article 327 of the Constitution, that governs the conduct of elections to Parliament and the state legislatures in India: the qualifications and disqualifications of candidates, the machinery of nomination, polling and counting, the definition of corrupt practices in Section 123, and the resolution of election disputes by petition to the High Courts under Sections 80 to 116. Its companion statute of 1950 governs electoral rolls. The Act's dispute code was the legal ground of the Allahabad case: Section 100 obliged the court to void an election once a corrupt practice stood proved, Section 123(7) defined the obtaining of a gazetted officer's assistance as such a practice, and Section 8A attached disqualification of up to six years. The Act has been amended many times, most consequentially for this history in 1966, when the trial of election petitions was transferred from ad hoc tribunals to the High Courts, and in 1975, when the Election Laws (Amendment) Act rewrote its provisions retrospectively during the Emergency.

### Q: Who was Raj Narain?

Raj Narain was the Samyukta Socialist Party candidate who contested Rae Bareli against Indira Gandhi in the general election of March 1971, lost by a margin of 111,810 votes, and then filed Election Petition No. 5 of 1971 in the Allahabad High Court challenging her election. A veteran Socialist organiser from Uttar Pradesh and an associate of Jayaprakash Narayan, he pursued the petition through four years of litigation argued by Shanti Bhushan, and won the judgment of 12 June 1975 that voided the Prime Minister's election on two of his seven charges. He was arrested in the first hours of the Emergency and remained in detention through much of it. Released as the 1977 general election approached, he contested Rae Bareli again under the Janata Party banner and defeated Indira Gandhi by 55,202 votes in March 1977, the only electoral defeat of her parliamentary career, and became Minister for Health in the government of Morarji Desai. His career joined the two modes of opposition the period offered: the courtroom petition and the mass movement, pursued by the same man to the same end.

### Q: What was Election Petition No. 5 of 1971?

Election Petition No. 5 of 1971 was the election petition Raj Narain filed in the Allahabad High Court in April 1971, under Section 80 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, seeking to void Indira Gandhi's election from Rae Bareli in the general election of March 1971 on grounds of corrupt practices. It alleged, among other charges, that she had obtained the assistance of government servants including her aide Yashpal Kapoor and the Rae Bareli district administration, had procured Air Force transport, had authorised the distribution of liquor and clothing, had appealed to religious sentiment through the cow and calf symbol, and had exceeded the statutory ceiling on election expenditure. The petition's trial ran for four years, through an interlocutory appeal to the Supreme Court decided in March 1972, litigation over the Prime Minister's security Blue Book, and the respondent's own testimony in March 1975. Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha's judgment of 12 June 1975 upheld two charges and rejected the rest, voiding the election and disqualifying the Prime Minister for six years.

### Q: Who was Yashpal Kapoor?

Yashpal Kapoor was a gazetted officer of the Government of India who served in the Prime Minister's Secretariat as an Officer on Special Duty to Indira Gandhi, and whose resignation chronology produced the first of the two findings that unseated her. He tendered his resignation on 13 January 1971 and took up organisational work on her Rae Bareli campaign, later serving as her election agent. The President accepted his resignation by an order passed on 25 January 1971, expressed to take effect from 14 January 1971, and the acceptance was gazetted on 6 February 1971. Justice Sinha held that a resignation takes effect in law when the competent authority accepts it, so that Kapoor remained a government servant until 25 January, and that his campaign assistance before that date was assistance obtained from a gazetted officer within Section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act. The Supreme Court had flagged precisely this question in its interlocutory ruling of March 1972, warning that retrospective acceptance of resignations could be used to defeat the section altogether.

### Q: What did the Krishna Iyer stay order of 24 June 1975 permit Indira Gandhi to do?

The order of Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer of 24 June 1975, delivered as vacation judge on Indira Gandhi's application for a stay of the Allahabad judgment and reported at AIR 1975 SC 1590, refused the absolute stay she had sought and granted a conditional stay under Section 116B of the Representation of the People Act. Its terms permitted her to continue discharging the functions of Prime Minister, and to attend and speak in the proceedings of Parliament, while the appeal was pending. It did not permit her to vote in the Lok Sabha, and it did not permit her to draw a salary as a member of Parliament, because the finding that her election was void and the disqualification pronounced at Allahabad stood unreversed until the appeal was decided. The order reasoned that a stay is a discretionary instrument in which the court cannot weigh the political consequences pressed upon it by either side. Its practical effect was to hold her in office in a diminished legal condition, neither restored nor removed, for the months the Constitution Bench would need.

### Q: What was the Thirty-Ninth Amendment?

The Constitution (Thirty-Ninth Amendment) Act, 1975, which received the President's assent on 10 August 1975 during the Emergency, inserted Article 329A into the Constitution and placed the elections of the President, the Vice-President, the Prime Minister and the Speaker of the Lok Sabha beyond the jurisdiction of the courts. Disputes over those elections were assigned to a body Parliament might create by law, and pending disputes in the courts stood transferred away from them. Clause (4) of the new article went beyond institutional design: it declared that the Allahabad High Court's judgment of 12 June 1975 in the election case against Indira Gandhi was void, that her election stood valid, and that the laws under which she had been convicted were to be read as if the judgment had never been delivered. In its judgment of 7 November 1975 the Supreme Court struck clause (4) down as a violation of the basic structure, the first time a constitutional amendment provision was annulled on that ground. Article 329A itself was repealed by the Forty-Fourth Amendment in 1978.

### Q: Why did the Supreme Court uphold Indira Gandhi's election in November 1975?

The Supreme Court upheld Indira Gandhi's election, in its judgment of 7 November 1975, because it applied the election law as Parliament had retrospectively rewritten it during the Emergency. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 1975, Act 40 of 1975, had restructured the definition of corrupt practice concerning the assistance of government servants and the accounting of election expenditure, with retrospective effect covering the 1971 election. The five-judge bench held that statute to be ordinary legislation within Parliament's competence, to which the basic-structure doctrine did not extend, and, judging the Allahabad findings under the amended definitions, held that neither the Yashpal Kapoor assistance nor the district officers' arrangements constituted a corrupt practice under the law as it then stood to be applied. The same bench struck down clause (4) of Article 329A, which had attempted to annul the Allahabad judgment by name, as destructive of free and fair elections, the rule of law and the separation of powers. Justice M.H. Beg, in his separate opinion, upheld the election on the amended statute without deciding the amendment's validity.

### Q: What does Section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act prohibit?

Section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 prohibits a candidate, or any person acting with the candidate's consent, from obtaining or procuring assistance, other than the giving of a vote, for the furtherance of the candidate's election prospects from persons belonging to specified classes of government service: members of the armed forces, police officers, excise officers, revenue officers, and, as the provision was applied at Allahabad, gazetted officers of the government. Its purpose is to keep the permanent administration out of electoral combat, so that the state machine that conducts an election cannot become one contestant's organisation. The prohibition is strict: the section does not require proof that the assistance changed any vote, and the margin of victory is irrelevant once the assistance and the consent are established. It was the basis of both findings against Indira Gandhi in 1975, the campaign work of her aide Yashpal Kapoor before his resignation took effect, and the Rae Bareli district officers' work on her election meetings. A finding under the section, combined with Section 100, voided the election and triggered disqualification under Section 8A.

### Q: How did the Kesavananda Bharati judgment shape the Allahabad case?

Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973 by thirteen judges voting seven to six, shaped the Allahabad case in three ways even though it decided a different dispute. Doctrinally, it established that Parliament's amendment power, restored by the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, stopped at the basic structure of the Constitution, the weapon the Supreme Court would use in November 1975 to annul clause (4) of Article 329A. Institutionally, the government's answer to the doctrine, the supersession of Justices Shelat, Hegde and Grover on 26 April 1973 in favour of Justice A.N. Ray, turned the judiciary's composition into a public controversy and taught every judge in the country the price schedule for adverse decisions before the Allahabad trial entered its final phase. Argumentatively, the Allahabad proceeding was litigated in Kesavananda Bharati's shadow: the petitioner's case that a Prime Minister stood under the election law as an ordinary candidate was the adjudicative form of the proposition that constitutional limits bound the powerful, the very proposition the doctrine existed to defend. Justice H.R. Khanna, whose concurrence had been decisive in 1973, sat on the bench that decided the election appeal.

### Q: Why did Nani Palkhivala return Indira Gandhi's brief in June 1975?

Nani Palkhivala, the Bombay advocate who had argued Kesavananda Bharati against the government in 1973 and had become the bar's leading constitutional lawyer, had been briefed to argue Indira Gandhi's appeal against the Allahabad judgment in the Supreme Court. After the proclamation of Emergency on 25 June 1975 and the imposition of press censorship, he returned the brief and withdrew from the case. The episode is documented in Soli Sorabjee and Arvind P. Datar's biography, Nani Palkhivala: The Courtroom Genius (2012), which treats the return of the brief as the advocate's refusal to lend his forensic standing to a government that had suspended the constitutional order his advocacy existed to serve. The appellant's case in the Constitution Bench hearings that ran from August to October 1975 was argued instead by Ashok Sen and Jagannath Kaushal, while Shanti Bhushan argued for Raj Narain in support of the Allahabad judgment and against the Thirty-Ninth Amendment. Palkhivala's withdrawal deprived the appeal of its most formidable advocate at the moment of its greatest legal difficulty, and it signalled the bar's judgment on the Emergency more audibly than any resolution.

### Q: Which charges in Raj Narain's petition did Justice Sinha reject?

Justice Sinha rejected five of the seven heads of charge in Raj Narain's petition, and the rejections are the strongest internal evidence of the judgment's independence. He rejected the charge that Indira Gandhi had procured the assistance of the armed forces of the Union, through the use of Air Force aircraft and helicopters for her campaign travel. He rejected the charge that Yashpal Kapoor and others had distributed clothes and liquor to voters as an inducement, the bribery allegation that had attracted the widest publicity. He rejected the charge that she and her election agent had made a systematic appeal to religion by deploying the cow and calf, the election symbol of her Congress, as a religious emblem. He rejected the charge that her workers had procured vehicles for the free conveyance of voters to the polling stations. And he rejected the charge that her election expenditure had exceeded the ceiling fixed by Section 77(3) of the Act read with Rule 90 of the Conduct of Elections Rules, holding the party's organisational expenditure not to be hers without proved consent. Raj Narain's cross-appeal against these five rejections failed in November 1975.

### Q: What did the Twenty-Fourth Amendment of 1971 change?

The Constitution (Twenty-Fourth Amendment) Act, 1971, which received the President's assent on 5 November 1971, amended Articles 13 and 368 of the Constitution to overturn the Supreme Court's 1967 judgment in Golak Nath v State of Punjab. Golak Nath had held that Parliament could not amend the Fundamental Rights, treating a constitutional amendment as law within the meaning of Article 13. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment declared that Parliament, in the exercise of its constituent power, could amend by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision of the Constitution, that nothing in Article 13 applied to such an amendment, and that the President was obliged to give assent to an amendment bill duly passed. It was the first instrument of the 1971 amendment cycle that also produced the Twenty-Fifth Amendment, inserting Article 31C for specified Directive Principle legislation, and the Twenty-Sixth, abolishing the privy purses. The Supreme Court upheld the Twenty-Fourth Amendment in Kesavananda Bharati in April 1973 while holding that the power it conferred stopped at the basic structure of the Constitution, the limit that governed the Allahabad appeal's outcome two years later.

### Q: How did Raj Narain defeat Indira Gandhi at Rae Bareli in 1977?

Raj Narain defeated Indira Gandhi at Rae Bareli in the general election of March 1977, held after the Emergency was lifted, by a margin of 55,202 votes: he polled 177,719 votes as the Janata Party candidate against her 122,517. The constituency that had returned her in 1971 by 111,810 votes had experienced the Emergency as the rest of northern India had, through compulsory sterilisation drives, arrests under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act and the censorship Raj Narain's own detention had dramatised, and the petitioner of 1971 campaigned as the man whose case the government had answered by suspending the Constitution. His victory was one element of the Janata sweep that ended the Congress government formed in 1971, drove Indira Gandhi from Parliament altogether, and brought Morarji Desai to the prime ministership, with Raj Narain himself entering the cabinet as Minister for Health. The result closed the electoral arc that the Allahabad judgment had opened: the removal the courts had pronounced in 1975 and the Supreme Court had reversed was pronounced again, in the only forum the Thirty-Ninth Amendment had been unable to close, by the voters of Rae Bareli.

### Q: What was the Election Laws (Amendment) Act of 1975?

The Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 1975, Act 40 of 1975, was the statute Parliament enacted during the Emergency to rewrite, with retrospective effect, the provisions of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 under which Indira Gandhi had been convicted at Allahabad. It restructured the law on the assistance of government servants, narrowing the circumstances in which such assistance constituted a corrupt practice, and recast the treatment of expenditure incurred by political parties and supporters so that it could not be attributed to the candidate without the candidate's proved authorisation, again reaching back to cover the election of 1971. The Act's retrospectivity made it the operative instrument in the Supreme Court's judgment of 7 November 1975: the Constitution Bench upheld it as ordinary legislation within Parliament's competence, to which the basic-structure doctrine did not extend, and applied its definitions to hold that neither Allahabad finding survived. In the same judgment the bench struck down clause (4) of Article 329A, the constitutional provision enacted alongside the Act to annul the Allahabad judgment by name, as a violation of the basic structure.

### Q: Who were the five judges who decided the Supreme Court appeal?

The Supreme Court appeal in Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain was decided by a Constitution Bench of five judges, every one of them a survivor of the thirteen-judge bench that had decided Kesavananda Bharati in 1973. The bench consisted of Chief Justice A.N. Ray, who had been elevated over three senior colleagues two days after Kesavananda Bharati, and Justices H.R. Khanna, K.K. Mathew, M.H. Beg and Y.V. Chandrachud. Each judge delivered a separate opinion in the judgment of 7 November 1975. Chief Justice Ray struck down clause (4) of Article 329A as violating the rule of law; Justice Khanna rested on the norms of free and fair elections as part of the basic structure; Justice Mathew held that the clause destroyed the adjudicative resolution of election disputes; Justice Chandrachud found it violated the separation of powers and the equality guarantee of Article 14; Justice Beg upheld the election under the retrospectively amended statute without pronouncing on the amendment's validity. The bench heard argument for some thirty days between August and October 1975, with the appellant in office under a proclaimed Emergency and the respondent in detention.
