Among farm bill myths, the claim that corn subsidies caused obesity carried the widest consequences, because the claim moved a public health crisis into the jurisdiction of agricultural legislation and assigned a remedy that agricultural legislation could not deliver. The claim had a plausible chain, and plausibility at every link explains the reach the claim achieved. Corn received public support. Support, in the chain, made corn artificially cheap. Cheap corn made high fructose corn syrup artificially cheap in turn. Manufacturers facing a cheap sweetener sweetened drinks and processed foods generously, enlarged sweetened portions, and saturated the food supply with inexpensive calories. Shoppers absorbed the surplus calories, and obesity followed as the harvest of a subsidy. Each step sounded reasonable in isolation, and repetition across commentary, classrooms and advocacy turned a chain of separate conjectures into a sequence that read as settled causation. A claim repeated in that manner acquired the authority of a finding without passing through the tests a finding has to survive.

![Farm Bill Myths Examined: What the Record Supports - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-71.webp)

The consequences of error ran in both directions. A false account of obesity directed attention and reform energy toward a lever connected weakly, if at all, to calorie consumption, and away from causes with stronger evidence behind them. The same habit of repetition protected weaker claims on every other subject the farm bill touched. A share of spending quoted without a baseline became the identity of a statute. A payment total accumulated across two decades became a description of a single year. A support price that never operated in a market became a price shoppers supposedly paid. Against that background, Abigail M. Okrent and Julian M. Alston tested the obesity chain directly in the American Journal of Agricultural Economics in 2012 and found that eliminating farm subsidies entirely would have very limited impact on calorie consumption. A chain that survived repetition collapsed under measurement, and the contrast between repetition and measurement supplies the reason for the treatment applied to every claim in the sections that follow.

![A harvested corn field, grain bins and a farmhouse in the American Midwest - Insight Crunch]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The method is uniform across the ten claims. Each claim appears first in the strongest form holders of the claim would recognize, stated with the evidence holders themselves would cite and without the qualifications that belong to the testing stage. The record then answers the claim in that strong form. The record consists of named statutes, named datasets and named studies, each attached to a date and an issuing body: the Congressional Budget Office baseline of April 2018 and the projection issued at enactment of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, the Congressional Research Service accounts of enactment scores and lapse episodes, the Economic Research Service Food Dollar Series and farm household record, the Government Accountability Office food safety reports, the payment database compiled by the Environmental Working Group, an advocacy organization, from Department of Agriculture records obtained under the Freedom of Information Act, and the price research of Okrent and Alston and of Alston, Daniel A. Sumner and Stephen A. Vosti. Each examination closes with a verdict drawn from a fixed vocabulary: True, Partly true, False and Contested. A contested verdict marks a claim the record had not settled by March 15, 2021, and the sections on contested claims show the exact point at which the evidence thins.

Two checks recur so often that the checks deserve naming at the outset. The first check asks about the title. A farm bill figure has meaning only after a reader establishes which title of the bill the figure describes, because a total dominated by the nutrition title describes food assistance rather than farm payments, and a share calculated against the whole budget of the Department of Agriculture describes a different universe from a share calculated against the farm bill alone. The second check asks about the year. A share belongs to a baseline and a period, because nutrition outlays respond to economic conditions and commodity outlays respond to market prices, so a percentage drawn from the April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 cannot describe the enactment scores of 2008 or 2014, and a payment total accumulated from 1995 to 2016 cannot describe any single year inside that span. Most durable errors in farm bill argument dissolve after the two checks, because the errors survived on a dropped title or a dropped year rather than on a disputed figure.

The claims under examination cover the composition of the bill, the distribution of payments, the price of food, the machinery of expiration and inspection, and the meaning of the organic label. Two claims merge into the record only in part. One claim remains contested on the evidence assembled for the March 15, 2021 vantage. The remaining claims fail against statutes, baselines and studies that any reader can inspect, and the failures share a pattern that the closing section draws together.

## Farm Bill Myths About What the Bill Is

Two stories about the farm bill circulate in opposite directions and both claim to expose a disguise. One story, told most sharply by fiscal critics of the legislation, holds that the farm bill is not really about farming at all and that its true identity is a welfare bill carrying a rural label for political protection. The other story, told by critics who approach the legislation from concern about who receives public money, holds that the farm bill is a narrow transfer to farmers and that every other purpose attached to the legislation is decoration around that transfer. The two stories cannot both be complete descriptions of the same statute, because one treats food assistance as the real substance hidden behind farm programs while the other treats farm programs as the only substance that matters. Each story draws strength from a real feature of the legislation. The first draws strength from the arithmetic of projected spending, in which the nutrition title accounts for roughly three quarters of the total in the scores that framed the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018. The second draws strength from the name of the legislation, from the commodity and crop insurance titles at its historical core, and from the plain fact that programs for producers occupy a central place in every farm bill debate. A fair examination has to give each claim its strongest form before testing that form against the record, because a weak version of either claim is easy to defeat and proves nothing. The record used for the test is the record assembled for the farm bill as a whole, including the Congressional Budget Office baseline of April 2018, the Congressional Budget Office projection issued at enactment of the 2018 act, the Congressional Research Service account of how projected shares moved between enactments, and the twelve title structure that the 2018 act carried, all set out in [the farm bill's structure and history](/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/).

The claim that the farm bill is really a welfare bill deserves a precise statement before any qualification enters the discussion. In its strongest form, the claim runs as follows. A legislature that wished to enact a large and durable program of food assistance for low income households would face resistance if that program stood alone under its own name, while a legislature that attached the same assistance to legislation for farmers, conservation, and rural communities could protect the assistance behind a coalition that no single interest could assemble on its own. On that account, the farm label performs political work. The label gathers votes, softens scrutiny, and allows the largest share of the money to flow to a purpose that the title of the legislation never mentions. Fiscal critics who advance this claim point to the proportions rather than to any single program detail. If roughly three dollars in every four dollars of projected farm bill spending belong to the nutrition title, then the honest name for the legislation, on that view, follows the money rather than the tradition. The strongest form of the claim therefore accuses the structure itself of misdirection, because debate centered on commodity prices, crop insurance, and conservation addresses a minority of projected outlays while the largest cost receives less direct attention.

### Is most farm bill spending nutrition spending?

Yes, in the scores that framed the 2018 act. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline put nutrition at about 77 percent of projected outlays from fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2028, and the projection at enactment put nutrition at 76 percent over five years.

The April 2018 baseline supplied the background against which Congress wrote the 2018 act. The Congressional Budget Office built that baseline as an extension of the 2014 farm bill and projected total outlays of 867 billion dollars from fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2028. Within that total, the nutrition title accounted for 663.8 billion dollars, or about 77 percent. Crop insurance accounted for about 78.0 billion dollars, or about 9 percent. Commodity programs accounted for about 61.1 billion dollars, or about 7 percent, and conservation accounted for about 59.7 billion dollars, or about 7 percent. The other eight titles together accounted for about 1 percent of projected outlays. Read on its own terms, that distribution gives the welfare bill claim a firm factual footing. No other title approached the nutrition title in scale, and the three titles most closely associated with farming in public discussion, namely commodities, crop insurance, and conservation, together accounted for less than one third of the nutrition figure. The projection issued at enactment of the 2018 act told a closely similar story over a shorter window. The Congressional Budget Office projected 428 billion dollars in outlays from fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2023 under the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, signed on December 20, 2018. Nutrition accounted for 76 percent of that total, or 326 billion dollars, while the remainder fell mostly to crop insurance at 8.9 percent, commodities at 7.3 percent, and conservation at 6.8 percent. Two different scores placed the nutrition share within a single percentage point of each other, and any honest treatment has to concede the proportions before examining what the proportions establish and what the proportions leave out.

### Did Congress combine farm support and food assistance on purpose?

The combination was deliberate. The Food Stamp Act of 1964 placed food assistance under the Department of Agriculture and the agriculture committees, and the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973 made food stamp reauthorization part of a farm bill for the first time.

Deliberation about the combination matters because the welfare bill claim often implies that nutrition spending drifted into farm legislation by accident or by quiet maneuver. The legislative history on the fact record shows design rather than drift. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, placed food assistance under the United States Department of Agriculture and under the agriculture committees of Congress, so jurisdiction over food assistance and jurisdiction over farm programs rested in the same institutional hands from that point forward. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, then became the first farm bill to incorporate food stamp reauthorization, joining the two subjects inside a single legislative vehicle rather than merely inside a single department. The resulting arrangement acquired a name in legislative practice. The logroll paired urban votes for farm support with rural votes for food assistance, so each side of the coalition supplied votes that the other side needed and neither side could reliably command alone. Defenders of the arrangement described mutual dependence and broad geographic representation as strengths, on the ground that legislation supported by districts of every kind faced broader examination and rested on a wider base than legislation serving a single region. Fiscal critics described mutual insulation from scrutiny as the cost, on the ground that each side of the coalition had reason to protect the other side from hard questions in order to protect its own programs. Both characterizations rested on a shared premise even where the evaluations diverged. The combination was a chosen political architecture with foreseeable effects, and arguments about the bill have to reckon with that purpose rather than treating the join as a scandal discovered in a spreadsheet.

The qualification that limits the welfare bill claim concerns movement over time, because a share quoted without a date presents a snapshot as though a snapshot were a permanent property. The Congressional Research Service recorded a markedly different distribution at enactment of the 2008 act, when the nutrition title accounted for 67 percent of the ten year projected total, or 406 billion dollars of 604 billion dollars. At enactment of the 2014 act, the nutrition title accounted for 79 percent of the ten year projected total, or 756 billion dollars of 956 billion dollars. A claim built on a fixed identity for the legislation cannot easily absorb a swing of twelve percentage points between two enactments separated by six years. The Congressional Research Service account of that swing assigned the growth overwhelmingly to changed economic expectations after the recession rather than to legislative expansion of nutrition programs. The 2014 act itself scored an 8 billion dollar reduction in nutrition spending across ten years, while expected ten year nutrition outlays rose by about 350 billion dollars on economic expectations alone. Legislation that trimmed a program in its own score while projections for that program climbed on economic grounds sits awkwardly inside a story about legislators expanding welfare behind a farm label. The deeper reason for the movement lies in the structure of the spending itself. Nutrition outlays are countercyclical and participation driven, meaning that outlays rise in recessions as eligibility and participation rise, while commodity outlays are price driven, meaning that outlays rise when market prices fall. Each title responds to a different economic signal, so the share held by any one title at any one enactment reflects the economic forecast attached to that enactment as much as any legislative preference. A share is a snapshot with a year attached, and the year does real work in every honest use of the figures.

The remaining question under the first claim concerns the word welfare itself and what that word does and does not describe about the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. The program is the largest program in the nutrition title, renamed from food stamps by the 2008 act with the new name effective on October 1, 2008, and financed as open ended mandatory spending driven by eligibility and participation. The word welfare captures part of that structure accurately. The program delivers food assistance to households that meet eligibility rules, the federal government bears the cost as mandatory spending rather than as an annual discretionary choice, and outlays expand automatically when economic conditions push more households into eligibility. The word also fails to capture part of the structure. Food assistance for the purchase of food differs in design from unrestricted cash support, and the program operates through one title of a bill whose other titles govern commodities, insurance, conservation, credit, trade, research, forestry, energy, rural development, and horticulture. The word further fails as an account of scale over time, because the Congressional Research Service record showed projections moving on economic expectations even in an enactment that scored a reduction. The verdict is partly true. The claim is true that nutrition spending dominated projected farm bill outlays in the April 2018 baseline and in the projection at enactment of the 2018 act, true that Congress joined food assistance to farm legislation deliberately in 1964 and 1973, and true that the resulting coalition protected both subjects. The claim fails as a complete identity for the legislation, because the nutrition share moved substantially between enactments for economic reasons, because the farm and conservation titles carried real money and real policy, and because welfare describes the financing and purpose of one title without describing the statute as a whole.

The opposite claim, that the farm bill only helps farmers, also deserves its strongest form before the record answers it. In that form, the claim holds that farm bill money begins and ends with producers, that commodity payments, crop insurance support, and related programs transfer public funds to farm operations and to the businesses that serve those operations, and that titles carrying other names exist to broaden the coalition rather than to broaden the benefits. A holder of this claim can point to the historical center of the legislation. Farm bills grew out of the commodity programs of the nineteen thirties, eighteen farm bills were enacted from 1933 to 2018 according to the Congressional Research Service, and the recurring fights that defined each enactment concerned the terms on which producers received support. A holder of the claim can also point to the distribution of attention in public argument, where the farm bill appears in news coverage chiefly when commodity support or crop insurance is at stake. From those premises, the strongest form concludes that any benefit reaching a person outside farming arrives as a side effect, incidental to the design and too small to change the character of the legislation. That conclusion is the part the record tests. Side effects that Congress wrote into separate titles, funded through distinct programs, and directed at named classes of recipients outside farming are not accidents of drafting, and the twelve title structure of the 2018 act supplies the evidence title by title.

### Does farm bill money reach people who do not farm?

Yes. Nutrition assistance reached households in every district, conservation payments reached landowners who did not operate farms, and rural development money paid for water systems, broadband, and community facilities used by residents whose work had no connection to agricultural production at all.

The nutrition title alone defeats the word only in the second claim, and the scale of that defeat deserves plain statement. The same figures that gave the first claim its strength return here with the opposite implication. A title accounting for about 77 percent of projected outlays in the April 2018 baseline and 76 percent in the projection at enactment delivered assistance to households in every congressional district, in cities and towns as well as in farming counties, and the recipients of that assistance were households that qualified for food assistance rather than farm operations that qualified for commodity support. Whatever disputes surround the proper size of that title, a statute in which the largest component by a wide margin serves households as eaters rather than producers cannot accurately carry a description that limits its benefits to farmers. The conservation title reached a different class of recipients outside the farm operator category. Conservation programs paid landowners, including non operating owners who did not farm the land under contract, and funded easements and habitat protection whose benefits ran to water quality, soil retention, and wildlife rather than to the income of an operating farm alone. A landowner who rented ground to a tenant, lived away from that ground, and enrolled acres in a conservation practice received farm bill money through a title designed in part for that arrangement. The credit title reached toward the edges of the producer category rather than toward its established center, directing credit to beginning producers and underserved producers who lacked the operating history that private lenders usually required. Even within titles associated with farming, in other words, the recipient categories extended past the established farm operator that the second claim pictures as the sole beneficiary of the legislation.

### What do the smaller farm bill titles pay for?

The smaller titles paid for export promotion and food aid, farm credit for beginning and underserved producers, forestry assistance, rural energy grants through the Rural Energy for America Program, and research and extension work carried out through land grant universities across the country.

The rural development title carried benefits to rural residents as residents, without regard to occupation. Programs under that title funded water and waste systems that served whole communities, rural broadband service that connected households and businesses of every kind, community facilities that housed services used by the general public, and rural business programs whose participants included enterprises with no production role in agriculture. A rural resident who drank water from a system financed under the title, worked in a business assisted under the title, or used a community facility built with support from the title stood inside the reach of the farm bill while standing entirely outside farming. The research title funded the land grant universities and the extension system, an arrangement that carried agricultural research into public universities and carried extension education to audiences that included farmers alongside other residents who used extension services. Knowledge produced under that title entered public circulation through institutions chartered to serve whole states, so the benefits of the research title diffused well past the boundary of any individual farm. The forestry title funded national forest management and assistance for state and private forestry, reaching public lands visited and used by the general public and private forest owners whose holdings served timber, habitat, and recreation purposes. The energy title funded the Rural Energy for America Program, which served rural small businesses alongside agricultural producers, so a Main Street enterprise that improved energy efficiency with program support received farm bill assistance on the strength of a rural location and a small business classification rather than on the strength of any crop or livestock operation. The trade title funded export promotion, which served producers through wider markets, and also funded food aid, which served recipients abroad who stood at the farthest possible distance from the category of American farmer. Title by title, the pattern held across the statute. Each title named recipients, and in title after title the named recipients included people and institutions outside farming.

The breadth also had a geographic dimension that the word only cannot accommodate. Nutrition assistance operated in every district, so the largest title of the bill produced recipients in urban districts with no commercial farming, in suburban districts, and in rural districts alike. Research and extension funding flowed through land grant universities, institutions spread across whole states, so the audience for that title extended to residents who used extension services without operating any land. Forestry assistance reached state agencies and private forest owners as well as the national forests, spreading that title across public and private ground in regions where timber and habitat mattered more than row crops. Rural development projects attached to places rather than occupations, and a water system or broadband network served every household connected to that system regardless of employment. Trade programs completed the spread in the outward direction, since food aid reached recipients outside the United States entirely. A statute whose recipients included foreign food aid beneficiaries, residents on a municipal water system, and non operating landowners under conservation contracts had outgrown any single occupation as a description of its reach.

An honest treatment of the second claim has to state the qualification that gives the claim whatever residual force the claim retains, and that qualification concerns money rather than reach. The other eight titles of the 2018 act, the titles beyond nutrition, commodities, crop insurance, and conservation, together accounted for only about 1 percent of projected outlays in the April 2018 baseline. Rural development, research, forestry, energy, trade, credit, horticulture, and miscellaneous programs therefore reached recipients across the country while commanding a very small share of the fiscal weight of the legislation. That fact cuts in both directions and neither direction should be suppressed. Against the second claim, breadth of reach is a fact about design that a small budget share does not erase, because Congress wrote the titles, defined the recipients, and renewed the programs across successive enactments, and a program that serves nonfarm recipients by design does not become a farm program merely because its appropriation is modest. In favor of the concern underneath the second claim, a reader weighing where the money concentrated would find the fiscal center of the legislation in nutrition on one side and in commodity programs, crop insurance, and conservation on the other, with the smaller titles together amounting to a rounding figure beside those totals. The accurate summary holds both halves at once. The farm bill reached far beyond farmers as a matter of statutory design and actual recipients, and most of the money outside the nutrition title flowed through the three titles closest to agricultural production. A later section examines Barrett Kirwan's 2009 finding that about 25 cents of each subsidy dollar passed through to landowners in higher rental rates, a finding that complicates even the farm centered titles from within. The verdict is false. The claim that the farm bill only helps farmers fails on the largest title in the legislation, fails on conservation payments to non operating landowners, fails on rural development benefits delivered to residents as residents, fails on research and extension carried through public universities, and fails on trade, forestry, energy, and credit titles whose recipient definitions extended beyond farm operators, even though the smaller titles carried only about 1 percent of projected outlays and the fiscal weight of the legislation rested elsewhere.

## Who Actually Receives the Money

Distribution is the point at which argument over the farm bill often stops describing the same thing. One side points to payment records and sees a small group of recipients collecting very large sums across long periods. Another side points to a census of farms and sees a sector made up almost entirely of family operations, most of them small by the standards of the Economic Research Service. Both pictures come from real records, and both become misleading when a count of farms is treated as a count of dollars, or when a record of payments to an operation is treated as a record of income in a household. Commodity payments follow production and acreage. Crop insurance support follows the purchase of policies, with the government paying a large share of the premium. Part of the value of a payment then moves a second time, through rents and land values, from the operator who received it toward the owner of the land. Any claim about who receives the money has to name which of those positions it describes, over which period, and in which unit, before the figures can carry the conclusion placed on them.

The two claims examined in this section pull in opposite directions, and each rests on a fragment of the record that is accurate on its own terms. The first claim holds that farmers get rich from the farm bill. The second holds that small farms receive most of the help. The first takes the concentration at the top of the payment distribution and reads it as a description of farm households in general. The second takes the numerical dominance of small family farms and reads it as a description of the payment distribution. The record supports neither reading in full. Each claim fails in the opposite direction from the other, and each borrows credibility from a statistic that answers a different question.

**The first claim: farmers get rich from the farm bill.** Stated in its strongest form, the claim rests on the sheer scale of the sums recorded at the top of the distribution. The Environmental Working Group, an advocacy organization working from United States Department of Agriculture payment records obtained under the Freedom of Information Act, compiled a database covering the years from 1995 to 2016 and found that the top tenth of recipients received 77 percent of the recorded subsidies across that entire period. For commodity payments alone, across the years from 1995 to 2010, the concentration was nearly as sharp: 76 percent of the money went to the top tenth of recipients, and the average amount recorded for a recipient in that top group was $447,873. A distribution in which a tenth of the names collect more than three quarters of the money, with average receipts for that group approaching half a million dollars across the period measured, gives the enrichment claim a factual core that cannot be dismissed as invention. The pattern was not confined to a single unusual year. In 2010 alone, the top tenth of recipients received 63 percent of commodity payments, a lower share than the long-period figure and a high one by any ordinary standard of distribution.

The production explanation travels with those figures, and the strongest honest statement of the claim has to carry it as well. Payments follow production and acreage, and production is itself concentrated among a minority of operations, so a concentration of payments partly mirrors a concentration of output. A large operation planting many acres and selling a large volume of a program crop generates a larger payment than a small operation on the same terms, without any difference in the rate of support per unit. That mechanical relationship explains part of the pattern before any judgment about fairness begins. The disagreement over the figures is interpretive rather than numerical, and the contested question is what those shares prove about enrichment. A payment record shows money delivered to a recipient under program rules. It does not, on its own, show profit, because the record carries no account of the costs of production, the debts carried by the operation, or the division of the payment among the persons and owners connected to it. The long-period figures also aggregate across years, so the average of $447,873 describes receipts accumulated across the years from 1995 to 2010 rather than income in a single year. None of those qualifications erases the concentration. Each one narrows what the concentration establishes, from a verdict about farmers as a class toward a finding about a specific slice of recipients.

### Do farm payments make the typical farm household rich?

No for the typical household, and yes for a narrow slice at the top. Payment records compiled by the Environmental Working Group showed sharp concentration, yet Economic Research Service figures for 2019 placed median farm income at $296, with off-farm earnings supplying most household income.

The distinction between a farm operation and a farm household does most of the work in cutting the claim down to size. A payment record and a household income record measure different units. The recipient in a payment database is an operation or a person receiving program money in connection with production. A farm household, in the measurement of the Economic Research Service, is the household of the farm operator, and its total income combines whatever the farm itself earned with earnings from every other source available to that household. Judged by the household measure, farm households in 2019 were not poor. The median total household income for all family farms was $83,111, against a median of $68,703 for all United States households, and the Economic Research Service conclusion, stated at its own strength, holds that farm households in general are neither low-income nor low-wealth, with median farm household income ahead of the all-household median in every year from the mid-1990s onward. Read in isolation, that comparison appears to rescue the enrichment claim, since the typical farm household stood above the national median. The same source, however, assigns the margin to a specific origin. The advantage came from off-farm earnings rather than from farm program payments.

At the median, the farm itself contributed almost nothing. Median farm income for family farm households in 2019 was $296, a figure so close to zero that the typical household, ordered at the middle of the distribution, earned essentially nothing from farming itself in that year. The record around the median points in the same direction: slightly more than half of farm households recorded negative farm income in the years covered by the Economic Research Service reporting, meaning that the farming activity, taken alone, lost money for the majority of households engaged in it. Program payments enter farm income, and farm income at the median stood at $296 with a majority of households in negative territory, so whatever the payment system delivered to the typical household did not lift the farm enterprise itself into comfortable profit. The enrichment visible at the top of the payment distribution and the near-zero result at the middle of the farm income distribution are both features of the same record, and a claim that describes farmers as a group has to answer for the middle as well as the top.

Off-farm income completes the household picture and explains the apparent contradiction between an above-median household income and a negligible farm income. Off-farm income supplied an average of 82 percent of total household income for family farms in 2019, and 96 percent of farm households received some off-farm income in that year. The typical farm household, in other words, functioned largely as a wage-earning or salary-earning household attached to a farm operation, rather than as a household living on the proceeds of farming and program payments. That composition matters for the claim under examination in two ways. First, it severs the inference from household comfort to program enrichment: a household can stand above the national median because of employment away from the farm, and the household income figure alone gives no reason to credit the farm bill for that standing. Second, it reframes what a payment means at the middle of the distribution. For a household whose farm income hovered near zero or ran negative, a program payment operated inside a marginal enterprise, offsetting losses or financing continued operation, rather than arriving as surplus wealth on top of a profitable business. The payment may have been decisive for the survival of the operation without making the household rich.

A further share of the money leaves the operator altogether through a channel that payment records do not display: capitalization into land rents and land values. Barrett Kirwan, writing in the Journal of Political Economy in 2009, estimated that about 25 cents of each subsidy dollar passes through to landowners in higher rental rates, while tenant operators keep about 75 cents. A 2003 estimate by the Payment Limitations Commission put the effect of subsidies on land values in a range from 15 percent to 25 percent, a separate measure pointing toward the same transfer. The size of that channel depends on how much farmland is farmed by someone other than its owner, and the tenure record shows that the rented share is large. The United States Department of Agriculture TOTAL survey of 2014 found that about 39 percent of farmland acres in the contiguous states were rented, and about 80 percent of those rented acres were owned by non-operator landlords, owners who did not farm the land themselves. On rented ground, then, the enrichment produced by a payment divides between the operator who received it and the landlord who captures a portion of it through rent, and where the landlord is a non-operator, that portion leaves agriculture as an occupation entirely and accrues to landownership as an investment position, a transfer traced in full in [the farm subsidy policy impact analysis](/2021/01/15/farm-subsidy-policy-impact/). The claim that farmers get rich therefore misassigns part of the gain even in cases where a payment is large, because a slice of the largest payments is bid away into the rental market and lands with owners whose connection to farming is a deed rather than a working life on the land.

The verdict is partly true. The claim holds for a thin top slice of recipients, the tenth of names that collected 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the years from 1995 to 2016 and averaged $447,873 per recipient in commodity payments across the years from 1995 to 2010, and it holds in a second, quieter way for landowners who captured roughly a quarter of each subsidy dollar through rents under the Kirwan estimate. As a description of the typical farm household, the claim fails against the household record: median farm income of $296 in 2019, negative farm income for slightly more than half of farm households in the years covered by the Economic Research Service reporting, and an average of 82 percent of household income arriving from off the farm. Farmers as a class did not get rich from the farm bill on that record. A concentrated minority of recipients received very large cumulative sums, landowners absorbed a material share of the value, and the median household earned its living mostly somewhere else.

**The second claim: small farms receive most of the help.** Stated in its strongest form, the claim rests on the composition of American agriculture itself, and the composition figures are genuinely striking. Family farms made up 98 percent of all farms and accounted for 88 percent of production in the Economic Research Service typology built on 2018 data, so the sector that farm programs support is, by count, a sector of families rather than of outside corporations. Within that family sector, small operations dominate the count by a wide margin. Small family farms, defined in the typology by gross cash farm income under $350,000, made up 90 percent of all farms. Those small family farms operated almost half of the farmland and generated 21 percent of the value of production. A claim built on those numbers has a real foundation: an industry in which nine of every ten farms are small family operations, working close to half the land in the country, invites the conclusion that a program serving the industry serves mainly the small. The imagery attached to the farm bill across decades of enactment draws on exactly that foundation, presenting the legislation as a compact with the family farm. If help followed the count of farms, the claim would be true by arithmetic, and the strongest form of the claim deserves to be stated in those terms before the payment record is brought against it.

The flaw in the claim sits in the step from counting farms to counting dollars, because the two counts run in opposite directions in the typology itself. Small family farms were 90 percent of farms and operated almost half the farmland, yet they generated only 21 percent of the value of production. Large-scale family farms, a minority within the same family farm population, supplied 46 percent of the value of production, the largest share of any group in the typology. Production, not headcount, is the variable that program payments track: payments follow production and acreage, and production is concentrated in the large-scale minority. A payment formula that pays in proportion to what an operation produces and the acres it plants will distribute its money in proportion to output, and output in the 2018 typology belonged disproportionately to the largest farms. The family character of the recipients does not soften that distribution, because the large-scale operations drawing the largest payments are themselves family farms under the typology. The 98 percent family farm figure and the concentration of payments are fully compatible, and the compatibility is the point that the claim obscures. Family describes ownership. It does not describe size, and it does not describe the share of money an operation receives. A defense of the programs that answers a question about size with a statistic about ownership has changed the subject, whether or not the change was intended.

### Do small farms receive most farm bill payments?

No. Small family farms were 90 percent of farms in the Economic Research Service typology for 2018, operated almost half the farmland, and produced 21 percent of output value. Because payments followed production and acreage, the minority producing the largest share drew the largest payments.

The recipient record confirms what the production shares predict. The database compiled by the Environmental Working Group, an advocacy organization working from United States Department of Agriculture payment records obtained under the Freedom of Information Act, found that the top tenth of recipients received 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the years from 1995 to 2016. From the recipient side, the same concentration appears in the commodity programs taken alone: across the years from 1995 to 2010, the top tenth of recipients took 76 percent of commodity payments, an average of $447,873 per recipient in that group, and in 2010 alone the top tenth received 63 percent. Those figures leave limited room for the claim that most of the help reached small farms. If the top tenth of recipients collected roughly three quarters of the money across a period of more than two decades, then the remaining nine tenths of recipients, a population that includes every small farm receiving a payment along with the midsize operations between the small and the large, divided the remaining quarter among them. Small farms received payments, and for individual small operations a payment could carry real weight in a thin-margin year. The claim under examination, however, asserts a majority share, and the recipient record assigns the majority share to the opposite end of the distribution. The concentration partly mirrors the concentration of production, as the production explanation attached to the figures acknowledges, and that acknowledgment explains the mechanism without altering the destination of the money. A mirror of concentrated production remains a concentrated distribution of help.

Land tenure adds a further distance between the small farm population and the largest payments, through the same capitalization channel that qualifies the enrichment claim from the other direction. About 39 percent of farmland acres in the contiguous states were rented, according to the United States Department of Agriculture TOTAL survey of 2014, and about 80 percent of rented acres belonged to non-operator landlords. Barrett Kirwan estimated in the Journal of Political Economy in 2009 that about 25 cents of each subsidy dollar passes to landowners through higher rental rates. Small operators farming rented ground therefore surrender a portion of any payment to owners who, in the large majority of rented acres, do not operate a farm of any size. That transfer does not appear in a count of farms and does not appear in a count of recipients, since the initial recipient remains the operator named in the payment record. It appears only in the economic destination of the money, which is the ground on which the claim asserts that small farms receive most of the help. Measured by destination rather than by the name on the payment, the small farm share shrinks below the share the payment records assign to it, because a quarter of the value, on the Kirwan estimate, moves upward to landowners as a class, a class defined by ownership rather than by farm size.

The structure of crop insurance support then compounds the production-based concentration with a legal asymmetry that has no counterpart in the commodity programs, and the asymmetry removes the ceiling that might otherwise have limited the flow of support to the largest operations. Crop insurance premium subsidies carry no per-person payment limit and no adjusted gross income test. The government pays an average of about 62 percent of the premium for an insured operation, and that share is paid on the policy the operation purchases, at whatever scale the operation farms. Inside the same farm bills, the commodity programs operate under explicit caps: a combined per-person limit of $125,000 applies to Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage payments under the 2018 act, and an adjusted gross income limit of $900,000 restricts eligibility for those payments, a contrast set out in [the federal crop insurance law](/2021/01/01/federal-crop-insurance-law/). No equivalent pair of limits disciplines the insurance title. An operation too large to collect more than the capped amount under the commodity programs faces no cap at all on the premium support attached to its insurance coverage, and an operation whose income places it beyond the commodity income limit faces no income test on that support either. Conservation compliance was reattached to premium subsidies by section 2611 of the Agricultural Act of 2014, so the insurance support carries environmental conditions on the land, but a compliance condition is not a payment limit and not an income test, and it does nothing to redistribute support toward smaller operations. The practical result is a two-tier architecture within a single statute: the title with fixed per-person limits serves all sizes under a ceiling, while the title without limits scales its support with the size of the insured operation, so the largest farms draw uncapped support through the insurance title in proportion to the scale that already concentrates the capped payments at the top.

The verdict is false. Small family farms were 90 percent of farms in the 2018 typology, operated almost half the farmland, and produced 21 percent of the value of production, while the large-scale minority produced 46 percent and the payment system, which follows production and acreage, directed 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the years from 1995 to 2016 to the top tenth of recipients. The family farm figures that give the claim its strength describe ownership and headcount, not the destination of the money, and the crop insurance title removes even the per-person ceiling and the income test that the commodity title imposes, leaving the largest operations with an uncapped channel of support. Small farms received help, and the record does not support any suggestion that they received none. The claim asserts that small farms received most of the help, and on the production shares, the recipient record, and the limit structure of the insurance title, most of the help went elsewhere.

## What Farm Support Does to the Price of Food

Two of the most durable arguments about farm bills concern the price a shopper pays, and the two arguments point in opposite directions. Defenders of commodity support present that support as a quiet protection for household budgets, a public investment that holds down the cost of feeding a family. Opponents of commodity support present the same payments as a hidden engine of harm, a public investment that pushed sweeteners and calories into the food supply at artificial prices and produced a public health crisis as a side effect. The claims cannot both carry the weight assigned to them. If farm support controlled retail prices strongly enough to reshape the national diet through cheap sweeteners, farm support would also have to control retail prices strongly enough to deliver the savings promised in the first claim. If farm support reaches the checkout weakly and unevenly, as the record assembled in this section indicates, both claims fail for related reasons. The paired treatment that follows gives each claim a full hearing on the same evidence, principally the structure of the food dollar, the design of the payments themselves, and two bodies of price research that tested the sweetener mechanism directly.

### Do Farm Subsidies Keep Grocery Prices Low?

No. The farm sector received 14.6 cents of each retail food dollar in 2017, according to the Economic Research Service, and the marketing system received the remaining 85.4 cents. A policy acting on the smaller share cannot govern the whole price, and parts of the same legislation raise particular prices rather than lowering them.

The strongest form of the defenders' claim deserves a clear statement, because the claim circulates in a weaker and vaguer form that no evidence could settle. In the strongest form, the claim runs as follows. Agriculture operates under weather risk, price risk and thin margins that few other industries face at the same scale. Without public support, production would contract, supply would tighten, and the contraction would reach the retail shelf as higher prices for bread, milk, meat, cereal and every other staple built on farm output. Support payments, crop insurance and related programs hold production steady through bad years, keep supply abundant through good years, and thereby spare shoppers the price spikes and shortages that an unsupported farm sector would impose. On this account, grocery prices in the United States are low in meaningful part because farm bills paid for stability, and a shopper who never reads a farm bill benefits from one at every checkout.

The first difficulty for the claim is arithmetic, and the arithmetic comes from the Economic Research Service Food Dollar Series, released in 2019. Of each dollar spent on food at retail in 2017, 14.6 cents reached the farm sector, and 85.4 cents went to the marketing system that stands between the farm gate and the retail shelf. The division matters more than any single program detail, because the division sets an outer limit on what a farm level policy can do to a retail price. A subsidy operates on production, on farm income, on land values, on risk, and on the price of a raw commodity at the first point of sale. A retail price contains all of that, plus the much larger block of costs and margins accumulated after the commodity leaves the farm. Even a farm policy powerful enough to change the farm level price by a large percentage changes only the value embedded in the 14.6 cent share, and the percentage change at the shelf shrinks in proportion. The leverage runs the wrong way for the claim. Farm support would need an implausibly large effect at the farm gate to produce even a moderate effect at the checkout, and the design of the programs, addressed below, does not supply an effect of that size.

The average also flatters the claim, because the average blends lightly processed foods, where the farm share stands higher, with the highly processed foods that fill much of a grocery cart. The fact sheet record states the point plainly: for highly processed foods the farm share is far smaller than the average. A boxed, sweetened, extruded, packaged product carries the cost of manufacturing, packaging, branding, distribution and retail handling layered over a small quantity of farm input, and the farm input itself may be a minor ingredient by value among several. For such products the ceiling on farm policy influence falls well below the 14.6 cent average. The claim therefore encounters a distribution problem as well as an average problem. Where shoppers spend much of the food budget, on processed and prepared items, the farm share is smallest and the room for a subsidy to move the price is narrowest. Where the farm share is largest, in the least processed foods, prices respond to supply and demand in commodity markets shaped by weather, energy costs, exchange conditions and export demand, forces that a domestic support payment touches only in part.

The second difficulty is program design. Most commodity support in the modern farm bills does not operate as a per unit price cut attached to food as food moves toward the consumer, and nothing in the design hands a shopper a discount at the register. A substantial part of support arrives as payments substantially decoupled from the production of a specific crop in a specific year, a feature the obesity literature also emphasizes when tracing price effects. A payment decoupled from current output supports farm household income and land values without instructing the recipient to produce more of any one commodity, and a payment that does not instruct additional production cannot be expected to flood a market and depress a price by force of volume. Another large part of support arrives as crop insurance, a risk management structure under which the government pays an average of about 62 percent of premiums, according to the Congressional Research Service. A premium subsidy lowers the cost of insuring a crop against loss. Insurance stabilizes a farm operation across a bad harvest and protects lenders and landlords as well as operators. Stabilization of an operation is a real effect, and a farm sector protected against ruinous loss may plan with more confidence, but the distance between a subsidized insurance premium and a lower price for a loaf of bread runs through several markets, each with separate pricing, and no step in the chain converts the premium support into a designated reduction in the retail price of any food item.

Capitalization supplies a further leak between support and shelf prices. Barrett Kirwan, writing in the Journal of Political Economy in 2009, estimated that about 25 cents of each subsidy dollar passes through to landowners in higher rental rates, while tenant operators keep about 75 cents. A Payment Limitations Commission estimate in 2003 put the effect of payments on land values in a range from 15 percent to 25 percent, attributed to that commission. Tenure figures from the TOTAL survey conducted by the Department of Agriculture in 2014 give the leak a scale: about 39 percent of farmland acres in the contiguous states were rented, and about 80 percent of rented acres were owned by non-operator landlords. A dollar routed into higher rent for a landlord who does not operate a farm has completed a journey into an asset price, not into a food price. Support capitalized into land also raises the cost structure confronting the next operator who rents or buys that land, which again points away from any simple story of cheaper food produced by larger payments.

The third difficulty is that the same farm bills contain provisions that push selected prices upward, and a claim that farm support keeps grocery prices low cannot survive by counting only the provisions that might point downward. Land retirement supplies one example. The Conservation Reserve Program, created by the Food Security Act of 1985, pays annual rental payments under contracts of ten to fifteen years to retire highly erodible and environmentally sensitive cropland to conserving cover. Enrollment peaked at 36.8 million acres in 2007, according to the Congressional Research Service, and the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 set a cap of 24 million acres for fiscal 2019 rising to 27 million acres by fiscal 2023. Retired acres do not produce crops. A program that pays to withdraw land from production, whatever the environmental justification, exerts upward pressure on the price of the output that the withdrawn land would otherwise have supplied, other conditions equal. Commodity support and land retirement sit inside the same legislative tradition, often inside the same enacted bill, and the net price effect of the whole package is therefore mixed by construction rather than uniformly downward. The sugar program, treated fully under the second claim below, supplies the sharpest example of an upward pushing provision, because the sugar program operates by holding a price above the level an open market would set. A defense of farm bills as a grocery price policy would have to net the downward possibilities against the upward certainties, and the record offers no basis for a net figure favorable to the claim.

A defender might retreat to a narrower position and argue that support prevents catastrophic price rises even if support does not measurably lower ordinary prices. The narrower position concedes the substance of the verdict, because a claim about insurance against an imagined catastrophe differs from the claim under examination. The claim under examination assigns farm support a standing role in keeping grocery prices low, an everyday benefit delivered to every shopper. The food dollar evidence caps that role at a small fraction of the price. The program design evidence weakens the role further, since decoupled payments and premium subsidies lack a direct transmission path to retail prices. The land retirement and sugar evidence reverses the sign for parts of the package. Each layer removes a portion of the effect the claim requires, and the layers compound.

Considered as a counterfactual, the claim also predicts a visible grocery price rise following any major reduction in support, scaled to the benefit shoppers supposedly receive from support in place. That prediction inherits the same ceiling and meets the same buffers in reverse. Production adjusts through land prices, rental rates, farm household earnings from off farm sources and the structure of farm operations before adjustment reaches the shelf, and each adjustment absorbs part of the change inside the farm sector rather than passing the change intact to a retail price. The marketing share, the decoupled design and the capitalization of payments into land values each act as a buffer, and buffers arranged in series leave little motion at the final price in either direction.

The verdict is false. Farm support may stabilize farm income, may protect an operation against a disastrous year, and may serve other purposes that other sections of this article weigh on separate evidence, but the structure of food prices leaves farm support with too small a share to govern, too indirect a mechanism to deliver savings reliably, and too many countervailing provisions inside the same bills to sustain the claim that subsidies keep grocery prices low.

### Did Corn Subsidies Cause Obesity by Making Sweeteners Cheap?

No. Abigail M. Okrent and Julian M. Alston reported in the American Journal of Agricultural Economics in 2012 that eliminating farm subsidies entirely would have very limited impact on calorie consumption, and Alston, Daniel A. Sumner and Stephen A. Vosti reached a net estimate of approximately zero in 2008.

The strongest form of the opposing claim is familiar enough to state in the terms its holders use. Corn receives large public support. Supported corn becomes artificially cheap. Cheap corn makes high fructose corn syrup, a sweetener manufactured from corn, artificially cheap in turn. Food and beverage manufacturers, facing a sweetener priced below its genuine cost, sweeten products generously, enlarge portions of sweetened drinks, and build a food supply saturated with inexpensive sugar calories. Shoppers absorb the surplus calories, and obesity follows as the predictable harvest of a subsidy. In the strongest form, the claim assigns causation, not mere association. Remove the subsidy, the argument holds, and sweeteners would cost more, sweetened products would cost more, consumption would fall, and the obesity epidemic would lose one of its principal drivers.

The price mechanism research tested exactly that chain, and the chain failed at the testing. Okrent and Alston, in the American Journal of Agricultural Economics in 2012, modeled the elimination of farm subsidies entirely, a counterfactual far more sweeping than any reform proposal needed to supply. The modeled elimination reached beyond direct subsidies on grains to include the indirect subsidies created by trade barriers on dairy, sugar, and fruit and vegetable commodities, so the test stripped away both the payments that might lower a price and the border protections that reshape one. The finding was that eliminating farm subsidies entirely would have very limited impact on calorie consumption and hence on obesity. The breadth of the counterfactual gives the finding its weight. A test that removes the whole structure, direct and indirect support together, leaves no remaining support to preserve the effect. If the entire apparatus disappeared and calorie consumption barely moved, then the apparatus cannot have been a principal cause of the calorie consumption that produced obesity, because a principal cause does not vanish without consequence.

Alston, Sumner and Vosti, writing in Agricultural and Resource Economics Update at the University of California, Davis, in 2008, reached a compatible conclusion by a related route. Farm subsidies, in that assessment, have had only very modest and mixed effects on the availability and prices of farm commodities. Mixed is the operative word alongside modest. The policies do not all push in one direction, so summing them does not produce a large push toward cheap calories. The net effect on consumption was approximately zero, and the true effect could run in either direction. An effect centered near zero cannot explain a large and sustained rise in obesity. The 2008 assessment also identified a reason the sum lands near zero rather than at a large negative figure for prices: some policies, notably import restrictions on sugar and dairy, raise food prices and so discourage consumption of the sweetened and calorie dense products those policies touch. The farm bill, in other words, contains a price raising apparatus alongside any price lowering tendency, and the two apparatuses work against each other inside the same statute.

Between the counterfactual at one end and the obesity outcome at the other end stand three transmission steps, and the literature locates weakness at every step. The first step runs from subsidy to commodity price. Subsidies move commodity prices only modestly, because payments are substantially decoupled from current production and production responses are small. A payment that does not depend tightly on planting more corn in a given year gives a grower little reason to plant more corn in that year, and a national supply that responds weakly produces a national price that moves weakly. The first link in the popular chain therefore transmits only a fraction of the force assigned to the link. The second step runs from commodity price to retail price. The farm commodity forms a small share of the retail price of a processed food, and sweetened beverages sit near the bottom of that distribution. The food dollar figures given under the first claim supply the scale: an average farm share of 14.6 cents in the dollar in 2017, with far smaller shares for highly processed products. A corn price movement, itself modest after the first step, applies only to the corn derived fraction of a beverage price dominated by processing, sweetening manufacture, bottling, transport, marketing and retail margin. The third step runs from retail price to consumption. Consumer demand responds weakly to small price changes, so the fractions of a cent surviving the first two steps meet a shopper whose purchasing barely registers them. Weakness compounds across steps. A modest effect, applied to a small share, met by a weak response, produces a final effect near zero, which matches the modeled results rather than contradicting them.

A documented episode illustrates the second and third steps together. When corn prices doubled in an earlier episode recorded by Alston, retail breakfast cereal prices barely moved. A doubling is not a modest movement. A doubling represents the kind of commodity price shock that, under the popular chain, ought to have surged through the cereal box to the shelf and from the shelf to the shopper. The shelf price instead absorbed the shock with little visible motion, because the corn inside a box of cereal accounts for so little of the price of the box. The episode functions as a natural check on the model results. Theory predicts small retail effects from commodity movements for processed foods, the models compute small calorie effects from removing subsidies, and the observed response of cereal prices to a corn price doubling displays small effects in the market itself. Three distinct approaches converge on the same scale of effect, and the scale is too small to carry the claim.

The sugar program then breaks the mechanism outright, because the one sweetener policy the farm bill operates directly runs in the opposite direction from the claim. The sugar program supports price through nonrecourse loans at 18.75 cents per pound for raw cane sugar, a rate set by the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 for the 2011 crop onward and retained by the Agricultural Act of 2014 and the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018. Around the loan rate stand tariff rate quotas limiting low duty imports, marketing allotments limiting domestic sales, and the Feedstock Flexibility Program, which diverts surplus sugar to ethanol production to prevent loan forfeitures. The design target is no net cost to the Treasury, and the target is achieved by holding the domestic price up rather than by paying the difference from public funds. The Farm Service Agency states that the United States price of sugar is usually considerably above the world price. The Government Accountability Office estimated in 2000 that the program cost consumers about 1.9 billion dollars a year in higher prices. A program that charges shoppers at the shelf to keep a sweetener expensive contradicts any portrait of the farm bill as a machine for cheap sweetness.

Substitution completes the reversal. Sweeteners substitute for one another in manufacturing, so a policy holding the price of sugar up also supports the price of corn sweeteners rather than undercutting that price. A beverage manufacturer choosing between sugar and high fructose corn syrup compares the two prices, and a floor under the sugar price removes the pressure that a collapsing sugar price would place on the corn product. The farm bill therefore props up the price of the very sweetener the claim blames the farm bill for cheapening, through the farm bill provision that most directly governs a sweetener. Whatever downward pressure grain support might theoretically place on corn sweetener prices must first overcome an upward pressure created by the sugar program inside the same legislation, and the modeled net results near zero reflect exactly that internal opposition. The claim requires a statute working steadily in one direction. The statute works in two directions at once, with the more direct sweetener instrument pointing against the claim.

Fair treatment requires a residue, and a genuine residue exists. Cheap calories in the United States food supply have many causes, among them processing technology, marketing practice and retail structure, and assigning the farm bill a near zero share of the explanation does not pronounce the resulting diet healthy or the commercial environment benign. The literature makes a point about attribution, not a point about nutrition. Obesity researchers associated with the food environment argument attribute rising obesity principally to the surroundings in which eating decisions occur, including portion norms, product placement and constant availability, rather than to any single input price set by agricultural policy. That attribution dispute belongs in the record because the verdict concerns a specific causal story, the subsidy to sweetener to obesity chain, and other causal stories stand or fall on other evidence. The verdict removes one cause from the list because the price evidence removes that cause, and the removal directs attention toward causes with evidence behind them.

The verdict is false. The claim fails at the subsidy step, where payments move commodity prices only modestly; at the shelf step, where the commodity forms a small share of a processed price, as the cereal episode demonstrated when a corn price doubling barely moved retail prices; at the consumption step, where demand responds weakly; and at the statute itself, where the sugar program raises sweetener prices and, through substitution, supports corn sweetener prices as well. Okrent and Alston found very limited calorie effects from eliminating farm subsidies entirely, and Alston, Sumner and Vosti found a net effect of approximately zero that could run in either direction. A mechanism contradicted by the models, by the market episode and by the direction of the governing sweetener program did not cause the obesity epidemic.

## What Happens When the Bill Expires, and Who Inspects the Food

Two machinery claims recur because each offers a simple picture of a complicated structure. One treats expiration as a shutdown, as if a date emptied the statute book. The other treats food inspection as a single chain of command. Each picture fails for a different reason. Expiration changes which law governs, and for commodities the change runs backward to older law rather than into a void. Inspection was never gathered into one pair of hands.

The expiration claim, in its strongest form, holds that everything stops when the farm bill expires. Farm programs end. Benefit and support payments cease. The Department of Agriculture closes the doors on the activities that the expired act authorized, and the legal landscape remains empty until Congress enacts a replacement. In that telling, expiration and repeal amount to the same event, and the deadline acquires its force from the prospect of nothing beyond it. The story gains plausibility from the scale of a farm bill, which reaches commodity support, nutrition assistance, crop insurance, conservation, credit, research, forestry, energy, trade, rural development and horticulture. If one act appears to contain the whole structure, the lapse of that act appears to remove the whole structure.

### What happens to farm programs when a farm bill expires?

Expiration does not end every program. Commodity support faces revival of permanent law from 1938 and 1949, with parity formulas tied to prices from 1910 to 1914. Nutrition assistance under the Food and Nutrition Act and crop insurance under the Federal Crop Insurance Act rest on permanent authorizations and do not stop.

The record begins with a distinction between a temporary suspension and a repeal. Permanent law consists chiefly of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949, the latter enacted as Public Law 81-439 on October 31, 1949. A modern farm bill does not erase that older law. Instead, the modern act suspends permanent law for a coverage period, and the support arrangements in the newer act operate while the suspension holds. When the coverage period ends and no successor or extension has taken its place, the suspension ends with it, and the permanent law revives. The consequence is not an absence of law. The consequence is the return of law that Congress had set aside for a defined period.

That distinction alters the whole character of an expiration deadline. A void would leave administrators without governing formulas and leave Congress free to legislate against a blank field. Revival does the opposite. Revival supplies formulas, and the formulas belong to an earlier agricultural economy. Permanent law rests on parity, with support benchmarked to the base period from 1910 to 1914. Parity relates farm prices to a historical relationship between prices farmers received and prices farmers paid, rather than to market conditions in the year of revival. A benchmark anchored in that base period therefore produces support levels detached from prevailing prices. In the dairy episode examined below, the Congressional Research Service placed prospective support near $40 per hundredweight against an average all milk price near $20 in 2013, roughly double the market level. The political pressure created by expiration flows from that mismatch. Congress faces a revived system whose terms could impose very high support prices and very large public costs if the system operated.

Commodity programs form the exposed part of the structure, and exposure takes the form of revival. The commodity title suspends the permanent law formulas for the crops and products within its coverage, substituting the mechanisms enacted for that period. Once the suspension lapses, the older formulas return in law. That return explains why deadlines concentrate so much attention on commodities. The risk is not that support disappears. The risk is that an old and expensive form of support replaces the form Congress had chosen, on terms benchmarked to the years from 1910 to 1914. Failure to extend or replace the suspension does not produce neutrality. Failure produces the permanent law.

Nutrition assistance follows a different legal path, and that path defeats the cliff story on its largest spending component. SNAP rests on the Food and Nutrition Act, a permanent authorization funded through appropriations. Its authority does not derive from a temporary suspension of an older commodity statute, and its existence does not end when a farm bill reaches the end of a coverage period. A farm bill may amend nutrition law and set terms on which later appropriations operate, but expiration of the farm bill does not stop nutrition assistance. Nutrition also dominates the spending profile of a modern farm bill, and that largest share rests on a permanent statute.

Crop insurance supplies a second, independent defeat for the claim. Crop insurance rests on the Federal Crop Insurance Act, also a permanent authorization. A farm bill may revise premium support, delivery and program terms, and the 2014 act reattached conservation compliance to premium subsidies by section 2611, but the underlying framework does not depend for its existence on the unexpired life of a particular farm bill. When a farm bill expires, crop insurance does not stop. Nutrition assistance and crop insurance thus stand outside the revival mechanism that governs commodity support. Each rests on a permanent act, and each continues through a lapse that revives permanent law elsewhere. The cliff story requires a single legal foundation under programs that stand on separate foundations built at separate times for separate purposes.

The episodes give the distinction a dated record. The 2008 act expired on September 30, 2012. No immediate termination of the farm and food structure followed. The American Taxpayer Relief Act, Public Law 112-240, signed on January 2, 2013, extended the expired act through September 30, 2013. Congress had time to extend because expiration had not abolished the programs, and Congress had reason to extend because the commodity suspension needed continuation before permanent law reasserted itself. The extension then ran its course, the act lapsed again, and the second lapse carried the sequence into the dairy confrontation of late 2013 and early 2014.

The dairy confrontation supplies the clearest test because permanent law actually revived, and the test separates revival in law from operation in the market. The suspension covering the dairy provision expired on December 31, 2013, and on January 1, 2014 the dairy provision of permanent law revived in law. For five weeks the older provision stood as governing law. Yet the Department of Agriculture made no purchases under it, no allotments or quotas operated, and the support price never operated in the market. Secretary Vilsack had stated publicly during the episode that an immediate January milk price rise was unlikely because implementation required rulemaking. Revival lasted from January 1 until the Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, signed on February 7, 2014, re-suspended permanent law. A description of January 2014 as the arrival of parity milk prices in stores would have converted a legal event into a market event that never occurred. A description of the same interval as a period with no dairy law would have made the opposite error. Law revived, administration required further steps, and Congress closed the interval before those steps produced purchases, allotments or quotas. The governing provisions during that interval were those of [the Agricultural Act of 1949 permanent law](/2020/12/01/agricultural-act-1949-permanent-law/).

The estimates attached to that episode explain why Congress acted, and the attribution of those estimates defines their proper weight. The Congressional Research Service put prospective permanent law support near $40 per hundredweight, against an average all milk price near $20 in 2013. A White House report of November 2013 put prospective support near $37 per hundredweight, at a cost of $10 billion to $12.5 billion a year, with retail prices potentially doubling. Those figures were prospective estimates from 2013, and neither described a price that consumers paid in January or February 2014. Their force lay in the gap between a parity based formula and the market average, and in the fiscal exposure that operation of the formula would have created. Expiration threatened a costly revival, not a cessation, and the prospect of support near double the average milk price gave legislators a reason to reimpose the suspension.

A later lapse confirms the pattern on a shorter timetable. The 2018 act lapsed after September 30, 2018, and the lapse closed when the successor act, Public Law 115-334, was enacted on December 20, 2018. Read together, the episodes from 2012 to 2013, from 2013 to 2014 and in 2018 trace the same machinery under different political conditions. An act expires. An extension or a successor follows. Permanent law waits behind the suspension for commodity support, and in the dairy case permanent law revives in law without operating in the market. Nutrition assistance and crop insurance, resting on permanent authorizations, do not stop at the deadline. A system assembled across the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, the Agricultural Act of 1949, the Food and Nutrition Act and the Federal Crop Insurance Act does not rise or fall as one piece.

The strongest form of the claim also fails on its own terms because each element of the imagined cliff meets a contrary element in the record. Programs end meets revival of permanent law for commodities. Checks stop meets permanent authorization for nutrition assistance and crop insurance. The department shuts its farm programs meets a five-week dairy revival in which the department made no purchases and operated no allotments or quotas, followed by re-suspension. The record supports urgency around farm bill deadlines, and urgency has a rational basis in the cost that an operating parity system could produce. Urgency, however, is not evidence for the cliff story. The deadline matters because old law returns, because separate permanent laws keep major programs running, and because the distance between revival in law and operation in practice gives Congress a narrow interval in which to act. The verdict is false.

The inspection claim, in its strongest form, holds that one agency inspects all food. A single food safety authority sets the standards, inspects the plants and answers for every category of food under one name and one chain of command. In that picture, a question about the safety of any product has one institutional address. Meat and milk, eggs and produce, seafood and frozen meals all pass through the same inspection system, and responsibility follows the product without interruption or division. The picture draws strength from the ordinary meaning of a national food supply and from the presence of prominent federal names in food regulation. Prominence, however, is not exclusivity, and the federal food safety system recorded by the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service bears little resemblance to a single authority.

### Which agency inspects all food in the United States?

No single agency does. The Government Accountability Office counted at least thirty federal food safety laws administered by fifteen federal agencies. The Food Safety and Inspection Service covers meat, poultry, processed egg products and catfish, while the Food and Drug Administration covers virtually all other food, including shell eggs and produce.

The Government Accountability Office stated the scale of fragmentation in its December 2014 report, Federal Food Safety Oversight, numbered GAO-15-180, and the Congressional Research Service primer on the federal food safety system carried the same structural account. At least 30 federal laws related to food safety are administered by 15 federal agencies. The numbers dispose of the claim before any product category enters the discussion. Fragmentation is not an inference drawn from a few anomalous products at the edge of the system. Fragmentation is the documented shape of the system, measured across its laws and its administering agencies, and the single authority model fails at that first measurement.

The duration of official concern reinforces the measurement without changing its meaning. Federal food safety oversight has sat on the high risk list of the Government Accountability Office since 2007. The designation signals that the oversight structure had drawn sustained attention as an area of governmental vulnerability. The designation does not, by itself, identify an ideal structure or establish that a single agency would cure the risks named. The designation does establish that fragmentation was not a passing administrative accident discovered in one audit. Oversight remained on the high risk list while responsibility remained divided among many agencies under many laws. Any description that begins with one agency therefore begins against the record, and any argument about reform must begin from division rather than from unity.

The operative split runs between the Department of Agriculture and the Food and Drug Administration, and the Department of Agriculture side belongs to the Food Safety and Inspection Service. That service inspects meat, poultry, processed egg products and catfish. Catfish arrived by a specific route. Catfish moved to the Food Safety and Inspection Service by amendments in the 2008 farm bill to the Federal Meat Inspection Act. The transfer matters beyond the fate of one fish. A farm bill, an act principally associated with commodity support, nutrition, conservation and crop insurance, altered the inspection map for a single seafood category, while leaving the broader division of food safety authority in place. The episode shows the farm bill touching food safety at the margin and demonstrates how specific the resulting boundaries can become.

The Food and Drug Administration holds the other and far broader side of the split. The administration is responsible for virtually all food outside the categories assigned to the Food Safety and Inspection Service, including shell eggs, seafood other than catfish, produce and dairy. The phrase virtually all other food captures both the breadth of the assignment and the reason confusion persists. Produce answers to the administration. Dairy answers to the administration. Seafood answers to the administration, except for the catfish category transferred to the Department of Agriculture. Breadth, however, stops at the statutory boundary around meat, poultry, processed egg products and catfish. The larger agency in product coverage is not the only agency, and the smaller list contains products central to the national diet. Neither prominence nor breadth converts a divided jurisdiction into a single one.

The boundary becomes vivid where similar products fall on opposite sides of it, and eggs provide the cleanest illustration. Shell eggs belong to the Food and Drug Administration. Processed egg products belong to the Food Safety and Inspection Service. The difference turns on the form of the product rather than on any difference in the animal source or in the consumer understanding of eggs as a single food. An egg in the shell and an egg product processed out of the shell travel through different federal inspection jurisdictions. A description that assigned all egg oversight to one agency would be wrong in one direction or the other, depending on the form of the product. The split also explains why product by product questions resist general answers. The governing question is not which agency inspects food, in the abstract. The governing questions are which product, in which form, under which statute, and the answers change as the form changes.

Frozen pizza supplies the second illustration, and the illustration carries the division inside a single package. A frozen pizza with meat answers to both agencies. A cheese pizza answers to the Food and Drug Administration alone. The topping decides the jurisdictional result. Meat brings the Food Safety and Inspection Service into a product that otherwise sits within the broad administration portfolio, while a cheese pizza without meat remains with the administration by itself. No single agency inspects the frozen pizza category as a whole, because the category itself is divided by ingredient. Ingredient based and form based boundaries produce shared jurisdiction in one package and sole jurisdiction in a nearly identical package, and an account of the system has to report those boundaries before evaluating them.

The farm bill relationship requires the same precision. The split predates and survives every farm bill, because the principal assignments rest on food safety statutes administered across the federal government rather than on a farm bill title that expires and revives. The farm bill touches the split only at the margins. The catfish transfer was a farm bill provision, enacted through amendments to the Federal Meat Inspection Act in 2008, and that transfer stands as the documented example of a farm bill moving one category from one side of the line to the other. A marginal change of that kind proves influence without proving control. The farm bill did not create the division, did not gather the 15 agencies counted by the Government Accountability Office into one authority, and did not disturb the at least 30 laws through which federal food safety oversight operates.

A final distinction separates the record from the argument built on the record. The existence of a divided system is a finding supported by the Government Accountability Office count, the Congressional Research Service primer and the product level assignments between the two principal agencies. Whether the divided system should remain divided is a separate question of policy. Consolidation proposals present a position on that question, not a finding that consolidation would produce any particular result. A position in favor of a single agency may draw on boundary cases such as meat pizza and shell eggs or on the high risk designation held since 2007, and a position against consolidation may draw on specialization and statutory history. Neither position follows automatically from the count of laws and agencies, and neither changes the description of the system operated through the period ending on March 15, 2021. Description and prescription must therefore travel separately. The description is division. The prescription remains argument.

Measured against that record, the strongest form of the inspection claim fails at every level at which the claim can be tested. One agency meets 15 federal agencies administering at least 30 food safety laws. One chain of command meets a principal split between the Food Safety and Inspection Service, covering meat, poultry, processed egg products and catfish, and the Food and Drug Administration, covering virtually everything else. One rule for similar products meets shell eggs divided from processed egg products, seafood divided from catfish, and a meat pizza answering to both agencies while a cheese pizza answers to one. One farm bill centered account meets a split that predates and survives farm bills and that a farm bill altered only at the margin in 2008. Sustained placement on the high risk list of the Government Accountability Office since 2007 confirms that officials charged with oversight regarded the structure as a continuing concern, while the same record leaves the merits of consolidation to policy argument rather than to factual verdict. The claim mistakes a desire for administrative simplicity, or an argument for it, for the structure that federal law built. The verdict is false.

## What the Organic Label Actually Certifies

The organic label generated two opposite errors, and the regulation text refuted both errors with the same mechanism. One error treated the label as a guarantee that no pesticide had touched the crop and that no residue could appear on the food. The opposite error treated any allowance for a pesticide as proof that the label carried no enforceable meaning at all. Between those two errors sat a federal standard built on a list. The standard defined which substances could appear in organic production, which substances could not appear, and which production methods a certified operation had to follow before the label could be used at all. Reading the label through the list, rather than through either error, produced a narrower and more durable account of certification under [the Organic Foods Production Act of 1990](/2021/03/01/organic-foods-production-act-1990/).

The first claim, stated in the strongest form its holders would recognize, held that organic meant pesticide free. In that picture, an organic field was a field managed without any pesticide of any kind, synthetic or natural, from planting through harvest. Pest pressure, in that picture, was absorbed by the crop, ignored by the grower, or handled by methods so gentle that the methods did not count as pest control in any meaningful sense. The label, in that picture, also functioned as a testing guarantee. A certified product was understood to carry zero pesticide residues because a certified process was understood to have excluded every pesticide at the source. The word organic did the work of three separate promises at once. The word promised an absence of pest control, an absence of chemical intervention, and an absence of residue in the finished food.

### Does organic mean no pesticides are used?

No. The National List allowed specified synthetic substances for crop production and allowed nonsynthetic substances unless prohibited, so the organic standard screened pesticides through a list rather than banning every pesticide. Certification audited compliance with that list and with required production methods.

The legal record began with a statute that created a certification program, not a purity pledge. The Organic Foods Production Act of 1990, enacted as Public Law 101-624 and codified at 7 U.S.C. 6501 and following sections, established the framework for national organic standards. The National Organic Program issued a final rule in December 2000, and that rule became fully effective on October 21, 2002. From that effective date, the meaning of the label rested on regulatory text that an inspector could apply and a certifier could audit. The governing mechanism sat at sections 205.600 to 205.607 of title 7 of the Code of Federal Regulations, the provisions known as the National List. That list, rather than a general impression of natural farming, decided the pesticide question.

The National List operated through opposite defaults for the two categories of substances. A synthetic substance was prohibited unless the substance appeared on the list of allowed synthetics. A nonsynthetic substance, meaning a natural substance, was allowed unless the substance appeared on the list of prohibited nonsynthetics. The asymmetry mattered for the pesticide free claim because the first default assumed that synthetics would enter organic production through the list itself. A closed list with named entries for crop production substances presupposed that pest control materials formed part of lawful organic practice. Section 205.601 carried the allowed synthetics for crop production, and the entries in that section described real pest control and sanitation tools with assigned functions. The regulation did not treat the presence of a listed material as a violation of the organic idea. The regulation treated an unlisted synthetic as the violation, and a listed synthetic used outside the terms of the listing as a compliance failure.

Pest control in organic production was real and routine, and the regulation described a layered practice rather than an absence of practice. Cultural methods came first in the sequence described for organic crop production, shaping the conditions under which a crop grew so that pest pressure met a less favorable setting. Mechanical methods followed as a physical layer, removing, blocking, or disrupting pests through direct action rather than through a substance applied to the crop. Biological methods formed a third layer, relying on living relationships in the production system to suppress pest populations. Only within and alongside those layers did listed materials enter the picture. The ordering carried an important implication for the pesticide free claim. A grower who exhausted or combined cultural, mechanical, and biological methods and then applied a listed material had not stepped outside organic production. That grower had followed the sequence the standard contemplated.

The allowed synthetics named in section 205.601 made the point concrete. Chlorine materials appeared on the list in three forms, calcium hypochlorite, chlorine dioxide, and sodium hypochlorite, and the listing assigned those materials to disinfecting and water treatment. A chlorine material used to disinfect equipment or treat water in an organic operation was a synthetic substance performing a sanitation function under an express listing. Copper sulfate and fixed coppers appeared as fungicides, and the listing placed restrictions on copper use, so a fungal disease problem in an organic crop could lawfully be met with a synthetic fungicide that the list had admitted under conditions. Soap based herbicides appeared on the list as well, giving an organic operation a listed synthetic option for weed suppression. Boric acid appeared as another allowed synthetic with a pest control function. Pheromone dispensers appeared on the list too, placing a synthetic delivery device for mating disruption inside lawful organic practice. Each entry weakened the pesticide free picture in a different way. Disinfection, water treatment, fungicidal action, herbicidal action, and pheromone based disruption were not marginal accidents at the edge of the standard. The list named those functions directly.

The listing structure also distinguished permission from unrestricted use. A substance on the allowed list entered organic production only for the purpose the listing assigned and only under any restriction the listing attached. Copper compounds illustrated that distinction with particular clarity, because the regulation treated copper sulfate and fixed coppers as fungicides under restrictions rather than as general purpose inputs available for any problem a grower faced. Chlorine materials carried a comparable limit through function, since the listing tied calcium hypochlorite, chlorine dioxide, and sodium hypochlorite to disinfecting and water treatment rather than to direct application against crop pests. Pheromone dispensers carried a limit through mechanism, since disruption of mating patterns addressed a pest population without a broad application across a field. The pattern across the entries mattered for the claim under examination. A standard that intended to exclude pest control would not have needed purpose limits or use restrictions for pest control materials. Purpose limits and use restrictions existed because the materials had a contemplated place inside production, and the regulatory task was to govern that place rather than to deny the place existed. Permission under the National List was therefore conditional permission, extended substance by substance, function by function, and restriction by restriction, within a system that presumed pest management as a normal part of crop production.

The certification logic completed the refutation. A certifier audited an operation against the standard, and the standard incorporated the National List by reference into the definition of compliance. An operation that avoided every pesticide and also violated other production requirements had not earned the label through avoidance alone. An operation that used a listed substance within the terms of the listing and satisfied the production requirements stood inside the standard. The label therefore certified a production process audited against the list. The label did not certify the absence of pest control, because pest control occupied a defined place inside the audited process. The label also did not operate as a residue guarantee attached to each item of food, because the regulatory text defined compliance at the level of the production system and the substances that system could lawfully use. A claim of zero residues read a testing promise into a process standard that the text never wrote.

The verdict on the first claim is false. Organic production under the National Organic Program used pesticides, screened pesticides, and assigned pesticides specific functions under section 205.601, while cultural, mechanical, and biological methods carried the first layers of pest management. The consumer picture of a field touched by no pesticide described a standard that Congress and the Department of Agriculture never enacted. The Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 built a list based certification regime, and a list with allowed synthetics cannot support a claim of total pesticide absence. The accurate statement ran in the opposite direction from the claim. Pesticides were not absent from organic production. Pesticides were screened by the list.

The second claim came from the opposite direction, and stated in the strongest form its holders would recognize, the claim held that the organic label meant nothing at all. Label skeptics advanced that position from a single observation. Because the National List allowed some pesticides, including synthetic substances, the distinction between organic and conventional production was said to collapse into marketing. In that picture, a copper fungicide in an organic orchard and a synthetic input in a conventional field belonged to the same category of chemical agriculture, and a label that admitted the first while condemning the second performed a verbal trick rather than a regulatory function. The premium attached to the label, in that picture, purchased a word and a story. The standard behind the word, in that picture, imposed no boundary that a producer had to respect and no prohibition that an inspector could enforce.

### If some pesticides are allowed, does the organic label mean nothing?

No. Prohibition by default for synthetics, prohibition of listed natural toxins, and five-year sunset review gave the label enforceable content. The standard barred every synthetic absent from the allowed list and barred named nonsynthetics in section 205.602, including arsenic and strychnine.

The same list that admitted named synthetics also prohibited named natural substances, and the prohibited list carried the first part of the answer. Section 205.602 listed nonsynthetic substances barred from organic production, and the entries reached substances that a natural farming picture might have assumed to be benign or traditional. Arsenic appeared on the prohibited list. Lead salts appeared on the prohibited list. Strychnine appeared on the prohibited list. Rotenone, a substance derived from plant roots and long associated with botanical insecticides, appeared on the prohibited list. Sodium fluoaluminate appeared on the prohibited list. Tobacco dust, identified in the regulation as nicotine sulfate, appeared on the prohibited list. Ash from manure burning appeared on the prohibited list. The Department of Agriculture reinforced the point in the Organic 101 explainer, which named strychnine and arsenic as natural toxic substances prohibited in organic production. A label that meant nothing would not have needed a prohibited list at all, and a label governed by a simple preference for natural substances would not have barred natural toxins by name. Each prohibited entry also carried enforcement weight, because a certifier who found arsenic, strychnine, or rotenone in an organic production plan confronted a listed prohibition rather than a debatable preference about natural inputs.

The composition of the two lists showed how the standard sorted substances. The standard did not sort by the natural and synthetic label alone. Natural origin did not earn a substance admission, because arsenic, strychnine, rotenone, and tobacco dust held natural origins and the regulation barred each substance. Synthetic origin did not produce automatic exclusion either, because chlorine materials, copper compounds, soap based herbicides, boric acid, and pheromone dispensers held synthetic identities and the regulation admitted each substance under a listing. The sorting ran instead through evidence and function as the National Organic Standards Board review process and the listing decisions expressed that sorting in regulatory form. A substance entered or remained in organic production because the list placed the substance there for a defined purpose, and a substance stayed out because the list or the default rule kept the substance out. That architecture contradicted the skeptic picture of an empty label. The label summarized a set of inclusion and exclusion decisions with legal effect.

The default rule for synthetics supplied the second part of the answer, and the default was strict. Every synthetic substance absent from the allowed list was banned from organic production without any need for a separate prohibition proceeding against that substance. The burden ran against admission. A producer who wanted to use a synthetic material had to find the material on the list, within the terms of the listing, or leave the material unused. Conventional agriculture operated under a registration system in which a pesticide could be registered for specified uses and then applied according to the registration. The organic default inverted the posture of a registration system. Registration asked whether a substance had gained authorization for a use. The National List asked whether a substance had gained a place on a short allowed list, and silence in the list meant prohibition. A standard with a closed default of that kind drew a boundary that separated certified production from production governed only by registration, and the boundary had practical consequences for every input decision on a certified operation.

Sunset review supplied the third part of the answer. A listing on the National List did not operate as a permanent entitlement for a substance. Listed substances faced review by the National Organic Standards Board on a five-year sunset cycle. The cycle forced a periodic reconsideration of whether an allowed synthetic or a prohibited nonsynthetic belonged in the position the list had assigned. A substance survived in an allowed position only through that recurring review structure, and the structure distinguished a living standard from a decorative seal. A decorative seal would have frozen a marketing claim in place. The sunset cycle instead treated each listing as a decision subject to renewed scrutiny at a fixed interval, with the board charged with the review function the regulation assigned. The label therefore pointed backward to a statute and a rule, sideways to two lists with opposite defaults, and forward to a review calendar that revisited the lists.

A plain statement of scope separated the genuine limits of certification from the false charge of emptiness. Certification promised audited process compliance. A certified operation had submitted to a system in which production methods and input substances were checked against the Organic Foods Production Act framework, the National Organic Program rule that became fully effective on October 21, 2002, and the National List at sections 205.600 to 205.607. Certification did not promise a residue guarantee for each unit of food, and the absence of that promise reflected the process based design of the standard rather than a defect in enforcement. Certification also carried no nutritional claim, and the regulation text made no representation that a certified product held a superior nutrient profile. Those limits defined what a buyer could and could not infer from the label. A buyer could infer that prohibited synthetics had no lawful place in the production process, that named natural toxins had no lawful place either, that listed substances could appear only within listing terms, and that cultural, mechanical, and biological methods formed the first layers of pest management. A buyer could not infer an absence of pest control, an absence of residues as a tested guarantee, or a nutritional advantage that the standard never asserted.

The verdict on the second claim is false. A label backed by a statutory certification program, a prohibited list that barred arsenic, lead salts, strychnine, rotenone, sodium fluoaluminate, tobacco dust, and ash from manure burning, a default rule that banned every unlisted synthetic, and a five-year sunset review conducted through the National Organic Standards Board imposed enforceable content at every point the skeptic claim denied content existed. The two errors examined in the paired claims shared a single mistake. Both errors treated the label as a statement about chemical purity, one error by asserting perfect purity and the other error by demanding perfect purity as the price of meaning. The regulation treated the label as a statement about an audited process governed by a list. Measured against the text at sections 205.601 to 205.607, organic certification meant neither pesticide absence nor regulatory emptiness. Certification meant compliance, verified through audit, with a standard that admitted some substances, barred others, and sorted both groups by listing rather than by slogan.

## The Claims the Record Cannot Settle

Some farm bill arguments end cleanly against a dated baseline, a statutory limit, or a named study. Other arguments do not end in that manner, and a responsible treatment has to leave them open rather than force a verdict the evidence cannot support. Contested, in the vocabulary of this article, is a finding about the state of the evidence as of March 15, 2021, and the finding carries a burden: to show where the record is solid, where the record thins, and why the remaining gap cannot be closed by repeating the solid part more loudly. Each claim here receives the strongest form its holders would recognize, then the record, then a verdict that stops where the record stops.

That discipline matters most for conservation, where a single word covers two different kinds of public purchase. One purchase retires land under a contract with a stated term and practice obligations. The other shares the cost of a practice, or pays for stewardship, on land that remains in production. The value of the second purchase depends on a comparison between the world with the payment and the world without the payment. If a producer would have adopted the same practice, at the same scale and time, without public funds, the payment bought income support but not an additional environmental gain. If the producer would not have adopted the practice, or would have adopted the practice later or on fewer acres, the payment bought a gain that would not otherwise have occurred. Land retirement can be verified acre by acre. Working lands assistance requires an inference payment data alone cannot supply.

### Would the practice have been adopted without the payment?

For land retirement under the Conservation Reserve Program, the answer is observable in the contract and in the cover on the enrolled acre, because an acre under contract is out of crop production for the contract term. For working lands programs, the answer is not observable in the payment record, and the additionality question remains open in the research record.

The claim at its strongest is that conservation payments buy environmental benefits that would not otherwise happen. Stated fully, the claim holds that public money causes additional erosion control, water quality protection, and habitat beyond what private returns to farming would have produced on the same timetable. The claim matters because conservation payments are defended as purchases, and a purchase implies delivery of something the buyer would not have received for free. Program critics press the opposite form with equal force: working lands payments reward practices producers would adopt without payment, practices that fit an operation for agronomic or economic reasons apart from the payment, so public funds pay for a decision the operation had already made or would soon have made. Between those forms lies the evidence, and the evidence divides by program type.

The Conservation Reserve Program was created by the Food Security Act of 1985. The program pays annual rental payments under contracts of ten to fifteen years to retire highly erodible and environmentally sensitive cropland to conserving cover. Enrollment peaked at 36.8 million acres in 2007, according to the Congressional Research Service. The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 set the cap at 24 million acres for fiscal 2019, rising to 27 million acres by fiscal 2023, and cut rental rates to 85 percent of the county average for general sign up contracts and 90 percent for continuous contracts, partly to offset the expansion, according to the Congressional Research Service. Practice obligations attach to an enrolled acre, and early exit requires repayment with interest plus a fee. The Economic Research Service records that program payments flow mostly to retirement, off farm occupation, and low sales farms, a profile different from commodity payments.

On retired land, the benefit record is documented. Reporting by the United States Department of Agriculture documents benefits in erosion reduction, water quality, and habitat on land retired under the program. A retired acre is a verifiable change in land use for the contract period, and the change is the direct object of the rental payment. Soil held under conserving cover, runoff from covered ground, and habitat held in place for a decade or longer stand in a short causal chain observation can follow. The precise size of each benefit varies by soil, slope, rainfall, and cover practice, and honest treatment does not convert documented direction into a single national quantity the record does not supply. The direction is not the contested part of the record.

The chain lengthens for working lands programs. The Environmental Quality Incentives Program and the Conservation Stewardship Program operate on land that remains in production, the first by sharing the cost of practices and the second by paying for conservation activity and stewardship. A funded practice may be one an operation values apart from the payment, because the practice reduces input costs, protects a field held for a long period, or fits an equipment and rotation plan already in place. A funded practice may also be one an operation would postpone without cost sharing, because the private return arrives slowly while the cost arrives at once, or because the benefit accrues downstream rather than to the operator. Both accounts describe real operations, and a record showing a practice was funded does not tell an evaluator which account describes a given contract. That gap between funded practice and caused practice is the additionality question. Whether those payments fund practices that would have occurred anyway is a live research question the evidence has not closed, and the evidence table in Article 266 of this series rates conservation as contested on exactly this ground. The verdict is contested, with the line drawn between retired land and working land.

Three further disputes belong on the same contested ground, but each dispute is open for a different reason. In the first dispute, two credible measurements point to related but different magnitudes, and no single settled number commands the record. In the second dispute, the legislative arrangement is agreed, and the disagreement is about whether a feature of that arrangement counts as a defect or as a strength. In the third dispute, the facts about the present rules are agreed, and the disagreement is about whether those rules ought to be extended from one program area to another. An open measurement question invites better measurement. An open question of institutional judgment invites argument about representation and values. An open question of program design invites a policy choice evidence can inform but cannot make.

### How much of each subsidy dollar is captured in rents and land values?

The record offers two related measurements rather than one settled figure. Barrett Kirwan estimated that about twenty five cents of each subsidy dollar passes through to landowners in higher rental rates, while a commission estimate from 2003 placed the effect on land values in a range from fifteen percent to twenty five percent.

The capitalization dispute begins from a mechanism neither side needs to deny. A payment stream tied to land that buyers and tenants expect to continue has value in farmland and rental markets, and competition for an acre can bid part of the payment into rent or purchase price, so a tenant operator may keep only part of a payment the record assigns to the operator. Tenure data give the mechanism scale. The United States Department of Agriculture Total Survey in 2014 found that about 39 percent of farmland acres in the contiguous states were rented, and about 80 percent of rented acres were owned by nonoperator landlords. Kirwan, in the Journal of Political Economy in 2009, estimated that about 25 cents of each subsidy dollar passes through to landowners in higher rental rates and that tenant operators keep about 75 cents. The Payment Limitations Commission in 2003 put the land value effect at 15 percent to 25 percent. The figures answer adjacent questions: rent is a flow paid for use of land in a period, and land value is a stock reflecting capitalized expected returns, so the two measures are not interchangeable and neither converts into the other without added assumptions. The size of the capitalization share is labeled open, with different instruments and different measures leaving no single settled number.

The logroll dispute has a different structure, because the arrangement is not in doubt. The Food Stamp Act of 1964 placed food assistance under the United States Department of Agriculture and the agriculture committees, and the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973 was the first farm bill to incorporate food stamp reauthorization. The combination pairs urban votes for farm support with rural votes for food assistance in a single legislative vehicle. Defenders and fiscal critics describe the same pairing and draw opposite conclusions, and both characterizations are attributed rather than asserted. Defenders describe mutual dependence and broad geographic representation as strengths, on the ground that a coalition spanning districts with very different economies forces negotiation across regional lines and produces law no narrow constituency could enact or sustain alone. Fiscal critics describe mutual insulation from scrutiny as the cost, on the ground that legislators who need the farm provisions tolerate the nutrition provisions, and legislators who need the nutrition provisions tolerate the farm provisions, so an exchange of tolerance substitutes for independent judgment on each title. Whether the logroll is a defect or a design strength is labeled open, because the dispute turns on institutional judgment that outlay data cannot decide alone.

The payment limit dispute is open for the third reason, as a dispute about design resting on agreed facts from the record in Section Two of this article. Premium subsidies for crop insurance carry no per person payment limit and no adjusted gross income test. Commodity programs in the same bills carry a combined per person limit of 125,000 dollars for Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage payments and an adjusted gross income limit of 900,000 dollars under the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018. The government pays an average of about 62 percent of crop insurance premiums, according to the Congressional Research Service, and conservation compliance was reattached to premium subsidies by section 2611 of the Agricultural Act of 2014. Agreement on that asymmetry is a matter of statutory text and of a named average. Disagreement begins with design. One position holds that a subsidy of that size, delivered without a per person limit and without an income test, should be brought under the limiting principles governing commodity payments. The opposing position holds that crop insurance functions as insurance, that risk pooling depends on broad enrollment across farm sizes, and that limits suited to direct payments would distort the pool or discourage coverage. Whether payment limits should extend to premium subsidies is labeled open as a dispute about design rather than about evidence.

Open questions are difficult enough when sources are quoted carefully, and the sourcing errors that propagate through farm bill debate manufacture false certainty where the record offers only a dated, titled, and bounded figure. The errors are patterned enough to support a namable diagnostic: check the title and the year. Title asks which title of the farm bill a figure describes, and whether a figure describes the farm bill at all or a larger budget universe instead. Year asks what period and baseline a figure carries, and across what span a total accumulated. Most durable errors survive because a title or a year was dropped between source and retelling, and restoring the dropped element usually dissolves the error without new research.

Title errors take two principal forms. The first form quotes a nutrition figure as a farm figure, or the reverse. The Congressional Budget Office baseline from April 2018, projecting outlays for fiscal 2019 to 2028 under an extension of the 2014 bill, put total projected outlays at 867 billion dollars, with nutrition at 663.8 billion dollars, crop insurance at about 78.0 billion dollars, commodity programs at about 61.1 billion dollars, and conservation at about 59.7 billion dollars. Quoting the nutrition figure, or a total dominated by nutrition, as the cost of farm payments changes the subject while keeping the grammar of evidence, and the reverse error understates nutrition by an order of magnitude. The second form treats a share of the entire budget of the United States Department of Agriculture as a share of the farm bill. The department budget contains farm bill programs alongside other activity, so a percentage calculated against the departmental denominator and quoted against the farm bill denominator misstates both quantities.

Year errors recur in three concrete instances. The first instance quotes the 77 percent nutrition share without its baseline and period. That share belongs to the April 2018 Congressional Budget Office baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, in which nutrition at 663.8 billion dollars accounted for about 77 percent of the 867 billion dollar total. At enactment of the 2008 act, nutrition was 67 percent of the ten year projected total, at 406 billion dollars of 604 billion dollars, according to the Congressional Research Service. At enactment of the 2014 act, nutrition was 79 percent, at 756 billion dollars of 956 billion dollars, and at enactment of the 2018 act the projection put nutrition at 76 percent over fiscal 2019 to 2023. Shares move because nutrition outlays are countercyclical and participation driven, while commodity outlays are price driven. The Congressional Research Service records that growth between the 2008 and 2014 scores was driven overwhelmingly by changed economic expectations after the recession, that the 2014 act itself scored an 8 billion dollar ten year reduction in nutrition, and that expected ten year nutrition outlays rose by about 350 billion dollars on economic expectations alone. A share is a snapshot with a year attached, never a fixed property.

The second instance quotes concentration totals without the span across which the totals accumulated. The Environmental Working Group, an advocacy organization working from United States Department of Agriculture payment records obtained under the Freedom of Information Act, reports that the top 10 percent of recipients received 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the period from 1995 to 2016. Quoted without the span, the total reads as a description of a single year. The record supplies the contrast. For commodity payments from 1995 to 2010, 76 percent went to the top tenth, while in 2010 alone the top tenth received 63 percent. The multi year share and the single year share differ because the periods differ. The production explanation also travels with the figures: payments follow production and acreage, production is concentrated, and concentration of payments partly mirrors concentration of output, so the disagreement is interpretive rather than a disagreement about the numbers.

The third instance quotes a support price from the dairy episode of 2013 and 2014 as a standing fact. Permanent law consists chiefly of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949, with parity support benchmarked to the base period from 1910 to 1914, and a modern farm bill suspends permanent law for a coverage period, so expiry revives the suspended provisions. The 2008 act expired on September 30, 2012, the American Taxpayer Relief Act extended that act through September 30, 2013, and the extension then lapsed. On January 1, 2014, the dairy provision revived in law because the suspension had expired on December 31, 2013, but the United States Department of Agriculture made no purchases under the provision, no allotments or quotas operated, and the support price never operated in the market. The Agricultural Act of 2014, signed on February 7, 2014, suspended permanent law again. Estimates from the episode were prospective. The Congressional Research Service put prospective support near 40 dollars per hundredweight against an average all milk price near 20 dollars in 2013, and a White House report from November 2013 put prospective support near 37 dollars per hundredweight, at a cost of 10 billion dollars to 12.5 billion dollars a year, with retail prices potentially doubling. Secretary Vilsack stated publicly that an immediate January milk price rise was unlikely because implementation required rulemaking. Quoting either figure without the 2013 source and the fact that the price never operated converts a warning about a suspended statute into a description of a market that never experienced the quoted price.

Each error performs the same work. Removal of the boundary that makes a figure testable allows the figure to be carried into any preferred conclusion, including conclusions about the contested questions with which this section began. Additionality cannot be assessed from a conservation figure quoted without the distinction between retired land and working land, capitalization cannot be assessed from a percentage quoted without the instrument that distinguishes rent from land value, and the logroll cannot be assessed from a share quoted without the baseline that explains why the share moved. The diagnostic named here, check the title and the year, settles none of those questions. The diagnostic performs a prior service by clearing away spurious settlements that appear decided only because a figure lost a title or a year in transit. What remains after that clearing, quoted with titles and years intact, is the proper subject of a closing assessment.

## What a Careful Reader Should Believe

The ten examinations produced a pattern with a distinct shape. Two claims survived in part, because each rested on a real feature of the record that the claim then stretched past the boundary of the evidence. Nutrition spending did dominate projected farm bill outlays in the April 2018 baseline and in the projection issued at enactment of the 2018 act, and a concentrated minority of recipients did receive very large cumulative payments across the period from 1995 to 2016. One claim remained contested, because the additionality question at the heart of working lands conservation turned on a comparison between a world with a payment and a world without a payment, and payment records alone could not supply that comparison. The remaining seven claims failed. Small farms did not receive most of the help, farm support did not keep grocery prices low, corn subsidies did not cause obesity, expiration did not stop the farm and food structure, no single agency inspected all food, organic certification did not mean pesticide free production, and the benefits of the farm bill reached far past farmers. Each failure traced to a specific defect rather than to a general shortage of evidence, and the defects repeated across subjects.

The pattern carries a lesson about the kind of error involved. The false claims rarely rested on invented numbers. The figures underneath the claims usually came from a genuine baseline, a genuine payment record or a genuine statute, and error entered during transit, at the point where a figure lost the boundary that made the figure testable. A nutrition share lost a baseline year and hardened into an identity. A concentration total lost a span of years and hardened into an annual description. A parity support price lost the distinction between revival in law and operation in a market and hardened into a retail prediction. An average farm share of the food dollar lost the distribution underneath the average, in which processed foods carried far smaller farm shares, and hardened into a theory of grocery prices and then into a theory of obesity. The two checks named at the outset guard against exactly that transit error. The title check restores the subject a figure describes. The year check restores the period, the baseline and the span a figure covers. A claim that survives both checks, stated in strong form and graded against a named source, has earned consideration. A claim that depends on a dropped title or a dropped year has disclosed the weakness a careful reader needs.

Durability belongs to the checks rather than to any single verdict, because verdicts rest on records with dates and records change as new baselines, new payment compilations and new studies appear. The April 2018 baseline described an extension of the 2014 act. The enactment scores of 2008, 2014 and 2018 placed the nutrition share at 67 percent, 79 percent and 76 percent across three different windows, and a subsequent baseline could move any share again for economic reasons that legislation did not cause. The concentration record accumulated across the period from 1995 to 2016 described that span and no other. The obesity findings of Okrent and Alston in 2012 and of Alston, Sumner and Vosti in 2008 answered the subsidy mechanism on the evidence available to those authors, and the sugar program rates retained by the 2018 act described the statute at the vantage date of March 15, 2021. A careful reader therefore holds each verdict together with the source and the date that produced the verdict, treats a contested verdict as an honest report on an open research question rather than as a failure of nerve, and extends the same discipline to claims encountered outside the ten examined here. The statute rewards that discipline, because the farm bill joins food assistance to farm support by deliberate design, distributes money by production rather than by headcount, touches retail prices weakly and unevenly, rests in part on permanent law that predates every modern enactment, and certifies organic production through a list that screens substances instead of banning a category.

That structure, rather than any slogan attached to the structure, is the subject a durable study file has to preserve, with statutes, scores and studies kept in the order that the two checks require. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is the farm bill really a welfare bill?

The verdict is partly true. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline projected total outlays of 867 billion dollars from fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2028 under an extension of the 2014 bill, and the nutrition title accounted for 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent of that total. The projection issued at enactment of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 placed nutrition at 76 percent over five years. Congress also joined food assistance to farm legislation deliberately, through the Food Stamp Act of 1964 and the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973. The claim fails as a complete identity, because the nutrition share moved between enactments for economic reasons and because the remaining titles carried real programs and real money.

### Q: Do farmers get rich from the farm bill?

The verdict is partly true for a narrow slice of recipients and false for the typical household. The Environmental Working Group, an advocacy organization working from Department of Agriculture payment records, found that the top tenth of recipients received 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the period from 1995 to 2016, and commodity payments to that group averaged 447,873 dollars per recipient across the period from 1995 to 2010. The household record points the other way. The Economic Research Service placed median farm income for family farm households at 296 dollars in 2019, and off-farm income supplied an average of 82 percent of total household income in that year.

### Q: Does the farm bill keep grocery prices low?

The verdict is false. The Economic Research Service Food Dollar Series, released in 2019, recorded that 14.6 cents of each retail food dollar reached the farm sector in 2017, while the marketing system received the remaining 85.4 cents. A farm level policy acts on the smaller share of the price, and the room for that policy to govern the whole price is correspondingly narrow. For highly processed foods the farm share is far smaller than the average, which narrows the room further. Parts of the same legislation also push selected prices upward, because the Conservation Reserve Program retires cropland from production and the sugar program holds the domestic sugar price above the world price.

### Q: Do small farms get most farm bill help?

The verdict is false. The Economic Research Service typology built on 2018 data recorded that small family farms, defined by gross cash farm income under 350,000 dollars, made up 90 percent of all farms and operated almost half the farmland, yet generated only 21 percent of the value of production. Large-scale family farms supplied 46 percent of production value. Payments follow production and acreage rather than headcount, so the distribution of money tracks the distribution of output. The payment record compiled by the Environmental Working Group confirms the result, with the top tenth of recipients receiving 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the period from 1995 to 2016.

### Q: Does everything stop when the farm bill expires?

The verdict is false. A modern farm bill suspends permanent law, chiefly the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949, for a coverage period, and expiration revives that older law for commodity support rather than leaving a void. Nutrition assistance rests on the Food and Nutrition Act, a permanent authorization funded through appropriations, and crop insurance rests on the Federal Crop Insurance Act, also a permanent authorization. Neither program stops when a farm bill expires. Commodity programs form the exposed part of the structure, because the parity formulas of permanent law, benchmarked to the base period from 1910 to 1914, return in law when a suspension lapses.

### Q: Do corn subsidies in the farm bill cause obesity?

The verdict is false. Abigail M. Okrent and Julian M. Alston reported in the American Journal of Agricultural Economics in 2012 that eliminating farm subsidies entirely, including direct subsidies on grains and indirect subsidies created by trade barriers, would have very limited impact on calorie consumption and hence on obesity. Alston, Daniel A. Sumner and Stephen A. Vosti concluded in 2008 that the net effect of farm subsidies on consumption was approximately zero and could run in either direction. The mechanism fails at three steps, because subsidies move commodity prices only modestly, the farm commodity forms a small share of a processed retail price, and consumer demand responds weakly to small price changes.

### Q: Does organic in the farm bill mean pesticide free?

The verdict is false. The Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 created a certification program, and the National List at sections 205.600 to 205.607 of title 7 of the Code of Federal Regulations governs substances in organic production. A synthetic substance is prohibited unless the substance appears on the list of allowed synthetics, and a nonsynthetic substance is allowed unless the substance appears on the list of prohibited nonsynthetics. Section 205.601 allows chlorine materials for disinfecting and water treatment, copper sulfate and fixed coppers as fungicides under restrictions, soap based herbicides, boric acid and pheromone dispensers. Certification audits compliance with that list rather than certifying an absence of pest control.

### Q: Does the farm bill only help farmers?

The verdict is false. The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 carried twelve titles, and several titles name recipients outside farming by design. The nutrition title, the largest title in projected outlays, delivers food assistance to households in every congressional district. The conservation title pays landowners, including non-operating owners, and funds easements and habitat protection. The rural development title funds water and waste systems, rural broadband, community facilities and rural business programs used by residents who do not farm. The research title funds land grant universities and the extension system, the forestry title funds national forest management and forestry assistance, and the trade title funds export promotion and food aid reaching recipients abroad.

### Q: Does the farm bill raise or lower the price of sugar?

The farm bill raises the price of sugar by design. The sugar program supports price through nonrecourse loans at 18.75 cents per pound for raw cane sugar, a rate set by the 2008 act for the 2011 crop onward and retained by the 2014 and 2018 acts. Tariff rate quotas limit low duty imports, marketing allotments limit domestic sales, and the Feedstock Flexibility Program diverts surplus sugar to ethanol to prevent loan forfeitures. The design target is no net cost to the Treasury, achieved by holding the domestic price up. The Farm Service Agency states that the United States price of sugar is usually considerably above the world price, and the Government Accountability Office estimated in 2000 that the program cost consumers about 1.9 billion dollars a year.

### Q: Who receives the largest farm bill payments?

The largest payments reach the operations with the largest production and acreage, because payments follow output rather than headcount. The Environmental Working Group, an advocacy organization working from Department of Agriculture payment records obtained under the Freedom of Information Act, found that the top tenth of recipients received 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the period from 1995 to 2016. For commodity payments across the period from 1995 to 2010, the top tenth received 76 percent, an average of 447,873 dollars per recipient in that group. The Economic Research Service typology supplies the production background, with large-scale family farms supplying 46 percent of the value of production while small family farms, at 90 percent of all farms, supplied 21 percent.

### Q: Do landlords capture part of farm subsidy money?

Yes. Barrett Kirwan estimated in the Journal of Political Economy in 2009 that about 25 cents of each subsidy dollar passes through to landowners in higher rental rates, while tenant operators keep about 75 cents. A Payment Limitations Commission estimate from 2003 placed the effect of subsidies on land values in a range from 15 percent to 25 percent. The tenure record gives the channel scale. The Department of Agriculture TOTAL survey of 2014 found that about 39 percent of farmland acres in the contiguous states were rented, and about 80 percent of rented acres were owned by non-operator landlords who did not farm the land.

### Q: What share of the grocery dollar reaches the farmer?

The farm sector received 14.6 cents of each retail food dollar in 2017, according to the Economic Research Service Food Dollar Series released in 2019. The marketing system that stands between the farm gate and the retail shelf received the remaining 85.4 cents. The average conceals a wide distribution across products. For highly processed foods the farm share is far smaller than the average, because manufacturing, packaging, branding, distribution and retail handling account for most of the price of a boxed or bottled product. The 14.6 cent average therefore sets an outer limit on the influence any farm level policy can exercise over the price a shopper pays at the checkout.

### Q: Is the nutrition share of the farm bill always about three quarters?

No. The share is a snapshot attached to a baseline and a period, and the recorded snapshots differ. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline placed nutrition at about 77 percent of projected outlays from fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2028, and the projection at enactment of the 2018 act placed nutrition at 76 percent over five years. The Congressional Research Service recorded nutrition at 67 percent of the ten year projected total at enactment of the 2008 act and at 79 percent at enactment of the 2014 act. Shares move because nutrition outlays are countercyclical and participation driven, while commodity outlays are price driven, so each enactment score reflects the economic forecast attached to that enactment.

### Q: Are crop insurance subsidies limited like commodity payments?

No. Crop insurance premium subsidies carry no per-person payment limit and no adjusted gross income test. The commodity programs inside the same farm bills operate under explicit caps, with a combined per-person limit of 125,000 dollars for Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage payments and an adjusted gross income limit of 900,000 dollars under the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018. The government pays an average of about 62 percent of crop insurance premiums, according to the Congressional Research Service, and that share applies at whatever scale an operation farms. Conservation compliance was reattached to premium subsidies by section 2611 of the Agricultural Act of 2014, but a compliance condition is neither a payment limit nor an income test.

### Q: What actually happened when the farm bill lapsed in the episode that produced the dairy revival?

The 2008 act expired on September 30, 2012, and the American Taxpayer Relief Act, signed on January 2, 2013, extended that act through September 30, 2013. The extension then lapsed, and on January 1, 2014 the dairy provision of permanent law revived in law because the suspension had expired on December 31, 2013. The Department of Agriculture made no purchases under the revived provision, no allotments or quotas operated, and the support price never operated in the market. Secretary Vilsack stated publicly during the episode that an immediate January milk price rise was unlikely because implementation required rulemaking. The Agricultural Act of 2014, signed on February 7, 2014, suspended permanent law again.

### Q: What pest control methods do organic farmers use?

Organic pest control runs in layers under the National Organic Program. Cultural methods shape growing conditions so that pest pressure meets a less favorable setting, mechanical methods remove or block pests through direct physical action, and biological methods rely on living relationships in the production system to suppress pest populations. Listed materials enter within and alongside those layers. The National List allows specified nonsynthetic materials and allows named synthetics at section 205.601, including chlorine materials for disinfecting and water treatment, copper compounds as fungicides under restrictions, soap based herbicides, boric acid and pheromone dispensers. Prohibited nonsynthetics at section 205.602, including arsenic, lead salts, strychnine and rotenone, have no place in the system.

### Q: Do most farm households depend on farming for their income?

No. Off-farm income supplied an average of 82 percent of total household income for family farms in 2019, according to the Economic Research Service, and 96 percent of farm households received some off-farm income in that year. Median farm income for family farm households in 2019 was 296 dollars, a figure close to zero, while median total household income for the same households was 83,111 dollars, against 68,703 dollars for all United States households. Slightly more than half of farm households recorded negative farm income in the years covered by the Economic Research Service reporting, so the typical household earned a living largely away from the farm operation.

### Q: Does the farm bill pay farmers not to farm?

The closest program is the Conservation Reserve Program, and the description fits that program only in part. The Food Security Act of 1985 created the program, which pays annual rental payments under contracts of ten to fifteen years to retire highly erodible and environmentally sensitive cropland to conserving cover. Enrollment peaked at 36.8 million acres in 2007, according to the Congressional Research Service, and the 2018 act set a cap of 24 million acres for fiscal 2019 rising to 27 million acres by fiscal 2023. Enrolled acres carry practice obligations, and early exit requires repayment with interest plus a fee. The Economic Research Service records that program payments flow mostly to retirement, off-farm occupation and low sales farms.

### Q: What should a student remember about farm bill claims for an exam?

A student answering an examination question on farm bill claims has a compact record to hold. The nutrition title accounted for about 77 percent of projected outlays in the April 2018 baseline, and that share belongs to that baseline and period rather than to the statute in general. Payments concentrate at the top of the distribution, with the top tenth of recipients receiving 77 percent of recorded subsidies across the period from 1995 to 2016, while median farm income stood at 296 dollars in 2019. Farm support reaches retail prices weakly, the sugar program raises sweetener prices, expiration revives permanent law instead of stopping programs, and organic certification screens substances through the National List. Each statement carries a named source and a date.

### Q: How can a reader check a farm bill claim before repeating it?

A reader checks a farm bill claim through the title and the year. The title check establishes which title of the bill a figure describes, because a nutrition figure quoted as a farm figure changes the subject while keeping the grammar of evidence, and a share of the whole Department of Agriculture budget describes a different universe from a share of the farm bill. The year check establishes the period and baseline a figure carries, because a share from the April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 cannot describe the enactment scores of 2008 or 2014, and a payment total accumulated from 1995 to 2016 cannot describe a single year. A claim that names a source, a title and a period can be tested, and a claim missing any of the three has disclosed a weakness.
