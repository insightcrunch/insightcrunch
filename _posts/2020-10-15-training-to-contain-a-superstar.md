Training a team to contain a superstar is not a shape problem, and treating it as one is why so many carefully prepared defensive weeks collapse inside the opening quarter of an hour. The plan is written, the video is watched, the back four walks through the sliding pattern on a Thursday, and then a forward receives on the half-turn eleven yards inside his own half with his back to a full-back who was told to press, and nobody on the pitch knows whether the full-back goes or holds. The shape was rehearsed. The decision was not. A containment plan lives or dies on about nine individual decisions repeated forty times each, and none of them are made by the team. They are made by one player, alone, in less than a second, with a consequence attached.

That is the behavior a containment week has to produce: the correct individual choice, made at match speed, by a tired player, when getting it wrong hurts. Everything below is built backwards from that sentence. The week described here installs a plan against any elite individual, whatever his threat type, using constraint designs that force the real decision rather than the comfortable one, coaching points that change what a player does rather than what he can recite, and an honest test at the end of it that tells a coach whether the work transferred or simply happened.

This article is the training end of the counter-plans in this cluster. The analysis lives elsewhere: the threat-type vocabulary sits in the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/), and the two worked plans this week most often installs are the ones set out in [how to stop Lionel Messi](/2020/06/15/how-to-stop-lionel-messi/) and [how to stop Cristiano Ronaldo](/2020/08/15/how-to-stop-cristiano-ronaldo/). What follows takes any of those plans, or any plan a coach has written himself, and turns it into four sessions, a warm-up, and a matchday reminder.

## Why Most Containment Sessions Fail

The common session runs like this. The coach sets the back four and the two central midfielders on a full pitch, walks the opposition shape into position with cones or bibs, and moves the ball around by hand or by slow pass while the defensive unit shuffles across. He stops it when a gap opens, explains the gap, resets, and runs it again. Players nod. Nothing is contested, nobody is beaten, and the whole thing looks organized. It is the most reassuring hour in football and one of the least productive.

The failure is not that the work is unopposed. Unopposed work has a real and defensible place, and the argument that it is a waste of time is wrong in a way worth taking seriously later in this article. The failure is that the exercise rehearses a pattern while the match will demand a choice. When the back four slides across on a Thursday, no defender is deciding anything. He is following a body next to him. On Saturday that same defender will have to decide whether to step, hold, or drop, and he will have to decide it while a forward is already moving, while his own midfielder is shouting something he cannot hear, and while the whole of his experience is telling him to do the safe thing, which is usually the wrong thing.

The second failure is speed. A pattern rehearsed at walking pace is a different motor and cognitive task from the same pattern executed at sprint. Coaches know this and still run the walkthrough at walking pace, because the walkthrough is where the coach gets to talk, and talking feels like coaching. What the player takes into the match is not the pattern from the walkthrough. It is whatever his body does when a genuinely fast forward attacks the space behind him, and if he has not felt that in the week, the week did not prepare him.

The third failure is consequence. In most defensive practices, being beaten costs nothing. The exercise stops, the coach explains, the players reset, and the defender who lost the duel begins the next repetition in a neutral position, unpunished and un-embarrassed. On Saturday, losing that duel means a shot, a corner, or a goal, and the emotional weight of that consequence is precisely what makes a player choose badly. A drill that removes the consequence removes the pressure that produces the error the coach is trying to fix.

### Why do defensive sessions not transfer to the match?

Because the session rehearses the shape and the match demands the decision. A player who has practiced sliding across in a line has practiced following the man beside him. He has not practiced choosing between stepping and holding under time pressure with a real penalty for choosing wrong, which is the only skill the plan actually requires.

Put those three failures together and you get the observation this article is built on. Most defensive preparation trains recognition without training selection. Players leave the pitch able to describe the plan accurately, which is why the coach believes it went well, and unable to execute it against a real forward, which is why it does not survive contact. The gap between describing and doing is the entire problem, and it is closed by one design principle.

## The Decision-Fidelity Standard

The standard this article argues for is simple to state and demanding to apply. A containment drill is only valid if a player who makes the wrong choice inside it suffers the same consequence he would suffer on Saturday. That is the decision-fidelity standard, and it disqualifies most of what teams currently do.

Fidelity here has three components, and all three have to hold at once. The first is decision equivalence. The drill must present the same choice the match will present, with the same information available. If the real decision is whether the near-side centre-back steps into the pocket to meet a dropping forward or stays on the line and lets the holding midfielder track, then the drill must contain a dropping forward, a pocket, a line, and a holding midfielder, and the ball must arrive at moments the defender does not control. A drill in which the coach calls out which option to take has removed the decision and is therefore rehearsing something else.

The second is speed equivalence. The choice has to arrive at the tempo the match will deliver it. This is usually where amateur and youth practices break, because running an exercise at real speed requires enough players, enough space, and a level of organization that takes a coach three or four attempts to get right. The workaround is not to slow the exercise down. It is to shrink it. A small area with fewer players, played at maximum intensity, produces the correct decision speed even when the full pattern cannot be reproduced. A full-pitch pattern at half speed does not.

The third is consequence equivalence. Losing must cost something inside the drill. The cheapest and most effective currency is a goal or a scoring chance, which is why almost every design in this article ends with a finish rather than with a whistle. The second cheapest is a restart: the defending side that concedes the situation starts the next repetition from a disadvantage, or the attacking side keeps possession of the ball and the defenders have to earn their rest. What the currency is matters less than that there is one. A defender who is beaten and feels nothing has learned nothing.

Applying the standard to an ordinary week is uncomfortable, because it condemns a lot of familiar work. Shadow play with no opposition fails decision equivalence and consequence equivalence. A pattern run against passive mannequins fails all three. Eleven versus eleven on a full pitch usually passes fidelity and fails on volume, because a given defender might face the relevant situation four times in forty minutes, which is not enough repetition to change a habit. The designs that pass are almost always mid-sized, overloaded in the attacker's favor, and repetitive by construction. That combination is the shape of the whole week that follows.

### What are the three tests of a valid defensive drill?

Three tests, and all of them must pass. The exercise presents the same choice the fixture will present. It arrives at fixture speed. And choosing wrongly produces a real consequence such as a shot conceded. Fail any one and a player is rehearsing a pattern rather than practicing a decision.

There is a fourth condition that is not part of the standard but is worth naming because it is where most well-designed practices still fail. The defender has to be tired at least some of the time. Almost every containment plan asks a player to make his hardest decision in the final third of the match, when a forward who has been quiet for an hour begins to find space precisely because the legs around him have gone. A week in which every defensive repetition happens in the first twenty minutes of a fresh session has prepared a player for the version of the problem he will handle anyway. Loading the key repetitions into the back end of a session, deliberately and with sensible limits on total volume, is one of the highest-return changes a coach can make. Where and how to place that load in the week without arriving at the fixture flat is a real question, and the general principles for it, along with warm-up design for high-intensity duel work, are covered further down and can be planned alongside the guidance on [training load and injury prevention on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/football-match-planner.html).

## How Much Bespoke Preparation Your Level Actually Justifies

Before any of the sessions below get built, a coach has to answer a question that most articles on this subject skip: how much of the week should this occupy at all. There are two loud and opposite positions in football discussion, and both are wrong in ways that are instructive.

The first position holds that preparing specifically for an opponent is negative coaching, that it teaches players to fear a name, and that a side should impose its own identity rather than reshape itself around somebody else's best player. The second holds that a bespoke plan is a small thing, installable on a Friday, a matter of telling two players who to watch. The first position confuses preparation with capitulation. The second confuses instruction with installation.

The resolution is a proportionality rule, and it is tied to two variables: how much the individual actually changes the match, and how much rehearsal time you have. At the elite end, where the opponent's best player produces a genuinely disproportionate share of his side's threat and the squad trains daily, dedicating two of five sessions to containment is proportionate, because the return per hour is enormous and the identity cost is negligible for a group that has hundreds of hours of its own principles already installed. At the semi-professional level, training three times a week, one session is right. At the amateur level, training once a week, the honest answer is twenty minutes inside the one session, and no more, because a side with fifty rehearsal hours a year cannot spend a fifth of a week on one opponent without damaging the things it does every week.

The identity objection has a real core, and it deserves the concession. A team that reshapes itself around every opponent never builds anything, and young players in particular learn defending as a set of instructions rather than as a skill. The answer is not to refuse to prepare. It is to make the containment plan an addition to your own method rather than a replacement for it. If your side defends in a mid-block with a compact midfield line, the plan against an elite individual should be expressed as two or three adjustments to that mid-block, not as a new shape. Players then arrive on Saturday doing what they always do with three modifications, which is a load a human being can carry. Players who arrive doing something entirely new carry nothing, because under pressure they revert to the habit anyway.

The one-session belief fails for the mirror reason. A plan communicated in a meeting is not installed. It is understood, which is a different and much weaker state. Understanding survives calm; habit survives pressure. The distinction matters most at the moment the plan is under stress, which is exactly the moment it is needed. This is why the week below has four contact points rather than one, and why the shortest of them, the reinforcement session, matters more than its length suggests.

### How long does it take to install a containment plan?

A full week for the first one, meaning four separate contact points plus matchday, because habit forms across exposures rather than within one of them. Sides that train once a week compress it into twenty focused minutes and accept a lower ceiling. Later plans against the same threat type install far faster.

## From Dossier to Three Instructions

The session week begins before anyone reaches the pitch, with a translation step that almost nobody does well. An analyst or a coach has watched the opponent, and what exists is a document: a threat description, a set of clips, a list of tendencies, a sense of where the danger comes from. That document cannot be coached. It has to be reduced first.

The reduction rule is that every containment plan must fit into three instructions, and each instruction must belong to a named player or a named pair. Not three principles, not three themes, three instructions of the form "when this happens, you do this." A back four cannot execute a paragraph. It can execute "when he drops between the lines, Kenny goes with him to the halfway line and no further, and Marcus slides across to cover Kenny's space." That is one instruction with an owner, a trigger, a limit, and a consequence for somebody else.

Building those three from a dossier takes an hour and follows a fixed order. First, identify the single action that produces most of the individual's threat. Not his best quality, his most frequent damaging action. A forward may have exceptional aerial ability and still produce two-thirds of his damage receiving to feet in the left half-space, in which case the aerial quality is a distraction. Second, identify the input that action requires. Every damaging action needs a supply: a specific pass, a specific type of space, a specific moment in the possession cycle. Third, decide which of those inputs you can actually deny given your personnel, which is usually one of them and never all of them. That is instruction one. Instruction two is what happens when instruction one fails, which it will. Instruction three is what you have deliberately chosen to give up so that instructions one and two can be executed with conviction.

That third instruction is the one coaches skip and the one players most need. A defensive plan without an explicit concession is a plan that asks players to defend everything, and a player asked to defend everything defends nothing decisively. Naming the concession out loud, in the meeting, changes behavior on the pitch: a full-back who has been told that the harmless switch to the far side is acceptable will stop chasing it, and stopping chasing it is what keeps him in position for the thing that actually hurts. The mechanics of building that dossier, deciding what to watch and in what order, belong to the analysis end of the series and are set out in [building a match plan against a superstar](/2032/02/15/match-plan-against-a-superstar/). What matters here is the output format, because the format determines whether the pitch work is coachable.

A useful discipline at this stage is to write the three instructions down and store them where the whole staff can see them and where they can be revised between fixtures rather than rebuilt from scratch each time. Most coaches at every level below the professional game rebuild the same dossier repeatedly because the previous one was on a phone, in a notebook, or in somebody's head. Keeping the opposition notes, the clip references, and the resulting session plan together in one place is exactly the kind of unglamorous administration that raises a coach's ceiling over a season, and it is what the free match planner on [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/football-match-planner.html) is built to hold.

## Session One: Ownership and Delay

The first session installs the two behaviors every containment plan rests on, whatever the threat type. The first is ownership, meaning that at every moment one player and only one player is responsible for the individual, and every other player knows who that is. The second is delay, meaning that the owner's first job is almost never to win the ball, it is to prevent the action that hurts for long enough that support arrives.

These are trained together because they fail together. A defender who does not know he owns the man does not delay, he reacts. A defender who tries to win the ball rather than delay it loses ownership the moment he commits, and the hand-off to the next defender happens in chaos rather than by design.

### The organization

Work in a rectangle roughly forty by thirty yards with a full-sized goal at one end and two small target goals at the other. Three defenders, two central and one wide, plus a goalkeeper. Four attackers: one designated as the individual, two supporting attackers wide, one attacking midfielder. The individual starts every repetition in possession, facing the goal, thirty yards out. The attacking side scores in the big goal. The defending side scores by playing through either target goal, which they must do inside six seconds of winning possession.

That last condition matters more than it looks. It stops the defenders from treating a clearance as a success, which is the habit that produces sustained pressure and eventually goals. Making the exit a scoring action forces the head up at the moment of the recovery, and heads-up recovery is what turns containment into a counter.

### The constraint that creates the decision

The single constraint that makes this exercise work is that the individual may not be tackled from the front. Contact is allowed only from the side or from behind once he is moving away from goal. The defender therefore cannot lunge, cannot dive in, and cannot resolve the problem by force. His only tool is body position, and the decision he is forced into is the exact one the plan requires: where to stand so the dangerous action becomes the hard action.

This constraint does three jobs at once. It teaches the shape of the delay, because the only way to slow an attacker you cannot tackle is to angle your body so that his preferred route is longer than his non-preferred route. It removes the dominant failure mode in amateur defending, which is the speculative tackle. And it reduces contact intensity in a phase of the week where a coach wants cognitive load rather than physical collisions, which matters for load management and for safety, and is discussed properly further down.

### The progression

Run it in three stages across the session. In the first stage, the individual is restricted to a single dangerous route, for example he may only attack the outside shoulder. Defenders learn the body position that denies that route while staying goal-side. In the second stage, the restriction comes off and the individual chooses, which converts a rehearsal into a decision and typically halves the defenders' success rate for the first ten minutes. In the third stage, a second attacker is permitted to make a run beyond the individual on either side, which introduces the ownership question: the defender must now delay while tracking whether he has help, and the covering defender must announce himself.

The most instructive moment in this practice arrives in stage three, and it is usually a failure. The owner delays well, the cover arrives, and then both defenders end up on the individual while the second attacker walks into the space behind them and scores. That is not a coaching failure. It is the exercise doing its job, because it has produced the specific error that ruins containment plans in matches, and it has produced it in an environment where it costs a goal in a drill rather than a goal on Saturday.

### The two coaching points

Two, not six. The first is the arrival line, meaning where the covering defender stops. He does not arrive at the ball, he arrives at a position from which he can take over if the owner is beaten, which is roughly five yards behind and inside. The instruction that changes behavior is not "cover him," which every player believes he is already doing, but "stop level with his back heel and stay there until you hear the hand-off."

The second is the hand-off itself, which must be verbal and must be a single word used consistently by the whole squad. The word does not matter. Consistency does. A team that uses three different phrases for the same event executes the hand-off half a second late, and half a second is a shot.

### The small-squad version

With eight outfield players, run three defenders against four attackers with the surplus player rotating in as a server, and cut the area to thirty by twenty-five. With six, run two against three in the same area with one goal and one target gate, and accept that the ownership question becomes a hand-off question between two players rather than three. The exercise degrades gracefully because its content is a duel and a decision, not a shape. Anything that preserves an outnumbered defense, a real finish, and a fast exit preserves the learning.

### The failure signals

Three signs tell a coach this session is not working. Defenders are diving in despite the constraint, which means the constraint is not being enforced and the referee role needs to be taken more seriously. Repetitions are ending in stoppages rather than in shots or exits, which means the ball is being lost out of play too often and the area is the wrong size. And the same defender is being beaten repeatedly without adjusting, which means the coaching point has been delivered as information rather than as a correction he has had to execute immediately.

## Session Two: The Opposed Session and the Overload Player

The second session is the centerpiece of the week and the one that most closely resembles Saturday. It is also the one that requires the most thought before anybody arrives, because it depends on a role that has to be created rather than selected: somebody has to play the superstar.

### Who should play the opponent's best player in training?

Choose for tendencies rather than quality. Your quickest available player simulates a depth runner even if he is a full-back, because the defenders are practicing against the run and not the finish. Give him a specific brief, a genuine advantage in his zone, and a reward for attempting the signature action.

That is the overload player, and the brief is the whole trick. A coach who says "you are the star today, do what you want" gets an inaccurate simulation and a player enjoying himself. A coach who says "you receive only in this channel, you attack the outside shoulder every time you can, and you get a point every time you get a shot away inside the width of the six-yard box" gets a repeatable problem that looks like the real one in the only way that matters, which is the decisions it forces on the defenders.

Choose the overload player for tendencies rather than for quality. The best simulator of a forward whose threat is depth running is your fastest available player, even if he is a full-back and even if his finishing is poor, because the defenders are not practicing against finishing, they are practicing against the run. The best simulator of a player who receives between the lines and turns is whoever on your squad is most comfortable with his back to goal, regardless of his usual position. The threat-type vocabulary that makes this selection quick sits in the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/), and a coach who has classified the opponent into a threat type has already chosen his overload player without knowing it.

There is a second and less obvious rule about the overload player. He must be given a genuine advantage rather than just a title, because a defensive unit that comfortably handles the simulator arrives on Saturday with false confidence, which is worse than arriving with none. The advantage can be numerical, meaning he plays with a permanent spare man in his zone. It can be spatial, meaning he alone may operate in a channel the defenders may not enter until he receives. Or it can be temporal, meaning he is the only player permitted a free touch on receiving. Pick one, not all three, and change which one across the week so defenders do not solve the artefact instead of the problem.

### The organization

Use two-thirds of a pitch with a full goal and goalkeeper at the defending end and two counter-attack gates on the halfway line. The defending side plays with the full defensive unit that will start, which is usually a back four, two central midfielders, and a goalkeeper, plus one wide midfielder on the side the individual favors. The attacking side plays with a superiority of two, including the overload player, and starts every repetition with a build-up from a coach's serve into a deep midfielder, so that the ball arrives in live circulation rather than from a stationary restart.

Repetition length is short by design. Each phase runs until a shot, a defensive exit through a gate, or thirty seconds elapse. The attacking side rotates back to the start immediately. A coach should be aiming for something like twelve to eighteen phases in a block, then a genuine rest, then a second block. Volume in a containment session comes from many short phases and not from a long free-flowing exercise, because the target situation has to recur often enough to become a habit.

### The constraints that create the decisions

Three constraints do most of the work here. The first is the entry rule: the ball must reach the overload player at least once before the attacking side may shoot. This guarantees the defenders face the exact problem in every single phase, rather than facing whatever the game happens to produce, and it is the difference between fifteen relevant repetitions and four.

The second is the goal-value rule. A goal scored after the overload player's signature action counts double, and a goal scored elsewhere counts one. Defenders very quickly stop treating all threats as equal, which is precisely the behavior the plan requires. It also means the attacking side keeps trying the dangerous action rather than taking the easy one, which keeps the simulation honest.

The third is the exit rule carried over from session one, tightened. The defending side scores by playing through a halfway-line gate within six seconds and with a minimum of two passes. Two passes rather than one stops the long clearance from counting and forces the recovery to be organized, which is where a containment plan turns into points.

### The progression

Begin with the entry rule enforced and the overload player restricted to his signature action, which lets defenders build the pattern with certainty. Then release the restriction so that he may take either option, which is the moment the practice becomes genuinely hard and where most of the learning sits. Then add a substitution wrinkle: after every four phases, the coach calls a switch and the overload role moves to a different attacker on the other side of the pitch. This rehearses the single most under-practiced element of containment, which is what happens when the opponent moves. Elite individuals do not stay where the dossier put them, and a defensive unit that has only ever rehearsed the plan on one side collapses when the individual drifts to the other.

The final progression, used only when the first three are secure, removes the entry rule and lets the attacking side play freely with the overload still in place. This is the closest thing to the fixture, and its purpose is diagnostic rather than instructional. The coach is watching for whether the defenders still prioritize correctly when they are not being forced to.

### The two coaching points

The first is the pass before the pass. Defenders instinctively watch the man they are marking, which means they react to the ball's arrival rather than anticipating it. The correction that changes behavior is to name the specific preceding action that signals the entry pass is coming, and to demand that the owner adjusts his body position on that action rather than on the pass itself. In most build-up structures that trigger is the moment the ball travels backwards or sideways into a position from which the entry pass becomes available, and it buys the defender roughly a second, which is the difference between arriving with the forward and arriving after him.

The second is the shape of the double. Doubling up on an elite individual is the most common instruction in football and the most commonly botched, because two defenders converging on the same shoulder is not a double, it is a queue. A functioning double has one player taking the space the individual wants to move into and one player taking the ball. Which of the two arrives first depends on where the individual receives, and rehearsing that split is worth more than any amount of general instruction to "get two around him."

### The small-squad version

With twelve outfield players, run seven defending against nine on two-thirds of a pitch by using the goalkeeper as an outfield player for the attacking build-up. With ten, cut to a back four plus one midfielder against six on half a pitch, and drop the counter gates to one central gate. With eight, the honest answer is to abandon the full-unit simulation and run a repeated three against four in the danger zone instead, accepting that the collective sliding cannot be rehearsed and that the duel and the double can. A coach with eight players who insists on running an eleven-a-side shape rehearsal is choosing the wrong half of the plan.

### The failure signals

Watch for four things. If the overload player is not getting the ball, the entry rule is not being enforced or the attacking build-up is too slow, and the practice is producing three relevant moments instead of fifteen. If the defenders are winning almost everything, the advantage given to the overload player is too small and confidence is being manufactured rather than earned. If phases are running long, the exit rule is being ignored and the practice has become a possession exercise. And if the coach is stopping play more than once every three or four phases, the instruction is being delivered in a way that prevents the very rhythm the practice exists to create.

## Session Three: Transitions and Set Pieces

The third session covers the two phases in which containment plans are most often undone, and it is the session most likely to be cut when the week gets busy. Cutting it is a mistake with a predictable cost, because an elite individual's most damaging moments frequently arrive when the defensive structure that was supposed to contain him does not exist yet.

### The transition problem

A containment plan is a description of an organized defensive block. The moment your side loses the ball in the opponent's half, that block is somewhere else, and the individual is running into space against whoever happens to be nearest. Every instruction in the plan is temporarily void. What replaces it has to be simpler, and it has to be rehearsed separately, because it is a different behavior rather than a faster version of the same one.

The instruction that works is a rest-defense assignment tied to the individual by name rather than by zone. One player, usually a holding midfielder or the far-side centre-back depending on where the individual starts, is responsible for his position at all times while your side has the ball. His job in possession is not to defend, it is to stand somewhere that makes the first pass to the individual unattractive. This is the least glamorous instruction in football and one of the highest-return, because it converts a large number of dangerous transitions into ordinary ones before they begin.

### The organization

Work on a full pitch width, three-quarters length, with the defending side in a genuine attacking structure rather than a defensive one, because the exercise starts from possession. A coach or a neutral server plays balls into your attacking third and the side attacks a goal. At a whistle, the ball is played immediately to the opposition's designated individual, who is stationed in the space he actually attacks, and the phase becomes a live counter-attack against whatever your structure has left behind.

The value of the whistle-and-serve method is that it produces the transition on demand rather than waiting for a turnover, which means twenty repetitions in twenty-five minutes rather than five. It also lets the coach choose the moment of the turnover: after a cross, after a wide combination, after a shot, after a corner, each of which leaves a different structure exposed and each of which the individual attacks differently.

### The constraint that creates the decision

The constraint here is a countdown. The defending side has five seconds from the whistle to make one of three things happen: win the ball back, force the individual sideways or backwards, or commit a foul in a location where the resulting free kick is not dangerous. If none of the three happen within five seconds, the attacking side gets a free shot from the edge of the area regardless of where play has reached.

That last condition is the consequence mechanism, and it converts a vague instruction about "getting back" into a race with a defined finish. It also makes the third option explicit, which coaches are often shy about and should not be. Stopping a counter-attack with a foul in a harmless area is a legitimate, legal part of professional defending, and it should be coached as a skill with clear boundaries: a foul is a controlled interruption, it must not involve the head or the standing leg, it must never be a challenge from behind at speed, and any player who cannot execute it safely should not be given the instruction. A containment plan is never a license for a reckless challenge, and a coach who blurs that line is coaching something other than football.

### The set-piece dimension

Elite individuals score a substantial share of their goals from dead balls, either as takers or as targets, and a containment plan that ignores the set piece has left a large hole in an otherwise good week. The set-piece work in this session is short and specific rather than comprehensive.

If the individual is a taker, the work is on the wall, the goalkeeper's start position, and the first defender's speed of closing on the short routine. If he is a target, the work is the marking assignment and, more importantly, the assignment for the player who blocks his run-up rather than the player who marks his landing spot. Elite aerial threats are beaten at the start of the run far more often than at the point of contact, and the drill that matters is a repeated ten-yard run against a defender whose only job is to be in the way legally and early.

The second-ball dimension deserves its own five minutes. A side that defends the first contact well and then loses the second ball has not defended the set piece, and the individual who scored the rebound is usually the one the plan spent all week worrying about. Rehearse the two players whose job is the edge of the area, and rehearse them with a live server hitting balls into the box at genuine pace rather than with a coach lobbing them in gently.

### The two coaching points

First, the rest-defense position is not a location, it is a relationship. The player responsible for the individual in possession should be able to see both the ball and the man without turning his head, which usually means standing several yards further from goal and further inside than instinct suggests. The instruction that changes behavior is to demand that he can name where the individual is at any moment the coach asks, and to ask, repeatedly, during the exercise.

Second, in the recovery run, the first defender back must not aim for the ball. He must aim for the inside line, meaning the route between the individual and the center of the goal, and accept that the individual keeps the ball. Recovering defenders who chase the ball get eliminated and produce the situation where the goalkeeper is exposed. Recovering defenders who take the inside line turn a clear chance into a bad angle, which is the whole objective.

### The small-squad version

With ten players, run the transition phase as six against four with the four representing only the counter-attacking threat, and use cones to mark where the missing attackers would be. With eight, drop the attacking build-up entirely and start each repetition with the defending players in a deliberately disorganized position, facing away from goal, then serve the ball to the individual and let them recover. It is a cruder exercise and it still trains the two things that matter, which are the inside line and the countdown.

### The failure signals

If the defenders are consistently beating the countdown with ease, the serve is too slow or the individual is starting too deep. If fouls are becoming reckless or contact is escalating, the exercise must be stopped and reset immediately, and the intensity brought down, because a training-ground injury in the week of a big fixture is a far larger cost than any tactical gain. And if the set-piece block is turning into twenty minutes of standing still, it has become a walkthrough and has lost fidelity.

## Session Four: The Short Reinforcement

The fourth contact point is the shortest and the most frequently mishandled. It happens on the day before the fixture or, in a compressed schedule, on the morning of it, and its purpose is not to teach anything. Nothing new can be installed here, and attempting to install something new is the most common error in the whole week.

Its purpose is retrieval. Human beings retain instructions better after a short, low-load recall than after a long high-load repetition, and a defensive plan that was installed on a Tuesday and never touched again arrives on Saturday degraded. Twelve minutes of correct recall is worth more than an hour of new work.

The format is a rehearsal of the three instructions at low physical intensity but full cognitive intensity. Set the defensive unit against an attacking group at walking or jogging pace, present the trigger situations one at a time, and ask the responsible player to name his instruction out loud before executing it. This is one of the very few places where an unopposed or lightly opposed exercise is the correct choice, and the reason is that the target here is memory rather than skill. The decision-fidelity standard governs skill acquisition. It does not govern recall, and a coach who insists on full-intensity opposed work the day before a fixture is following the principle past the point where it applies.

Two additions make the reinforcement more effective. The first is to run the trigger situations out of order and unannounced, so that recognition is being tested rather than sequence. The second is to include one situation that is not in the plan at all, and to see what the players do, because the honest answer to "what happens when the opponent does something we did not prepare for" is a question worth asking on Friday rather than discovering on Saturday.

Keep the physical load low. This is a day for movement quality, short accelerations, and a proper finish rather than for duels, and any collision work should be deliberately excluded. The general principles of tapering into a fixture, along with warm-up structures for sides that will be asked for repeated high-intensity duels, are the readiness side of the same problem and can be planned with [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/football-match-planner.html).

## Matchday: The Reminder, the Warm-Up, and the First Ten Minutes

The final contact point is not a session, and treating it as one damages the week. Matchday has three jobs: reduce the plan to a form a player can hold under adrenaline, prepare the body for the specific physical demands the plan creates, and manage the opening ten minutes, which is when the plan is most likely to be abandoned.

The reminder is delivered individually rather than to the group. The centre-back who owns the individual gets his instruction, spoken to him, in one sentence, with his name in it. The covering player gets his. The goalkeeper gets his. Group instructions on matchday dissolve because every player hears them as being about somebody else. A team meeting can restate the shape and the concession, but the three instructions belong to their owners and should be handed over person to person.

The warm-up matters more before this kind of fixture than before an ordinary one, because a containment plan asks specific players for repeated maximal accelerations, decelerations, and changes of direction under contact, sometimes within the first minute. The general principle is that the warm-up should include progressive exposure to the actions the plan will demand, meaning short accelerations building to near-maximal, deceleration and re-acceleration work, and a small amount of controlled shoulder-to-shoulder contact at the end rather than the beginning. Specific protocols and progressions belong to a qualified physical preparation professional, and any player with a current or recent injury concern should be managed by qualified medical staff rather than by a tactical plan.

The first ten minutes deserve an explicit instruction of their own, because this is where containment plans die. The individual will do something impressive early. He will beat his marker once, or receive in a pocket the plan was supposed to close, and at that moment the whole defensive unit will feel that the plan does not work. It almost always does work; a plan that reduces an elite player's threat by a third is a resounding success and still leaves him beating you occasionally. Telling players this in advance, in plain terms, is one of the highest-value sentences a coach delivers all week: he is going to get his moments, the plan is about how many, and nobody changes anything for the first twenty minutes.

The psychological dimension of facing a famous opponent, and the way a squad's belief moves during a fixture like this, is a subject in its own right and is treated properly in [the psychology of facing a famous team](/2032/05/01/psychology-of-facing-famous-teams/). For the purposes of the training week, one practical rule covers most of it: the more clearly a player knows what he is responsible for, the less the reputation of the man opposite him affects his behavior. Clarity is the antidote to awe, and clarity is produced on the training pitch rather than in the dressing room.

## Coaching Points That Change Behavior and Coaching Points That Do Not

A coaching point is not information. It is an intervention intended to change what a player does the next time the situation occurs, and most of what coaches say fails that test while sounding excellent.

The interventions that fail share a family resemblance. They describe outcomes rather than actions: "do not let him turn" tells a defender what should not happen, not what he should do differently. They arrive too late: a correction delivered forty seconds after the event lands on a player who is already thinking about something else. They arrive during the action, which is the most common error of all, because a defender processing a shouted instruction is a defender not processing the attacker in front of him. And they are plural: three corrections delivered at once produce zero changes, since a player under load can hold one.

The interventions that work are specific, physical, and immediate. Specific means naming a body part, a foot, an angle, or a distance rather than a concept: "left shoulder in front of his right" rather than "get tight." Physical means the correction can be executed rather than merely understood, which is the difference between "be more aggressive" and "your first step is backwards, make it sideways." Immediate means the repetition is stopped or a fresh one is started within a few seconds, while the sensation is still available to the player.

There is a fourth property that separates good coaching from adequate coaching, and it is the hardest to apply. The correction should be given as a question when the player has the answer available and as an instruction when he does not. A defender who has been beaten by the same move twice and can articulate why usually needs the question, because the retrieval makes it stick. A defender who does not know what happened needs the instruction, because a question to a player who cannot answer produces embarrassment and no learning. Coaches who use one mode exclusively are half as effective as coaches who choose.

### What separates a useful correction from a useless one?

A useful correction names a body position, a distance, or a first step, and it lands within seconds of the event rather than during it. A useless one describes an outcome. Naming the shoulder that must be in front changes behavior; telling a defender not to let his man turn changes nothing.

One more practical rule governs the whole week. Decide before the session which two coaching points you will make and write them on your hand or your board. A coach who arrives without that decision coaches whatever he happens to see, which across an hour amounts to eleven different messages and one confused defensive unit. The grid above allocates two per contact point on purpose, and holding to that discipline is worth more than any individual insight a coach might have on the day.

## The Goalkeeper in a Containment Session

Goalkeepers are the most commonly forgotten participants in defensive preparation, and the omission is expensive. A containment plan changes the goalkeeper's job in three specific ways, and none of the three are obvious to a keeper who has not been included in the work.

The first is starting position. A plan that deliberately concedes the wide area and protects the center requires a different keeper position from a plan that pushes the defensive line high and accepts the ball in behind. Against an individual whose threat is the run in behind, the keeper is effectively a sweeper and his starting depth determines whether the defensive line can hold ten yards higher, which in turn determines whether the plan works at all. That is a conversation, and it has to happen on the pitch during the work rather than in a corridor afterwards.

The second is the near-post decision. Many elite individuals have a signature shooting position, and the keeper's positioning against that position is a specific technical choice with a specific cost elsewhere. A keeper who covers the favored side more heavily is deliberately conceding the other, and he should know that the outfield players have been told this and are covering accordingly. A defensive unit and a goalkeeper making opposite assumptions about which side is being protected is one of the most reliable ways to concede a goal that looks like nobody's fault.

The third is communication. The keeper is the only player who can see the individual, the ball, and the defensive line at once, and in a containment plan he is the natural owner of two calls: the announcement that the individual has moved to a different zone, and the hand-off trigger when the covering defender is in position. Building those calls into the sessions means the keeper is practicing them, and the outfield players are practicing hearing them, which is a real skill that does not appear by itself on Saturday.

Including the keeper costs nothing in session design terms, since every exercise above already has a goal and a goalkeeper in it. What it costs is coaching attention, which is why it gets dropped. The correction is simply to allocate one of the two coaching points in at least one contact point in the week to the goalkeeper, and to make the transfer test include a keeper behavior.

## The Amateur, Youth, and School Versions

Everything above assumes a squad that trains several times a week on a decent surface with enough players to run an eleven against thirteen. Most coaches reading this have none of those things, and the honest translation of the week is not a scaled-down copy of it. It is a different selection of the same content.

### What do you cut when you only have ten players?

Keep the duel and drop the collective shape. Run a repeated outnumbered exercise in the danger zone with a real goal and a real finish. Ownership, delay, the hand-off, and the double all survive at three against four. A back four sliding as a unit does not, and pretending otherwise wastes the evening.

The one-session-a-week side gets twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes go to session one. That choice is deliberate and worth defending. Ownership and delay transfer to every fixture the side will ever play, against every opponent, which means the time spent on them is never wasted even if the specific plan fails. The opposed simulation, by contrast, is opponent-specific and expensive, and a side with fifty rehearsal hours a year cannot afford to spend them on a problem that appears once. A coach at this level who has a strong individual to face should install ownership and delay in the twenty minutes, deliver the three instructions verbally before the fixture, and accept that the rest is beyond his time budget. The broader argument about which elite ideas survive the journey down to amateur football, ranked by return per hour of pitch time, is made in [amateur teams using elite counter-plans](/2032/05/15/amateur-teams-elite-counter-plans/).

The poor-pitch version changes the constraints rather than the exercises. On a heavy or uneven surface, ground passes into feet are unreliable, so the entry rule in session two should permit the serve to be thrown or driven rather than passed, and the exit rule should be widened from two passes to one pass plus a carry. Defenders should not be asked to make sliding recoveries on a bad surface, and any exercise that produces repeated sliding challenges on hard or rutted ground should be modified or stopped, since the injury exposure is not worth the tactical content.

The school version is twenty minutes with two constraints and one instruction. Play four against six towards one goal, forbid tackling from the front, and tell the defenders that their job is to make the attackers go sideways. That is it. Every principle in this article is present in that exercise in a form that children can hold, and adding anything else reduces its effect. Where the group is young, the coach should also drop the countdown mechanism, because a race with a punishment attached produces rushed, high-risk challenges from players who have not yet developed the technique to make them safely.

The youth version generally requires three modifications regardless of the specific age group. Contact work is progressive and introduced last, with shoulder-to-shoulder before anything else and nothing that resembles a challenge from behind at speed. Volume is lower and rest is longer, because the exercises above are neuromuscularly demanding rather than aerobically demanding, and it is the accelerations and decelerations rather than the running that accumulate. And the language is developmental rather than corrective: a young defender who is beaten repeatedly by a stronger, quicker teammate playing the overload role needs a coach who reframes the objective towards delay rather than one who keeps announcing that he has been beaten. Decisions about training volume for growing players, and about any player carrying an injury concern, belong to qualified medical and physical preparation staff, not to a tactical plan.

### What a player practices on his own

The individual work is smaller than players expect and more useful than they believe. Three things carry: the first step, the body angle, and the recovery run.

The first step is the one that decides most duels and the one nobody trains. A defender's instinctive first movement when an attacker takes a touch towards him is backwards, which surrenders the yard that determines everything afterwards. The practice is a simple reaction exercise against a partner or a wall: react to a signal by moving sideways rather than backwards, twenty repetitions, both directions, at genuine intensity. Two minutes, three times a week, changes a habit that fifteen years of matches has installed.

The body angle is trained by walking backwards, and the exercise is exactly as unglamorous as it sounds: retreat at an angle with one shoulder consistently ahead, hold the position while an imaginary attacker takes an outside route, and check that the hips stay open enough to turn without crossing the feet. It looks absurd and it works, because the fault is almost always in the hips rather than in the feet. A player who films himself doing it once will usually find that his hips square up within three steps, which is the whole reason he keeps getting turned.

None of these three cost equipment, a partner, or a pitch, which matters because the players who most need them are the ones with the least access to organized coaching. A defender who does two minutes of first-step work and six curved recovery runs a week is doing more for his defending than an hour of the shuttle running most players substitute for it.

The recovery run is trained as a curve rather than a sprint. Straight-line speed matters less than the shape of the line taken, and a player who practices accelerating on a curved path towards the inside line will beat a faster player who runs straight at the ball. Six repetitions after a session, once a week, and the pattern becomes automatic.

## Load, Intensity, and Safety Through a Big Fixture Week

A containment week is physically distinctive, and coaches who plan it as though it were an ordinary week arrive at the fixture with tired defenders, which is the one outcome guaranteed to make the plan fail. The distinctive feature is not total running volume. It is the density of accelerations, decelerations, changes of direction, and contact, all of which sit in the same physical category and all of which accumulate faster than the aerobic work coaches habitually monitor.

The general shape that works is a rise and a taper. The heaviest duel work sits early in the week, far enough from the fixture that the neuromuscular cost has time to resolve. The middle of the week carries the opposed simulation, which is intense in short bursts with real recovery between phases rather than continuous. The day before is deliberately light and cognitive rather than physical. Where a side plays twice in a week, the whole structure compresses and something has to be cut, and the honest order of cutting is the transition and set-piece work first, the opposed simulation second, and the principle work never, because the principle work is the part that transfers to every fixture.

Several safety points apply to every exercise in this article, and they are not optional. Duel and contact work must be introduced progressively within a session rather than at full intensity from the first repetition, and any contact element belongs at the end of a properly prepared warm-up rather than at the beginning. Challenges from behind at speed have no place in a training exercise at any level. Sliding recoveries should not be rehearsed repeatedly on hard, frozen, or rutted surfaces. Where an exercise begins to escalate, meaning that the competitive element is producing challenges the coach would not accept in a match, the correct response is to stop it immediately rather than to manage it, since the cost of a training-ground injury in a fixture week is far larger than any tactical benefit the remaining ten minutes might deliver.

None of the guidance here is individual medical advice, and it is not a substitute for qualified input. Decisions about how much load a specific player can absorb, whether a player carrying a niggle should participate in duel work, and when a player returns from injury belong to qualified medical professionals who know that player. A coach's job in this area is to build a structure that makes those decisions easier to implement, which means planning the week in advance rather than deciding on the day, and keeping a record of what the squad has actually done rather than what he intended them to do. That record-keeping, and the general readiness and injury-prevention material that sits alongside it, is what [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/football-match-planner.html) exists to support, while the tactical dossier and the session plans themselves are worth storing together on [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/football-match-planner.html) so the whole week can be reviewed and rebuilt rather than reinvented.

One further physical point is specific to containment and rarely discussed. The plan usually asks one or two players to do far more running and far more maximal work than the rest of the side, because they are the ones tracking, doubling, and recovering. A full-back given a containment instruction against a genuinely elite winger may cover his usual distance with double the number of high-intensity efforts. That has consequences for how long he lasts, which means the plan should include an explicit answer to what happens when he fades, and it should be rehearsed. A plan whose owner is substituted in the seventieth minute and whose replacement has never practiced the instruction is a plan with a seventy-minute lifespan.

## How to Know the Work Transferred

The honest test is the part of the week that almost nobody does, and without it a coach is left with impressions, which are unreliable in exactly the direction that flatters the coach.

Do not measure the outcome. Whether the individual scored is the worst available metric, because it is dominated by variance and by things outside the plan: a deflection, a refereeing decision, an error by a player who was executing correctly. A plan can be executed perfectly and lose, and a plan can be ignored entirely and win, and a coach who reviews on the result learns the wrong lesson in both cases.

Measure the behaviors instead, and choose them before the fixture rather than after it. Three or four are enough, and they should be the specific actions the sessions were designed to produce. Did the owner adjust his body position on the preceding action rather than on the entry pass, and how often. Did the covering defender stop at the arrival line rather than continuing to the ball. Was the hand-off word audible. Did the rest-defense player hold his position through the attacking phases, or did he drift forward when the ball was in the final third. Each of those is countable from a single viewing of the fixture, or from the touchline by an assistant with a notebook, and each maps directly onto a session.

The interpretation matters as much as the count. A behavior that appeared consistently in training and disappeared in the fixture tells you the practice lacked fidelity, most often consequence or speed, and the session design needs changing. A behavior that appeared in the first half and disappeared in the second tells you it is not yet a habit and needs another week rather than a redesign. A behavior that never appeared at all, in training or in the fixture, tells you the instruction was never understood, which is a communication problem rather than a coaching problem and is usually fixed by making the instruction shorter and giving it an owner.

### What should a coach measure after the fixture?

Behaviors rather than outcomes. Choose three specific actions the week was built to produce, then count them. Actions that appear in rehearsal and vanish under real pressure indicate a practice that lacked consequence or speed. Actions that appear early and fade indicate something not yet installed as habit.

There is a second, cruder test that costs nothing and is surprisingly informative. Ask the three owners, individually, on the following training day, what their instruction was. Not what the plan was, what their instruction was. If any of them cannot say it in one sentence, the instruction was too long, and the week's real failure happened in the translation step rather than on the pitch.

## The Mistakes That Ruin a Containment Session

Some errors reduce the value of the work. These ones destroy it, and every coach reading this has made at least three of them.

Rehearsing the shape without opposition and calling it preparation is the largest. It fails the decision-fidelity standard on two of three components and produces players who can describe the plan and cannot execute it. Running everything at unmatchable intensity is the second, and it is subtler, because it looks like the opposite of the first error: an exercise so demanding that players cannot execute the technique teaches them that the instruction does not work, which is worse than teaching them nothing.

Delivering coaching points during the action is the third and the most common of all. A player mid-duel cannot process a shouted correction, and the shout usually causes the error the coach then corrects. The discipline is to let the repetition finish, then intervene inside a few seconds.

Making the exercise a competition between friends rather than a rehearsal of a problem is the fourth. When the overload player is a teammate with something to prove and the defender is a teammate who does not want to be embarrassed, the intensity goes up and the fidelity goes down, because both are now playing to win an argument rather than to reproduce Saturday. The coach's job is to keep the brief in front of both of them.

Ignoring the goalkeeper is the fifth, and the cost of it appears on Saturday as a goal that nobody can explain. Changing the plan on the Friday is the sixth, and it is almost always driven by a coach's anxiety rather than by new information: a plan installed across a week and altered the day before arrives as two half-plans. Preparing for the individual and forgetting his teammates is the seventh, and it is the specific danger of a week like this. An elite individual almost always plays alongside other good players, and a defensive unit that has spent five days thinking about one man can be beaten comfortably by the number eight arriving unmarked at the far post.

The eighth is subtler and belongs to good coaches rather than poor ones. It is over-instruction: a plan with seven components, each defensible, delivered to players who can hold three. Every additional instruction reduces the execution quality of the others, and the coach who adds the seventh has usually just removed the effectiveness of the first. If a plan cannot survive being cut to three instructions, it is not a plan, it is a wish list.

## Adapting the Week to the Threat Type

The five contact points stay the same whoever you are facing. What changes is the overload player's brief, the constraint that creates the decision, and the transfer test. Four threat types cover most elite individuals, and each demands a different rehearsal even though the framework is identical.

Against the depth runner, whose damage comes from attacking the space behind a defensive line, the overload player's brief is to make one run per phase and to make it early, and the constraint that matters is a line marked on the grass that the defending unit must hold until the ball is played backwards. The decision being rehearsed is the drop trigger: when does the line retreat and who calls it. The transfer test is whether the line moved together on a call rather than individually on instinct, which is visible from the touchline in a single viewing. This is the type where the goalkeeper's starting depth is decisive, and where the plan's concession is usually possession in front of the block.

Against the receiver between the lines, whose damage comes from taking the ball on the half-turn in the pocket, the overload player's brief is to check into the space at the moment the ball goes sideways, and the constraint is that he may not be tackled until he has turned, which forces the defenders to solve the problem before the reception rather than after it. The decision is the step: does the centre-back come out, does the holding midfielder drop, or does the ball-side midfielder screen. The transfer test is whether the same player made that decision each time. Ambiguity here is the whole danger, and a week that produces two different players stepping at different moments has failed even if neither individual choice was wrong.

Against the wide isolator, whose damage comes from receiving in space one against one near the touchline, the overload player's brief is to demand the ball wide and attack the full-back immediately, and the constraint is a channel the covering midfielder may not enter until the isolator takes his first touch. The decision is the show, meaning whether the defender forces him inside towards the covering player or outside towards the byline, and the honest answer varies by opponent and by your own personnel. The transfer test is consistency: the plan fails when the full-back shows inside and the midfielder covers outside, which happens whenever the pair have not rehearsed together.

Against the aerial and box threat, whose damage comes from arriving into the penalty area, the work moves into the box and the overload brief is to attack a specific zone repeatedly from a live cross. The constraint is that the defenders must clear beyond a marked line to score a point, which prevents the weak clearance that produces the second-phase goal. The decision is the choice between marking the man and attacking the ball, and the transfer test is the location of the first contact rather than whether the header was won. A defender who wins nothing but consistently makes contact five yards in front of the target has executed the plan.

Most elite individuals are more than one of these, which is precisely what makes them elite, and this is where the concession instruction earns its place. A forward who is both a depth runner and a box threat cannot be defended against both simultaneously, since the line height that denies one invites the other. The week must pick, and the pick should follow the frequency of the damaging action rather than its spectacular quality. The way that trade-off resolves for two specific and very different elite forwards is worked through in [how to stop Lionel Messi](/2020/06/15/how-to-stop-lionel-messi/) and [how to stop Cristiano Ronaldo](/2020/08/15/how-to-stop-cristiano-ronaldo/), and the way the same individual's threat type shifts across a career, which changes the whole plan without changing the name on the team sheet, is covered in [how Messi and Ronaldo aged differently](/2020/10/01/messi-ronaldo-aging-comparison/).

## What Changes If You Defend High Rather Than Deep

The exercises above are written so that they work at any block height, but the block height a side actually uses changes which of them matter and how they are set up. A coach who runs the same containment week regardless of whether his team presses at halfway or defends the edge of its own area is rehearsing somebody else's problem.

If your side presses high, the containment plan is mostly a plan about the ball's supply rather than about the individual. An elite player starved of clean entries in the middle third cannot hurt you with anything except a ball over the top, which is why the pressing side's real containment work is a screen assignment and a rest-defense position rather than a duel. In rehearsal terms that shifts the emphasis heavily towards session three, since a high line lives and dies on transitions, and it changes the constraint in session two: instead of guaranteeing that the ball reaches the designated opponent, the defending side scores points for preventing the entry pass entirely across a phase. The decision being trained is the trigger, meaning who presses the passer and when, and the transfer test is whether the screening midfielder held his position between ball and man rather than pressing the ball himself.

If your side defends in a mid-block, which covers most teams at most levels, the week as written is the correct week. The individual will receive, the duel will happen, and the plan is about where that reception occurs and who owns it. This is the block height where ownership, delay, the arrival line, and the hand-off carry the largest share of the value, and where the split shape of the double is worth the practice time.

If your side defends deep, the whole problem changes character, and coaches routinely underestimate how much. In a low block the individual receives in space almost every time, so denying reception is not available and the plan becomes entirely about what he is allowed to do after he receives. The rehearsal moves inside the penalty area and just outside it, the constraint changes from delay to angle, meaning the defender's job is to make the shot or pass he wants unavailable rather than to prevent the ball arriving, and the practice needs many more repetitions of the crowded box than of the open duel. The second-ball work in session three becomes the most important twenty minutes of the entire week, because a deep block concedes shots by design and the goals come from what happens after the first save or block.

There is a common error attached to all three, and it is worth stating plainly. Coaches frequently write a containment plan whose block height contradicts the block height their team actually uses, usually because the plan was copied from a fixture they watched. A side that defends high all season and drops into a low block for one fixture because the opponent has a famous forward has not implemented a plan, it has introduced an unrehearsed system in the week of its hardest match, and it will execute both badly. If the block height has to change, that change is the plan, it consumes the whole week, and the individual instructions have to be dropped to make room. That trade-off is a real one and a coach should make it consciously rather than discovering halfway through Thursday that he has asked for two installations at once.

The related question of how much a side should compromise its own method against a stronger opponent, and where the honest limits sit for a squad that is simply outmatched, belongs to the underdog problem and is treated in [coaching underdogs against better teams](/2032/04/15/coaching-underdogs-guide/). The point for session design is narrower: pick the block height first, then build the containment week inside it, and never let the plan for one player quietly rewrite the way the team defends.

## The Meeting That Precedes the Pitch Work

The three instructions have to be delivered somewhere, and the delivery is part of the session design rather than a preliminary to it. Most defensive meetings are too long, too visual, and too general, and the resulting pitch work spends its first fifteen minutes repairing the confusion.

Keep it under ten minutes. The purpose is not to prove the analysis, it is to hand over the instructions, and a long presentation of evidence has the perverse effect of making the individual seem larger rather than more solvable. Show the problem twice rather than eight times: one clip of the damaging action working, one clip of it being prevented, which is available for every player who has ever lived and which almost nobody bothers to find. The second clip does more work than the first, because it converts the opponent from an inevitability into a problem with a known solution.

State the concession explicitly and early. A group told that the plan gives up the harmless switch, the deep possession, or the far-post cross behaves differently from a group left to infer it, and the inference is always the same wrong one, which is that they are expected to stop everything. Then hand the instructions to their owners by name, in front of the group, so that everyone knows who is responsible for what. Public assignment matters: it removes the ambiguity that causes two players to step at once and neither to hold.

Close the meeting with the twenty-minute rule and then stop talking. The last thing a defensive unit should hear before it walks onto the training pitch is that the individual will get his moments and that nobody changes anything early, because that sentence is what keeps the plan alive at the moment it is most likely to be thrown away.

A practical addition for coaches working alone, which is most coaches. Write the three instructions, the concession, and the two coaching points for each session on a single page before the week starts, and carry that page to every practice. It is the difference between a week that builds and a week in which four sessions each pursue whatever seemed important that day, and it takes twenty minutes to produce. Keeping the page with the dossier and the clips in one place, where it can be revised for the next fixture rather than rebuilt, is precisely the administrative habit that separates coaches who improve across a season from coaches who repeat one.

## Reviewing the Week and Building the Next One

The week does not end at the final whistle, and the review is where the compounding happens. Two reviews are worth running, and they answer different questions.

The first is the behavior review described above, run within a day or two while the fixture is still available in memory and on video. It asks whether the players did what the sessions were designed to make them do, and its output is a judgement about the practices rather than about the players.

The second is a design review, run once every several fixtures rather than every time, and it asks a harder question: which exercises in your library actually produce change. Most coaches accumulate practices and never retire any, which means the weakest exercises survive on familiarity. Grade each one against the decision-fidelity standard, honestly, and delete the ones that fail two of the three components. The typical coach's library shrinks by a third in this exercise, and the remaining week gets substantially better, because the deleted time goes to the practices that worked.

There is one more compounding effect worth naming. The instructions in a containment plan are reusable across opponents of the same threat type. A side that has installed the drop trigger against one depth runner needs a fraction of the time to install it against the next, and by the third or fourth such fixture the plan is a reminder rather than an installation. This is why the proportionality rule loosens over a season: the first bespoke week is expensive, and the fifth is cheap, provided the earlier ones were recorded rather than remembered. A coach who keeps his sessions, his dossiers, and his behavior reviews in one place is building an asset; a coach who keeps them in his head is starting from zero every time and wondering why the work never seems to accumulate.

## The Arguments Worth Answering

Six positions come up whenever this subject is discussed, in coaching rooms and in football discussion generally, and each deserves a verdict rather than a shrug.

The first is that preparing for a specific opponent is negative coaching. The verdict is that it is negative only when it replaces your method rather than adding to it. A side that arrives with its own principles plus three adjustments is not being negative, it is being informed, and the alternative position, which is that you should ignore what the opponent does best, is not bravery, it is a decision to be surprised. The legitimate core of the objection concerns young players, and there the answer is real: at development ages the ratio should tilt heavily towards your own principles, because the point of those years is to build players rather than to win a specific fixture.

The second is that amateur sides cannot rehearse anything this specific. The verdict is that they cannot rehearse the collective pattern and they can rehearse the duel, and the duel is the larger share of the value. An amateur side that installs ownership and delay will defend better against every opponent for the rest of the season. The false step in the objection is treating the plan as all or nothing, when the components have very different costs.

The third is that drills never transfer. The verdict is that badly designed exercises never transfer, and the observation behind the complaint is accurate, which is why the decision-fidelity standard exists. A practice that reproduces the decision, the speed, and the consequence transfers reliably enough to be measurable within one week. A practice that reproduces the pattern only transfers to other practices.

The fourth is that unopposed shape work is a waste of time. The verdict is that it is a waste of time as skill acquisition and valuable as recall, which is why it appears in this week exactly once, on the day before the fixture, where its properties are an advantage rather than a defect. A coach who uses unopposed work to install a behavior is misusing a tool; a coach who bans it entirely has thrown away the cheapest retrieval mechanism available.

The fifth is that players stop thinking when they are given a plan. The verdict is the reverse, provided the plan is short. Three clear instructions free a player to attend to everything else, because the recurring decisions are already settled and his attention is available for the situation in front of him. What genuinely does stop players thinking is a long plan, since a player attempting to recall the seventh instruction is not watching the ball. The objection is really an argument against over-instruction, and in that form it is correct.

The sixth is that rehearsing man-marking is obsolete. The verdict is that pure man-marking as a system is largely gone at elite level and that the individual skills it required have not gone anywhere. Every zonal system produces moments where one player owns one opponent for several seconds, and the player who has never practiced that job executes it poorly. What is obsolete is the assumption that ownership means following a man everywhere. What is not obsolete is knowing how to own him for the six seconds that matter, which is what the hand-off and the arrival line exist to teach.

## Closing Verdict

The week described here works because it is built around one standard rather than around a set of favorite exercises. A containment drill is valid only when a player who chooses wrongly inside it suffers what he would suffer on Saturday, which means the decision has to be real, the speed has to be genuine, and something has to be at stake. Apply that standard honestly and most of a typical defensive week gets deleted, which is the point: what remains is short, hard, repetitive, and it changes behavior.

The rest of the structure follows from it. Three instructions with named owners, because that is what a human being holds under pressure. Four contact points rather than one, because understanding is not habit. An overload player with a real advantage and a specific brief, because a simulation the defenders beat comfortably is a confidence trick. A transfer test chosen before the fixture, because impressions flatter the coach who formed them. An explicit concession, because a unit asked to defend everything defends nothing with conviction. And a load plan that respects what the work actually costs, with the medical decisions left to the people qualified to make them.

Two final judgements are worth stating plainly. The first is that the containment plan is never the reason a side wins; it is the reason a side is still in the fixture at the point where its own qualities can decide it. Coaches who expect a plan to neutralize an elite player entirely have set a standard nobody has ever met, and they abandon good plans in the twentieth minute because of it. Reducing his output by a third, on the day, is a large achievement.

The second is that almost everything in this week transfers beyond the fixture it was built for. Ownership, delay, the arrival line, the hand-off, the inside-line recovery, the rest-defense relationship: none of these are opponent-specific, and a side that installs them for one famous individual defends better against everyone for the remainder of the season. That is the strongest argument against the charge of negative coaching, and it is the reason a coach should build this week even when the fixture is lost before it starts. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you train a side to defend a star opponent?

Reduce the plan to three instructions with named owners, then rehearse those instructions across four contact points rather than one. The first installs ownership and delay, the second runs the plan against a live opponent given a deliberate advantage, the third covers transitions and dead balls, and the fourth is a short recall the day before. Every exercise has to present the real decision at real speed with a real consequence for choosing wrongly, which usually means small areas, outnumbered defenses, and finishes rather than whistles. Then choose three behaviors to count during the fixture, before it starts, so the review measures execution rather than the scoreline.

### Q: What does a containment session actually look like?

Short phases, an outnumbered defense, a full-sized goal at one end and counter-attack gates at the other, and a constraint that forces the decision the plan depends on. A typical block runs twelve to eighteen phases of under thirty seconds each, with the ball entering through a designated opponent every time, followed by genuine rest and a second block. It looks less organized than a walkthrough and far more like the fixture. The coach speaks between repetitions rather than during them, makes two corrections and not eight, and stops the exercise only when the pattern of error is worth interrupting for.

### Q: How do you simulate a star opponent in training?

Replicate the problem rather than the person. Pick the squad member whose tendencies match the threat type, brief him privately on the two or three actions he must attempt, and give him a real advantage in his zone: a spare man, a channel nobody else may enter, or a free first touch. Reward his signature action inside the scoring system so he keeps attempting it rather than taking the easy option. A simulation your defenders handle comfortably is worse than no simulation, because it manufactures confidence that the fixture will remove within ten minutes.

### Q: How many sessions do you need to prepare a defensive plan?

Four contact points plus matchday for a side that trains three or more times a week: one principle practice, one heavily opposed practice, one transition and set-piece practice, and one short recall before the fixture. The number matters less than the spacing, since habit forms across separated exposures rather than within a single long one. A side training twice a week runs the principle and opposed work only. A side training once a week gets twenty focused minutes and a verbal briefing, which installs the individual skills but not the collective pattern, and a coach should be honest with himself about that ceiling rather than pretending otherwise.

### Q: Which constraints make a containment drill realistic?

The ones that force the choice the plan depends on rather than the ones that make the exercise tidy. Forbidding the tackle from the front removes the lunge and leaves body position as the only tool. Requiring the ball to reach the designated opponent before a shot guarantees the situation recurs. Doubling the value of his signature goal stops defenders treating all threats as equal. A five-second countdown on transitions converts recovery into a race with a defined finish. Requiring two passes to exit stops the clearance counting as success. Each of those creates a decision; cones and bibs do not.

### Q: How do you coach a hand-off between two defenders?

Give it a single word used by the whole squad and rehearse it with a live attacker rather than a walkthrough. The receiving defender arrives roughly five yards behind and inside the owner, stops level with the attacker's back heel, and does not move to the ball until he calls. The owner does not release until he hears it. The two most common faults are both fixable in one practice: the cover arriving at the ball rather than at a position, and the pair using different phrases, which delays the exchange by the half second that turns a contained attack into a shot.

### Q: How do you train a back four to hold a line together?

Mark the line on the grass, make holding it the scoring condition, and give one player the call. The defenders may only retreat when the ball goes backwards or when the designated caller announces the drop, and an attacker played onside behind them costs a goal in the exercise. Then remove the marking and keep the call. The behavior to watch for is whether the line moved together on a voice or individually on instinct, since a line that retreats one player at a time is not a line. Running this without a goalkeeper is pointless, because his starting depth determines how high the line can honestly sit.

### Q: Which drills improve one on one defending?

The ones where the defender cannot solve the problem by tackling. Restricting contact to the side or from behind once the attacker is moving away from goal forces the defender to work through body angle and distance, which is what actually decides the duel. Add a real goal so being beaten costs something, keep the areas small so the tempo is genuine, and give the attacker a restriction before removing it so the defender builds the pattern before facing the choice. Individually, three things carry: a sideways rather than backwards first step, an open hip position, and a curved recovery run towards the inside line.

### Q: How do you rehearse doubling up without leaving gaps?

Rehearse the split rather than the number. A functioning double has one defender taking the ball and one taking the space the attacker wants to enter, and which arrives first depends on where he receives. Two defenders converging on the same shoulder is a queue, not a double, and it leaves precisely the gap the question asks about. The practice that exposes this adds a second attacker making a run beyond the individual: the pair who both commit to the ball concede immediately, which teaches the lesson faster than any explanation. The covering midfielder's arrival line, and who fills behind him, has to be assigned rather than assumed.

### Q: How do you run this session with a small squad?

Preserve the duel and abandon the collective pattern. With twelve players, run seven against nine on two-thirds of a pitch using the goalkeeper in the build-up. With ten, cut to a back four plus one midfielder against six on half a pitch with a single counter gate. With eight, run repeated three against four in the danger zone and accept that sliding as a unit cannot be rehearsed tonight. The content that survives compression is ownership, delay, the hand-off, the double, and the recovery line, and those are the components that transfer to every other fixture anyway.

### Q: Which coaching points actually change defensive behaviour?

Corrections that name a body part, an angle, a distance, or a first step, delivered within seconds of the repetition rather than during it. "Stop level with his back heel" changes something. "Be more aggressive" does not, because it describes an attitude rather than an action. Limit yourself to two per practice, decided before you arrive, since a player under load holds one instruction and a coach delivering eleven messages produces a confused unit. Use a question where the player can already articulate what went wrong, because retrieval fixes it more durably, and use a direct instruction where he cannot.

### Q: How do you know a session transferred into the contest?

Choose the evidence before the fixture, not after it. Pick three behaviors the work was designed to produce, such as whether the owner adjusted position on the preceding action rather than the entry pass, whether the cover stopped at the arrival line, and whether the hand-off call was audible, then count them. Ignore whether the individual scored, since that outcome is dominated by variance. Behaviors that appear in rehearsal and vanish under pressure mean the practice lacked consequence or speed. Behaviors that fade after an hour mean the habit is not built yet and needs another exposure rather than a redesign.

### Q: How do you prepare a squad mentally to face a famous opponent?

Clarity does most of the work. A player who knows exactly what he is responsible for is far less affected by the reputation of the man opposite him than a player holding a general instruction to be careful. Beyond that, three things help. Show one clip of the opponent's threat being prevented rather than eight of it working. State the concession out loud so nobody believes he is expected to stop everything. And tell the group in advance that the individual will produce moments regardless, and that nobody changes anything for the first twenty minutes, which is the sentence that keeps a good plan alive past its first setback.

### Q: How do you manage training load in a big fixture week?

Plan the shape in advance and respect what the work costs, which is accelerations, decelerations, changes of direction, and contact rather than distance. The usual structure places the heaviest duel work early, the opposed simulation in short intense phases with real recovery in the middle, and a deliberately light cognitive recall the day before. When the calendar compresses, cut the transition and set-piece block first and the principle work never. Keep a record of what the squad actually did rather than what was planned. Decisions about individual players, particularly anyone carrying a concern, belong with qualified medical staff rather than with a tactical plan.

### Q: Which warm-up prepares defenders for high intensity duels?

One that progressively exposes the body to the actions the plan will demand rather than one built around jogging and static stretching. In general terms that means movement preparation first, then short accelerations building towards near-maximal, then deceleration and change of direction work, then a small amount of controlled shoulder-to-shoulder contact placed at the end rather than the beginning. The principle is progression: the first duel of the afternoon should not be the first hard acceleration of the afternoon. Specific protocols, volumes, and any modification for an individual player belong to qualified physical preparation and medical professionals who know that player.

### Q: Which mistakes ruin a containment session?

Eight recur. Rehearsing shape with no opposition and calling it preparation. Running everything at an intensity where technique cannot be executed. Shouting corrections during the action instead of between repetitions. Letting the exercise become a private contest between two teammates rather than a rehearsal of the fixture. Leaving the goalkeeper out. Changing the plan the day before. Preparing for the individual and forgetting the good players around him. And over-instruction, which is the error good coaches make: a seventh instruction does not add anything, it degrades the execution of the first three.
