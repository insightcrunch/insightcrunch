In 1832 a separated Irish writer who needed the money published a two-volume study of Shakespeare's women and, inside it, the chapter that fixed Juliet's image for a century. Anna Jameson's reading of Juliet in *Characteristics of Women, Moral, Poetical, and Historical* is the foundation document of the Victorian Juliet: the first sustained, book-length defence of the heroine written by a woman, addressed in large part to women, and built on the claim that Juliet is not a type of girlish romance but a complete and credible woman whose passion has the force of a destiny. The verdict of this article is that the chapter deserves both halves of its fame and neither half of its caricature. It is more daring than the angel-making parody later centuries made of it, because Jameson defends Juliet's desire, defends her secrecy, defends her deception of her parents, and scolds the prudes who flinched at her wedding-night longing. It is also more limited than its admirers allow, because the same chapter explains Juliet's courage away as an accident of passion, declines to count her age, and files her under an eternal feminine nature that no evidence could disturb. The Victorian century took the portrait and dropped the argument: it kept the halo and lost the brief for the defence. Recovering the whole chapter, the daring and the limits together, is the only way to see what Jameson actually did, and why her Juliet ruled for so long.

Jameson herself announced the difficulty at the threshold. It is not without emotion, she writes, that she attempts to touch on the character of Juliet, since so many beautiful things have already been said of the heroine that nothing better can be hoped for, only something more. What she then supplied was more in the strict sense: more of the play, followed scene by scene and speech by speech, than any earlier critic had brought to the character, and a framework, the classification of the heroines by their ruling faculty, that made Juliet legible as one term in a system rather than as a decoration on a famous story. Quotations from the play in this article follow the Folger Shakespeare edition, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, and are cited by act, scene, and line.

Two caricatures of the chapter circulate in its place, and the argument below refuses both. In the first, Jameson is the angel-maker, the critic who wrapped a desiring, disobedient girl in muslin and set her on the Victorian mantelpiece, where she stood for a century as the sanctioned daydream of schoolgirls. In the second, she is the secret insurgent, a feminist in disguise whose professions of feminine essence were camouflage for a raid on the citadel of male criticism. The chapter itself is more interesting than either figure. It contains the acquittal of a heroine's desire in language an evangelical bookseller could stock, and it contains the refusal to grant that heroine a mind of her own; it rebukes prudes in one paragraph and enters the heroine's deficiencies in a moral ledger in the next. A critic capable of both movements in a single study is not a mascot for anyone's thesis. She is a working intelligence negotiating, page by page, between what the text of the play showed her and what her moment permitted a woman to say about it, and the negotiation, not a slogan extracted from it, is her bequest to the criticism of the play.

## What Did Anna Jameson Say About Juliet?

The core of the reading can be stated in one sentence of hers that the century never forgot: all Shakespeare's women love or have loved or are capable of loving, but Juliet is love itself, the passion her very state of being, outside which she has no existence. Around that sentence Jameson builds a portrait with two governing faculties, the two that give her class of heroines its name. Juliet's character, she argues in her conclusion, resolves into passion and imagination: passion concentrates the impulses, affections, and energies that give the character its inward charm and moral power, while imagination supplies the splendid accompaniments, the imagery in which every feeling arrives already clothed. The reading is developmental as well as static. Jameson traces the heroine from the dutiful, silent girl of the first scenes, through the lover of the balcony, to the wife who dismisses her nurse and faces the potion alone, insisting at every stage that the impression of simplicity conceals a complexity, depth, and variety that analysis almost destroys in the act of displaying, as a half-blown rose is destroyed by being pulled apart leaflet by leaflet to show its fragrance.

Three commitments hold the portrait together. The first is sympathy as method: Jameson reads Juliet from inside, reconstructing the pressures of the household, the indulgence of the nurse, and the severity of the parents, until the heroine's choices present themselves as the natural result of her situation. The second is contrast: Juliet is defined against Portia's intellect, Miranda's innocence, Helena's strength, and Schiller's Thekla, each comparison sharpening what is unique in her. The third is defence. Again and again the chapter takes the form of an answer to an implied accuser: the critic who finds Juliet too forward, too sudden, too sensual, too deceitful. Jameson's Juliet emerges from a courtroom in which the heroine was, in 1832, genuinely on trial, and the chapter's warmth is the warmth of an advocate who believes the charges disgrace the prosecution. That forensic energy, more than any single phrase, is what separates her chapter from the appreciative rhapsodies of the male Romantics who preceded her, and it is the quality that later, quieter centuries allowed their readers to forget.

The method deserves a closer description, since it is the chapter's machinery and the source of its authority. Jameson proceeds by saturation. She follows the heroine through every appearance in sequence, quoting the speeches entire where her rivals quoted tags, and she builds the portrait as an accumulation of witnessed moments rather than as an essence announced in advance. The first scene's silence, the balcony's graduated confessions, the impatience of the nurse's delay, the violence and recovery of the third act, the appeals, the dismissal, the cell, the potion, the tomb: each is entered as evidence, and the character is what the evidence, taken together, will support. She cross-examines her own witness as she goes, marking the places where the impression resists analysis, conceding an excess here and a deficiency there, and she keeps a second heroine always in the margin as a control, so that a quality is never praised in Juliet until it has been weighed against the different economy of a Portia, a Miranda, or a Helena. Her Portia study performs the comparison in reverse, setting Portia's confession, spoken openly in the presence of attendants with a tender seriousness approaching solemnity, against Juliet's passionate self-abandonment breathed from a moonlit balcony, and ruling that the two surrenders differ in kind because the minds surrendering differ in organization. This is the opposite of the swoon with which women critics of the period were credited. It is closer, in procedure, to a barrister's brief cross-checked against a naturalist's notebook, and it explains the chapter's durability: readers who rejected its conclusions still had to work through its evidence.

One consequence of the method is a claim about the play itself that later criticism has had to take seriously. Because she reads the heroine continuously, Jameson reads the drama as a development, an unfolding in which the action serves to develop the character even as the character determines the action; the catastrophe, in her handling, is not an accident that befalls a finished girl but the last term of a sequence legible from the first scene. Structural readings of the play as a tragedy of character rather than of chance have older roots in the German critics she admired, and hers is coloured by them, but the English tradition received the argument substantially through her pages, domesticated into a study of a girl becoming a woman under pressure. When modern editors and critics trace Juliet's growth from obedience through desire to command, they are walking a path her chapter surveyed first, whatever they conclude about the furniture she placed along it.

## Who Was Anna Jameson, and Why Did She Write Characteristics of Women?

Anna Brownell Jameson, born Murphy in Dublin in 1794, was the daughter of Denis Brownell Murphy, a miniaturist and enamel painter, and she was at work early: a governess at sixteen, first in the family of the Marquis of Winchester. A continental tour as companion to a young charge produced the manuscript that, after famous mishaps in the trade, appeared in 1826 as *The Diary of an Ennuyée*, a semi-fictional travel journal whose success announced a writer. She had married the lawyer Robert Jameson in 1825; the marriage failed, the pair separated, and he departed for a legal career in the colonies that ended in the vice-chancellorship of Upper Canada. The engagement had itself been long and interrupted, contracted in 1820 and followed by years in which she supported herself in other families' houses while the match waited, so that the woman who would anatomize Juliet's confinement in the Capulet palace had spent her own youth in the two stations her century offered an educated girl, the governess's chair and the postponed marriage. Biography should not be asked to do criticism's work, but the resonance is hers to claim: no male critic of the play had waited for a wedding as a condition of economic survival, or taught other men's daughters under a roof that was not hers, and the chapter's authority on subjection and indulgence was earned in those rooms before it was argued in print. The separation made authorship a livelihood rather than an accomplishment. *The Loves of the Poets* followed in 1829, and the studies of the heroines, written, as her biographer Clara Thomas establishes in *Love and Work Enough: The Life of Anna Jameson* (1967), previous to October 1831 for the most part, appeared from Saunders and Otley in 1832. The professional conditions matter for the reading. Jameson was not a don at leisure or a clergyman with a living; she was a woman of letters selling interpretation to a reading public that contained, in unprecedented numbers, women like herself, and her book's form, a gallery of female characters examined with moral and poetical seriousness, was designed for that public's needs as much as for its pleasure.

Her standing in the literary world was real and rising. She moved among the Kembles, the Brownings, the Carlyles; she was the friend of Fanny Kemble, the most celebrated young actress of the day, and of Lady Byron and, in Germany, of Ottilie von Goethe. The *Characteristics* made her reputation for original thought, as the eleventh edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* later put it, and it opened the career that produced the art histories, the *Sacred and Legendary Art* volumes, and the social writings on women's labour that filled the rest of her working life until her death in 1860. Two features of the biography bear directly on the Juliet chapter. She knew Italy and its literature at first hand, the *Diary* being steeped in both, which is why her Juliet is so insistently Italian, a portrait in national colouring rather than an English rose transplanted. And she knew dependence and its humiliations from inside, which is why her account of a girl kept in severe subjection by austere parents, and fondled and spoiled by a foolish old nurse, reads like evidence taken from life. The sympathy of the chapter is not a pose adopted for the market; it is the method of a writer who recognized the heroine's predicament as a version of a predicament she understood.

The later career confirms that the *Characteristics* was a beginning and not an episode. Jameson travelled to Canada in the winter of 1836 and 1837, the visit recorded in *Winter Studies and Summer Rambles in Canada* (1838); she became the first serious English historian of Christian art with the *Sacred and Legendary Art* series from 1848; and in the 1850s she stepped from interpretation into advocacy, her lectures *Sisters of Charity* (1855) and *The Communion of Labour* (1856) arguing for the organized employment and education of women before audiences that included the young reformers, Barbara Leigh Smith Bodichon among them, who would found the organized women's movement. The trajectory matters for the reading of 1832. The woman who ended by lecturing on women's labour began by insisting, in print, that a girl in a play could be judged only by reconstructing the whole economy of constraint and indulgence that had formed her; the social imagination of the lectures is present in embryo in the literary criticism, in the attention to households, nurses, dowries, and obedience that fills the Juliet chapter's middle pages. Clara Thomas's biography makes the continuity plain by restoring the earnings, the deadlines, and the obligations that the saintly later image of Mrs Jameson had varnished over: this was a writer who worked, and whose work kept enlarging the territory a woman's judgment was licensed to cover.

Her formation was continental as much as English, and the chapter's intellectual accent marks it. The authorities she leans on at the decisive points are German: Schlegel for the play's catastrophe and its sigh, Schiller for the heroine's great comparison, Goethe for the closing emblem of youthful passion, Madame de Staël for the final moral arithmetic. English reviewing of the period was still largely insular, conducting its Shakespeare criticism as a domestic property dispute among editors; Jameson, who had lived in Italy, would visit Germany in 1833, and counted Ottilie von Goethe among her closest friends, wrote as a citizen of the European republic of letters, importing its comparative method and its respect for the heroine as a subject of philosophy. The Victorian Juliet is thus a translated artifact twice over, an Italian girl read through German criticism by an Irish writer for an English public, and the fact helps explain both her novelty at home and her portability abroad, where the book's translations and reprints found readers already formed by the criticism she had absorbed.

## How Is Characteristics of Women Built, and Where Does Juliet Stand in It?

The book's architecture is a classification. Its half title announces Shakespeare's female characters, and its full title, *Characteristics of Women, Moral, Poetical, and Historical*, promises character study under three aspects. Jameson sorts twenty-three heroines into four classes by the faculty that rules in each. The Characters of Intellect are Portia, Isabella, Beatrice, and Rosalind. The Characters of Passion and Imagination are Juliet, Helena, Perdita, Viola, Ophelia, and Miranda, with Juliet standing first in the class and occupying its longest, most elaborated study. The Characters of the Affections are Hermione, Desdemona, Imogen, and Cordelia. The Historical Characters, women judged against the chronicle record, run from Cleopatra and Volumnia through the queens of the histories to Lady Macbeth. The scheme is easy to mock as a filing system, and Jameson's own practice keeps breaking it, since she concedes that Imogen mingles every tint and that Juliet alone shows passion under every variety of aspect. But the scheme does critical work: it insists that Shakespeare's women are not interchangeable ideals, that each is the expression of a distinct organization of mind, and that a woman critic's business is discrimination among them, the exact opposite of the undifferentiated worship that the phrase Shakespeare's heroines usually announced.

The frame is a dialogue. In the introduction, set in a library, Alda and Medon debate the whole enterprise: Medon teases, doubts, and plays the conventional male reader, while Alda, the author's persona, argues that Shakespeare's characters combine history and real life, complete individuals whose hearts and souls lie open, so that all may behold and all judge for themselves. The fencing is substantive. Medon throws down the period's favourite counter, that there is not one of Shakespeare's women who, as a dramatic character, can be compared to Falstaff, and Alda's answer concedes the scale and disputes the standard: the want of comparative power in the women, their lesser bulk of wit, will, and self-assertion, may be only an additional excellence, the delineation of a different order of being rather than a failed attempt at the same one. The exchange is the whole book in miniature, the male measure produced, examined, and quietly set aside as the wrong instrument, and it licenses the studies that follow to judge the heroines by laws drawn from their own organization. A reader who skips the introduction, as excerptors taught the century to do, misses the fact that the gallery was built as an argument about measurement itself. The device is more than decoration. It stages, inside the book itself, the encounter between a woman who claims interpretive authority and a man who concedes it only under argument, which is precisely the encounter the book then performs for hundreds of pages. It also declares the book's pedagogic intent without embarrassment: these studies are offered as instruments for the formation of judgment in their readers, and the dedication of the first edition, to Fanny Kemble, binds the printed criticism to the living stage, since Kemble's name carried the memory of her sensational Covent Garden performances as Juliet herself.

### Did Jameson write her book mainly for women readers?

Yes, substantially, though never exclusively. The introduction stages a woman defending the moral seriousness of female character study against a male skeptic; the studies keep asking what a woman reader can learn from each heroine's organization of mind; and the book's sale ran through the channels, libraries, gift editions, and family reading, where women were the decisive public.

Men read and praised it too; but its design, its persona, and its pedagogic frame address the woman reader first, and that address is itself one of its historical acts.

The physical book made the same claim as its argument. The first edition, in two volumes, carried fifty vignette etchings executed by Jameson herself, the miniaturist's daughter illustrating her own criticism, so that the volume presented a woman's hand in both its arts. It succeeded immediately and kept succeeding: edition followed edition, American reprints multiplied, and later nineteenth-century issues carried the work under the title by which it is usually read, *Shakespeare's Heroines*. A book designed to be given, kept, and reread, it became the standard account of the heroines for the generations between the Romantics and the moderns, and no subsequent writer on Juliet, favourable or hostile, could avoid reckoning with the portrait it had fixed.

The Diary's own history taught her the terms on which a woman's book would be received, and the lesson is legible in the *Characteristics*' defensive architecture. The journal of the Italian tour had appeared anonymously in 1826 and been read, by a public charmed with its pathos, as the genuine record of a young woman's decline; its author was consumed as a confession rather than credited as a construction. The studies of the heroines answer that reception in advance. They are signed, systematized, and argumentative, prefaced by a dialogue in which the woman's right to judge is litigated before the judging starts, as if the author had resolved that this book, whatever else was said of it, would not be mistaken for a diary. The resolution held for exactly as long as the first reviews; the culture preferred its women writers confessional, and spent the next century excerpting the *Characteristics* as if it were one.

The classification scheme repays one more look, because its intellectual ancestry explains both its power and its limits. To sort minds by a ruling faculty was the period's common intellectual gesture, kin to the phrenology and the moral philosophy of the day, and Jameson turns the gesture to a polemical purpose: if each heroine is a distinct organization, then the lazy habit of praising Shakespeare's women as a choir of interchangeable angels is a failure of reading, and the critic who discriminates among them performs the genuinely respectful act. Alda, in the introduction, grounds the practice in a theory of judgment itself: the characters are discussed, praised and dispraised, liked and disliked, as real human beings, and readers form their opinions under the influence of their own characters, habits, and feelings, exactly as with their acquaintances. Criticism, on this account, is a branch of moral knowledge, and the woman reader's trained perception of character, exercised daily in drawing rooms and sickrooms where men's formal instruments could not go, is an expertise the republic of letters had failed to credential. That is the book's deepest claim, made almost in passing, and the Juliet chapter is its demonstration piece: no reader who had not watched girls confined, indulged, and married off could have written its account of the Capulet household, and Jameson knew that her sex's enforced expertise was the credential her title page could not print.

The chapter's argument is woven through comparisons, quotations, and defences, which makes it powerful to read and easy to misremember as a mist of admiration. The tabular form is a homage as well as an audit: Jameson was herself a classifier, a maker of classes, galleries, and handbooks, and her criticism trusts arrangement to reveal what rhapsody conceals, so that a summary in rows is the tribute her own method would have demanded. What the rows cannot hold is the chapter's temperature, the emotion she confessed at its threshold and never quite banks again, and a reader who knows the table should still read the chapter for the heat the table was built to measure. The table also exposes the reading's silhouette, its concentration on the heroine's inward life and its corresponding silence about the feud's machinery and the city's politics, in a way that thirty pages of eloquence can conceal. A faithful summary must register the silence as carefully as the claims, since the Victorian Juliet descended as much from what the chapter declined to discuss as from what it proclaimed.

A summary of one portrait also has to record what the portrait borrows from its neighbours, because Jameson's classes are comparative instruments and Juliet's rows in the table mean little until their neighbours are priced. She heads the class of passion and imagination as its purest instance, but the class exists to register her difference from Helena within it, and the whole gallery exists to register her difference from the intellects across the hall. Portia and Rosalind command their plays by wit and management; Juliet is commanded by hers, and Jameson's scale of admiration is not a scale of power, since the heroine she ranks as the most perfect type of her class is the one with the least command of circumstance in the book. That paradox is the reading's signature and its vulnerability at once. If perfection of type is the criterion, the girl who can do little but love perfectly outranks the women who can do everything else, and the system canonizes the very helplessness its closing diagnosis deplores. Jameson appears to feel the strain, because the Juliet chapter keeps reaching for the vocabulary of energy and then withdrawing it, crediting the heroine with a strength that its own analysis must immediately reclassify as accidental. The table makes the strain visible in a way the prose conceals: read down the column of her claims and the row for reflection stands nearly empty against a column of superlatives, and the emptiness is the diagnosis her last paragraph was written to explain. No summary devised by a later hand could show the reading's architecture more plainly, which is why the artifact is offered here not as a substitute for the chapter but as its floor plan, the structure a reader can hold while the chapter's heat does its work. The summary has a final arbitrating use, which the disputes below exploit: it fixes the proportions. The verdict that the Victorian Juliet was sweetened into insipidity looks different against rows dominated by defences of frankness, energy, and desire; the sweetness is real, but it sits at the frame, in the rose and the halo, while the substance is combative. Conversely, the claim that Jameson was a feminist critic before the term looks different against a final row recording a moral deficiency: the recovery is real, but it stops at the threshold of the heroine's mind.

## Why Does Jameson Call Juliet Love Itself?

The famous sentence is not a compliment but a thesis, and Jameson earns it by a route her summarizers usually skip. She begins from La Rochefoucauld's maxim that there is but one love, though a thousand different copies, and argues that the single sentiment takes its colouring from character, temperament, climate, and circumstance. The love that is calm in one soul shows vehement in another; the love that is wild and passionate in the south is deep and contemplative in the north. Shakespeare's heroines are the copies: chaste and dignified in Portia, airy-delicate and fearless in Miranda, sweetly confiding in Perdita, playfully fond in Rosalind, constant in Imogen, devoted in Desdemona, fervent in Helena, tender in Viola. Juliet is the original from which the copies seem struck, since each and all of these qualities are in her, and she reminds the reader of nothing but her own sweet self. The claim's audacity is easy to miss behind its lyric dress. Jameson is asserting that this one character exhausts the subject of her entire class, that the taxonomy of Shakespeare's women, which the book exists to draw, collapses at its centre into a single figure in whom the ruling passion exists without admixture or reserve. No male critic of the period made a comparably total claim for the heroine; Hazlitt's rhapsody keeps Romeo beside her as joint subject, and the German critics she read subordinated her to the play's design. Jameson gives the play to Juliet entire.

Her instrument for the claim is comparison, and the comparison she lavishes most care on is not with any English heroine but with Schiller's Thekla, the princess of the *Wallenstein* trilogy, whom she calls the German Juliet. The pairing licenses her finest piece of discrimination. Thekla's devotion is frank and dignified, her apology for her own unreserve is quite in Juliet's style, and the two heroines would divide the loves and suffrages of mankind as women; but as dramatic characters they divide completely. Thekla's love is calm and reposes on itself, giving the idea of eternity, a rock on which love shelters as in a home; Juliet's love is vehement, an increasing tide pouring to the ocean, giving the idea of infinitude, a heart in which love reigns a crowned king. Schiller's lovers, she adds, speak in reflective set pieces, describing a father's astrological chamber, where Shakespeare's lovers speak only of themselves, see only themselves in the universe, and let every word, however poetical, bear directly on their situation and their feelings. The passage matters beyond its beauty. It is a woman critic, writing in a form often dismissed as appreciation, performing comparative dramatic analysis of a high order, and concluding that it is rashness and ignorance to compare Schiller with Shakespeare at all: Thekla is a fine conception in the German spirit, but Juliet is a lovely and palpable creation.

### Why does Jameson set Schiller's Thekla beside Juliet?

Because Thekla is the nearest rival the European stage offered: another heiress divided from her lover by a father's feud, another frank confession of love, another resolute despair. The comparison lets Jameson isolate what is specifically Shakespearean in Juliet by contrast, the vehemence against Thekla's calm, and the palpable life against the pale German colouring.

The pairing also converts a commonplace of the period's criticism into a discrimination between two national poetries, German reflection against Italianate English life, and Jameson's handling of it, respectful to Schiller and final against him, is among the most professional performances in the book.

The national argument runs through the whole reading and does more ideological work than any other strand. Juliet, for Jameson, is allied to Boccaccio's Gismunda and Fiammetta not in character or circumstance but in the Italian spirit, the glowing national complexion of the portrait; her imagination itself is a part of her southern temperament. Climate explains precocity, vehemence, and imagery in one gesture, and the gesture has a double edge that the article must weigh. It rescues Juliet from English standards of decorum by naturalizing her passion as a product of place, an Italian girlhood ripening under an Italian sun; and it confines her within a ethnographic essence, a south in the blood, that the heroine herself never invokes. The same doubleness attends the chapter's imagery. The rose passed through the prism of the spectrum, catching every tint of the divided ray while remaining the same sweet rose, is Jameson's emblem for a love exhibited under every variety of aspect and every gradation of feeling it could assume in a delicate female heart. The emblem is beautiful, and it is also a quiet essentialism: the tints vary, the rose is one, and its nature is given before the play begins. Where the argument needed a psychology, Jameson sometimes supplies a climate and a sex instead, and the Victorian Juliet's famous purity owes as much to those substitutions as to anything Shakespeare wrote.

A second instrument of the claim is pictorial. Early in the chapter Jameson borrows a painter's secret, that the whole effect of colour consists in white upon black and black upon white, and reads the play as its exemplification: the lovers are all love surrounded by all hate, all harmony surrounded by all discord, all pure nature in the midst of polished and artificial life, and Juliet herself appears as Romeo first sees her, the snowy dove trooping with crows (1.5.48). The doctrine of contrast does quiet structural work. It explains why the heroine must be read against her household rather than in isolation, why the Nurse's coarseness and the parents' statecraft are not blemishes on the portrait but its ground, and why the feud, which Jameson never anatomizes as politics, is indispensable to her as chiaroscuro. It also governs her sense of the play's surface: a tissue of beautiful poetry weaving principal and subordinate figures together, the whole lit by a sunny brilliance as though the poet had transported himself into Italy and drunk of her atmosphere. Byron's Haidee enters as the limiting case, a copy of Juliet in the Oriental costume, she rules, whose development is epic rather than dramatic, as if to say that the type could travel anywhere except back onto a stage with its concentration intact. The pictorial habit is the art historian's in embryo; the critic who would later teach England to read Madonnas was already reading a heroine as a figure composed against a ground, and the composition's legibility is what her century inherited as the portrait's glow.

## Did Jameson Defend Juliet's Frankness, or Apologize for It?

The balcony scene was the scandal the nineteenth century required every critic of the play to process. Juliet, alone and unchaperoned in the night, confesses a love the object of which is standing beneath her window, then designs the marriage, sets its machinery in motion, and dictates its terms. A tradition of unease ran from the Augustan editors to the conduct books: the girl is too quick, too knowing, too forward for her sex and years. Jameson's handling of the scene is the chapter's most consequential performance, because she neither denies the facts nor accepts the verdict. She quotes the heroine's own apologies at length, the shrinking from her confession, the wish to dwell on form and deny what she has spoken, the offer to frown and be perverse and say him nay if he will woo, the great self-indictment: "In truth, fair Montague, I am too fond, / And therefore thou mayst think my 'haviour light" (2.2.141-142). Then she rules on them. This, she writes, is the ingenuous simplicity of the avowal, guarded by that jealousy of female honour which precept and education have infused into the heroine's mind, without one real doubt of his truth or the slightest hesitation in her self-abandonment. The apology is read as evidence for the defence: a girl who fears being thought light while she surrenders everything is not light but honourably afraid, and her frankness, precisely because it costs her shame, acquits her of the charge.

The ruling deserves its reputation for nerve. Jameson concedes everything the prosecution alleged, the speed, the initiative, the unmaidenly directness, and denies only the inference, relocating modesty from behaviour to feeling: Juliet is modest not because she conceals her love but because she suffers in revealing it. The same structure governs her treatment of the boundless-giving speech that closes the scene, "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep" (2.2.133-134), which she reads as a picture of the young heart that sees no bound to its hopes because experience, indifference, and disappointment, the three checks on transport, are all still strangers to it. Innocence is redefined as inexperience rather than ignorance or restraint, a definition that lets the heroine keep both her desire and her halo. The discrimination is finer than the halo suggests. Juliet's innocence, Jameson insists, is not the innocence of a desert island, not Miranda's blankness before a world she has never seen; the Capulet girl has heard that there is such a thing as falsehood, though she can scarcely conceive it, has been warned by mother and nurse against flattering vows and men's inconstancy, has perhaps turned the tale by Ariosto told, of fair Olympia loved and left of old. Her doubt in the balcony scene, the bashful shrinking dispelled almost as soon as felt, is therefore a knowingness without experience, a girlhood educated by warning rather than by event, and it is exactly the psychology of the sheltered households Jameson wrote for: daughters armed with maxims against a danger they cannot picture, meeting the danger in its most disarming form. The defence of the frankness rests on that psychology at every point, since only a girl warned and yet unwary could confess as Juliet confesses, at once too fond and more true than those who have more cunning to be strange. Hazlitt had reached a neighbouring position by rhapsody, declaring the lovers in love but not love-sick, a judgment Jameson quotes with warm approval; her own route is forensic, and it convinced where his merely enchanted, because it gave mothers, governesses, and reviewers a form of words in which Juliet's forwardness could be repeated without offence.

### Was Juliet's openness a fault in Jameson's eyes?

No; it is the quality she most labours to acquit. She treats the balcony confession as ingenuous simplicity, the frankness of a nature without cunning, and argues that the heroine's very fear of seeming light, her plea not to have her yielding imputed to light love, is itself the proof of her honour.

Openness becomes the signature of innocence in the chapter, and concealment, where it later appears, is laid at the door of the circumstances that forced it, never at the heroine's.

The limits of the defence appear at its borders, and honesty requires them stated. Jameson's acquittal is purchased by keeping the desire marital in destination and singular in object: the bounty speech is innocent because it flows toward a husband, and the frankness is honourable because it will never be repeated for another. Desire that aimed elsewhere, or survived its object, falls outside the brief she has written for her client, as her comparatively cool handling of every other passionate woman in the volume shows. Within those borders, though, the defence is complete and, for 1832, strikingly unembarrassed: she will not call the heroine unwomanly for wanting, proposing, and arranging, and she makes the wanting itself the warrant of the womanhood.

The forensic pattern extends to the play's other defendant, and the extension shows how systematic her advocacy is. Romeo entered the nineteenth century under a charge nearly as damaging as Juliet's: inconstancy, the fickleness of a boy who sighs for Rosaline in the first act and forgets her by the fifth scene. Jameson acquits him with a historian's evidence. The Petrarchan posture, she argues, was the fashion of the age and especially of Italy, the last remains of the age of chivalry, in which every young cavalier devoted himself to the service of some fair and rigorous lady, the more hopeless the slavery the more honourable; the Surreys, the Sydneys, and the Bayards of the time were all of that fantastic school. Romeo's oxymorons, "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1.181), are therefore costume, a waking dream indulged in picked phrases, and the proof is the transformation when the real sentiment arrives: he is no longer for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in, no longer making a confidant of his gay companions, but consecrated, earnest, rapturous in the feeling and the expression. The Rosaline incident, retained from the original story, is in her verdict far from a fault in taste and sentiment; it is a beauty in the drama, contrasting the fancied and the real sentiment and stamping the lover as an historical as well as a dramatic portrait. A critic determined to manufacture angels would have cut the episode or apologized for it in a sentence. Jameson spends her learning on it, because her client is not an angel but a person, and persons are defended on the facts of their world.

The same advocacy governs the one place where the heroine's conduct might have forfeited even a sympathetic reader of the period, the haste. Juliet meets, loves, proposes, and marries inside a single revolution of the sun and moon, and the moral tradition disposed to call the catastrophe her punishment had its text ready. Jameson's answer is woven into her psychology of the bounty speech: transport is checked in ordinary hearts by experience, by indifference, or by disappointment, and the heroine has met none of the three; her ardour is what a heart of that capacity must do when nothing has yet taught it measure. Haste, in the chapter, is not a sin to be pardoned but a datum to be explained, the predictable behaviour of a nature of immense capacity kept in a nursery. The explanation does not acquit the world that kept her there; if anything it indicts the keeping, since the energies that destroy her were, on Jameson's own showing, the product of severe subjection on one side and spoiling indulgence on the other. The defence of Juliet keeps turning, in her hands, into an evidence against her upbringing, which is more than the conduct tradition that prosecuted the heroine ever managed against anybody.

## How Does Imagination Drive Jameson's Account of Juliet?

If passion is the chapter's thesis, imagination is its evidence, and the analysis of Juliet's fancy is the most technically accomplished writing in the study. Jameson's doctrine is reciprocal: in Juliet, imagination is in the first instance the medium of passion, and passion again kindles the imagination, until the two are interfused like moonlight through the summer air. The poetry of the part is therefore not an adornment laid over the character but its result, blended with its essence: the heroine speaks in pictures because pictures are how her mind receives the world. Jameson then does something no mere appreciator does; she counts. In the balcony scene she notes the speech of twenty-two lines beginning "Thou know'st the mask of night is on my face" (2.2.87) and observes that it contains but one figurative expression, the mask of night itself, and that the plainness is not fortuitous: where confusion, anxiety, and earnest self-defence predominate, the excitability and play of the imagination would be checked and subdued for the time. A few pages later she sets against that plainness the falconer passage, "O for a falconer's voice / To lure this tassel-gentle back again" (2.2.160-161), with its bondage grown hoarse and its Echo torn from her cave, three images in six lines, and reads the density as the signature of feeling set free. This is stylistic criticism of a professional order, attentive to the distribution of figure as an index of consciousness, and it stands up beside anything the century produced on the play's language.

The doctrine is then marched through the acts. The impatience soliloquy of the second act's fifth scene, with its lame nurse and its heralds of love that should be thoughts gliding ten times faster than the sun's beams (2.5.4-7), delights her for the way the lines mount and float responsive to the sense. The great wedding-night invocation, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1), she crowns with the title that scandalized the squeamish and thrilled the sympathetic: a hymn to the night. Her handling of that speech is the chapter's boldest stroke, and the accuracy of the record matters here, because later summaries habitually describe the Victorian reading as a conspiracy to drape the speech. Jameson does the opposite. She records her shock at the utter want of taste and refinement in those who, with coarse derision or in a spirit of prudery yet more gross and perverse, had dared to comment on the soliloquy, and she grounds the acquittal in dramatic fact: Juliet is not addressing an audience or even a confidante; she is thinking aloud, the young heart triumphing to itself in words, and within the vehemence of her call for night there is something almost infantine in her perfect simplicity, her impatience that of a child before a festival, with new robes it may not wear. The speech's desire is defended by its privacy and its innocence in a single motion.

### Why does Jameson call the wedding-night soliloquy a hymn?

Because the name relocates the speech from the bedroom to the liturgy. A hymn is praise addressed upward, and by calling the invocation of night a hymn Jameson insists that its longing is worshipful rather than furtive, an adjuration breathed in solitude by a wife awaiting the husband she has married that day.

The word is her verdict compressed: the speech that prudes treated as the play's indelicacy is, in her reading, among its purest utterances, and the indelicacy lies in the commentary, not the text.

The same shaping spirit, she argues, darkens as the play darkens: in the friar's cell it heaps together the battlements, the thievish ways, the serpents and roaring bears and charnel-houses of the great refusal speech (4.1.77-88), and in the potion soliloquy it conjures one terrible apprehension after another until the imagination realizes its own hideous creations and the heroine sees her cousin Tybalt's ghost (4.3). Read as a sequence, her exhibits compose a physiology of the faculty across the whole action, and the sequence has a shape she clearly intends. In the balcony's self-defence the imagination is checked, subdued by anxiety into a single figure; in the impatience and wedding-night speeches it is liberated, mounting and floating with the feeling it serves; in the cell it turns enumerative under desperation, cataloguing horrors as a mind ransacks itself for arguments; and in the potion soliloquy it escapes government altogether, the pictures it summons acquiring a hallucinatory independence until the heroine is spectator of her own terrors. Jameson never states the sequence as a scheme, but her ordering of the evidence states it for her, and it converts her prettiest doctrine, that imagination is the medium of passion, into a tragic mechanism with a plot of its own: the faculty ripens with the passion, and both ripen toward the vault. Later criticism of the play's imagery, the school of Caroline Spurgeon and the image-pattern analysts, works a neighbouring seam with different tools; Jameson's version has the advantage of being attached to a psychology, a single mind in which the pictures are symptoms, so that the luxuriance other critics catalogued as the poet's ornament she reads as the heroine's biography written in light. The reading also disciplines her own prose, which grows more figurative as the chapter proceeds, as if the critic's fancy were keeping time with her subject's; whether that sympathy of style is a method or a surrender is a question her book leaves open, and the feminist adjudication below returns to it. Even the oxymoron storm on the news of Tybalt's death, "O serpent heart, hid with a flowering face" (3.2.73), which she concedes may seem to wander into excess, she defends with Schlegel's authority and her own: such exuberance is natural in a fancy that kindles every thought into a picture, under the conflict of opposing sentiments. Imagination in this account is finally a tragic mechanism, since the faculty that gilds the balcony also peoples the vault; the Victorian Juliet is often described as a study in purity, but the imagination chapters of Jameson's study are a study in a mind whose glory and whose undoing are the same power.

Her reading of loveliness belongs to the same analysis, and it is subtler than the rhapsodies it is confused with. Juliet's beauty, Jameson observes, is almost never described; it is reflected. The heroine is beheld as she is mirrored in the heart of her lover, like a single bright star in the bosom of a deep, transparent well: in the white wonder of her hand, in lips that blush as thinking their own kisses sin (2.2.67-68 passim in Romeo's speech), in the friar's exclamation at her approach, so light a step that will ne'er wear out the everlasting flint (2.6.16-17), and in her father's similitude of death lying on her like an untimely frost upon the sweetest flower of the field (4.5.28-29). The observation is a genuine discovery about the play's technique, that Shakespeare builds the heroine's presence out of other minds' registers of it, and it doubles as a description of Jameson's own critical procedure, which likewise assembles its Juliet from reflections, the parents', the nurse's, the lover's, Schiller's, the reader's. It also contains her one concession to the charge of excess in the part's language, immediately withdrawn by being explained: the warmth and vivacity of a fancy which plays like light over every part of the character, kindling every thought into a picture, would naturally, under strong excitement and the conflict of opposing sentiments, run into some extravagance of diction. Excess is reclassified as evidence. The style that Augustan taste had condemned as the play's disease becomes, in her physiology of imagination, the symptom by which the heroine's constitution is diagnosed, and a symptom cannot be a fault.

## Which Scene Does Jameson Call the Crisis in Juliet's Character?

Jameson's developmental claim stands or falls on one scene, and she knows it. The arc requires its low first term, and she is careful to fix it: the quiet manner in which Juliet steals upon the audience in her first scene, the serene and graceful girl whose feelings are as yet unawakened and whose energies are all unknown to herself and unsuspected by others, her silence and her filial deference charming in the single speech that promises to look to like if looking liking move, and to endart her eye no deeper than her parents' consent shall give it strength to fly (1.3.97-99). The starting point matters to the system, since a heroine who begins in command would falsify the class she heads; passion and imagination must be shown asleep before they can be shown sovereign, and the girl of the first act is therefore read as pure potential, a rose not yet half blown. The reading flattens something real in the scene, the precision with which the thirteen-year-old negotiates the terms of her own inspection of Paris, but it purchases the arc's legibility, and the crisis scene is where the purchase pays out. When the nurse, fearful of discovery and true to her vocation, counsels Juliet to forget Romeo and marry Paris, Jameson writes that the moment which unveils the weakness and baseness of the confidante is the moment which reveals Juliet to herself. This scene, she declares, is the crisis in the character. The analysis that surrounds the declaration is the chapter's most searching piece of narrative criticism. Juliet does not break into upbraidings; it is no moment for anger. What possesses her is incredulous amazement succeeded by the extremity of scorn and abhorrence, and the exchange Jameson quotes is the coldest in the play: "Speakest thou from thy heart?" (3.5.226), the nurse's blustering oath in reply, and the single word "Amen!" with which the heroine closes the account of her childhood. Then comes the dismissal, "Go, counsellor! / Thou and my bosom henceforth shall be twain" (3.5.240-241), and the resolve, delivered in the scene's last line, "If all else fail, myself have power to die" (3.5.242), which Jameson finds sublime in its pathos. The fond, impatient, timid girl, she concludes, puts on the wife and the woman: she has learned heroism from suffering, and subtlety from oppression.

The preparation for the crisis is traced with the same care. Jameson follows the appeals in their order, to the father on her knees, "Good father, I beseech you on my knees" (3.5.158), to the mother, "O sweet mother, cast me not away" (3.5.198-199), to the nurse in the helplessness of anguish, and reads the sequence as the remedies which would first suggest themselves to a gentle and virtuous nature, the dagger grasped only as the last resource against dishonour. Even the earlier violence of the third act's second scene is folded into the design: the invectives against Romeo on the news of Tybalt's death, then the magnificent revulsion, "Upon his brow shame is ashamed to sit" (3.2.91), then the self-reproach of the three-hours' wife who has mangled her lord's name, exhibit the quick transitions of feeling that belong to the character and rehearse the greater transition the crisis completes. Readers who want the heroine's initiative audited scene by scene, rather than as a critic's portrait, will find the modern case assembled in [Juliet's Agency: The Girl Who Drives the Plot](/2022/07/08/juliet-agency-character-analysis/); Jameson's version of that case is older than the agency vocabulary and, in its insistence that the crisis is a revelation rather than a change, arguably subtler.

The defence reaches its most daring point on the question of the lie. Juliet's dissembling submission to her parents, the feigned repentance that sends her smiling to the friar's cell, was exactly the sort of feminine deceit the conduct tradition existed to condemn. Jameson declines the condemnation outright. It is idle to criticize her dissembling submission, she writes; a higher duty has taken the place of that which she owed to them, and a more sacred tie has severed all others. The dissimulation, like the courage, though it springs from passion, is justified by principle, and she quotes the heroine's own theology of the vow as the warrant: "My husband is on earth, my faith in heaven" (3.5.205), and, before the friar, "God joined my heart and Romeo's, thou our hands" (4.1.55). A married woman's first allegiance runs to her husband and her sacrament, and a girl who lies to escape a bigamous second marriage is not a liar but a wife defending the one estate her world allowed her to choose. That an evangelical age's bestselling critic of the heroines printed this reasoning, and was rewarded rather than ruined for it, says as much about her authority as any sentence in the book: she had made herself the trusted interpreter of female character to the very readers whose daughters the reasoning concerned.

The household portraits on which the crisis depends show the sociological eye at its sharpest. Lady Capulet sweeps through the chapter in her train of velvet, her black hood, her fan, and her rosary, the very beau-ideal of a proud Italian matron, her offer to poison Romeo in revenge for Tybalt stamping her with the characteristic trait of the age and country; and yet she loves her daughter, and Jameson pauses over the remorseful tenderness of the lament for the one poor and loving child, the one thing to rejoice and solace in (4.5.46-49 passim), because the tenderness explains the daughter's timid softness and the harsh subjection in which she had been kept. The Nurse receives the volume's most famous piece of character painting outside the heroines themselves: drawn, Jameson writes, with the most wonderful power and discrimination, her low humour, shallow garrulity, dotage, subserviency, secrecy, and total want of elevated principle brought before the reader like a living and palpable truth, with the prosaic homeliness of a Dutch painting whose figures start back at the viewer as from a reality. Even the choice of confidante is argued as a stroke of preservation: set beside a commonplace waiting-woman, or Portia's Nerissa, or Desdemona's Emilia, Juliet's sweetness and dignity would have been lowered by the company; the foster-mother's vulgarity keeps the heroine's character inviolate to the fancy even amid the wilfulness of passion. This is criticism thinking in systems of relation, a whole household arranged as the heroine's explanation, and it anticipates by a century the social readings that pride themselves on having replaced her.

### What does Jameson say about the lovers' tomb?

She reserves the catastrophe for separate consideration and then treats it as transfiguration. Against the source tradition, in which the heroine wakes in time for a final interview, she sides with Schlegel in approving Shakespeare's refusal of the added horror: the tale is one of love and sorrow, not of anguish and terror.

The tomb is made a shrine of martyred and sainted affection, consecrated for the worship of all hearts, not a dark charnel vault. The heroine wakes calm and collected, asks where her Romeo is, utters no reproach, and dies, leaving an impression of melancholy that Schlegel compared to one long, endless sigh.

## What Did Jameson Refuse to See in Juliet?

The chapter's silences are systematic, and a verdict that omitted them would repeat the century's error in reverse. The first silence concerns the heroine's strength itself. Having traced Juliet's rise to majesty in despair, Jameson immediately refunds the achievement: the energy she displays is founded in the strength of passion, not in the strength of character; it is accidental rather than inherent, rising with the tide of feeling and with it subsiding. The contrast case is ready to hand in her own class, for Helena, she writes in the next study, is the union of strength of passion with strength of character, and Juliet is pointedly not. A heroine may thus perform courage, command, deception, and self-destruction, and still be denied character, because the critic's system requires passion's heroines to be moved rather than movers. Modern readers need no theory to feel the strain in the formula; the girl who audits her nurse, dismisses her, plans her next move, and holds her nerve through the potion terrors is exercising exactly the faculties the formula reserves for Portia and Helena. The full study of the women who surround and form the heroine, [The Women of Verona: Juliet, Lady Capulet, Nurse](/2023/05/05/women-of-verona-romeo-and-juliet/), shows how much of Juliet's making Jameson saw clearly in the household, her haughty parents and her plebeian nurse placed around her as both cause and excuse of her conduct, and how the system's categories kept her from drawing the conclusion her own scenes supported.

The second silence is a number. Nowhere in the long chapter does Jameson reckon with the heroine's stated age, the not-yet-fourteen of the play's first act. She writes of a young heart, a child before a festival, a fond and impatient girl, but the arithmetic that makes the play's haste a scandal of child marriage is never performed, and the climate theory does the work the number would have disturbed: a southern girlhood ripens early as a law of nature, and what might have been an outrage becomes a botany. Readers who want the age weighed on its own terms should go to [Juliet at Thirteen: Shakespeare's Daring Heroine](/2022/07/01/juliet-thirteen-years-old-heroine/); the point here is narrower, that the Victorian Juliet's acceptability was partly an effect of this tact, a refusal to say thirteen in a chapter that quotes nearly everything else. The third silence is the feud as a system. Jameson sees the parents as persons, a self-willed, violent, tyrannical father to whom his daughter is a property, the appanage of his house, and she sees the household's regime of subjection and indulgence with a sociologist's eye; but the street war, the Prince's failed law, the marriage market as civic machinery, all the structures later criticism would put at the centre, enter her chapter only as weather around the lovers, all hate surrounding all love. Her Verona is a drawing room with a tomb attached.

The fourth limit is announced by the chapter itself, in its closing diagnosis, and no account of the reading is honest that suppresses it. With all this immense capacity of affection and imagination, Jameson concludes, there is in Juliet a deficiency of reflection and of moral energy, arising from previous habit and education; the action of the drama, while it serves to develop the character, appears but its natural and necessary result. She seals the judgment with Madame de Staël's maxim to her daughter, that the mystery of existence is the relation of our errors to our sufferings. The moral frame of the whole book here closes over its most sympathetic portrait: the heroine is deficient, her catastrophe is the arithmetic of her errors, and the chapter that defended her desire ends by entering her in a ledger of fault. That Jameson could hold the defence and the diagnosis together is not hypocrisy but the period's critical settlement, admiration disciplined by moral bookkeeping; that her century remembered the admiration and the halo, and forgot both the defence and the diagnosis, is the accident of reception the next sections trace.

Two of her own set pieces give the limits their sharpest local form. She lingers over the heroine's waking in the tomb and finds there the chapter's most quoted tenderness: the profound slumber has tranquillized her nerves and stilled the fever in her blood, and she wakes like a sweet child who has been dreaming of something promised to it by its mother, and opens her eyes to ask for it. The sentence is exquisite, and it is a falsification, since the Juliet who wakes in the fifth act is the woman of the crisis scene, who takes in the whole horror at a glance, reproaches nobody, wastes no word, and chooses her instrument with a wife's economy. Jameson's imagination chapter had established the mechanism exactly: fancy, once thrown off its poise, realizes its own creations. In the tomb paragraph the critic's fancy does the same to the heroine, softening a command performance into a nursery awakening because the icon under construction required a child at the centre of the shrine. The companion piece is her defence of Romeo's last speech, which she will not allow to be read as the raving of a disappointed boy: in its deep pathos, its rapturous despair, its glowing imagery, she finds the very luxury of life and love, and the catastrophe is wrapped in a quotation from Goethe, youthful passion as a shell fired by night, rising to dwell a moment with the stars before it falls and bursts. The Goethe emblem is beautiful and evasive in the same way: a meteor has no biography, no household, no nurse, no choices; it rises and bursts by ballistics. Where her method had been social and sequential all chapter, her catastrophe is meteorological, and the deficiency she diagnosed in the heroine, of reflection and moral energy, is quietly transferred to the heavens.

The charter text of the icon is her sentence on the deaths themselves. Romeo and Juliet must die, she writes; their destiny is fulfilled; they have quaffed off the cup of life, with all its infinite of joys and agonies, in one intoxicating draught, and what have they to do more upon this earth? The sentence deserves scrutiny because it converts a catastrophe of failed messages, civic violence, and adult coercion into a consummation, a life completed rather than a life destroyed, and it does so by an act of pure critical will: no evidence in the play suggests that the lovers have exhausted existence, and the friar's machinery of the ending protests the opposite on every page. Yet this is the sentence the century metabolized. A Juliet whose death is her fulfilment can be adored without being interrogated; her story asks nothing of the world that arranged it. Jameson's own chapter contains the antidote, in the household sociology, the diagnosis of deficient moral energy laid at the door of habit and education, and the acquittal that made the heroine's choices intelligible as choices. The icon was manufactured by keeping the cup-of-life sentence and discarding the antidote, and any honest account of the Victorian Juliet has to record that the raw material of the falsification was supplied, in her most lyrical paragraph, by the critic herself.

## Was Jameson the First Woman to Write Criticism on Shakespeare?

Not as the claim is usually made, and precision here is a debt the series owes its subject, because the inflated version erases two predecessors as thoroughly as the old dispensation erased Jameson herself. Charlotte Lennox had published *Shakespear Illustrated* in 1753 and 1754, a source study of the plays' debts to the Italian novella tradition that treated the poet's handling of his originals with a severity Dr Johnson, who contributed to its framing, did not soften. Elizabeth Montagu's *Essay on the Writings and Genius of Shakespear* of 1769, a defence of the plays against Voltaire's neoclassical indictment, was a European success and made its author, for a generation, the most famous woman writing on the national poet. Any history that calls Jameson the first woman critic of Shakespeare is therefore false, and her champions do her no service by repeating it.

The predecessors deserve their outlines, because the comparison defines the novelty exactly. Lennox's project was philological and combative: a collation of the plays against their Italian and French sources, arguing, often against the grain of bardolatry, that the poet's alterations were as often worsenings as improvements, and that judgment required the evidence of the originals on the desk. Montagu's was national and philosophical: a vindication of Shakespeare's irregular genius against the measured drama of France, which made the Essay a standard text of the bluestocking circle and its author a European reputation. Both women wrote about Shakespeare as one subject among others, in single works, from positions secured by rank, marriage, or salon. Jameson's difference is categorical in three respects at once. Her subject is the women alone, sustained across a system; her position is professional, the book her livelihood's foundation rather than her leisure's ornament; and her implied reader is female, addressed not as an auditor of men's conversation but as the proprietor of the subject. Priority claims in literary history usually conceal an argument about what counts as the real thing; here the argument can be made openly. If criticism means occasional writing on Shakespeare by a woman, the line starts with Lennox and Montagu. If it means a woman making the interpretation of his heroines her life's public work, it starts in 1832, and the second sense is the one the subsequent century ratified by living inside it. What she was first in is narrower and, on examination, more significant. She was the first writer of either sex to build a whole book, and a critical system, on the heroines as a company: to take the women of the plays, all twenty-three of them, as a subject sufficient for two volumes of classification, comparison, and judgment. She was the first woman to make Shakespeare criticism a profession's foundation rather than an occasion, the book from which a fifty-year career in print was launched. And she was the first to address the criticism of the plays systematically to women readers as their own intellectual property, a transfer of custody whose consequences the Victorian decades display.

Julie Hankey, surveying the tradition, put the consequence in a sentence that has become the standard summary: after Jameson it became commonplace to describe Shakespeare as the champion of women. The phrase catches the reversal her book performed in the culture's account of the poet. Before it, Shakespeare's women were ornaments of his genius, praised in passing within studies of the plays; after it, the heroines were the standing proof of his sympathy with female nature, and the poet was rebranded as women's advocate, a brand that served publishers, teachers, and lecturers for the rest of the century. Jameson herself encouraged the rebranding, presenting the plays as a gallery in which women's hearts and souls lay open for judgment by all, and her own sex's judgment in particular. The Victorian Juliet, adored, quoted, embroidered, and set before schoolgirls, is the largest single deposit of that rebranding, and the speed with which the culture converted a critical argument into a devotional image is the standing warning her reception offers to every later recovery, including the feminist one.

The rebranding had a commercial engine that pure intellectual history understates. A Shakespeare whose glory was his heroines was a Shakespeare who could be sold, without impropriety and at a profit, into the fastest-growing market in the book trade: women readers, girls' schools, and the family circle. Publishers built series, gift volumes, and expurgated texts on the foundation her book had certified, and the heroines circulated as a licensed form of female literary property in drawing rooms where the plays entire might have raised a question. Jameson did not design that market and cannot be charged with its tastes; her book's rigour, its German learning, its refusal to flatter the prudes, exceeded anything the market required. But the market took her warrant and declined her rigour, and the distinction between the critic and her commercial afterlife is the one her modern defenders and detractors both need, since the icon's crudities belong to the trade that excerpted her, while the chapter's crudities, the essentialism and the tactful silences, belong to her alone.

## How Did the Victorian Century Turn a Reading into an Icon?

The transmission began with the book's own afterlife. Edition succeeded edition through the middle decades of the century, the title softening from the analytic *Characteristics of Women* to the devotional *Shakespeare's Heroines*, and the studies passing into the common stock of quotation from which lecturers, editors, and gift-book compilers drew without acknowledgment. The Atlantic crossing came early and mattered: American reprints put the book into the hands of the republic's rapidly growing female readership within the decade, and Jameson's own winter in Canada in 1836 and 1837, recorded in *Winter Studies and Summer Rambles*, made her a transatlantic personage whose Shakespeare studies travelled with her name. Clara Thomas, her Canadian biographer, documents the loyal and discriminating audience her critical works won on two continents; the heroines were read in Boston and Toronto as attentively as in London, and the Juliet they standardized was exported, already idealized, into the schoolrooms and parlours of the English-speaking world. A reading composed in a London lodging by a separated Irishwoman thus became, within a generation, the common sense of two continents about what Shakespeare's heroine was, which is the scale on which her achievement, and her responsibility for the icon, must both be measured. Jameson died in 1860, before the period that bears the queen's name had reached its height, yet the Juliet her chapter had composed was precisely the Juliet that high Victorian culture required: passionate yet pure, desiring yet innocent, dead before experience could complicate her, and available as a pattern of feeling for girls who were to admire the passion and imitate none of the conduct. The icon was assembled by selection. Her century reprinted the halo passages, the love-itself sentence, the shrine of sainted affection, and let the forensic passages, the acquittals of frankness, deception, and disobedience, fall out of the common version. A portrait that had argued became an image that merely glowed.

John Ruskin's lecture Of Queens' Gardens, delivered in 1864 and published in *Sesame and Lilies* in 1865, shows the transmission at its most authoritative. Ruskin instructs the nation's daughters that Shakespeare has no heroes, only heroines, that his women are the guides and redeemers of his erring men, and that Romeo himself is an impatient boy, a judgment that would have startled the critic who found the lovers equal in singleness of purpose. The lecture is Jameson's project with the argument removed and the pedagogy retained: the heroines remain a curriculum for female formation, but where she had discriminated twenty-three distinct organizations of mind, Ruskin flattens them into a single office, guidance, and where she had defended a heroine's desire against the prudes, he commends the heroines to girls as patterns of a purity that costs the desiring Juliet her desire. The line from the *Characteristics* to the queens' gardens is direct and documented in the habits of the age's reading, and it is the clearest case of the century converting her criticism into its ideology.

The actresses carried the reading onto the stage and into a second life as performance criticism. The role itself had become the era's great debut vehicle and touchstone, a tradition that runs from Fanny Kemble's sensational first appearance in the part to Ellen Terry's Juliet at the Lyceum in 1882, and each generation's leading lady was measured against a standard of youthful purity that Jameson's portrait had done more than any staging to define. Helena Faucit, the leading tragic actress of the middle century, published her own studies of the heroines in 1885 as *On Some of Shakespeare's Female Characters*, writing on Juliet as a practitioner where Jameson had written as a reader, and the succession marks the tradition's maturity: the woman critic of Shakespeare was no longer a novelty to be apologized for but an office with incumbents. The novelistic succession ran in parallel. Mary Cowden Clarke, Jameson's heir in the character tradition, took the method one step past criticism into invention, imagining the heroines' childhoods in the tales gathered as *The Girlhood of Shakespeare's Heroines*; her Juliet is treated whole in [Mary Cowden Clarke and Juliet's Girlhood](/2026/11/13/mary-cowden-clarke-juliet-girlhood/), and her presence here is the measure of what Jameson founded, a licensed imaginative commerce between women writers and Shakespeare's girls. Between the lecture platform, the stage, and the tale, the Victorian Juliet was complete by the century's last decades: an icon of youthful, sanctified, safely deceased passion, bearing Jameson's features and almost none of her arguments.

The machinery of the conversion is worth naming, since it operated on the critic as well as the character. Jameson herself was processed into an icon by the memoir her niece Geraldine Macpherson prefixed to her posthumous remains in 1878: the professional writer of Thomas's later research, who had bargained with publishers and destroyed her own letters, reemerged as a saint of feminine letters, all patience and uplift, so that the woman was subjected to the same selection as her heroine. The material carriers did the rest. Family editions of the plays, the tradition Hazlitt had already protested when a family Shakespeare cut the wedding-night speech outright, taught generations to meet a Juliet from whom the desire had been editorially removed before criticism ever began; school and gift printings of the *Characteristics* itself excerpted the portraits as improving reading, detaching them from the dialogue frame in which Alda wins her arguments; and the illustrated Juliet of the gift books, all moonlight and balcony, completed the substitution of image for argument. An icon, unlike an argument, cannot be cross-examined, and the Victorian Juliet's long immunity to question is the measure of how completely the conversion succeeded. What the feminist recovery of the late twentieth century had to recover was therefore double: a critic from her memoir, and a chapter from its excerpts.

## Do Feminist Critics Acquit Anna Jameson, or Convict Her?

The twentieth century inherited the icon and, in its first scholarly dealings with the critic, mistook the icon for the book. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, in the very article that credits the *Characteristics* as the first work to display her original thought, describes its author as a penetrating but essentially feminine mind, a compliment engineered to contain what it praises: the criticism is delicate, intuitive, a woman's insight into her own sex, and therefore not quite criticism at all. Clara Thomas's *Love and Work Enough* (1967), the biography on which all later work rests, recovered the life with sympathy and labour, establishing the professional facts, the separation, the earnings, the friendships, that this article has relied on; but Thomas's Jameson remains substantially the Britannica's, a writer of taste and feeling whose criticism charms more than it argues. On that settled view, the Juliet chapter is a rhapsody in the feminine mode, and its Victorian fame a fashion's accident. The disagreement that defines the modern scholarship begins when feminist critics reopened the book and reported a different contents page.

Judith Johnston's *Anna Jameson: Victorian, Feminist, Woman of Letters* (1997) makes the recovery's strongest case. Johnston reads the professions of separate spheres and feminine essence as the period costume of a writer who used them as a passport: inside the conduct-book cover, Jameson was conducting an argument about women's judgment, women's reading, and women's authority over the national poet that her market would not have bought undressed. The dialogue frame is Johnston's Exhibit A, a woman reasoner defeating a male skeptic on the record, in print, as the book's front door; the defences of the heroines are Exhibit B, a systematic refusal to judge female characters by the rules their author knew her readers had been taught. Anne Russell, in her 1991 study of Jameson and Victorian women readers, supplies the reception evidence that complements the recovery: women readers used the heroines actively, as instruments for imagining and negotiating their own lives, so that the book's career among its intended public was less a discipline imposed than a resource seized. Gail Marshall's *Shakespeare and Victorian Women* (2009) extends the same line across the century's actresses and writers, treating the heroines as a sanctioned space in which Victorian women articulated ambitions and frustrations that had no other licensed venue; on Marshall's account the icon was never merely an icon to the women who lived with it, but a tool whose official meaning and actual use diverged. Cheri Larsen Hoeckley, editing the *Characteristics* for Broadview Press in 2005, adds the formal observation that the book's generic restlessness, its refusal to settle as conduct manual, criticism, or dialogue, is itself a resistance to the categories its author appeared to accept. Against these stands the older containment reading in its sophisticated form: whatever the chapter's local audacities, the system that houses them, an eternal feminine nature distributed among fixed classes, taught two generations to meet Juliet as an essence rather than an agent, and the icon her century made of the heroine is the system's natural fruit, not its betrayal.

The adjudication this article reaches is a split verdict with the weight on recovery. On the chapter itself, Johnston wins, and the proof is textual: no merely feminine rhapsodist rebukes the prudes over the wedding-night soliloquy, acquits a wife's lie to her parents on the theology of her vow, or performs the imagistic census of the balcony scene; those are acts of criticism and of advocacy, executed in full knowledge of the charges they answer. On the frame, the containment reading wins: the classes do naturalize a female essence, the southern-temperament argument does excuse by climate what the text presents as choice, and the closing diagnosis of deficient reflection and moral energy does hand the heroine back to the moral ledger. The frame's premise is stated in the chapter's most famous sentence's own preamble, that all Shakespeare's women, being essentially women, either love or have loved or are capable of loving; the essence precedes the evidence, and a criticism that begins by knowing what women essentially are will end, as hers does, by forgiving its heroine her mind. The recovery and the containment are therefore not rival descriptions of one object but descriptions of the object's two storeys, and the error of each party is to report its storey as the whole house. Marianne Novy's reading of the play in *Love's Argument: Gender Relations in Shakespeare* (1984) supplies the standard by which both verdicts can be measured, since Novy finds in the lovers' language a mutual, reciprocal creation, two voices building a shared world against the patriarchal economy of Verona; measured by that text, Jameson saw more of the reciprocity than any critic before the moderns, in her account of the lovers who speak only of themselves and see only themselves in the universe, and less of the agency, since her system forbade her to call the heroine's strength character. The Victorian Juliet was thus neither Jameson's invention entire nor her betrayal entire: it is what a culture keeps when it takes a portrait's glow and declines its arguments, and her chapter remains the document in which both the glow and the arguments can still be recovered.

The chronology of the reputation explains why the recovery took the shape it did. Jameson died in 1860 with her fame at its height, and the fame then set like plaster: the art books were superseded by Ruskin's more polemical histories, the travel books by later travellers, and the *Characteristics*, kept in print by its school and gift market, was read as a devotional classic long after it had ceased to be read as criticism. Professional Shakespeare scholarship, organizing itself in the universities around the edition and the source study, had no shelf for character portraiture by a woman amateur, and the mid-twentieth century met her, when it met her at all, through the Britannica's formula. Thomas's biography of 1967 is the hinge: by restoring the professional life it made the condescension untenable, though Thomas's own estimate of the criticism stayed within the older frame. The feminist scholarship of the 1990s then did for the *Characteristics* what Jameson had done for the heroines, reading the whole performance in sequence instead of in excerpts, and found the argument the excerpts had hidden. Hoeckley's Broadview edition of 2005 marks the institutional completion of the turn, the book installed as a teachable classic of criticism with its dialogue frame, its polemics, and its heroines entire. The dispute between recovery and containment is thus not only about what Jameson meant; it is about which Jameson the evidence of two centuries of reading supports, the one constructed by her excerptors or the one recoverable from her pages, and the recovery's documentary advantage is that it can quote the pages.

## Anna Jameson's Juliet: The Final Verdict

Jameson's contribution, judged whole, is threefold. She founded a genre: the heroines as a system, read by a professional woman critic for a public that included the women the heroines were said to school, and every later inhabitant of that office, from Faucit to the feminist scholars who quarrel over her, works in rooms she built. She wrote the strongest defence of Juliet the nineteenth century produced, a defence that met the charges of forwardness, sensuality, and deceit on the evidence of the text and defeated them with the heroine's own speeches, and whose central sentences, Juliet is love itself, the passion her state of being, remain the starting point from which accounts of the character still depart or against which they still rebel. And she set the terms of the error that followed her, since the essentialism of her classes and the tact of her silences taught her century which Juliet could be loved in public: the desiring, deceiving, disobedient girl her own chapter had acquitted was re-hung in the national gallery as an angel, and stayed there until critics armed with her own book began, a century and a half later, to take the angel down and read the acquittal again. The cliche says the Victorians sentimentalized Juliet. The record says one Victorian woman argued for her, in print, against the prudes and the moralists of her own moment, and that the sentimentality was the price later readers charged her argument for its fame. Her Juliet is not the play's last word; no single portrait could be. It is the first word spoken with full authority by the sex the heroine belonged to, and the play's criticism has been answering it, in agreement and in revolt, ever since.

For a reader of the play, the chapter's practical bequest is a discipline and a warning in equal parts. The discipline is evidentiary: follow the character through every appearance, quote the speeches entire, weigh each quality against its opposite number in another soul, and let the portrait be what the accumulation supports. No account of Juliet written since has improved on that procedure, and the weakest modern readings are usually those that abandon it for a thesis the scenes were never asked to confirm. The warning is the frame: a critic's system will decide in advance what a heroine is permitted to be, and Jameson's classes permitted passion everything except a mind. Reading her with the summary in hand, acquitting her where the text acquits Juliet and convicting her where her own scenes convict her system, is the exercise her book was always, at its best, training its readers to perform.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Jameson summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Jameson summary gathers the load-bearing claims of Anna Jameson's 1832 chapter on Juliet into a single table, each claim stated in her terms and keyed to the passages she rests it on. It collects her classification of the heroine, her central claim that Juliet is love itself, her accounts of passion, imagination, frankness, and the crisis of the nurse's betrayal, her acquittals of the deception and the wedding-night speech, and her closing diagnosis. The pattern it exposes is the chapter's double motion: a forensic defence of the heroine's desire and conduct, conducted inside a system of fixed feminine classes that finally limits what the defence may claim.

### Q: Who was Anna Jameson before she wrote on Shakespeare's heroines?

She was Anna Brownell Murphy of Dublin, born in 1794, the daughter of a miniaturist, at work as a governess from the age of sixteen. A continental tour in a companion's role produced *The Diary of an Ennuyée*, published in 1826 to considerable success, and her marriage to the lawyer Robert Jameson in 1825 ended in separation, leaving authorship as her livelihood. *The Loves of the Poets* followed in 1829. By the time the studies of the heroines appeared in 1832 she was already a professional writer moving among the Kembles, the Carlyles, and the Brownings, and the book made her reputation for original thought.

### Q: When was Characteristics of Women first published, and under what later title is it read?

It was first published in London in 1832, in two volumes from Saunders and Otley, with the full title *Characteristics of Women, Moral, Poetical, and Historical* and a half title announcing Shakespeare's female characters. The first edition carried fifty vignette etchings drawn by Jameson herself. Edition followed edition through the nineteenth century, with American reprints multiplying its public, and later issues carried the work under the title by which it is generally read, *Shakespeare's Heroines*. The Broadview Press edition of 2005, prepared by Cheri Larsen Hoeckley, is the form in which modern students most often meet the original text.

### Q: In which class of heroines does Jameson place Juliet, and who shares the class with her?

Juliet stands first among the Characters of Passion and Imagination, the second of the book's four classes. Her companions in the class are Helena, Perdita, Viola, Ophelia, and Miranda, heroines in whom feeling and fancy rule over intellect and over the settled affections. The other classes are the Characters of Intellect, Portia, Isabella, Beatrice, and Rosalind; the Characters of the Affections, Hermione, Desdemona, Imogen, and Cordelia; and the Historical Characters, from Cleopatra and Volumnia to Lady Macbeth. Within her own class Juliet is the extreme case, the one heroine in whom the ruling faculties exist without admixture.

### Q: What does Jameson mean by calling Juliet love itself?

She means that the passion in Juliet is not an episode or a trait but her state of being, outside which she has no existence. Her argument begins from the maxim she takes from La Rochefoucauld, that there is but one love though a thousand different copies: the single sentiment appears chaste in Portia, confiding in Perdita, devoted in Desdemona, tender in Viola, and in Juliet each and all of these at once. Juliet is therefore not the best of the copies but the original of them, the heroine who reminds the reader of nothing but herself, and in whom the subject of the whole class stands complete.

### Q: How does Jameson distinguish Juliet from Helena, who stands in the same class?

Helena is her chosen contrast, passion joined to strength of character. In the study that follows Juliet's, Jameson argues that Helena loves with the same unity of purpose but commands herself in the prosecution of her design, her heart immovable under the most imperious causes of emotion. Juliet's energy, by contrast, is founded in the strength of passion and not in the strength of character; it is accidental rather than inherent, rising with the tide of feeling and subsiding with it. The distinction is the class's internal frontier, and it marks the limit of Jameson's admiration: Juliet has the greater glow, Helena the greater government.

### Q: How does Jameson defend Juliet's frankness in the balcony scene?

She quotes the heroine's apologies for her own openness and rules that they acquit her. Juliet's wish to dwell on form, her offer to frown and be perverse if Romeo will woo, and her plea that her yielding not be imputed to light love (2.2.137-149) are read as the jealousy of female honour that education has infused, operating without one real doubt of his truth. Modesty is relocated from behaviour to feeling: the girl is modest not because she hides her love but because she suffers in confessing it. Frankness becomes the signature of innocence, and the charge of forwardness is returned on the accusers.

### Q: How does Jameson answer the critics who called the wedding-night speech indelicate?

With open contempt for the commentary rather than the speech. She records her shock at the want of taste and refinement in those who, with coarse derision or in a spirit of prudery yet more gross and perverse, had dared to comment on the soliloquy (3.2.1-31), which she names a hymn to the night. Her defence is dramatic before it is moral: Juliet addresses no audience and no confidante, but thinks aloud in the silence of her chamber, and her impatience has the simplicity of a child before a festival with new robes it may not wear. The indelicacy, she rules, lies in the critics.

### Q: Which scene does Jameson treat as the crisis of Juliet's character?

The interview in which the nurse counsels Juliet to forget Romeo and marry Paris (3.5). Jameson writes that the moment which unveils the weakness and baseness of the confidante is the moment which reveals Juliet to herself, and she calls the scene the crisis in the character. The heroine answers amazement with scorn, closes her childhood with a single word of assent to the nurse's oath, and dismisses her counsellor forever: henceforth, in Jameson's formula, the fond, impatient, timid girl puts on the wife and the woman, having learned heroism from suffering and subtlety from oppression.

### Q: How does Jameson justify Juliet's deception of her parents?

She rules the criticism of it idle. A higher duty has taken the place of the filial duty, she argues, and a more sacred tie has severed all the others: the marriage vow outranks obedience, so the dissimulation, like the courage, though it springs from passion, is justified by principle. She rests the warrant on the heroine's own theology of the sacrament, her declaration that her husband is on earth and her faith in heaven (3.5.205), and her plea before the friar that God joined her heart and Romeo's, and the friar their hands (4.1.55). The feigned submission is not deceit but a wife's defence of her estate.

### Q: What deficiency does Jameson finally diagnose in Juliet?

A deficiency of reflection and of moral energy, arising from previous habit and education. The sentence closes her chapter and qualifies everything in it: with all the heroine's immense capacity of affection and imagination, the weighing faculty and the principled stamina are wanting, so that the action of the drama appears the natural and necessary result of the character. She seals the diagnosis with Madame de Staël's maxim that the mystery of existence is the relation of our errors to our sufferings. The judgment is the moral frame of the whole book closing over its most sympathetic portrait, admiration entered in a ledger of fault.

### Q: Does Jameson discuss Juliet's stated age anywhere in her essay?

She never performs the arithmetic. The chapter is full of youth, of the young heart, of a child before a festival, of a fond and impatient girl, but the play's precise figure, a heroine not yet fourteen (1.3), is nowhere confronted, and the work the number might have done is done instead by her theory of climate, in which a southern girlhood ripens early by a law of nature. The omission is structural, not accidental: the Victorian Juliet's acceptability depended in part on not saying thirteen in a chapter that quotes nearly everything else, and the tact tells a reader as much about 1832 as about the play.

### Q: Why does Jameson call Juliet's temperament southern?

Because climate is her master explanation for the heroine's precocity, vehemence, and imagery. Love, she argues, is wild and passionate in the south and deep and contemplative in the north, and Juliet's imagination is part of her southern temperament, controlling and modifying the rest of her character. She allies the heroine to Boccaccio's Gismunda and Fiammetta in the Italian spirit and the glowing national complexion of the portrait. The theory rescues Juliet from English standards of decorum by naturalizing her passion as a product of place, and in the same motion confines her within an essence of blood and latitude that the heroine herself never invokes.

### Q: Was Anna Jameson the first woman to publish criticism of Shakespeare?

Not in the unqualified form the claim usually takes. Charlotte Lennox had published *Shakespear Illustrated*, her source study of the plays, in 1753 and 1754, and Elizabeth Montagu's *Essay on the Writings and Genius of Shakespear* of 1769, a defence of the poet against Voltaire, was a European success. Jameson's priority is narrower and stronger: the first book-length system built on the heroines as a company, twenty-three of them classified and compared; the first foundation of a professional critical career laid by a woman on Shakespeare; and the first systematic address of the plays' criticism to women readers as its rightful owners.

### Q: To whom was Characteristics of Women dedicated, and why does the dedication matter?

The first edition was dedicated to Fanny Kemble, Jameson's friend and the most celebrated young actress of the period, whose Covent Garden performances as Juliet had made her the era's defining embodiment of the heroine. The dedication binds the printed criticism to the living stage and declares the book's allegiance: the study of Shakespeare's women is offered to the woman who was, in the public's freshest memory, Juliet herself. It also marks the sociology of the enterprise, a woman critic and a woman performer sustaining one another's authority in a culture that granted neither automatically, each lending the other a portion of her fame.

### Q: How did John Ruskin take up Jameson's way of reading the heroines?

In the lecture Of Queens' Gardens, delivered in 1864 and published in *Sesame and Lilies* in 1865, Ruskin instructed the nation's daughters that Shakespeare has no heroes, only heroines, and set the women of the plays before girls as guides and patterns of conduct. The project is Jameson's, the heroines as a curriculum for female formation, with her argument removed: where she discriminated twenty-three distinct organizations of mind, Ruskin flattens the heroines into a single office of guidance, and where she defended Juliet's desire, he commends purity and dismisses Romeo as an impatient boy. The transmission shows exactly how her criticism was converted into the century's ideology.

### Q: What happened to Jameson's Juliet in the later Victorian decades?

It was canonized by selection. Editions multiplied under the softer title *Shakespeare's Heroines*, lecturers and gift books reprinted the halo passages, the sentence that Juliet is love itself and the tomb made a shrine of sainted affection, and the forensic passages, the acquittals of frankness, deception, and disobedience, fell out of the common version. Helena Faucit's studies of the heroines in 1885 carried the woman-critic tradition onto the stage's own ground, and Mary Cowden Clarke's invented girlhoods extended it into fiction. What descended was an icon of passionate purity, bearing Jameson's features and almost none of her arguments.

### Q: How does Judith Johnston's reading of Jameson differ from the older biographical view?

The older view, fixed by the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*'s penetrating but essentially feminine mind and sustained in Clara Thomas's biography of 1967, treats the criticism as delicate appreciation, a woman's intuition about her own sex. Johnston's *Anna Jameson: Victorian, Feminist, Woman of Letters* (1997) reads the feminine professions as a passport: inside the conduct-book cover, Jameson argues for women's judgment, women's reading, and women's authority over the national poet, staging her claim in a dialogue where a woman reasoner defeats a male skeptic. The disagreement is over whether the frame contains the argument or smuggles it.

### Q: What does Marianne Novy's reading of the play restore that Jameson's century lost?

Reciprocity. In *Love's Argument: Gender Relations in Shakespeare* (1984), Novy finds in the lovers' language a mutual creation, two voices building a shared world of value against the patriarchal economy of Verona, in which the heroine is a full partner in the making of the relationship rather than its ornament. Jameson had glimpsed the fact, in her lovers who speak only of themselves and see only themselves in the universe, but her system, which denied Juliet strength of character, could not name it agency. Novy's reading supplies the standard by which the Victorian portrait can be measured: right about the mutuality, silent about the power.

### Q: How can an essay use the InsightCrunch Jameson summary to argue that Jameson defended Juliet?

By working from the table's forensic rows rather than from the critic's reputation. The summary keys each acquittal to its passage: the frankness row to the balcony apologies (2.2.137-149), the deception row to the vow's theology (3.5.205; 4.1.55), the wedding-night row to the hymn passage and its rebuke of the prudes (3.2.1-31), and the crisis row to the nurse's dismissal (3.5.235-242). An essay built on those rows can show that the Victorian reading of Juliet was an argument before it was an icon, and can then weigh, from the strength and diagnosis rows, exactly where the defence stopped and the period's limits began.
