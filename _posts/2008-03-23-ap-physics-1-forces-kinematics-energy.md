Mechanics is where the AP Physics 1 score is mostly decided, and the deciding skill is not formula recall. A student can memorize every equation on the reference sheet and still stall on a problem that hands over a height instead of a time, or a collision instead of a slope, because the memorized list says nothing about which tool the problem is asking for. The exam's mechanics questions are built around three great principles, force and Newton's second law, energy and its conservation, and momentum with impulse, and each problem quietly announces which principle governs it through the quantities it gives and the quantity it requests. Readers who hear that announcement solve in two lines what other readers grind through in two pages. Readers who miss it default to kinematics, the first tool the course teaches, and discover halfway down a page of algebra that the problem never mentioned time at all.

This article teaches the mechanics core the way the exam tests it, as a choosing discipline wrapped around solid content. The full architecture of the course, its units, its format, and its scoring, lives in [the complete AP Physics 1 guide](/2008/03/28/ap-physics-1-complete-guide/), and a broader method for attacking any mechanics problem lives in [the mechanics problem-solving framework](/2006/05/14/ap-physics-1-problem-solving-framework/). What follows here goes deep on the core itself: kinematics and its graphs, Newton's laws and the free-body diagrams that carry them, friction in its two forms, circular motion, energy and the work-energy theorem, and momentum through collisions, with worked examples whose arithmetic is shown and checked. The governing artifact is the InsightCrunch mechanics principle guide, and its claim deserves stating plainly at the start: mechanics points come from picking the right conservation principle, so studying when to use each principle beats memorizing formulas, every time the two habits compete.

## Where the mechanics core sits inside the exam

The course framework opens with kinematics, builds into forces and dynamics, passes through circular motion and gravitation, then turns to energy, momentum, oscillation, and rotation. The territory this article covers, motion description, forces, energy, and momentum with circular motion, forms the foundation under everything after it, because oscillation borrows its energy reasoning from this ground and rotation borrows its force reasoning. By framework emphasis the mechanics units together carry roughly half the examination's weight, and their share of free-response points runs higher still, since experimental design and paragraph-length response questions frequently dress themselves in mechanics clothing. A collision question can test graph reading. An energy question can test experimental design. The core is not one topic among seven; it is the language the rest of the exam speaks.

Format sharpens the point. Multiple-choice items reward fast principle recognition because each question offers perhaps ninety seconds of working time, and a reader who spends forty of those seconds choosing a method has already lost the margin. Free-response questions reward the same recognition more slowly and more explicitly, because the opening part of a long problem usually asks for a setup, a diagram, or a derivation, and every later part inherits that choice. A wrong principle selected in part (a) poisons parts (b) through (d) with a thoroughness no later brilliance repairs. Graders award the setup points to readers whose first written line names the right governing idea, which is why this article treats selection as a skill in its own right rather than as a prelude to the real work.

## Kinematics: describing motion before explaining it

Kinematics is the grammar of the course, the vocabulary of position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration, stated precisely enough that equations can be built from it. Displacement measures the change in position, a vector with direction built in, while distance measures the path length, a scalar that ignores direction entirely. Average velocity divides displacement by elapsed time. Instantaneous velocity is that ratio shrunk to a moment, and acceleration repeats the construction one level up, the rate at which velocity itself changes. Students lose early points by treating these definitions loosely, writing speed where the problem asked about velocity or distance where it asked about displacement, and the exam writes distractors that harvest exactly that looseness.

Graph reading is where kinematics becomes a scoring skill rather than a vocabulary list. On a position-time graph, slope gives velocity, and curvature betrays acceleration: a straightening curve means velocity is settling, a steepening curve means it is growing. On a velocity-time graph, slope gives acceleration and the signed area under the curve gives displacement, with area below the time axis counting negative. On an acceleration-time graph, the area gives the change in velocity. A single worked reading shows the economy of the method. Suppose a velocity graph rises in a straight line from rest to 6 meters per second across 3 seconds, then holds level. The slope of the rising part is 6 divided by 3, an acceleration of 2 meters per second squared. The displacement across those 3 seconds is the triangular area, one half times 3 times 6, which is 9 meters, found without a single kinematic equation because the graph already contains the answer.

The kinematic equations themselves apply under one strict condition: acceleration stays constant across the interval being analyzed. Under that condition, final velocity equals initial velocity plus acceleration times time; displacement equals initial velocity times time plus one half acceleration times time squared; and the time-free relation connects final velocity squared to initial velocity squared plus twice the acceleration times displacement. That third relation deserves special respect because it is the bridge the exam loves: any problem that gives a displacement and asks for a speed, with no mention of time, is either this equation or its energy twin, and strong readers check the energy route before committing. A clean example: a cart starts from rest and accelerates at 2 meters per second squared for 5 seconds. Final velocity is 0 plus 2 times 5, which is 10 meters per second. Displacement is one half times 2 times 25, which is 25 meters. Both answers arrive in one line each because the constant-acceleration condition genuinely holds.

### What should your eyes do in the first thirty seconds of a mechanics problem?

Your eyes should inventory the problem before solving it: name the quantity requested, list what is given with its units, and mark which of the four common quantities, time, height, force, or interaction, the problem centers on. That inventory, not an equation, is the correct first act, because the givens announce the governing principle and the requested quantity confirms it.

Thirty seconds spent this way feels slow to students who equate motion with progress, yet it is the cheapest time on the exam. The inventory exposes the classic ambush, a problem that supplies a time and a height and asks for a force, where each principle contributes one step and no single method runs the whole course. It also exposes missing information, which in physics is information: when a problem never gives a mass, the intended method almost always cancels mass, and when it never gives a time, energy or momentum is almost always the intended road. Reading for absence is a skill the inventory builds deliberately.

## Projectile motion: two one-dimensional problems sharing a clock

A projectile under gravity is two independent one-dimensional motions stapled together by time. Horizontally, no force acts once air resistance is set aside, so horizontal velocity never changes and horizontal displacement is simply that velocity times time. Vertically, gravity supplies a constant acceleration of about 10 meters per second squared downward, and the full kinematic machinery applies. The components of the launch velocity come from the launch speed and angle, and the trajectory's symmetry, equal times up and down, equal speeds at equal heights, falls out of the vertical analysis alone.

A worked launch fixes the pattern. Fire a projectile at 20 meters per second at 37 degrees above horizontal, the familiar three-four-five angle, so the horizontal component is 16 meters per second and the vertical component is 12. Time to the top is 12 divided by 10, which is 1.2 seconds, and the full flight over level ground lasts twice that, 2.4 seconds. Peak height is 12 squared divided by twice 10, which is 144 divided by 20, or 7.2 meters. Range is the horizontal component times the full flight time, 16 times 2.4, which is 38.4 meters. Every number came from splitting the vector once and then treating the vertical side as an ordinary kinematics exercise, which is precisely the treatment the exam's projectile items reward.

The traps are consistent enough to memorize as a set. The velocity at the peak is not zero; only its vertical component vanishes, while the horizontal component cruises on unchanged. Acceleration at the peak is not zero either; gravity does not pause at the top of an arc. A projectile launched and landing at different heights breaks the up-down symmetry, and the honest method is to solve the vertical equation for the actual landing condition rather than borrowing the level-ground range formula. Finally, the angle that maximizes range on level ground is 45 degrees, a fact worth knowing and worth deriving once from the range expression rather than merely quoting.

## Newton's laws: the three statements the whole core obeys

Newton's first law says an object maintains its velocity, constant in magnitude and direction, unless a net external force acts. The law sounds like a special case of the second and is not; it defines the frames in which the second law works and establishes that force explains changes in motion rather than motion itself. A sliding puck needs no force to keep sliding, and the everyday intuition that moving things naturally stop is friction's propaganda, not physics. On the exam, first-law reasoning answers the equilibrium family: objects at rest, objects gliding at constant velocity, and objects drifting in deep space all share zero acceleration and therefore zero net force, whatever individual forces act on them.

Newton's second law is the core's engine: the vector sum of external forces on a system equals the system's mass times its acceleration, or equivalently the net force divided by mass gives the acceleration. Two disciplines make it work. First, the system must be chosen before forces are listed, because the law counts only forces crossing the system's boundary; forces between parts inside the boundary cancel in pairs and never appear. Second, the equation is a vector equation applied component by component, so a careful reader picks axes, resolves forces along them, and writes one scalar equation per axis. Sloppy axis choice is the hidden tax on dynamics problems; aligning an axis with the acceleration converts two tangled equations into one clean equation and one equilibrium condition.

Newton's third law pairs every force with an equal, opposite force on the other object: the book pushes down on the table and the table pushes up on the book, and Earth pulls the ball down while the ball pulls Earth up. The exam's favorite confusion lives here, because third-law pairs act on different objects and therefore never cancel in a single free-body diagram. The normal force balancing gravity on a resting book is not a third-law pair, since both act on the book; gravity's pair is the book pulling Earth upward, and the normal force's pair is the book pushing the table downward. Readers who sort pairs by the objects involved stop donating these points.

Weight deserves its sentence because the exam exploits it: weight is the gravitational force, mass times g, directed toward Earth's center, while mass itself is inertia, the resistance to acceleration measured in kilograms. An astronaut's mass survives the trip to orbit intact; the weight reading on a scale does not, because a scale reports the normal force it supplies, not gravity itself. That distinction becomes points in an elevator: a 60 kilogram person standing on a scale in a car accelerating upward at 2 meters per second squared reads a normal force of 60 times the sum of 10 and 2, which is 720 newtons, against a resting weight of 600 newtons. Accelerating downward reverses the correction, and free fall erases the reading entirely, which is why orbiting astronauts, in continuous free fall, register as weightless while gravity still acts on them strongly.

## The free-body diagram: the drawing that decides the answer

Every force problem on the exam is graded, in effect, twice: once for the diagram and once for the algebra the diagram permits. A free-body diagram isolates one object, replaces it with a point, and draws every external force acting on that object as an arrow from the point, labeled by the agent supplying it. Gravity always appears, pulling toward Earth's center. Contact forces appear only where something touches the object: normal force perpendicular to the surface, friction parallel to it, tension along a rope, a push along the pusher's direction. Nothing else earns an arrow. Velocity gets no arrow. Acceleration gets no arrow. The fictitious extra force that students invent to explain motion, the force of the throw or of momentum, does not exist and its appearance in a diagram marks the exact misconception graders hunt.

Construction follows a discipline worth rehearsing until it is automatic. Choose the object and draw it alone. List every other object that touches it or pulls it at a distance, since each interaction contributes exactly its forces and nothing contributes unlisted forces. Draw, label, and only then choose axes, tilting them to match an incline or the acceleration direction when that simplifies components. Then write the second law per axis, with signs fixed by the axis choice, before substituting a single number. The order matters because substituting early locks arithmetic errors into symbolic relationships that a later check cannot untangle.

Consider a 2 kilogram block on a 30 degree incline with kinetic friction coefficient 0.2, the canonical diagram exercise. Gravity, 20 newtons straight down, resolves into a component along the slope of 20 times sine 30, which is 10 newtons pulling the block downhill, and a component into the slope of 20 times cosine 30, about 17.3 newtons. The surface answers the inward component with a normal force of 17.3 newtons, and kinetic friction opposes the sliding at 0.2 times 17.3, about 3.5 newtons directed up the slope. Net force along the slope is 10 minus 3.5, which is 6.5 newtons, and the acceleration is 6.5 divided by 2, about 3.3 meters per second squared down the slope. Each step is visible in the diagram before it appears in arithmetic, which is the entire point.

### Why do free-body diagrams decide more points than formulas?

Formulas merely execute a decision the diagram has already made, because the second law sums whatever forces the diagram admits and nothing else. A missing friction arrow or an invented push corrupts every later line while leaving the algebra internally clean, so graders see flawless mathematics answering a problem that was never asked. The diagram is where the physics happens.

The scoring structure rewards the drawing directly as well. Free-response dynamics questions routinely devote their opening part to the diagram alone, and later parts award method points that presume the correct force inventory. An elegant consequence follows for study habits: drilling diagrams without numbers, fifty prompts answered with arrows only, builds the exact skill the exam isolates, and builds it faster than full problem sets because each repetition isolates the decision step. Students who treat the diagram as scratch work to rush through are skipping the graded event itself.

## Friction: the force with a ceiling and a direction problem

Friction opposes relative sliding, or the tendency toward it, between surfaces in contact, and it comes in two forms whose rules differ in one crucial detail. Kinetic friction acts between surfaces already sliding, with a magnitude fixed by the kinetic coefficient times the normal force, dependable and directionally simple. Static friction acts between surfaces at rest relative to each other and adjusts itself to match the applied push, newton for newton, up to a maximum set by the static coefficient times the normal force. That ceiling is the trap: static friction equals the static coefficient times the normal force only at the instant of slipping, and writing the maximum value for a gently pushed object that is not moving manufactures acceleration out of stillness.

The distinction settles a standard problem family. A 5 kilogram crate rests on a floor with static coefficient 0.4 and kinetic coefficient 0.25, and a horizontal push grows slowly. The crate's weight and normal force are both 50 newtons. Static friction matches the push exactly, 10 newtons against a 10 newton push, 18 against 18, up to the ceiling of 0.4 times 50, which is 20 newtons. A 25 newton push exceeds that ceiling, so the crate breaks free, and once sliding, kinetic friction takes over at 0.25 times 50, which is 12.5 newtons. The net force at a steady 25 newton push is then 12.5 newtons and the acceleration 2.5 meters per second squared. Notice the strange consequence the exam loves: an object can sit motionless under a 19 newton push and then accelerate under a 25 newton push with friction dropping as motion begins, because the static ceiling exceeds the kinetic value.

Direction errors rival magnitude errors. Friction on a walking person points forward, since the foot pushes backward on the ground and the ground's friction drives the walker. Friction on a driven car wheel points forward for the same reason, while friction on a rolling free wheel points backward. On an incline, friction flips with the motion or the tendency: a block held by friction alone feels it up the slope, a block sliding down feels it up the slope as well, and a block sliding up feels it pointing down. The reliable procedure is never to assign friction's direction from habit but to ask, each time, which way the contact surface would slide without it.

Friction also writes energy's fine print, previewed here and developed in the energy section: kinetic friction converts mechanical energy into thermal energy in an amount equal to the friction force times the sliding distance, and that term is the work that breaks naive conservation. Any problem sliding an object across a rough surface to a stop is solvable two ways, by second-law kinematics or by the work-energy theorem, and comparing the two is a fine self-check. A 4 kilogram block entering a rough patch at 6 meters per second with kinetic coefficient 0.25 faces friction of 0.25 times 40, which is 10 newtons, a deceleration of 2.5 meters per second squared, and a stopping distance found from velocity squared over twice the deceleration: 36 divided by 5, which is 7.2 meters. The energy method agrees exactly, since the initial kinetic energy of 72 joules divided by the 10 newton friction force gives the same 7.2 meters, and agreement between independent routes is the strongest verification a student can perform mid-exam.

## Systems and connected objects: choosing the boundary that simplifies

Connected-object problems, blocks pushed together, masses roped over pulleys, trains of carts, reward one strategic decision above all others: apply the second law to the whole system first. Internal forces, the push between touching blocks, the tension along a rope joining two masses, cancel inside the whole-system boundary, so the system's acceleration falls out of the external forces alone, usually in a single line. Only after that acceleration is known does the analysis narrow to one part, where the connection force becomes external and solvable. Students who start with individual diagrams inherit two unknowns per object and a system of equations; students who start with the whole inherit one unknown and a plan.

A pushed pair shows the rhythm. A 10 newton force pushes a 3 kilogram block against a 2 kilogram block on a frictionless floor. Treated as one 5 kilogram system, the acceleration is 10 divided by 5, which is 2 meters per second squared. Now isolate the 2 kilogram block: the only horizontal force on it is the contact push from its partner, and that push must supply its acceleration, so the contact force is 2 times 2, which is 4 newtons. The remaining 6 newtons of the original push is what accelerates the first block itself. The asymmetry surprises students every year, the applied force is 10 newtons but the blocks touch with only 4, and the surprise dissolves once the system-first order is habitual.

The Atwood machine, two masses hanging from a rope over a pulley, is the same logic wearing a vertical costume. With masses of 3 and 5 kilograms, the heavier side descends. The whole system's driving force is the weight difference, 50 minus 30, which is 20 newtons, acting on the total 8 kilograms, giving an acceleration of 2.5 meters per second squared. Tension then comes from either mass alone: for the 3 kilogram mass rising at 2.5, tension minus its 30 newton weight equals 3 times 2.5, so tension is 37.5 newtons; checking against the descending mass, 50 minus tension equals 5 times 2.5, and tension is again 37.5 newtons. The agreement is not a coincidence but a verification, and writing both is a legitimate exam habit when seconds allow. Tension in an Atwood machine always lands strictly between the two weights, a sanity bound worth checking reflexively: any answer outside that interval is wrong before it is graded.

Ropes and pulleys carry assumptions the exam states or implies: a massless, frictionless pulley changes a rope's direction without changing the tension, and a rope that does not stretch forces connected objects to share one acceleration magnitude. Inclined Atwood variants, one mass on a slope joined to a hanging mass, yield to the same treatment with the slope mass contributing only its along-slope weight component as its external drive. When friction enters an Atwood or a pushed train, it is external and belongs in the whole-system equation from the start, opposing the motion of whichever part slides.

### When does a system beat a single object as the thing you analyze?

A system wins whenever the question asks about a shared acceleration or an overall motion and the connecting forces are not themselves requested, because the boundary swallows those internal forces whole. The single object takes over the moment a connection force, a tension, or a contact push is the quantity requested, since only an isolated diagram exposes it.

The judgment generalizes beyond ropes. Choosing the Earth plus a falling object as the system turns gravity internal and produces energy conservation; choosing the object alone keeps gravity external and produces work. Choosing two colliding carts as one system turns their violent interaction internal and produces momentum conservation. Across the whole mechanics core, the boundary choice is not bookkeeping; it is the move that decides which conservation law the problem obeys, which is exactly why the principle guide's rows begin with features and systems rather than with formulas.

## Circular motion: acceleration wearing a direction change

Uniform circular motion delivers the conceptual jolt that speed can stay fixed while velocity changes continuously, because direction is half of velocity's identity. The acceleration points toward the center, with magnitude equal to speed squared divided by radius, and the second law then demands a net force of mass times speed squared over radius, also toward the center. The crucial discipline, stated in the principle guide because the exam tests it relentlessly, is that centripetal force is not a new force to add to a diagram. It is the name for whatever net inward force real agents supply: tension for a whirling ball, gravity for an orbiting satellite, friction for a turning car, the normal force's component on a banked road.

A ball of 0.5 kilograms on a 0.8 meter string, swung in a horizontal circle at 4 meters per second, needs a net inward force of 0.5 times 16 divided by 0.8, which is 10 newtons, supplied entirely by the string's tension. Double the speed and the required force quadruples, a squared dependence that multiple-choice items probe with ratio questions far more often than with raw computation. Vertical circles add gravity's changing allegiance: at the top, gravity and tension both point toward the center and share the job; at the bottom, tension points in while gravity points out, so tension must cover the required net force and the weight besides, which is why strings and riders feel the bottom of a loop as the violent point.

The flat-curve car is the friction chapter's reunion with this one. A car rounding an unbanked curve of radius 25 meters on a surface with static coefficient 0.4 depends on static friction for its entire inward force, since the wheels are not sliding sideways when the maneuver succeeds. The maximum friction is 0.4 times the car's weight, and setting that equal to mass times speed squared over radius, the mass cancels, leaving maximum speed as the square root of 0.4 times 10 times 25, the square root of 100, which is 10 meters per second. Faster, and no force available can bend the path tightly enough; the car continues straighter than the road, a failure students misdescribe as being flung outward when the truth is that inward force ran out.

Gravitation extends the same structure to orbit: gravity alone supplies the inward force, so mass times speed squared over radius equals the gravitational attraction, and orbital speed falls out with the satellite's mass canceling entirely. That cancellation explains why a bolt and a station share an orbit, and it foreshadows a theme the energy section develops: in mechanics, mass frequently cancels, and answers independent of mass are a signature of gravity-driven problems rather than a sign of error.

## Work and energy: the bookkeeping that skips the middle of the story

Energy methods answer questions about speeds and positions while ignoring the path and the clock, and that indifference is their power. Work, defined as force times displacement times the cosine of the angle between them, measures how much a force contributes along a motion. Kinetic energy, one half mass times speed squared, depends on speed alone, never on direction. The work-energy theorem binds them: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, a statement derivable from the second law and therefore guaranteed wherever the second law holds. Gravitational potential energy, mass times g times height, and elastic potential energy, one half the spring constant times stretch squared, store work in configurations, recoverable later as motion.

The frictionless incline shows the economy at its purest. A block released from rest at the top of a ramp 5 meters high reaches the bottom at a speed found from mass times g times 5 equals one half mass times speed squared; mass cancels, and the speed is the square root of twice 10 times 5, which is 10 meters per second. Kinematics could reach the same answer only after finding the acceleration down the slope and the slope's length, two quantities the problem may never state. Energy never asked for them. Height for speed is the exchange the principle guide's second row encodes, and the exchange rate involves squares: doubling the drop height multiplies the speed by the square root of two, not by two, a ratio the multiple-choice section tests with great reliability.

Springs store the same currency in a different account. A spring with constant 200 newtons per meter compressed by 0.1 meter holds one half times 200 times 0.01, which is 1 joule, of elastic potential energy. Released against a 0.5 kilogram block on a frictionless surface, that joule becomes kinetic, and the block leaves at the square root of twice 1 divided by 0.5, which is 2 meters per second. The spring force varies across the release, growing weaker as the spring extends, so second-law kinematics would need calculus the course does not use; energy steps over the variation entirely, comparing only the endpoints of the story.

Friction writes its amendment into every energy account. When kinetic friction acts across a sliding distance, it removes mechanical energy equal to the friction force times that distance, so the full statement becomes initial mechanical energy minus the friction loss equals final mechanical energy. Return to the rough patch from the friction section: a 4 kilogram block arriving at 6 meters per second carries 72 joules of kinetic energy, friction of 10 newtons taxes it 10 joules per meter, and after 5 meters the block keeps 22 joules, a speed of the square root of 11, about 3.3 meters per second. Stop the block entirely and the tax consumes all 72 joules across 7.2 meters, matching the kinematic stopping distance to the decimal, because the theorem and the second law are the same physics wearing different clothes.

## Energy bar charts and the system choice hiding inside them

The course's signature representation for energy is the bar chart, columns for kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, and internal or thermal energy, drawn at chosen instants with a bar for work by external forces when the system demands it. The representation is not decoration; it enforces the system decision in visible form. Choose the object alone as the system, and gravity becomes an external force doing work, represented by a work bar; choose object plus Earth, and gravity turns internal, its effect appearing as potential energy instead. Both charts balance, because energy accounting never fails when the books are kept honestly, but mixing conventions mid-problem, counting potential energy and gravity's work, charges the same transfer twice and breaks the sum.

Reading a bar chart is a vocabulary the exam samples directly. Bar heights are relative unless a scale is given, so questions ask which bar grows, which vanishes, and what total height does across instants, with friction announcing itself as a thermal bar that only ever grows within a closed accounting. A pendulum demonstrates the reading beautifully: released with its bob 0.45 meter above the lowest point, the chart at release is all gravitational potential; at the bottom it is all kinetic, and the bob's speed there is the square root of twice 10 times 0.45, which is 3 meters per second; at the far extreme the chart mirrors the release. Air resistance and pivot friction, when a problem admits them, skim a little height off each swing into the thermal column, and the chart's shrinking total mechanical portion tells that story at a glance.

### Why does mass cancel out of so many mechanics answers?

Mass cancels whenever gravity supplies the only driving force or the only energy source, because gravitational force and gravitational potential energy both scale in direct proportion to mass, and the second law or the energy equation then divides it away. Free fall, frictionless slides, pendulums, and orbits all inherit the cancellation from that shared proportionality.

Recognizing the cancellation in advance is a strategic asset. When a problem withholds the mass entirely, it is confessing that the answer cannot need it, which points the reader directly at gravity-driven methods and away from, say, friction calculations whose forces do not cancel so cleanly against a push. When a problem gives a mass that the algebra then destroys, the destruction is a confirmation rather than a waste: the setter included the mass precisely to test whether the reader trusts the physics over the inventory. Either way, an answer that still contains the mass after simplification deserves a suspicious second look in any gravity-only scenario.

## Power and the rate dimension of work

Power completes the work family by adding the clock back in: power is work divided by the time the work takes, or for a force driving a moving object, force times velocity along the force's direction. The exam's power questions are usually ratio questions in disguise, two machines lifting the same load to the same height in different times, two cars reaching the same speed with different accelerations. Equal work in half the time is double the power, and the watt's small size makes everyday figures humble: raising a 50 kilogram crate by 2 meters costs 1000 joules of work against gravity, and doing it in 4 seconds averages 250 watts. The concept matters to this article chiefly as a completeness point and as a trap, since students confuse power with work or with force whenever a question's wording blurs rate and amount.

A second comparison sharpens the rate idea for moving objects. A car cruising against a steady resisting force of 500 newtons at 20 meters per second spends power equal to force times speed, which is 10,000 watts, merely to hold its velocity; double the speed against a similar resistance and the required power doubles with it, even though the resisting force barely changed. Accelerating adds a second demand on top, since the engine must also supply the growing kinetic energy, which is why the same car accelerates briskly from rest and lazily near its top speed, where the cruising tax consumes most of the available power. Items built on this scenario test whether the reader separates the force a situation needs from the rate at which energy must flow to sustain it, and the separation is the whole skill: force answers how hard, work answers how much, power answers how fast.

## Momentum and impulse: the interaction principle

Momentum, mass times velocity, is a vector whose conservation governs interactions the way energy's conservation governs journeys. Impulse, force times the time it acts, equals the change in momentum, a theorem parallel to the work-energy result with time in the role displacement played there. That parallelism is worth memorizing as a map: work accumulates force across distance and changes kinetic energy; impulse accumulates force across time and changes momentum. Problems that narrate forces acting across a stretch of road belong to work; problems that narrate forces acting across a stretch of clock belong to impulse, and the principle guide's rows encode the fork.

Impulse explains the engineering of survival. A 20 newton force applied for 0.5 seconds delivers an impulse of 10 newton-seconds and changes a 2 kilogram object's velocity by 5 meters per second. The same momentum change delivered by a larger force over a shorter time, or a smaller force over a longer time, leaves the velocity identical and the experience entirely different, which is why airbags, crumple zones, and bent knees matter: they stretch the time of a fixed momentum change and shrink the force proportionally. On a force-time graph, impulse is the area under the curve, so a triangular pulse and a rectangular pulse with equal areas deliver equal momentum changes, another graph reading the exam folds into multiple choice.

Conservation of momentum follows from the third law: when two objects interact, their forces on each other are equal and opposite and act for the same duration, so their impulses cancel and the total momentum of the two together does not change, provided no net external impulse intrudes. The proviso is the exam's pressure point. During the brief violence of a collision, external forces like gravity and friction act too, but their impulses over a fraction of a second are negligible beside the collision's enormous internal forces, so momentum conservation holds to an excellent approximation across the instant, even on a rough road. Across a longer interval, a rolling aftermath with friction, the approximation expires and energy or second-law methods take over. Knowing the conservation law's warranty period is part of knowing the law.

## Collisions: three families and the energy question that separates them

Collisions sort into three families by what happens to kinetic energy. In a perfectly inelastic collision the objects stick, share one final velocity, and lose the maximum kinetic energy the momentum constraint permits. In an elastic collision both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved, the signature of billiard balls and atomic encounters. Between them lies the broad inelastic family, objects separating after impact with some kinetic energy lost to deformation, sound, and heat. The exam's classification questions reduce to two checks: compute total momentum before and after, which must match in every family, and compute total kinetic energy before and after, whose survival or loss names the family.

The perfectly inelastic case solves in one line. A 3 kilogram cart rolling at 4 meters per second strikes and sticks to a stationary 1 kilogram cart; total momentum is 12 kilogram-meters per second, total mass after is 4 kilograms, and the shared velocity is 3 meters per second. Kinetic energy falls from 24 joules before the impact to 18 joules after, so 6 joules left the mechanical account into deformation and heat. Notice that the loss is forced, not chosen: momentum conservation fixes the final velocity completely once the masses stick, and the energy loss is whatever the arithmetic leaves behind.

The elastic case conserves both quantities and therefore supports two equations. A 2 kilogram ball moving at 6 meters per second meets a stationary 1 kilogram ball elastically. The conservation pair solves to give the incoming ball continuing at 2 meters per second while the struck ball departs at 8. Momentum checks: 12 before, and 2 times 2 plus 1 times 8, again 12, after. Kinetic energy checks: 36 joules before, and one half times 2 times 4 plus one half times 1 times 64, which is 4 plus 32, again 36, after. The pattern generalizes memorably: a moving object striking a stationary equal mass elastically stops dead and hands over its velocity entire, while a much heavier striker barely slows and a much lighter one rebounds at nearly its incoming speed. These limiting cases let a reader sanity-check any elastic computation in seconds, and setters build options around them.

Explosions are collisions read backward, one object becoming several with internal energy converting into kinetic, so momentum is conserved while kinetic energy grows; a firework shell at the top of its arc and a spring-loaded pair of carts released from rest both obey the rule. Recoil problems are the same family wearing plain clothes. In every interaction problem, the opening written step that earns method credit is the conservation statement with a defined positive direction, because momentum's vector nature means signs carry the physics: an object rebounding from a wall changes momentum by twice its incoming magnitude, not zero, and that doubling is a classic distractor's foundation.

### How do you keep signs from quietly destroying a correct setup?

Declare the positive direction in writing before substituting any value, then let every velocity, force, and displacement enter the algebra carrying its own sign, never a magnitude whose direction is remembered rather than written. Signs fail silently because arithmetic cannot flag them; only a convention applied without exception from the first line keeps rebounds, downward accelerations, and backward friction honest.

The convention pays for itself most visibly in momentum problems, where a single dropped negative sign converts a correct conservation equation into a plausible wrong total that matches one of the choices precisely because the setter computed that same error. It pays again in energy problems with friction, where the loss term's sign decides whether a final speed comes out real or imaginary, and an imaginary speed is the algebra's way of reporting that the object stopped before reaching the point in question. Treating signs as part of the data, rather than as commentary added afterward, is less a trick than a professional habit, and it generalizes to every graph question where slopes and areas below an axis carry negative meanings.

## The choosing itself: force, energy, momentum, in that order of questions

The misconception this article exists to retire is the belief that kinematics is the default method and the others are special cases. Kinematics is a description language with strict entry requirements, constant acceleration and a known time or a solvable substitute, and a large share of exam problems deliberately withhold exactly those. The choosing discipline runs as a short interrogation instead. Does an interaction, a collision, an explosion, a sticking or splitting, dominate the problem? Momentum leads. Does the problem connect two positions or speeds across a height, a spring, or a stretch of road, with time nowhere mentioned? Energy leads. Does the problem ask for a force, an acceleration, or a tension at an instant? The second law leads, with its diagram. Does a time interval sit at the center with constant acceleration genuinely guaranteed? Only then does kinematics lead, and even then the reader checks whether an energy or impulse route reaches the target faster.

The three worked miniatures below compress the interrogation into reflexes. First: a pendulum bob released from a known height, find the speed at the bottom. No time, no force requested, one height: energy answers in a single line, and kinematics cannot even begin, since the acceleration varies along the swing. Second: two skaters push apart on frictionless ice, find the lighter skater's speed from the heavier one's. An interaction with no external impulse and no interest in forces: momentum answers in a single line. Third: a hanging mass accelerates a cart across a table, find the tension. A force at an instant: the second law on the system gives the acceleration, the second law on one part gives the tension, and neither conservation law is asked anything. Each miniature is trivial once classified and miserable until it is, which is the entire claim of the principle guide restated as experience.

Mixed problems chain the principles in sequence, and the chains follow a grammar worth learning: an energy stage delivers a speed to a momentum stage, whose output feeds a second-law stage, whose answer closes the problem. A cart slides down a ramp, collides with a second cart, and the stuck pair compresses a spring; energy governs the ramp, momentum governs the instant of sticking, energy governs the spring. Free-response questions are built from these chains deliberately, part by part, so recognizing the seam between stages is literally recognizing the problem's structure. The reader who narrates each stage's principle aloud before writing, energy to the collision, momentum through it, energy into the spring, has effectively outlined the solution the rubric expects.

## The question types that test the mechanics core

Multiple-choice items in this territory come in recognizable species. Ratio items give one scenario and ask what changes when a variable doubles, punishing formula-hunting and rewarding proportional reasoning about squares and roots. Graph items hand over a position, velocity, force, or energy representation and ask for a reading, a conversion between representations, or the scenario the graph describes. Ranking tasks present three or four variants of a setup and demand an ordering by speed, force, or energy, solvable by principle-level reasoning without full computation. Two-statement items test whether a claim and its reason both hold and genuinely connect. Each species has a clock discipline: ratio and ranking items should fall in under a minute, graph readings in about one, and any item still resisting after two minutes gets marked and abandoned for the return pass.

Free response divides into the archetypes the course repeats: the qualitative-quantitative translation, which moves between a scenario, a graph, and equations; the experimental design question, which asks for a measurable procedure to test a mechanics claim with real equipment and honest uncertainty; the paragraph-length argument, which demands a coherent written justification of a claim about forces or energy; and the multi-stage computation chain described above. Written conventions decide the margin in all four. Define the system before invoking conservation. State the principle before writing its equation. Carry units through the final line. Justify with physics words, not with algebra narration, since the sentence the energy is conserved because no non-conservative work acts earns what ten lines of substitution cannot. The dedicated treatment of these archetypes across all four physics exams lives in [the physics free-response strategy article](/2008/02/21/ap-physics-frq-strategy-all-exams/), and this article's remaining business is the traps and the connections.

## Traps that cost points in the mechanics core

The point-loss catalog for this territory is short enough to memorize and consistent enough to be worth it. Normal force is not automatically equal to weight: it shrinks on inclines, grows in accelerating elevators, and vanishes in free fall, and every one of those cases has appeared wearing a simple costume. Kinetic friction depends on the normal force and the coefficient, not on surface area or speed, two decoys the options love. A constant velocity does not imply zero forces, only zero net force, and a net force does not imply motion in the force's direction, only acceleration in it, as every projectile at its peak demonstrates.

Energy traps run parallel. Potential energy's zero level is a free choice, so only changes carry meaning, and negative potential energies are bookkeeping, not errors. Work by a force perpendicular to the motion is zero, which is why the normal force and centripetal forces never change a speed. Momentum traps complete the set: momentum is conserved only when the net external impulse is negligible, kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic events, and the two statements are independent, so a collision can conserve one, the other, both in principle never, or neither. Students who recite conserves momentum does not imply conserves energy as a single breath stop donating the classification questions wholesale.

The meta-trap above all of these is method inertia, continuing with the first approach that came to mind after the problem has signaled it will not fit. The exam allots enough time to restart a problem once, not three times, so the discipline is to diagnose the principle within the first reading and to abandon a route the moment its required givens prove absent. A problem that refuses to mention time is not being coy; it is instructing.

## Rotation in overview, and where its depth lives

Rotational motion extends every idea in this article by analogy: torque plays force's role as the cause of angular acceleration, moment of inertia plays mass's role as the resistance, angular momentum plays momentum's role as the conserved interaction quantity, and rotational kinetic energy joins the energy ledger. The overview a mechanics-core reader needs is genuinely this compact: a torque arises when a force acts at a distance from a pivot with a component perpendicular to the lever arm; objects with mass distributed far from the axis resist angular acceleration more strongly; and angular momentum conservation explains the spinning skater's speedup as arms draw inward, shrinking the inertia while the product holds. Rolling motion marries translation and rotation through the no-slip condition, and a rolling object's energy splits between the two forms in proportions set by its shape.

Depth beyond that overview, torque computations, rotational dynamics, angular momentum in collision analogues, belongs to [the rotation, torque, and angular momentum deep dive](/2007/11/27/ap-physics-1-rotation-torque-angular-momentum/), and this article deliberately leaves it there. The strategic point for sequencing study is that rotation's analogies only pay once the linear originals are automatic: a student still shaky on free-body diagrams will find torque diagrams doubly confusing, while a student fluent in the core reads rotation as a translation exercise with a new vocabulary. Master this article's territory first, and the rotational unit becomes the cheapest points in the back half of the course.

## A full chain, worked end to end

One extended example shows the grammar of stages operating under exam conditions. A 2 kilogram cart rests at the top of a frictionless ramp whose surface sits 1.8 meters above the floor. Released, it rolls down, crosses a level frictionless floor, strikes a stationary 4 kilogram cart, sticks to it, and the joined pair rolls into a spring of constant 300 newtons per meter mounted against a wall. The questions the exam would ask arrive in order: the first cart's speed at the floor, the pair's speed just after impact, and the spring's maximum compression.

Stage one is energy, because a height is given, a speed is requested, and time never appears. Gravitational potential converts to kinetic across the drop: mass times 10 times 1.8 equals one half mass times speed squared, mass cancels, and the speed is the square root of 36, exactly 6 meters per second. Stage two is momentum, because an interaction dominates and the carts stick. Total momentum before impact is 2 times 6, which is 12; total mass after is 6 kilograms; the shared speed is 2 meters per second. Kinetic energy falls across the impact from 36 joules to 12 joules, a loss the momentum method neither needs nor apologizes for. Stage three returns to energy, because a compression is requested and the floor is frictionless: the pair's 12 joules become elastic potential, one half times 300 times compression squared equals 12, so compression squared is 24 divided by 300, which is 0.08, and the compression is about 0.28 meters.

Read the solution's shape rather than its numbers. Each stage begins by naming its principle and its system, carts alone, then carts together, then carts plus spring, and each stage consumes exactly the output of the stage before. No stage reaches for time, because no stage was given any. A reader who had opened with kinematics would have needed the ramp's angle and length, neither supplied, and would have discovered the omission only after real minutes had burned. The classification step, five seconds of asking what each part of the problem displays, is what made the arithmetic trivial.

## A dynamics problem cross-examined by two methods

Agreement between independent methods is the strongest verification available on a timed exam, and dynamics with friction offers the cleanest stage for it. Take a 5 kilogram block released from rest on a 37 degree incline, the three-four-five slope, with kinetic friction coefficient 0.25, sliding 10 meters along the surface. By the second law: the along-slope weight component is 5 times 10 times 0.6, which is 30 newtons; the normal force is 5 times 10 times 0.8, which is 40 newtons; kinetic friction is 0.25 times 40, which is 10 newtons opposing the slide; the net force is 20 newtons and the acceleration 4 meters per second squared. Kinematics then gives the final speed from rest across 10 meters: speed squared equals twice 4 times 10, which is 80, so the speed is about 8.9 meters per second.

Now the energy audit of the same journey. The block drops through a vertical height of 10 times sine 37, which is 6 meters, losing 5 times 10 times 6, or 300 joules, of gravitational potential energy. Friction taxes the journey at 10 newtons across 10 meters, removing 100 joules into thermal energy. The remainder, 200 joules, must be the kinetic energy at the bottom, and one half times 5 times speed squared equals 200 returns speed squared of 80, the identical result. Two methods sharing no algebraic steps agree to the last digit because both are the second law, integrated along different variables. On the exam, performing the second computation is rarely affordable in full, but performing its skeleton, estimating the energy terms and comparing magnitudes, catches dropped friction terms and wrong components before they cost a part's full value.

## Momentum in two dimensions: the vector refuses to be ignored

Collisions on a frictionless table scatter in the plane, and momentum conservation then splits into two independent scalar statements, one per axis, each as binding as the single-line version. A worked glancing case builds the habit. A 2 kilogram puck sliding east at 3 meters per second strikes a 1 kilogram puck sliding north at 4 meters per second, and the two stick. East momentum totals 2 times 3, which is 6; north momentum totals 1 times 4, which is 4; the joined 3 kilogram mass must carry both components at once, so its velocity has an east component of 2 meters per second and a north component of about 1.33. The speed is the square root of 36 plus 16, divided by the total mass, which is the square root of 52 over 3, about 2.4 meters per second, directed at the arctangent of 4 over 6, roughly 34 degrees north of east.

Two habits keep such problems clean. Components are conserved separately, so the work is two one-dimensional problems sharing a mass, never one tangled vector problem. And direction answers should be stated as an angle from a named axis, since a bare number invites the grader to guess which reference was meant. Elastic glancing collisions add the famous special case: equal masses, one initially at rest, separate at right angles to each other, a consequence of the two conservation laws together and a pattern multiple-choice items test by recognition rather than by algebra.

## Circular motion in depth: the loop and the cone

Vertical circles test whether a reader tracks the center's location as the object moves, because the required net inward force is constant in magnitude for steady speed while the contributing forces rotate their allegiances. At the top of a loop of radius 0.5 meter, gravity points toward the center and helps; the minimum speed for a string or a track to remain engaged occurs when gravity alone supplies the entire requirement, mass times speed squared over radius equals mass times g, giving a speed of the square root of 10 times 0.5, about 2.2 meters per second. Below that speed at the top, a string goes slack and a car leaves the track, not because a force throws it outward but because its natural straight-line path falls inside the circle. At the bottom, the same object at the same speed needs tension or normal force covering both the inward requirement and the full weight, which is why bottom-of-loop forces in worked problems dwarf top-of-loop forces and why riders feel loops at the bottom.

The conical pendulum, a mass circling at the end of a tilted string, resolves into an equilibrium vertical axis and a centripetal horizontal axis, a pairing that teaches component discipline better than any other standard problem. For a 1 meter string held at 37 degrees from vertical, the circle's radius is the string length times sine 37, which is 0.6 meter. Vertically, tension's component balances weight; horizontally, tension's other component supplies the inward force; dividing the two equations eliminates tension and mass together, leaving speed squared equal to g times radius times tangent of the angle, which is 10 times 0.6 times 0.75, or 4.5, so the speed is about 2.1 meters per second. The elimination step is the lesson: dividing equations to destroy unknowns is a general dynamics technique, and the conical pendulum is its friendliest classroom.

## Equilibrium: the zero-acceleration family in full dress

Equilibrium problems look static and test dynamics, because the second law with acceleration set to zero becomes a pair of component equations whose solution is pure force bookkeeping. A sign of mass 10 kilograms hangs from two ropes running symmetrically upward at 37 degrees above horizontal. Each rope's tension has a vertical component of tension times sine 37, which is 0.6 of the tension; the two components together must carry the 100 newton weight, so 1.2 times the tension equals 100 and each rope carries about 83 newtons. The horizontal components cancel by symmetry, as they must, and the exercise's real content is the recognition that two ropes share a load through components, not by simple halving. Halving would be correct only for vertical ropes, and the options in such items always include the halved value, waiting for the reader who skips the diagram.

Friction-held equilibrium sharpens the static ceiling lesson. A block resting on an incline of 30 degrees, held by static friction alone, needs friction of mass times g times sine 30 up the slope, while the ceiling allows the static coefficient times mass times g times cosine 30; the mass and g cancel in the comparison, leaving the condition that the coefficient must be at least the tangent of 30 degrees, about 0.58. Any shallower slope or rougher surface holds; any steeper slope or slicker surface slips, and the boundary depends on angle alone. That tangent relation recurs in banked curves, ladder problems, and cornering limits, and recognizing it on sight converts a family of exam items into one remembered shape.

## Springs in depth: the vertical case and the reference trap

Horizontal springs are kind; vertical springs contain a trap that separates careful readers. Hang a 0.5 kilogram mass from a spring of constant 50 newtons per meter and it settles where the spring force balances weight: the stretch is mass times g over the constant, 5 divided by 50, which is 0.1 meter. Energy work around that equilibrium must respect both potentials at once, gravitational and elastic, and the elegant fact is that oscillations about the equilibrium behave as if gravity were absent, with the equilibrium stretch absorbed into a redefined rest position. Problems that hang a mass, let it fall from the spring's unstretched position, and ask for maximum stretch punish anyone who forgets gravity keeps working during the stretch: energy conservation between release and maximum stretch, where kinetic energy vanishes at both ends, returns a stretch of twice the equilibrium value, 0.2 meter in this case, because gravitational loss across the fall must equal the elastic gain, mass times g times stretch equals one half the constant times stretch squared.

The verification of that doubling is worth performing once by hand, since the result feels wrong to most first readers. Elastic gain at stretch 0.2 is one half times 50 times 0.04, which is 1 joule. Gravitational loss across 0.2 meter is 0.5 times 10 times 0.2, which is also 1 joule. The books balance, the kinetic energy is zero at both instants as required, and the doubling stands. Intuition protests because it imagines the mass stopping at equilibrium, where in truth it arrives at maximum speed and overshoots, trading its kinetic account down to zero exactly one equilibrium-stretch further on. The exam revisits this overshoot in oscillation clothing later in the course, and the energy accounting learned here is the whole of its mathematics.

## Reading the setter's language: the coded phrases of mechanics problems

Exam problems speak a compressed dialect in which single adjectives carry entire assumptions, and fluency in the dialect is worth measurable points. Smooth and frictionless both delete friction from the model, and their absence is equally eloquent: a surface described with a coefficient, or merely called rough, has friction switched on. Light, applied to strings and pulleys, deletes their mass, which is what licenses one tension throughout a rope and a pulley that only redirects. Inextensible deletes stretching, which forces connected objects to share an acceleration magnitude. Released from rest fixes the initial velocity at zero without saying so in numbers. Comes momentarily to rest fixes a final velocity at zero at one instant, usually the turning point where an energy equation loses its kinetic term.

Other phrases mark the instant that matters. Just after the collision and just before separate the conservation instant from everything that follows; stuck together announces the perfectly inelastic family; slides off the edge starts a projectile whose initial velocity is purely horizontal; at the top of its path zeroes the vertical velocity component while leaving the horizontal untouched. A reader who translates each coded phrase into an equation or a diagram feature as it is encountered, rather than after a full reading, is doing the same work as the inventory habit from the article's opening, at the level of vocabulary. Setters rely on hurried readers treating these phrases as flavor text. They are the problem, compressed.

## Experimental design inside the mechanics core

The framework's science practices surface in mechanics as design questions with apparatus a school laboratory actually owns: carts and tracks, springs and masses, pendulums, ramps, motion sensors, and photogates. The recurring task shapes are few. Measure a quantity indirectly, such as finding a friction coefficient from a stopping distance or an acceleration from a slope. Test a claimed relationship, such as whether a spring's period depends on amplitude or a pendulum's period on mass. Determine an unknown, such as the mass of a cart from a collision with a known partner. Each shape grades the same habits: identify the measured and manipulated variables, choose the representation whose slope or intercept carries the target quantity, and state how repeated trials and honest uncertainty enter the result.

Linearization is the master habit. A pendulum's period squared is proportional to its length, so plotting period squared against length yields a straight line whose slope contains g; a spring's period squared is proportional to the hanging mass, so the analogous plot yields the spring constant from its slope. Students who plot raw period against length get a curve they cannot read; the exam rewards the transformation that straightens, because a line admits a slope and a slope admits an interpretation. The same instinct serves kinematics data, where position plotted against time squared straightens a constant-acceleration record into a line whose slope is half the acceleration. Designing the graph before touching the apparatus, deciding in advance which plot must come out straight if the physics claim holds, is the mark of the strongest experimental answers, and it is trainable entirely on paper.

## Potential energy curves and the force hidden in a slope

One representation fuses the energy chapter into a single picture: the graph of potential energy against position. The force at any point is the negative slope of that curve, so a particle resting where the curve is flat feels no force, a particle on a hillside of the graph feels a force pushing it toward lower potential, and a valley is a stable equilibrium while a peak is an unstable one. Turning points appear where the total energy line crosses the curve, since there the kinetic energy, drawn as the gap between the total and the potential, shrinks to zero. Questions built on such graphs ask for speeds from gaps, forces from slopes, and equilibrium character from curvature, all without a single formula beyond those readings.

A numerical reading cements the skill. Suppose the curve shows potential energy of 8 joules at a position where the total energy line sits at 11 joules; the kinetic energy there is the gap, 3 joules, and a 1 kilogram particle carries a speed of the square root of 6, about 2.4 meters per second. Where the curve climbs to meet the total line, the particle turns around. Where the curve dips lowest, the particle moves fastest. These three readings, gap, crossing, and valley, answer the large majority of energy-graph items, and they extend the bar-chart literacy from earlier in this article into continuous form.

## Checking answers: the habits that catch errors before graders do

Professional verification in this territory runs on three cheap tests. The units test asks whether the final expression's units reduce to the requested quantity's units; a speed emerging with units of kilograms per meter has failed before its number is read. The limiting-case test pushes a variable to an extreme where the answer is known on sight: a friction coefficient of zero must reproduce the frictionless result, equal Atwood masses must give zero acceleration, a collision with a wall-like partner must rebound the light object at nearly its incoming speed. The magnitude test compares the answer against the problem's own scale, rejecting speeds of hundreds of meters per second for falling classroom objects and tensions outside an Atwood machine's weight interval.

Each test takes seconds and each catches a different failure class: algebra slips, modeling slips, and arithmetic slips respectively. Free-response graders cannot award method credit to a result the writer plainly should have disowned, and multiple-choice options are constructed from the outputs of exactly the errors these tests expose. The habit also compounds across a study season, because a student who verifies develops a felt sense of reasonable magnitudes, what speeds ramps produce, what tensions ropes carry, and that felt sense is precisely what ratio and ranking items measure.

## Kinematics in depth: symmetry, chases, and the falling body

Free fall is kinematics with the acceleration supplied by the planet, and its symmetries are the fastest points in the unit. Toss a ball upward at 15 meters per second. It rises for 15 divided by 10, which is 1.5 seconds, reaches a height of 15 squared over twice 10, which is 11.25 meters, and returns to the launch height after 3 seconds carrying a downward velocity of 15 meters per second, the launch speed mirrored. At any height on the way down it moves at exactly the speed it carried at that height on the way up. These mirrorings let a reader answer half of a free-fall item from the other half's arithmetic, and they explain why the exam's tossed-object questions so often ask for a quantity on the descent: the setter is testing whether the reader owns the symmetry or merely the equations.

Chase problems, two movers whose positions must coincide, teach the position-function habit that later serves in relative motion everywhere. A car cruises at a steady 20 meters per second past a second car waiting at rest; the waiting car accelerates at 2 meters per second squared from the moment the first passes. Position equality demands 20 times t equals one half times 2 times t squared, which reduces to t equals 20 seconds, the trivial t equals zero solution being the start itself. In those 20 seconds the cruising car covers 400 meters, and the accelerating car arrives at the meeting point traveling at 40 meters per second, twice the cruiser's speed. That doubling is no accident: meeting a constant-velocity target from rest under constant acceleration always ends at twice the target's speed, because the chaser's average speed across the chase is half its final speed and the averages must match. Patterns like this, once seen, convert whole item families into recognition.

## Projectiles from a height: where the symmetry ends

The level-ground projectile is the friendly case; the cliff launch is the honest one. A ball rolling off a table 20 meters high at a horizontal 5 meters per second begins its fall with zero vertical velocity, so the fall time comes from the vertical drop alone: 20 meters under gravity's acceleration requires the square root of twice 20 over 10, which is 2 seconds. The horizontal component, unaccelerated, carries the ball 5 times 2, which is 10 meters out. Impact speed combines the untouched horizontal 5 with a vertical component grown to 10 times 2, which is 20, giving the square root of 25 plus 400, about 20.6 meters per second. Every element of that solution used one dimension at a time and a clock shared between them, and no element borrowed the level-ground range formula, which would have failed silently and plausibly.

Unequal launch and landing heights also destroy the up-down time symmetry, and the repair is always the same: write the vertical position equation with the true landing height and solve it as written, accepting the quadratic when it appears. The exam signals this family by drawing the landing surface below or above the launch, and the drawing is the instruction. A related signal is the phrase horizontal launch itself, which zeroes the initial vertical component and simplifies the vertical side to pure falling, the single most common projectile configuration in the course's laboratories as well as its exams.

## Dynamics in depth: the angled pull and the normal force that shrinks

Few standard problems teach component discipline as efficiently as the angled pull. A 4 kilogram block is pulled across a rough floor by a 20 newton force angled 37 degrees above horizontal, with kinetic friction coefficient 0.25. The pull resolves into 16 newtons horizontal and 12 newtons vertical. The vertical component lifts against gravity, so the normal force is the 40 newton weight minus 12, which is 28 newtons, and kinetic friction falls to 0.25 times 28, which is 7 newtons. The net horizontal force is 16 minus 7, which is 9 newtons, and the acceleration is 9 over 4, or 2.25 meters per second squared. Pulling at an angle buys horizontal drive at the price of a component spent on lifting, yet the friction reduction can make the trade profitable, a comparison the exam poses directly by asking whether the angled pull out-accelerates a horizontal pull of the same magnitude. Here a horizontal 20 newton pull would fight friction of 10 newtons for a net of 10 and an acceleration of 2.5, slightly better; at other angles and coefficients the verdict reverses, which is why the components, not a rule of thumb, must decide.

The generalizable lesson is that the normal force is a response, not a datum. It adjusts to whatever presses the surfaces together: less when a pull lifts, more when a push presses down, less on an incline by the cosine factor, more at a loop's bottom. Every friction magnitude and every friction ceiling in this article descends from that single response, so the reader who computes the normal force fresh in every problem, from the perpendicular equation, rather than assuming it equals weight, has disarmed the largest single trap family in dynamics.

## Work by variable forces: the area under the curve

The work definition generalizes past constant forces through the force-position graph, where work is the area under the curve between the start and finish positions. A force that grows linearly from zero to 10 newtons across 4 meters, as a spring being stretched by an external agent does in reverse, encloses a triangle of area one half times 4 times 10, which is 20 joules of work. A force that falls across the interval encloses its own shape; a force with a rectangular pulse contributes its rectangle. The reading mirrors the impulse reading on force-time graphs and the displacement reading on velocity-time graphs, and the three together form a single transferable skill: in this course, an accumulation is always an area, and the axes announce what has accumulated.

Springs make the area concrete because their force grows with stretch, so the work to stretch one is the triangular area that the elastic potential formula, one half constant times stretch squared, already encodes. Recognizing the formula as an area explains its one half, which students otherwise memorize as a mystery, and it explains why doubling a spring's stretch quadruples the stored energy: both the base and the height of the triangle double. Energy questions about springs are therefore ratio-friendly, and the multiple-choice section exploits that friendliness constantly.

## Momentum in depth: the center of mass refuses to accelerate

Behind momentum conservation stands a picture worth owning: the center of mass of a system moves as if all the mass sat there and all external forces acted there, and when the net external force is zero, that point drifts at constant velocity through the wildest internal rearrangements. Two skaters at rest on frictionless ice, 60 and 40 kilograms, push apart. Their center of mass cannot move, so their momenta must cancel at every instant: when the 40 kilogram skater glides off at 3 meters per second, carrying momentum of 120, the 60 kilogram partner recoils at 120 over 60, which is 2 meters per second, in the opposite direction. Distances from the meeting point accumulate in the inverse ratio of the masses for the same reason. Collision problems, explosion problems, and even the walking-across-a-boat family all reduce to this still point and the momenta balancing around it.

The picture also clarifies the conservation law's limits. External impulses move the center of mass, so a collision on a rough surface conserves momentum only across the brief interval where friction's impulse is negligible; stretch the interval and the center of mass decelerates as the second law demands. Readers who hold the picture know the warranty instinctively: conservation is exact for isolated systems and an approximation whose quality depends on how brief and how violent the interaction is compared with the external forces patiently acting.

## The derivative chain: one picture for every graph in the core

Position, velocity, and acceleration graphs are three renderings of one motion, linked by slopes running down the chain and areas running up it. Given any one graph drawn honestly, the other two are determined in shape: a parabolic position curve has a linear velocity graph and a constant acceleration value; a velocity graph with a kink produces an acceleration graph with a jump. Force graphs join the family through the second law, since net force and acceleration graphs share a shape exactly, scaled by the mass, so a force-time graph's peak is the acceleration graph's peak wearing different units. Exam items that ask a reader to select the velocity graph matching a given force graph, or the scenario matching a given position graph, are testing this chain and nothing else.

The practical drill is translation practice in all six directions: from each representation to each other, in words before numbers. A student who can narrate a motion from its position graph, then sketch the velocity and acceleration partners without computing, owns the representational fluency that the framework names as a science practice and that the experimental questions presume. It is also the fastest review the unit offers, because one well-chosen graph set rehearses definitions, slopes, areas, and the second law simultaneously.

## Sequencing a study season across the core

The core's internal dependencies dictate a study order more than the course's calendar does. Kinematics and graph fluency come first because every later topic borrows their language. Dynamics follows, with diagram drills front-loaded, because friction, circular motion, and connected systems are all dynamics in costume. Energy should be mastered before momentum, since the collision classification turns on comparing kinetic energies, and the choosing discipline comes last of all, deliberately, because it can only be trained on problems whose individual principles are already secure. Interleaving begins only after that point: mixed sets, timed chains, and classification drills layered over a mistake log that by then has entries worth mining.

A realistic season gives the choosing discipline its own dedicated weeks rather than treating it as a byproduct, because the evidence of every practice exam is that students lose more points to wrong-principle openings than to any algebraic weakness. The final fortnight before the exam belongs to full mixed rehearsals and to re-solving, from blank pages, the problems the log flags as personal traps. A student whose log shows friction ceilings and sign errors rehearses those; a student whose log shows method inertia rehearses classification under a timer. The core is large, but an individual's losses inside it are narrow, and the season's last job is narrowing the preparation to match.

## The ballistic pendulum: where energy is forbidden and momentum is king

No standard problem teaches the boundary between the principles like the ballistic pendulum, in which a projectile embeds itself in a hanging block and the pair swings upward. A 0.02 kilogram bullet arriving at 200 meters per second strikes a stationary 1.98 kilogram block and sticks. The impact stage is momentum's exclusive territory: total momentum is 0.02 times 200, which is 4, the combined mass is exactly 2 kilograms, and the pair leaves the impact at 2 meters per second. The swing stage belongs to energy: the pair's kinetic energy converts to gravitational potential, and the rise height is speed squared over twice g, which is 4 over 20, or 0.2 meters. Each stage uses the other's output and neither uses the other's method.

The forbidden move is instructive: running energy conservation across the whole event, from the bullet's flight to the swing's top, would predict an absurd rise, because the kinetic energy collapses from 400 joules before impact to 4 joules after, the missing 396 joules spent deforming wood and making heat and sound. Energy is not conserved across a perfectly inelastic collision in its mechanical form, and the ballistic pendulum exists in the course to make that sentence unforgettable. The two-stage structure, momentum through the violence and energy through the calm, is the template for a whole family of exam chains, and recognizing which stages are violent is the classification skill at its most consequential.

## Incline geometry: why the components trade places

The incline's component split confuses students because the sine and cosine seem to swap roles compared with flat-ground intuition, and the cure is to derive the split from angles rather than memorize it. The weight points straight down. The slope tilts the surface by the incline angle, and the perpendicular to the surface tilts by the same angle away from vertical, so the weight's component along the perpendicular, which the normal force must answer, carries the cosine, while the component along the surface carries the sine. Two limiting cases certify the assignment: at zero incline the along-slope component must vanish and the normal must equal the full weight, which sine-zero and cosine-one deliver; at a vertical 90 degree drop the along-slope component must become the full weight and the normal must vanish, which sine-90 and cosine-90 deliver. Any remembered assignment failing those limits is wrong, and checking limits takes five seconds.

The trading has a physical moral worth stating: as a slope steepens, the driving component grows at the normal component's expense, so acceleration rises for two reasons at once, more push along the surface and less friction gripping it. That double effect is why friction-held equilibrium fails so decisively past the tangent boundary derived earlier, and why a modest steepening can flip a system from rest to rapid sliding. Problems that vary the angle and ask when motion starts, or how acceleration changes, are probing exactly this coupled trade.

## The work-energy theorem, derived once and trusted forever

The theorem is not an independent law but the second law integrated along a path, and seeing the derivation once explains both its power and its limits. Start with net force equals mass times acceleration, multiply both sides by a small displacement, and sum along the path: the left side accumulates into net work, while the right side, using acceleration as velocity's rate of change, accumulates into the change of one half mass times velocity squared. Time never enters, which is the theorem's gift; the detailed force history collapses into work totals, which is its price. Where forces vary too intricately for the work to be computed, the theorem still holds but cannot help, and the honest methods revert to the second law directly.

Two consequences organize practice. Net work is the sum of individual works, so each force's contribution can be computed and signed separately, gravity positive on a descent and negative on a climb, friction negative always against sliding, the normal force zero on any surface-bound motion. And the theorem applies to the center of mass motion of a system under external work, with internal energy changes handled by choosing the system so the interesting transfers become potential terms. Readers who treat the theorem as an accounting identity rather than a formula stop hunting for the one right equation and start listing transfers, which is the professional posture the energy bar charts were designed to teach.

## Orbits: falling around the planet

Gravitation closes the circular motion story by supplying an inward force that needs no contact. A satellite in a circular orbit is in continuous free fall toward Earth while its tangential velocity keeps carrying it past the planet's edge, so its acceleration is centripetal and gravity alone provides it. Writing mass times speed squared over radius equal to the gravitational force shows the satellite's mass canceling, again, and the orbital speed depending only on the central mass and the radius: speed squared equals the gravitational parameter over radius. Double the orbital radius and the speed falls by a factor of the square root of two, while the period grows, a pairing that Kepler's third law generalizes into the statement that period squared grows with radius cubed. The exam uses these as ratio items almost exclusively, which suits the physics: the relationships are the content, and the constants merely dress them.

Energy completes the orbital picture with a signature result: a bound circular orbit's total mechanical energy is negative and equals half its potential energy, the kinetic energy being positive half the potential's magnitude. Raising an orbit therefore costs energy even though the satellite ends up moving more slowly, a paradox that dissolves once the potential gain is seen to be twice the kinetic loss. Escape requires bringing the total to zero, which is why escape speed exceeds circular orbital speed at the same radius by the square root of two. These are the kinds of elegant, derivable relationships the free-response section builds its gravitation parts around, and each rests squarely on this article's two pillars, the second law pointed at a center and energy kept in honest books.

## Braking distances and the tyranny of the square

The stopping-distance scaling is the energy chapter's most consequential civilian fact. Kinetic energy grows with speed squared, while friction's braking force is fixed by the road and the car's weight, so the distance needed to spend the energy grows with speed squared as well: distance equals speed squared over twice the friction coefficient times g. At a coefficient of 0.5, a car at 10 meters per second stops in 100 over 10, which is 10 meters; at 20 meters per second it needs 400 over 10, which is 40 meters; at 30 it needs 90. Doubling speed quadruples the braking distance, and tripling multiplies it ninefold, before any reaction distance is added. Drivers experience this as the road's betrayal at highway speeds; physicists recognize it as the work-energy theorem with the friction term isolated, and the exam tests it as a ratio item with unusual social weight.

The same square governs spring compressions in collisions, penetration depths, and the heights of ballistic rises, which is why this article keeps returning to squared relationships whenever ratios are discussed. A student with a reflexive sense that speeds live under square roots and energies live over squares reads whole families of options correctly at a glance, eliminating the linear-scaled distractors without computing anything.

## How setters build mechanics items: the distractor anatomy

Wrong options on mechanics items are not random numbers; each is the output of a specific, predictable error, and cataloging the error generators is a legitimate study method. The friction-forgotten answer sets the coefficient to zero. The component-swapped answer exchanges sine and cosine on the incline. The mass-misplaced answer divides where it should multiply, or carries a mass that physics cancels. The radius-doubled answer uses a diameter where the formula wants a radius. The sign-dropped answer adds momenta that should subtract, or reports a rebound's momentum change as zero. The ceiling-confused answer sets static friction to its maximum for an object that is not slipping. Nearly every multiple-choice mechanics item contains at least three of these fossils, each preserving the error that generated it.

The countermeasure is to finish every computation by asking which error the neighboring options embody and whether the chosen answer's neighbors make sense as errors rather than as alternatives. When the computed value is absent from the options, the catalog reverses into a diagnostic: find which catalog error transforms the computed value into a listed one, and that transformation names the mistake in the setup. This is distractor archaeology, and strong candidates practice it deliberately on review sets, annotating each wrong option with the error that would produce it. The exercise converts the setter's traps into a curriculum, which is the deepest form the choosing discipline takes: not only selecting the right principle, but knowing the price of each wrong one in advance.

## Tension: the force that transmits, and the idealizations that simplify it

Tension deserves a dedicated pass because it is the most misdrawn force in student diagrams. A rope can only pull, never push, and it pulls along its own length at both ends with equal strength whenever the rope is light and any pulley it crosses is light and frictionless. Those idealizations are not laziness; they are what make a single tension value per rope the correct model, and the exam states them in its coded language whenever they apply. Where a rope has genuine mass or a pulley has genuine inertia, tension varies along the rope, and that richer treatment belongs to the rotational unit, since a pulley's spin is where the difference lives.

The drawing discipline follows from the physics: tension arrows point away from the object along the rope, always, at whatever angle the rope runs. A hanging object at rest splits its weight among its supports by components, as the sign example showed. A pulled object feels tension at the pull's angle, shrinking the normal force when the angle lifts, as the angled-pull example computed. And in any system of joined objects, tension is the internal force the whole-system view hides and the isolated view exposes, which is why so many two-part questions ask for acceleration first and tension second, in that order, teaching the dependency by their sequencing.

## Energy clinic: three short cases with the seams showing

A pendulum of length 2 meters is released from rest with its string at 60 degrees from vertical. The bob's drop to the lowest point is the length times one minus cosine 60, which is 2 times 0.5, or 1 meter, so energy gives a bottom speed of the square root of twice 10 times 1, about 4.5 meters per second. Kinematics along the arc could not have managed this without calculus, since the acceleration changes direction and magnitude throughout the swing; energy required only the endpoints' heights.

A roller coaster car starts from rest at 25 meters and passes through the top of a loop of radius 8 meters, whose top sits 16 meters above the ground. The drop to the top is 9 meters, so the speed there is the square root of twice 10 times 9, about 13.4 meters per second. The loop's own minimum speed at the top, set by gravity alone supplying the centripetal requirement, is the square root of 10 times 8, about 8.9 meters per second, so the car passes comfortably engaged with the track, and the margin between the two speeds is itself a typical follow-up question. Notice how the problem chained an energy stage into a circular-motion stage, the standard two-part architecture.

A block sliding at 8 meters per second meets a spring of constant 200 newtons per meter on a frictionless floor; if the block masses 1 kilogram, its 32 joules of kinetic energy become elastic potential at maximum compression, and one half times 200 times compression squared equals 32, giving a compression of the square root of 0.32, about 0.57 meter. Add friction before the spring and the energy account subtracts friction's work first, which is the natural third part such a problem grows when the setter wants the full ledger tested.

## Momentum clinic: the bounce and the average force

A 0.2 kilogram ball strikes a wall at 6 meters per second and rebounds at 5 meters per second, with contact lasting a hundredth of a second. Taking the rebound direction as positive, the momentum change is 0.2 times the difference between 5 and negative 6, which is 0.2 times 11, or 2.2 kilogram-meters per second. The average force during contact is that impulse divided by the contact time, 2.2 over 0.01, which is 220 newtons, a figure that startles students until they compare it with the ball's 2 newton weight and grasp how violent brief contacts are. Had the ball stuck to the wall instead, the momentum change would have been only 0.2 times 6, which is 1.2, and the force nearly halved; the rebound's sign flip is worth almost a second collision, which is why bouncy impacts break things that sticky impacts merely dent.

The same computation, run with the incoming direction as positive, returns the same magnitude with the opposite sign convention, a small demonstration that physical answers survive convention changes while bookkeeping errors do not. Graders see the magnitude and the direction stated in words; the convention lives in the work, where it belongs.

## The reference sheet: what it gives and what it pointedly withholds

The equations sheet provided with the exam is an ally with a specific shape. It supplies the kinematic relations, the second law in component form, the definitions of work, kinetic and potential energies, momentum and impulse, and the centripetal acceleration relation, among others across the full course. What it withholds is equally instructive: no statement of when each relation applies, no constant-acceleration warning beside the kinematic set, no friction ceiling beside the friction model, no reminder that centripetal force is a net rather than a substance. The sheet is a dictionary, not a grammar, and this article's principle guide is the grammar the sheet presumes.

Study should therefore treat the sheet as a checklist of relationships to be able to deploy, not to recall, and timed practice should be done with the sheet present, mirroring exam conditions. The student who practices without it builds a recall habit the exam never tests and neglects the selection habit the exam tests constantly. Fluency targets shift accordingly: given a relationship's name, the trained reader states its entry conditions, its system requirements, and its favorite trap, which is a deeper possession than the symbols themselves.

## Why the setters love two objects

Scan a season of mechanics problems and a pattern emerges: the examinable situations overwhelmingly involve two objects, two instants, or two surfaces. Pairs create the decisions this article has trained. Two objects force a system choice and expose an internal force worth asking about. Two instants invite an energy or impulse ledger between them. Two surfaces stage the static-to-kinetic transition or a friction change mid-problem. Even the single-object problems usually hide a pair, an object and the Earth sharing potential energy, an object and a spring exchanging it.

Preparing with pairs in mind reorganizes practice productively. For any single-object drill, the two-object variant is one invention away: add a rope, a collision, a second slope. For any two-instant energy drill, the momentum variant asks what changes if the midpoint holds an impact. Working the variants teaches the seams between principles, and the seams, as the chains in this article keep showing, are where the exam's parts divide and its points concentrate.

## Dynamics in the laboratory: slopes that measure the constants

The mechanics constants of this article, g, friction coefficients, and spring constants, are all measurable with school equipment, and the experimental questions ask students to design those measurements rather than merely recall the values. The shared design pattern is to arrange for the target constant to appear as the slope of a straight-line graph, because slopes average away single-trial noise and expose proportionality directly. Hang known masses from a spring and plot the force against the stretch: the slope is the spring constant. Slide objects under varying normal forces and plot friction against the normal force: the slope is the coefficient. Run an Atwood machine with a fixed total mass and a growing mass difference, plotting acceleration against the difference: since acceleration equals the difference times g over the total, the slope returns g divided by the total mass, and g falls out of one multiplication.

Each design also grades its controls. The friction plot demands the same surface pair throughout and a pull at constant velocity, so the spring scale reads kinetic friction rather than a mixture of friction and acceleration. The Atwood design demands the total mass stay fixed while the difference changes, moving mass from one side to the other rather than adding it, because the relation's denominator must hold still for the slope to mean anything. Photogates and motion sensors enter as precision instruments for the time and velocity measurements, and their honest treatment, including what a measurement uncertainty does to the final claim, is what separates design answers that describe an afternoon in a lab from answers that describe an experiment. The framework's insistence on these designs is why this article keeps treating graphs and slopes as first-class physics rather than as reporting.

A collision variant measures the unmeasurable elegantly: fire a projectile into a pendulum catcher, as in the ballistic pendulum, and the swing height reports the projectile's speed through two conservation stages, letting a laboratory measure velocities far too fast for stopwatches. Students asked to justify the method must name the stages and their principles in order, momentum across the capture where mechanical energy is not conserved, energy across the swing where it is, and that two-sentence justification, applied to the specific apparatus, is the design question's heart.

## One scenario, three readings: the principles dividing a journey

Close the article's argument with a single journey read three ways. A 1 kilogram block rests at the top of a frictionless 45 degree incline whose surface is 2 meters above a level floor. The floor is rough, with kinetic friction coefficient 0.2, and after crossing 3 meters of it, the block strikes a stationary 1 kilogram block and sticks. Find the pair's speed after the impact, and account for every stage.

The descent belongs to energy, or equivalently to kinematics, since the acceleration is constant. Energy is shorter: the speed at the floor is the square root of twice 10 times 2, the square root of 40, about 6.3 meters per second. Kinematics agrees, taking the along-slope acceleration of 10 times sine 45, about 7.1, over a slope length of 2 over sine 45, about 2.83 meters, and returning the same speed, at the price of two extra computations and a time the problem never asked for. The rough crossing belongs to either the work-energy theorem or the second law with kinematics, and the two agree as this article has shown before: friction removes 0.2 times 10 times 3, which is 6 joules, from the initial 20 joules of kinetic energy, leaving 14 joules and a speed of the square root of 28, about 5.3 meters per second. The impact belongs to momentum alone: the incoming momentum of about 5.3, shared across 2 kilograms, gives a final speed of about 2.65 meters per second, with the kinetic energy dropping from 14 joules to about 7, the perfectly inelastic signature.

The narration is the lesson. Each stage announced its principle through its features: a height for a speed, a rough stretch with a distance, a sticking impact. No stage consulted time. A reader trained by the principle guide hears those announcements without effort and spends the saved minutes on verification, running the descent by kinematics as a check, bounding the impact answer between the incoming speed and half of it, and confirming the friction loss sits below the available kinetic energy so the block provably reaches the collision. That is the article's discipline operating as a single reflex, and it is the form in which the exam will meet it.

## Inside a long free-response problem: how the parts talk to each other

Long mechanics problems are conversations in which each part hands the next a quantity and a responsibility. An opening part typically fixes the representation: draw the free-body diagram, sketch the energy bars, state the conservation law with its system. The middle parts spend what the opening established, an acceleration from the diagram, a speed from the bars, a tension from the isolated object. A closing part then changes one condition, a coefficient appears, a mass doubles, the surface tilts, and asks what changes, which tests whether the solution was understood or merely executed. Reading a long problem whole before writing anything lets a student see the conversation's arc and avoid answering part (a) in a way that makes part (c) impossible, such as choosing a system in the diagram part that the energy part cannot use.

The scoring follows the conversation. Method points attach to the openings, the named principle, the defined system, the correct diagram, and they survive arithmetic errors downstream, which the rubric treats as local accidents rather than conceptual failures. The reverse also holds: a correct final number reached through an unnamed or wrong principle earns the result point at most and often not even that, because the grader cannot certify reasoning that never appeared. Writing the principle statement first, in words, is therefore not ceremony but insurance purchased at the cheapest moment. Students should practice converting every practice problem's first line into a sentence of the form for this system, with these external forces, the governing relation is, until the sentence writes itself under pressure.

When a part stalls, the conversation offers a recovery that panic obscures: later parts often reveal the intended value or method for an earlier one, since setters build chains whose links are guessable from the requested units and the givens nearby. A student may legitimately state an assumed result for the stalled part and continue, earning the downstream method points that depend on structure rather than on the missing number. Abandoning the whole problem over one link is the costliest response available, and the chain architecture means it is never necessary.

## Applied readings: the core at work in vehicles and bodies

The principles keep their promises outside the classroom, and applied scenarios on the exam borrow that credibility. A car's crumple zone is an impulse device and an energy device at once: it lengthens the stopping time for the occupants' momentum change, cutting the average force, and it converts kinetic energy into deformation work across a designed distance, which is why modern fronts are built to collapse in a controlled way rather than to resist like a wall. A seatbelt and airbag then spread the remaining force across the body's strongest structures and across more time, the same two variables every impulse problem manipulates.

The pole vault is the energy ledger performed in public. The vaulter's sprint builds kinetic energy; the plant transfers it into elastic potential in the bent pole and gravitational potential as the body rises; the pole's return converts its store back into height. The ceiling on the clearance is set by the run-up speed through the familiar exchange, speed squared over twice g giving the possible rise of the center of mass: a 9 meter per second approach bounds that rise near 4 meters above its starting height, before the athlete's technique adds the final push and clearance skill. Every stage names its principle, and the whole performance is a chain problem with a person inside it.

Even walking is friction's advertisement. The foot plants and pushes backward against the ground; static friction, pointing forward, is the external force that accelerates the walker's center of mass, which is why ice, where the static ceiling collapses, makes the everyday motion nearly impossible despite healthy muscles supplying the same internal forces. The exam returns to such scenes because they discriminate sharply between students who track external forces on a system and students who narrate effort. Effort is internal. Only the ground can push the walker, and it pushes through friction.

## Under the clock: the two-pass discipline for mechanics sets

Time pressure changes what good method means, and mechanics sets reward a deliberate two-pass rhythm. The first pass harvests the items whose classification is instant: graph readings, ratio questions, and any computation whose principle announces itself in the first sentence. Items demanding a system decision under uncertainty, a multi-stage chain, or an unfamiliar costume get marked and left, because a stuck first attempt consumes the minutes that five later items needed. The second pass returns with the banked points providing calm, and classification often resolves on rereading, since the inventory habit works better the second time a prompt is seen than when it is forced the first.

Within a single problem, the clock argues for symbolic patience. Substituting numbers at the last responsible moment keeps one arithmetic pass instead of several, exposes cancellations while they are still visible, and lets the limiting-case and unit checks operate on the expression rather than on a bare number. The exceptions are teaching moments in themselves: when a symbolic tangle grows past a few lines, substituting early can simplify the landscape, and experienced solvers develop a feel for that threshold. What never pays is the halfway habit, numbers for some quantities and symbols for others, which forfeits cancellation, checking, and clarity simultaneously.

Free response under the clock is a writing discipline as much as a physics one. The principle sentence and the diagram go down first, before any algebra, because they are worth points in their own right and because they organize everything after. Justifications should be drafted in the vocabulary of the principles, naming systems, external forces, and conservation conditions, rather than narrating the algebra's steps, which the grader can see unaided. When the clock runs short, a stated principle with a correct setup out-earns a page of unfinished manipulation, so the order of writing is itself a scoring strategy: claims first, machinery second, arithmetic last.

## The verdict on the mechanics core

Forces, kinematics, and energy, with momentum completing the set, are not four topics but one skill viewed from four sides: reading a physical situation and naming the principle that governs it. The student who builds that naming reflex, on top of honest diagrams, disciplined signs, and the trap catalog, converts the exam's largest territory into its most predictable points. The student who collects formulas without the reflex owns a reference sheet's worth of knowledge that activates too slowly to score. Principle first, system second, algebra third; that order is the article, and it is also the exam.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How is kinematics tested on AP Physics 1?

Kinematics appears through graphs, verbal descriptions, and short computations rather than through long equation chains. Items hand over a position, velocity, or acceleration representation and ask for a reading: a displacement from the area under a velocity curve, an acceleration from a slope, a matching graph from a sibling representation. Direct equation use survives in constant-acceleration scenarios, often with the time-free relation doing the work when time is withheld. Free response embeds kinematics inside larger dynamics and energy problems, where the motion description is one stage among several. The through-line is representational fluency: the exam assumes a student can move among words, graphs, and equations for the same motion without friction, and it prices that assumption into every unit that follows.

### Q: How do you apply Newton's laws on AP Physics 1?

Application begins with choosing a system and drawing its free-body diagram, since the second law sums only the external forces on that chosen system. The first law handles every zero-acceleration case, from a resting book to a coasting spacecraft, by setting the net force to zero. The second law, written component by component along chosen axes, converts a force inventory into an acceleration. The third law sorts force pairs by the two objects involved, preventing the classic error of canceling forces that act on different bodies. Most exam dynamics is this sequence executed carefully: system, diagram, axes, components, then numbers. Students who write the sequence in that order find that the laws feel less like three statements to recall and more like one procedure to run.

### Q: How is energy conservation tested on AP Physics 1?

Energy questions connect two instants and ask what the books require: a speed from a height, a height from a spring compression, a stopping distance from an entry speed. Bar charts are the tested representation, with students reading which forms grow, which vanish, and where a thermal bar or a work bar must appear for the accounting to balance. Friction is the standard complication, subtracting a computable loss between the instants. The exam also probes the system choice directly, because counting gravitational potential energy requires Earth inside the system while counting gravity's work requires Earth outside it, and double counting the same transfer is the error the options are built to catch. Verbal justification parts ask why conservation applies, and the expected answer names the absence of non-conservative work.

### Q: How is momentum tested on AP Physics 1?

Momentum testing centers on interactions: collisions in one and two dimensions, explosions and recoils, and impulse delivered by forces acting over time. Students must classify an event before computing, conserving momentum always across a brief isolated interaction and kinetic energy only when the event is elastic. Force-time graphs appear as impulse areas, and comparison items ask which of two deliveries changes momentum more. Two-dimensional cases split conservation into components, each axis balanced separately. The recurring justification demand is the warranty statement: momentum conservation holds because the net external impulse during the brief interaction is negligible, and students who can write that sentence in their own words, tied to the specific scenario, earn the reasoning points that pure computation leaves behind.

### Q: How do you solve force problems on AP Physics 1?

Force problems yield to a fixed procedure. Isolate the object or system and draw the free-body diagram with one arrow per interaction, gravity always present and contact forces only where contact exists. Choose axes aligned with the acceleration or with a surface when that simplifies components. Write the second law for each axis separately, with signs fixed by the axis directions, keeping symbols until the structure is complete. For connected objects, solve the whole system first for the shared acceleration, then isolate one part to expose the connection force. Substitute numbers only at the end, then check the result against limiting cases: a frictionless version, an equal-mass version, an interval the answer must fall inside, such as an Atwood tension lying strictly between the two weights.

### Q: What is the work-energy theorem on AP Physics 1?

The theorem states that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, and it is the second law restated in the language of distance rather than time. Each force contributes its own signed work, force times displacement times the cosine of the angle between them, and the contributions sum to the change in one half mass times speed squared. The theorem powers the fastest solutions whenever a problem connects speeds across a stretch of road, a fall, or a spring's release, because it never asks how long the journey took or how the acceleration varied along it. Its honest limits matter as much: when a force's work cannot be computed from the given information, the theorem holds but cannot help, and the analysis returns to forces directly.

### Q: How do you handle projectile motion on AP Physics 1?

Split the motion at the launch instant into horizontal and vertical components and never let them mingle again except through the shared clock. Horizontally, velocity is constant and displacement is velocity times time. Vertically, gravity accelerates the projectile downward at about 10 meters per second squared, and the standard constant-acceleration relations apply. Level-ground launches give symmetric flights, equal rise and fall times and mirrored speeds at equal heights; launches landing at a different height must be solved from the vertical position equation with the true landing height. Two facts defeat the standard distractors: at the peak only the vertical velocity component vanishes, and the acceleration is g downward at every point of the flight, including the peak and both endpoints.

### Q: How are collisions tested on AP Physics 1?

Collision items ask for a classification, a computation, or both. The classification rests on two audits: total momentum, which must match before and after in every collision family, and total kinetic energy, whose survival identifies an elastic event, whose partial loss identifies an inelastic one, and whose maximum loss accompanies objects that stick. Computations conserve momentum across the interaction instant, splitting into components when the motion is two-dimensional. Justification parts ask why momentum conservation is legitimate despite friction or gravity, and the credited answer cites the brevity of the interaction and the negligibility of external impulse across it. Explosions and recoils are tested as collisions running in reverse, with kinetic energy increasing from an internal source while momentum still balances.

### Q: How do you draw a free-body diagram on AP Physics 1?

Replace the object with a dot and draw one arrow per external force acting on it, each arrow starting at the dot and labeled by its agent. Gravity always appears, drawn toward Earth's center. Contact forces appear only at genuine contacts: the normal force perpendicular to a surface, friction parallel to it opposing sliding or its tendency, tension along a rope away from the object, and pushes along their applied direction. Velocity and acceleration are never drawn, and no arrow represents motion itself or a supposed force of momentum. The diagram must match the system chosen for the second law, so forces between parts of the system stay off it. Graders award the diagram as its own event, and every later equation inherits its inventory, which is why accuracy here outperforms speed.

### Q: How is friction handled on AP Physics 1?

Friction is modeled in two forms with different rules. Kinetic friction acts between surfaces already sliding, with magnitude equal to the kinetic coefficient times the normal force, directed against the sliding. Static friction acts between surfaces not sliding and adjusts to match the applied force exactly, up to a ceiling of the static coefficient times the normal force; only at the point of slipping does it equal that maximum. The normal force must be computed fresh in each problem, since inclines, angled pulls, and vertical acceleration all change it from the weight. Direction is decided case by case by asking which way the surface contact would slide without friction. In energy problems, kinetic friction reappears as a loss equal to its magnitude times the sliding distance.

### Q: What is impulse on the AP Physics 1 exam?

Impulse is the product of a force and the time it acts, and the impulse-momentum theorem equates it to the change in an object's momentum. On a force-time graph, impulse is the area under the curve, so pulses of different shapes with equal areas deliver equal momentum changes. The concept explains why extending a collision's duration, through airbags, crumple zones, or flexed joints, reduces the force needed for a fixed momentum change, a reasoning pattern the exam tests in applied scenarios. Impulse also supplies the bridge between the second law and momentum conservation: internal impulses between interacting objects cancel by the third law, which is why an isolated system's total momentum survives its collisions. Items frequently ask students to compare impulses rather than compute them, rewarding area and duration reasoning over arithmetic.

### Q: How do you use kinematic equations on AP Physics 1?

The equations are licensed only when acceleration is constant across the interval under study, and the craft lies in matching the equation to the quantities present. When time is given or requested, the velocity-time and position-time relations lead. When time is absent from both the givens and the request, the time-free relation connecting speeds and displacement is the intended tool, and its presence in a problem often signals that an energy method would serve equally well. Variables must share one interval and one direction convention, with signs carried honestly for downward motion and reversed directions. A frequent error is chaining equations across an interval where acceleration changes, at a bounce or a surface boundary; the correct move is to close one interval at the boundary and open a fresh one with the boundary values as its givens.

### Q: How is circular motion tested on AP Physics 1?

Circular motion items test whether the reader treats centripetal force as a requirement rather than a substance. The net force toward the center must equal mass times speed squared over radius, and the exam asks which real force or combination supplies it: tension in a horizontal swing, friction on a flat curve, gravity in an orbit, or a normal-force component on a banked track. Vertical circles add position dependence, with the required supplement largest at the bottom and smallest at the top. Ratio reasoning dominates, since the force grows with speed squared and shrinks with radius. Diagrams are graded for the absence of an extra outward or centripetal arrow, because drawing one announces the misconception the item was written to find.

### Q: How do you connect force and acceleration on AP Physics 1?

The connection is the second law read in both directions. Given a force inventory, acceleration is the net force divided by the mass, a vector sharing the net force's direction exactly. Given an observed acceleration, the net force is mass times that acceleration, so an object can be moving opposite its net force, as a rising projectile with downward acceleration shows, or moving with no net force at all, as any coasting object shows. Acceleration graphs and net force graphs share a shape, scaled by the mass, which lets the exam translate between representations. The discipline is to keep velocity out of the relationship entirely: force says nothing about how fast an object moves, only about how its velocity is changing at that instant.

### Q: How do you read an energy bar chart on AP Physics 1?

Read the chart as double-entry bookkeeping at a single instant. Each bar's height represents one energy form for the chosen system: kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, and internal or thermal energy, sometimes joined by a bar for work done by external forces when the system boundary places a transfer outside. Compare charts across instants rather than admiring one alone: the total across all bars must balance when the system and its transfers are honestly chosen, friction reveals itself as a thermal bar that grows and never shrinks, and a falling kinetic bar must be matched by growth somewhere else. If potential energy appears, Earth or the spring is inside the system; if a work bar for gravity appears instead, Earth is outside it, and a chart counting both has charged one transfer twice.

### Q: How do you choose between Newton's second law and energy conservation for a mechanics problem on AP Physics 1?

Read the problem's givens and request for the deciding features. Choose the second law when a force, an acceleration, or a tension at an instant is requested, when forces must be inventoried to know what acts, or when the path and its times matter. Choose energy when two positions or speeds are connected across a height, a spring, or a distance, when time is neither given nor requested, and when the route between the instants is complicated or irrelevant. Friction tilts the choice: its work is easy to add in an energy account when the sliding distance is known, while a second-law treatment needs the acceleration to be constant. Many full problems use both in sequence, energy delivering a speed that a force analysis then spends, and the seam between the methods is usually marked by a change in what the question asks.

### Q: How do you handle an Atwood machine or connected-objects problem on AP Physics 1?

Treat the connected objects as one system first. The external driving forces, in an Atwood machine the difference of the two weights, act on the total mass, and the shared acceleration follows in one line. Then isolate a single object to expose the connection force: for one Atwood mass, its weight and the tension combine to give its mass times the shared acceleration, and solving yields the tension, which always lies strictly between the two weights, a bound worth checking before trusting the arithmetic. The same order serves pushed blocks, where the whole system gives the acceleration and one isolated block gives the contact force, and trains of carts joined by strings. The method rests on the rope's constraints: an unstretching rope forces one acceleration magnitude, and a light pulley changes direction without changing tension.

### Q: What is the difference between static and kinetic friction in AP Physics 1 problems?

The difference is behavioral. Kinetic friction has a fixed magnitude, the kinetic coefficient times the normal force, whenever surfaces slide, and it always opposes the sliding. Static friction has no fixed magnitude; it matches the force tending to cause sliding, newton for newton, from zero up to a ceiling set by the static coefficient times the normal force, and the object moves only when the tendency exceeds that ceiling. The static coefficient usually exceeds the kinetic one, so breaking free takes more force than staying in motion does. Problem errors cluster on two moves: assigning static friction its ceiling value for an object under a gentler push, which invents motion that never happens, and keeping static friction in a problem whose surfaces are already sliding, which overstates the resistance throughout.

### Q: How do you analyze motion from a position or velocity graph on AP Physics 1?

Start with the axes, because the same curve means different quantities on different graphs. On a position-time graph, the slope at any point is the velocity, a steeper slope meaning faster motion, a negative slope meaning reversed direction, and curvature meaning acceleration. Displacement is the net change in position between two times, read directly from the endpoints. On a velocity-time graph, the slope is the acceleration and the signed area between the curve and the time axis is the displacement, with area below the axis counting negative. On an acceleration-time graph, the area is the change in velocity. Most graph items are answered by choosing the right one of those three readings and executing it on the drawn feature, a tangent slope, a triangle, or a rectangle, rather than by reaching for an equation.

### Q: How do you handle elastic and inelastic collisions differently on AP Physics 1?

Conserve momentum across both, then split on the energy audit. In an elastic collision, kinetic energy is also conserved, giving two equations for two unknown final velocities; in one dimension with a stationary target, the results follow patterns worth recognizing, equal masses exchanging velocities and a light striker rebounding from a heavy target at nearly its incoming speed. In an inelastic collision, kinetic energy is partly lost to deformation, heat, and sound, so only momentum constrains the outcome, and additional information, a sticking condition or a measured final velocity, must close the problem. When objects stick, the event is perfectly inelastic, the final velocity is fully determined by momentum alone, and the kinetic energy loss is whatever the arithmetic leaves. The exam's classification questions reduce to computing both totals before and after and naming which audits pass.
