The Motor Vehicles Amendment Act 2019 is remembered, almost everywhere it is discussed, as the law that made traffic fines ten times larger. That memory is accurate about one schedule of figures and wrong about almost everything that matters. The amendment did rewrite the penalty schedule of the Motor Vehicles Act 1988, and the rewritten figures were large enough to provoke several state governments into softening them within weeks. But the same statute also rebuilt the compensation chapters of the parent Act, created a fund intended to stand behind every road user in the country, protected the stranger who stops to help an accident victim, made the builder of a defective road answerable for the defect, and brought the digital platforms that summon taxis within the licensing net for the first time. Above all, buried in the claims chapter and debated by almost nobody, it restored a limitation period for accident claims that had been absent from the Act for twenty five years, so that a family which delays beyond six months of an accident can lose its claim altogether.

![The Motor Vehicles Amendment Act 2019, showing the rewritten penalty schedule, the restored claim deadline and the new compensation scheme - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-13.webp)

This article follows the amendment as a trail: what it changed, section by section and figure by figure, when each change began to operate, and why its operation differs from state to state. The parent statute, the Motor Vehicles Act 1988 as enacted, is the subject of a companion article, [the Motor Vehicles Act 1988 explained](/2020/07/15/motor-vehicles-act-1988-explained/), and nothing in that account is repeated here. Where the amendment replaced a 1988 figure, the older figure is identified as superseded, because the two schedules are routinely confused and the confusion produces wrong advice. Where the amendment raised a penalty, the figure given is the 2019 figure, labelled as such, because several of those figures are subject to state compounding notifications that can lawfully produce a lower amount payable on the spot. The distinction between what Parliament enacted and what a driver in a particular state is actually asked to pay is the single most misunderstood feature of this amendment, and it runs through everything that follows.

## The shape of the amending Act

The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019 is Act 32 of 2019. It received the assent of the President on 9 August 2019, after the Lok Sabha passed the Bill on 23 July 2019 and the Rajya Sabha passed it, with amendments that required a further Lok Sabha approval, on 31 July 2019. Its predecessor Bill had been introduced in an earlier Lok Sabha and had lapsed on dissolution, a history that matters because the 2019 text was not a hurried response to a single event. It was the product of a long reform process that had begun with an attempt, years earlier, to replace the 1988 Act wholesale with a road transport and safety statute, and had settled instead for the largest single amendment the 1988 Act has received.

Section 1 of the amending Act left commencement to notification, and provided that different dates could be appointed for different provisions. That single drafting choice explains the amendment's complicated life in force. The Central Government appointed 1 September 2019 as the commencement date for a first and large tranche of sixty three clauses, covering the penalty schedule, licensing, registration and the enforcement architecture. The compensation chapters, which required new rules, new schemes and a new fund to be designed before they could operate, waited far longer. Sections 50 to 57 of the amending Act, which omit the old Chapter X of the parent Act and substitute new Chapters XI and XII material, were brought into force only on 1 April 2022, by a notification dated 25 February 2022. Between September 2019 and April 2022, therefore, India had the new fines and the old compensation law running side by side, and claims arising in that interval were governed by the compensation provisions as they stood before substitution. Any account that gives the amendment a single commencement date is wrong, and the error is not pedantic, because the date decides which compensation regime governs a given accident.

The staging did not end with those two dates. Individual provisions continued to be appointed as their subordinate machinery was readied: Section 211A, which gives legal recognition to electronic forms, documents and payments across the Act, was brought into force on 1 October 2020, and other provisions followed in smaller notifications as rules were framed for them. The amendment therefore has no single birthday in force. It has a principal commencement for its enforcement and penalty scheme on 1 September 2019, a deferred commencement for its compensation scheme on 1 April 2022, and a scatter of intermediate dates for provisions that depended on digital systems or new rules, and any statement about when a given provision began to bind has to be made provision by provision from the Gazette rather than inferred from the year in the Act's title.

The amending Act has ninety two sections. Its work falls into groups that are best kept separate, because they operate through different machinery. The first group rewrites penalties for offences, the subject of the next section. The second concerns licensing, registration and vehicle standards, including a power to recall defective vehicles. The third builds an enforcement architecture based on electronic monitoring. The fourth substitutes the insurance and compensation chapters. The fifth creates institutions and funds, including the National Road Safety Board and the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund. The sixth reaches new subjects the 1988 Act had never regulated, among them aggregators and the liability of road contractors. Each group is taken in turn below, and the table later in this article sets the whole trail out row by row with dates.

## The road to 2019: three decades of attempted reform

The amendment's size becomes intelligible against the failure that preceded it. The 1988 Act was itself a consolidation, and for its first two decades Parliament amended it at the margins: the 1994 amendment that removed the claim limitation, changes to insurance and permit provisions, and periodic increases in individual figures that inflation promptly undid. By the second decade of the century the gap between the statute and the road was the standard opening of every committee report on the subject. Vehicle numbers had multiplied beyond anything the 1988 scheme anticipated, the highways programme had rebuilt intercity travel, app based transport had appeared in the cities, and the Act's penalty schedule, its paper licensing system and its silence on vehicle recall, electronic enforcement and platform transport belonged to an earlier transport economy.

The first attempt at wholesale replacement came in the middle of the decade, when a draft road transport and safety bill proposed to retire the 1988 Act entirely and build a new architecture around a national authority, unified licensing and a modern penalty regime. That bill was never introduced in Parliament. Opposition gathered from several directions at once: state governments read the proposed authority as a centralising device in a Concurrent List field, transport unions read its permit reforms as a threat, and the drafting itself, which attempted everything, gave every objector a target. The government abandoned replacement and chose amendment instead, accepting the 1988 skeleton and rewriting its contents group by group. The choice shaped the 2019 Act's character: it is a renovation so extensive that the renovated building is barely recognisable, executed inside a frame that its drafters had already concluded was inadequate, because the frame was the only structure that could pass.

The amendment route was itself slow. A Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha in 2016, examined by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Transport, Tourism and Culture, and passed by the Lok Sabha in 2017. In the Rajya Sabha it stalled, was sent for further committee scrutiny, and lapsed when the Sixteenth Lok Sabha dissolved in 2019. The 2019 Bill reintroduced substantially the same scheme in the Seventeenth Lok Sabha within weeks of its constitution, passed the Lok Sabha on 23 July 2019, and passed the Rajya Sabha on 31 July 2019 with amendments on which the Lok Sabha then concurred, receiving assent on 9 August 2019. The speed of the second passage, after a decade of failure, is part of the trail: the government treated the bill as unfinished business of the previous Parliament, the state level objections that had defeated replacement had been answered, at least on paper, by the compounding and multiplier devices described in this article, and the political cost of the fines was accepted in advance as the price of the reform. What no part of that long process examined with comparable energy was the claims chapter, which travelled through both Bills inside the larger package, largely in the form the ministry's draftsmen gave it.

## Licensing, registration and the vehicle itself

The penalty schedule dominates the amendment's reputation, but its licensing and registration group is where the statute touches every driver and every vehicle in ordinary times rather than at the moment of an offence. The group pursues three objects: making the licence a document that certifies continuing competence, making registration a single national fact rather than a stack of state papers, and making the vehicle's own conformity to standards a condition policed at manufacture rather than after the accident.

On licensing, the amendment strengthened the consequences of the conduct it repriced. Section 19 empowers the licensing authority to disqualify a person from holding a licence or to revoke it on grounds that include the offences in the amended schedule, and the amendment attached licence consequences directly to the penalty provisions themselves, so that the dangerous driving, speeding, helmet and seat belt offences carry disqualification or impounding of the licence for the periods the sections state, with a driver refresher training course interposed as the route back: a person whose licence is suspended or revoked under Section 19 must undergo the refresher training the section prescribes before the licence revives. The licence thereby stops being a lifetime permission punctuated by fines and becomes a status that conduct can suspend and training can restore. The same group modernised the process of obtaining and renewing licences, providing for application and issue through electronic means and extending validity periods for older applicants in the manner the amended sections prescribe, with the Act's recognition of electronic forms and documents in Section 211A supplying the legal basis for the digital process.

On registration, the amendment provided for the registration of new vehicles by dealers, so that the first registration of a vehicle sold through a dealership is completed at the point of sale rather than through the buyer's separate attendance at the registering authority, and it built on the national register of motor vehicles, the single database in which the particulars of vehicles registered across the states are held together. The point of both changes is the same: the register ceases to be a local record consulted after an offence and becomes infrastructure, the database on which electronic enforcement, recall directions, insurance verification and hit and run investigation all depend. A camera challan is only as good as the register that names the owner; a recall direction is only as good as the register that finds the vehicles. Registration reform reads as administration and operates as enforcement.

On the vehicle itself, the amendment substituted and strengthened the type approval framework in Chapter VII, under which a manufacturer or importer must submit the prototype of a vehicle for testing by an accredited testing agency and obtain a type approval certificate before vehicles of that type may be sold or used in a public place. The testing agency that certifies a vehicle answers for its certificate: where a vehicle holding a type approval certificate is recalled under Section 110A, the agency that granted the certificate is liable to have its accreditation and registration cancelled. The construction links the three provisions into one chain of responsibility. The agency certifies, the manufacturer sells on the strength of the certificate, and the Central Government may direct a recall when a defect emerges, with the certifier's own existence staked on the quality of its certification. Before 2019, recall in India ran on an industry voluntary code and on the reputational calculus of manufacturers; after it, recall is a direction the state can give, and the amendment's vehicle group is best read as the construction of that power and of the register that makes it executable.

## The Motor Vehicles Amendment Act 2019 penalty schedule, rewritten

The penalties chapter of the 1988 Act had decayed in a way that no one seriously defended. The general penalty under Section 177 stood at one hundred rupees for a first offence, a figure fixed when that sum had real purchasing power, and most specific fines in the chapter shared the same vintage. A fine that costs less than the fuel consumed in the journey it punishes does not deter, and enforcement officers and courts alike treated many of the figures as nominal. The amendment's answer was a wholesale repricing. Every figure stated in this section is a 2019 figure, and the corresponding 1988 figure, where one is given, is stated only to show the scale of the change and is superseded.

The general penalty in Section 177 rose from one hundred rupees to five hundred rupees for a first offence and to fifteen hundred rupees for a subsequent offence, and a new Section 177A set the penalty for violating the rules of road regulation at five hundred rupees. Driving without a licence, under Section 181, rose from five hundred rupees to five thousand rupees, with imprisonment of up to three months available as before. Allowing an unauthorised person to drive, under Section 180, rose from one thousand rupees to five thousand rupees. Driving while disqualified, under Section 182, rose from five hundred rupees to ten thousand rupees. Disobedience of the orders of authorities, under Section 179, rose from five hundred rupees to two thousand rupees.

The offences that kill received the largest increases. Exceeding the speed limit, under Section 183, which had carried a single figure of four hundred rupees, was graduated by vehicle class: for a light motor vehicle, a fine of one thousand to two thousand rupees, and for a medium or heavy goods or passenger vehicle, two thousand to four thousand rupees, with the licence liable to be impounded on a repeat offence. Dangerous driving under Section 184, which includes jumping a red light, using a hand held communication device while driving and driving against the authorised flow of traffic, carries on a first offence imprisonment of six months to one year, or a fine of one thousand to five thousand rupees, or both, and on a second offence imprisonment of up to two years, or a fine of ten thousand rupees, or both. Driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs, under Section 185, rose from two thousand rupees to ten thousand rupees with imprisonment of up to six months on a first offence, and to fifteen thousand rupees with imprisonment of up to two years on a second offence. Racing and trials of speed under Section 189 rose from five hundred rupees to five thousand rupees.

The equipment and document offences were repriced in the same operation. Driving without insurance, under Section 196, rose from one thousand rupees to two thousand rupees for a first offence and four thousand rupees for a subsequent offence, with imprisonment of up to three months available in either case. Failure to wear a seat belt, under Section 194B, rose from one hundred rupees to one thousand rupees, and the section extends to carrying a child below fourteen years without a safety belt or child restraint system. Failure to wear protective headgear, under Section 194D, attracts one thousand rupees and disqualification from holding a licence for three months. Carrying passengers beyond the permitted number in a two wheeler, under Section 194C, attracts two thousand rupees and the same three month disqualification. Overloading of passengers, under the new Section 194A, attracts one thousand rupees for every extra passenger. Failure to give way to an emergency vehicle, under the new Section 194E, attracts ten thousand rupees, with imprisonment available for repeat conduct.

Overloading of goods vehicles received the steepest single increase in the schedule. Section 194 had prescribed two thousand rupees plus one thousand rupees for every additional tonne of excess load. The 2019 figure is twenty thousand rupees plus two thousand rupees for every additional tonne, and the amendment strengthened the machinery around the offence by empowering states to designate agencies to detect overloaded vehicles and impose the penalty, so that the figure is not left to depend on a chance interception. Driving an oversized vehicle, under the new Section 182B, attracts five thousand rupees. Using a vehicle without a permit, under Section 192A, rose to a fine of up to ten thousand rupees.

Three features of the new schedule matter as much as the figures. The first is Section 199B, which provides that the fines in the Act shall be increased every year, on the first day of April, by an amount not exceeding ten per cent of the existing fines, as notified by the Central Government. The schedule is therefore not a fixed table but a table with a built in escalator, and the figure enacted in 2019 is a floor for later years rather than a permanent resting point. The second is Section 210B, under which an authority empowered to enforce the Act that itself commits an offence under the Act is liable to twice the penalty corresponding to that offence. The third is Section 210A, which runs in the opposite direction from everything else in the schedule: it empowers a state government, subject to conditions made by the Central Government, to notify a multiplier of not less than one and not more than ten to be applied to the fines, which is a power to raise and not to lower the enacted figures. The lawful route by which several states made the schedule cheaper lies elsewhere, in compounding, and it is the subject of the federal dispute taken up after the next section.

### What is the fine for not wearing a helmet after the amendment?

Under Section 194D, riding without protective headgear conforming to the prescribed standards attracts a fine of one thousand rupees and disqualification from holding a driving licence for three months. The corresponding 1988 figure of one hundred rupees is superseded. The provision reaches the rider and every person carried on the vehicle alike.

### What is the fine for not wearing a seat belt?

Section 194B prescribes one thousand rupees for driving or riding without a seat belt, and the same provision reaches a vehicle in which a child below fourteen years is carried without a safety belt or a child restraint system fitted as prescribed.

### What is the penalty for driving without insurance?

Section 196 prescribes, for a first offence, imprisonment of up to three months, or a fine of two thousand rupees, or both, and for a second or subsequent offence imprisonment of up to three months, or a fine of four thousand rupees, or both.

## The architecture of the new schedule: escalator, multiplier and the officer inside the Act

Three provisions that sit at the edge of the penalty chapter repay attention together, because together they show the drafters thinking about the schedule as a system that must maintain itself. Section 199B, the escalator, addresses time: a fine fixed in nominal rupees is a fine with a half life, and the 1988 schedule died of exactly that decay. By permitting the Central Government to raise every fine by up to ten per cent each first of April, the section converts repricing from a parliamentary event, which happened once in thirty years, into an executive routine which can happen annually. The cap matters as much as the power. Ten per cent is roughly the rate at which the schedule's real value would otherwise erode in a high inflation decade, so the escalator is calibrated to preservation rather than to escalation in real terms, and a government that notifies the full increase every year merely holds the 2019 deterrent constant.

Section 210A, the multiplier, addresses space. It allows a state government, subject to conditions the Central Government makes, to notify a multiplier between one and ten to be applied to the fines, with different multipliers for different classes of vehicles. Read beside the compounding power, the section completes a deliberate asymmetry: the states hold two instruments, one that can only raise the price of offending and one that can only settle it lower, and the choice of instrument is itself a policy statement. A state that believes the enacted fines too low for its metropolitan traffic may multiply them upward for the classes of vehicle it names, and no state is required to wait for Parliament to share its judgment. That no state has rushed to use the multiplier at the top of its range, while several used compounding at once, is the political fact the architecture records: the pressure in the system ran downward, and the drafters, who gave the states both valves, appear to have expected exactly that.

Section 210B, the doubling provision for enforcing authorities, addresses the schedule's legitimacy. A penalty regime enforced by officials who stand outside it invites the citizen to read every challan as extraction, and the Indian roadside gives that reading a long history to draw on. By pricing an enforcing authority's own offence at twice the corresponding penalty, the section makes the schedule formally harder on its own administrators than on the public, and it converts the officer from a collector standing above the price list into a person named inside it. The same instinct appears in the amended compounding scheme, which contemplates community service for specified offences, substituting visible reparation for a payment the offender may be able to treat as a fee. None of these provisions will feature in a fine list shared on a messaging application, which is where the amendment's public identity was formed. They are, all the same, the provisions that reveal its drafters' theory: that a penalty system fails through decay, through regional misfit and through illegitimacy, and that each failure mode needs its own repair built into the text.

## Penalties in practice: the challan, the compounding and the court

Between the enacted figure and the money a driver hands over stand two procedures, and the amendment's real penalty regime is the interaction of the three. The first procedure is the challan: the offence is detected, increasingly by the electronic means Section 136A contemplates, and a notice issues to the offender or the registered owner demanding the amount. The second is compounding under Section 200, by which a specified offence may be settled, either before or after prosecution is instituted, by payment of the amount the appropriate government has notified for that offence to the officer or authority the notification designates. Compounding is not a discount offered by an officer; it is a statutory settlement at a notified price, and once compounded, the offender is discharged and no further proceeding lies for that offence. The third is prosecution in court, where the magistrate may impose any penalty up to the enacted maximum, including imprisonment where the section provides it, and where the enacted 2019 figures apply in full.

The practical geography of the amendment follows from this structure. In a state that notified compounding amounts at or near the enacted figures, the roadside price and the court price converge, and the amendment operates much as its parliamentary advocates described. In a state that notified compounding amounts well below the enacted figures, the two prices diverge, and the operative deterrent is the lower one for every offence the driver expects to settle, which is most of them. The enacted figure in such a state is not fiction: it governs the driver who contests the charge and loses, and it anchors the notified figure politically, since a compounding notification must be defended as a proportion of the enacted penalty. But a summary of the amendment that recites only enacted figures describes the law of the courtroom, while the law of the road is a joint product of Parliament's schedule and each state's notification, and varies by state exactly as the table in this article records.

The amendment also armed the schedule with consequences beyond money, and these are uniform across states because they attach to the licence rather than to a notified amount. The penalty sections themselves carry disqualification for the helmet, two wheeler overloading and emergency vehicle offences, impounding of the licence on repeat speeding, and the suspension machinery of Sections 19 and 206 reaches the offences the Act lists for that purpose, among them Sections 183, 184, 185, 189, 190, 194C, 194D and 194E. For a professional driver the licence consequences are the heavier punishment by an order of magnitude: a fine is a cost of the month, a suspended licence is the loss of the livelihood, and transport operators understood this immediately, which is why the industry argument against the amendment concentrated as much on disqualification as on the rupee figures. The design is deliberate. Money penalties can be absorbed, priced into freight rates and passed to customers; licence consequences cannot be passed on, and they fall on the person whose conduct the law is trying to change.

## Why the penalty increases became a federal dispute

Mechanically propelled vehicles sit in the Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule, at Entry 35, and road transport is a field in which both Parliament and the state legislatures may legislate, subject to the doctrine that resolves a conflict between them, which is the subject of the companion study of [repugnancy in the concurrent field](/2018/09/01/article-254-repugnancy-explained/). That placement had never produced a serious conflict over the 1988 Act, because the Act was a central statute whose amounts everyone accepted as the common floor of practice. The 2019 repricing changed the politics of the arrangement overnight. A fine of five thousand rupees for driving without a licence is a deterrent in a metropolitan income and a catastrophe in a district income, and state governments, which run the traffic police who must collect the fines and face the anger they produce, were the level of government that heard the complaint first.

The case for the increases, as the Union Minister for Road Transport and Highways, Nitin Gadkari, made it in Parliament and in public, rests on three legs. The first is deterrence arithmetic: a penalty that has lost its real value deters nothing, and the 1988 figures had lost theirs many times over. The second is the scale of the harm: the Minister told the Lok Sabha that more than one and a half lakh people die and five lakh are injured on Indian roads every year, figures drawn from the Ministry's own accident reporting, and a legal order that prices the conduct producing those deaths at one hundred rupees is not serious about them. The third is instrument choice: among the tools available to the state, a deterrent fine is the least intrusive, since it neither imprisons nor bans, and it falls only on those who break rules that are themselves uncontroversial.

The case made by the states rests on three legs of its own, and deserves statement at the same strength. A penalty is paid out of income, and a schedule calibrated to be felt by a salaried metropolitan driver is punitive to a rural driver whose monthly income may be a fraction of the single fine. A very high fine collected at the roadside enlarges the bargaining power of the collecting officer, and the risk that enforcement discretion converts into extraction grows with the amount at stake; states that police their own roads are entitled to weigh that risk. And the constitutional design itself, which placed mechanically propelled vehicles in the Concurrent List rather than the Union List, contemplated a state role in calibrating transport law to local conditions, so a state that moderates the schedule is exercising a designed function and not obstructing a central one.

The mechanism the moderating states used is essential to understanding the episode, because it was lawful and remains lawful. Section 200 of the parent Act permits specified offences to be compounded, before or after the institution of prosecution, by such officers or authorities and for such amounts as the appropriate government may notify. The amount for which an offence is compounded is therefore a state notified figure, distinct from the fine a court could impose after trial. Within weeks of the September 2019 commencement, Gujarat notified compounding amounts that cut many of the new figures by roughly half, and its government defended the step publicly on the ground that the central amounts were excessive for its population. Other states moved at different speeds: some adopted the schedule as enacted, some notified reductions of their own, and some, among them West Bengal and Madhya Pradesh, held back from applying parts of the new regime while their governments reviewed it, with Rajasthan's government also calling for a review of the higher amounts. No state purported to amend the central Act, and none could have. What varied was the price of settling an offence on the spot, which is the price most drivers ever encounter.

The honest summary is that the amendment created two penalty schedules in practice: the enacted schedule, which governs in court and in states that compounded at the full figure, and the compounded schedule, which governs at the roadside in states that notified less. Commentary that recites the central figures as the fines payable in India misleads, and commentary that describes the states as having defied Parliament misleads in the opposite direction. Compounding at a notified amount is a power the central statute itself confers, and its exercise is politics within the law, not resistance to it.

The absence of a constitutional challenge to the penalty schedule is itself worth explaining, because the doctrine was available and the provocation was real. A state law repugnant to a parliamentary law on a Concurrent List subject yields under Article 254, but the state action in this episode was not legislation at all in the relevant sense: compounding amounts are notified under the authority of the central Act, and a notification issued under a power the central statute grants cannot be repugnant to that statute. The states that moderated the schedule therefore never placed themselves in the path of the doctrine. What they did was closer to the ordinary working of Indian fiscal federalism, in which the Centre legislates the norm and the states, who must operate it, adjust its incidence; and the Centre, for its part, held a counterweight in Section 210A, the multiplier power, which allows a state to raise fines above the enacted level but confers no power to lower them, marking the boundary of the design: downward adjustment is available only through the settlement route of compounding, upward adjustment by notification, and the enacted figure itself beyond the reach of either. The one state instrument never used was a state amendment to the Act itself, which the Constitution permits on a Concurrent subject with Presidential assent, and its non use is the measure of how completely the compounding route satisfied the states' political need without a constitutional confrontation.

## Electronic enforcement and the camera as witness

The amendment's enforcement group is built on a single inserted provision, Section 136A. It obliges the state government to ensure electronic monitoring and enforcement of road safety on national highways, state highways, roads and in urban cities up to population limits prescribed by the Central Government. The monitoring is to be carried out by means prescribed centrally, which in practice means speed cameras, camera based detection of red light and seat belt and helmet violations, and automatic number plate recognition feeding an electronic challan. The state rule making power in Section 137 was enlarged to provide for the limits of urban cities and for electronic monitoring under the section, so the architecture is central in design and state in operation, the same division of labour that produced the penalty dispute.

The significance of electronic enforcement is procedural as much as technological. A challan raised by a camera and delivered to the registered owner reverses the practical burden of the old roadside encounter: the driver no longer negotiates with an officer at the point of the offence, and the compounding amount, whatever the state has notified, is what the notice demands. That is precisely why the two halves of the amendment interact. Electronic enforcement removes discretion from the encounter, which answers the states' objection that high fines enlarge roadside bargaining power, while compounding preserves state control of the amount, which answers the objection that the Centre has priced offences without regard to local incomes. Neither side of the federal argument obtained the whole of what it wanted, and the settlement the statute produces is an automated demand at a state moderated price.

The same group of provisions professionalised the flow of accident information. The police report of an accident, which the Act requires to be forwarded to the Claims Tribunal, feeds a claims process that the substituted chapter then accelerates through interim and detailed accident reports, and Section 211A recognised electronic forms, documents and payments across the Act. For the claimant the practical consequence is that the evidentiary record of the accident increasingly originates in systems rather than in the memory of witnesses, a shift whose effects on tribunal practice are still working themselves out. The same systems generate the data on which public argument about road deaths depends, and those who document dangerous stretches of road, officials who speak about enforcement failures and families who press for a blackspot to be fixed all handle records whose exposure can carry a cost, which is why the working methods described in [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) belong to the same story of evidence becoming digital, attributable and in need of protection.


The shift carries a further consequence that tribunals and High Courts have had to absorb: the registered owner becomes the first respondent of the enforcement system whether or not the owner was driving. The notice travels to the name in the register, and the burden of showing that another person held the vehicle, or that the detection is mistaken, falls in the first instance on the person the register names. That allocation is defensible where the register is accurate and transfers are recorded promptly, and harsh where it is not, and it gives the registration reforms described in this article their enforcement significance: dealer registration and a single national register are the accuracy conditions on which camera based liability fairly depends. The amendment's groups, once again, stand or fall together.

## Compensation rebuilt: the substitution of Chapters X and XI

The 1988 Act compensated accident victims through a structure whose account belongs to the companion article and is not repeated here. The amendment demolished and rebuilt that structure in two moves. Section 50 of the amending Act omits Chapter X of the parent Act, the chapter on liability without fault in certain cases, and Section 51 substitutes a new Chapter XI on insurance of motor vehicles against third party risks for sections 145 to 164. Because these sections were commenced only on 1 April 2022, the rebuilt scheme governs accidents from that date, and the older provisions continued to govern accidents before it, a transition that tribunals and the High Courts have had to police claim by claim.

What the omitted chapter contained is worth one paragraph, because its removal is the cleanest single marker of the substitution's intent. Chapter X had imposed liability without proof of fault in a narrow class of cases, and the superseded Section 140 fixed its sums at fifty thousand rupees where the accident caused death and twenty five thousand rupees where it caused permanent disablement, figures that had themselves been left unchanged since 1994 and had decayed to gestures. The old chapter also carried the rule that a no fault claim did not prejudice the claimant's right to pursue the larger fault based compensation, with the no fault amount adjusted against it. The substituted scheme keeps the principle and discards the scale: the no fault idea is absorbed into Sections 161 and 164 at ten and twenty times the superseded figures, the adjustment principle survives in the Fund's deduction rules, and the separate small chapter that had housed the idea for three decades disappears because the main chapter has taken its function over.

The centre of the new chapter is Section 164, which replaces the older no fault provisions with a fixed sum payable without any proof of wrongful act, neglect or default. The owner of the vehicle or the authorised insurer, as the case may be, is liable to pay compensation of five lakh rupees in respect of the death of a person, and two lakh fifty thousand rupees in respect of grievous hurt, where death or grievous hurt has resulted from an accident arising out of the use of a motor vehicle. The provision is an election: where a claimant accepts compensation under Section 164, the claim under the fault based route merges in that acceptance, and the claimant cannot pursue both. The figures are five lakh and two and a half lakh rupees exactly, and they are the 2019 figures; the far smaller no fault sums of the omitted chapter are superseded and must not be quoted as the law governing an accident after the substitution took effect.

The election deserves emphasis, because it is the point at which the new chapter can work against an unwary claimant. Five lakh rupees is a certain, immediate and undefended sum, and to a family facing funeral costs and lost wages it presents itself as help rather than as a settlement. But the fault based claim it displaces is the claim the tribunal system exists to compute, in which compensation for a deceased earner is built from income, dependency and future prospects, and routinely reaches sums several times the fixed figure where the deceased was young and employed. The insurer that offers the Section 164 sum promptly is not acting outside the design; the design intends prompt payment. What the design requires, and what practice must supply, is that the election be informed: a claimant who accepts the fixed sum without advice has traded the larger claim for the faster one, and the six month deadline presses on that decision from the first week. The fixed sum is best understood as a floor the legislature has made available on demand, valuable precisely to the claimant whose fault based case is weak, whose need is immediate, or whose deceased left no provable income, and hazardous to the claimant who mistakes it for the measure of the loss.

Section 164A adds a power for the Central Government to frame schemes for interim relief to claimants, so that money can reach a family before the tribunal process concludes, with a mechanism to recover sums disbursed from the owner where the claim arises out of the use of that owner's vehicle. The design recognises what tribunal practice had long shown, that the delay between accident and award is itself one of the injuries the system inflicts, and it attacks the delay at the level of cash flow rather than procedure alone.

## What the deterrence argument assumes

The penalty debate was conducted as though the only variable were the number, and the amendment's design is more intelligent than the debate, because a fine deters through a chain of assumptions that the other groups of the Act exist to repair. A penalty deters when the offender expects to be detected, expects the demand to survive his attempts to negotiate it away, and prices the expected payment above the benefit of the offence. The 1988 regime failed at every link rather than only at the figure. Detection was episodic and human, which made the expected penalty a small probability multiplied by a small sum. Negotiation was structural, since the roadside encounter placed the amount, the compounding and the threat of court in one conversation. And the sums, as the federal section records, had decayed to the level of a toll.

The amendment repairs the chain link by link, and the repair explains provisions that a fine list never mentions. Electronic enforcement under Section 136A raises the probability term, because a camera detects at a rate no cadre of officers can match and creates the record at the moment of the offence. The e-challan delivered to the registered owner separates detection from collection and removes the negotiation from the roadside, which is why the same amendment that raised the fines also invested in the machinery that delivers them. The licence consequences attack the benefit term for the offender who can absorb money, since a disqualification cannot be expensed. Section 199B then maintains the repaired schedule against time. Read this way, the schedule is the least interesting part of the deterrence design, and the states' use of compounding is best understood as a recalibration of the final term of the product: a lower amount, more reliably collected through automated detection, may deter more than a higher amount sporadically enforced, and the states that compounded down while investing in camera enforcement were, whether they said so or not, trading the size of the fine against the certainty of it. The countervailing risk is equally structural. Automated detection generates challans at industrial volume, errors in the register convert directly into demands on innocent owners, and a system that cannot correct its mistakes at the speed it makes them will spend the legitimacy that Section 210B was inserted to buy. The deterrence the amendment promises is therefore an administrative achievement before it is a legislative one, and its measure will be found in collection and correction rates rather than in the schedule itself.

## The claims process the deadline governs

The six month gate in Section 166(3) does not stand alone; it closes a process the substituted scheme had just redesigned to move faster, and the two changes have to be read together to be understood. Under the machinery the amended Act and the Central Motor Vehicles Rules construct around an accident, the state begins the claim before the family does. The investigating officer forwards the First Accident Report to the Claims Tribunal and to the insurer, an Interim Accident Report follows within the period the rules prescribe, and a Detailed Accident Report, assembling the police investigation, the medical record and the particulars of the vehicles and their insurance, is placed before the tribunal within ninety days, with the tribunal empowered to extend the period for reasons recorded. Section 166(4) directs the tribunal to treat the report of the accident forwarded to it under Section 159 as an application for compensation under the Act. The insurer, on receipt of the detailed report, is to make an offer of settlement within the period the rules prescribe, and where the claimant accepts, the tribunal records a consent award; where liability is accepted but the amount is not, the tribunal hears the parties on quantum; where liability is disputed, it conducts the enquiry. The rules set target periods for each stage measured in months from the accident, a timetable that presupposes a claim crystallising early.

Read against that design, the limitation period has an internal logic its critics must answer: a process built to conclude within a year of the accident cannot be fed claims arriving a decade late, and the police generated record, the insurer's offer and the tribunal's enquiry all assume the freshness of the evidence the reports collect. The amendment's architects can fairly say that the deadline and the fast process are one reform, not two, and that the pre 2022 regime of unlimited time was the companion of a process that took unlimited time as well. The answer the critics return is about who bears the risk of the new speed. The reports move on the state's timetable and the insurer's, and the family's six months run whether or not the reports arrive, whether or not the police station serves the papers the rules contemplate, and whether or not the injured claimant is conscious. A deadline designed to discipline institutions operates, in the text as enacted, only against the claimant, since no corresponding consequence visits the officer who delays the detailed report or the insurer who delays the offer. The litigation over condonation is, at bottom, the courts deciding whether to repair that asymmetry by interpretation, and the differing answers the High Courts have given are the reason the subject belongs in an amendment trail rather than in a summary of settled law.

The hit and run limb of the scheme, which matters most to the families with the least, is processed outside the tribunal altogether. Under the Compensation to Victims of Hit and Run Motor Accidents Scheme 2022, which replaced the 1989 solatium scheme on the same 1 April 2022 commencement, an application for the Section 161 sum is made to the Claims Enquiry Officer for the district in which the accident occurred, an officer drawn from the district's revenue administration. The enquiry officer examines the application and the police record, and the claim is sanctioned through the district level authority the scheme designates, with payment released from the Fund's hit and run compensation account. The design choices are deliberate: no court fee, no adversarial contest, no need for the family to identify an opponent, and a sum fixed by the section rather than assessed by argument. The scheme's weakness is the mirror of its strength, since a process that runs on police reports and district administration moves at the speed of both, and the family's remedy for delay is persistence rather than appeal.

The substitution also adjusted the appellate end of the process. Section 173, as amended in the substitution group, provides that no appeal shall lie against an award of the Claims Tribunal where the amount in dispute in the appeal is less than one lakh rupees, a change that withdraws the High Court's appellate time from small disputes and leaves the tribunal's award effectively final across the large class of modest claims. Together with the fixed sums of Section 164, which remove quantum from dispute altogether in the cases they govern, the direction of the group is consistent: fewer contests, earlier money, smaller appellate load. Whether that direction serves claimants depends entirely on the level at which the fixed sums and the thresholds are set and on the speed with which the machinery actually moves, which is why the assessment of the substitution cannot be separated from the working of the fund and the scheme that the following sections describe.

## The untraced vehicle: hit and run compensation and the Accident Fund

For the victim of a hit and run accident, where the identity of the vehicle cannot be ascertained in spite of reasonable efforts, fault based litigation is impossible because there is no defendant. Section 161 therefore provides statutory compensation payable in accordance with a scheme framed by the Central Government: two lakh rupees in the case of death, and fifty thousand rupees in the case of grievous hurt, or such higher amount as may be prescribed. The corresponding 1988 figures, twenty five thousand rupees for death and twelve thousand five hundred rupees for grievous hurt, are superseded; the death figure rose eightfold, and the increase is the largest proportional change in the entire compensation scheme. Claims under the section run through the claims enquiry machinery of the scheme rather than through adversarial litigation against an insurer, and compensation received under the section is adjusted against any later award, so that the same loss is not paid twice, a set off for which Section 163 provides.

The money stands behind a new institution. Section 164B requires the Central Government to constitute a Motor Vehicle Accident Fund, constituted for the purpose of providing compulsory insurance cover to all road users in the territory of India. Into it are credited payments of a nature notified and approved by the Central Government, grants and loans from the Central Government, the balance of the fund that had existed under the earlier hit and run scheme provision, and other prescribed sources. The Fund was in fact constituted through the Central Motor Vehicles (Motor Vehicle Accident Fund) Rules, 2022, with a trust deed executed in January 2022. Section 164B then directs what the Fund may be used for: the treatment of persons injured in road accidents under the golden hour scheme framed under Section 162; compensation to the representatives of a person who died in a hit and run accident under the Section 161 scheme; compensation to a person grievously hurt in such an accident; and compensation to such other persons as the Central Government prescribes. The maximum liability payable in each case is as prescribed centrally, and where money paid from the Fund for treatment later becomes part of a successful claim against an insurer, the amount is deducted, so the Fund operates as payer of first resort rather than as a source of double recovery.

In operation the Fund is organised through separate accounts that mirror the risks it absorbs: an account for insured vehicles, an account for uninsured vehicles and hit and run accidents, and a hit and run compensation account, administered with the General Insurance Council under the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund Trust that the 2022 Rules establish. The architecture matters because it prices the social layer honestly. The insured account is fed by the industry whose policyholders generate the claims; the uninsured and hit and run accounts exist for the cases in which the central promise of the scheme, that no road user stands outside the cover, would otherwise fail for want of a defendant or a policy. A victim struck by an uninsured vehicle and a victim struck by an untraced one are, for compensation purposes, in the same position: someone must pay who did not cause the accident, and Section 164B's answer is a pooled fund rather than the state exchequer acting case by case. The Supreme Court's road safety proceedings, in which the General Insurance Council was charged with building the portal through which hit and run claims are uploaded and processed, show the fund becoming administrative reality through the same judicial supervision that produced the golden hour scheme.

Section 162 attacks the interval in which accident victims most often die untreated. It obliges the general insurance companies carrying on business in India to provide for the treatment of road accident victims, including during the golden hour, in accordance with a scheme the Central Government is required to make for cashless treatment of victims during that hour, and the scheme may provide for a fund for the purpose. The golden hour is defined in the substituted chapter as the period of one hour following a traumatic injury during which prompt medical treatment has the highest likelihood of preventing death. The obligation to frame the scheme proved easier to enact than to discharge. In S. Rajaseekaran v. Union of India, the Supreme Court held that the provision, which had been in force from 1 April 2022, sought to uphold the right to life under Article 21, that framing the scheme was a statutory obligation of the Central Government on which more than reasonable time had already been spent, and it directed the government to finalise the scheme by 14 March 2025, observing that its implementation would save the lives of injured persons who die for want of treatment in that first hour. A cashless treatment scheme was thereafter notified in 2025, with pilot operation reported in several states and union territories before wider rollout. The sequence is instructive for the whole amendment: Parliament creates the duty and the fund in 2019, the provision commences in 2022, and the operative scheme arrives years later under judicial pressure. The trail from text to treatment is measured in years, and the article's table records the stages separately for exactly that reason.

## The deadline that returned: the six month limitation

The provision of the amendment that will decide the fate of the largest number of ordinary claims is Section 166(3) of the parent Act, inserted by the substitution of Section 166. It provides that no application for compensation shall be entertained unless it is made within six months of the occurrence of the accident. Six months is not a long time in the life of a bereaved family. It runs from the accident, not from the end of mourning, the settlement of the deceased's affairs, the recovery of an injured claimant from hospital, or the day the family learns that a claim exists. A family that spends those months on funerals, hospital bills and the search for the deceased's documents can find, on first consulting a lawyer, that the claim the law gave it has already expired.

The history makes the change sharper. The Act as enacted in 1988 carried a limitation period for claims, with a power to condone delay. The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 1994 deleted the limitation provision altogether, on the reasoning that accident victims and their families, often poor and often unaware of the process, should not lose compensation to a calendar. For twenty five years after that deletion, a claim could be filed at any time, and tribunals entertained claims brought years after the accident, subject to the ordinary discipline that stale claims are harder to prove. The 2019 amendment restored the bar, and restored it in a harder form than the 1988 original, because the new sub section contains no condonation power of its own. The restoration attracted almost no public argument. The fines filled the newspapers and the television debates; the deadline, which affects only people who have already lost someone, passed through Parliament in the noise.

The courts have spent the years since April 2022 working out what the restoration means. Three questions have dominated. The first is prospectivity: does the six month bar govern accidents that occurred before 1 April 2022, when the substitution commenced? The Kerala High Court answered no in Sathy v. Dileep, reported at 2022 LiveLaw (Ker) 324, holding that the limitation would apply to accidents after the introduction of the amendment, because a right that had already accrued to a claimant could not be taken away by a provision the legislature had not made retrospective, and the Gauhati High Court reached the same destination in setting aside a tribunal order that had dismissed, as time barred, a claim arising from an accident of May 2019. The second question is whether delay can be condoned at all under a provision that grants no condonation power. The Kerala High Court, in a batch of petitions led by Akshay Raj, held that claim petitions filed beyond six months cannot be dismissed in limine, because the Act does not exclude the Limitation Act, whose Section 29(2) carries the condonation provisions into such proceedings, and because limitation in a claims enquiry is a mixed question of fact and law that a tribunal must try rather than assume. Other benches have been pressed by insurers to hold that the words shall not be entertained mean exactly what they say and admit no condonation, and tribunal practice across the country has not been uniform. The third question is constitutional: petitions challenging Section 166(3) reached the Supreme Court, and the Court, while the challenge awaits final adjudication, directed that no motor accident claim petition be dismissed as time barred under the sub section. The position, stated precisely, is therefore this: the deadline is on the statute book and in force for accidents from 1 April 2022, its application to older accidents has been denied by the High Courts, its condonation is supported by reasoned High Court authority and opposed by the text, and its validity is under challenge with interim protection in place. A claimant inside six months should file at once and treat the period as hard; a claimant outside it should file anyway and plead the authorities, because the interim direction protects the filing.

Two further features of the deadline deserve record. The period runs against legal representatives and injured claimants alike, and the Act makes no exception in the sub section for a claimant who is a minor or under a disability when the accident occurs; arguments that the Limitation Act's provisions for persons under disability should soften the bar form part of the condonation debate and have not been finally resolved. And the deadline interacts with the Section 164 election in a way families discover late: the fixed sum route, which needs no proof of fault and no contest, is itself claimed through the claims process, so a family that hesitates between the certain five lakh rupees and the larger fault based claim must still move within the same six months, and the hesitation is itself time spent against the gate.

The pending constitutional challenge takes the argument one level higher, and its shape can be stated without anticipating its result. The petitions attack the sub section as a denial of access to compensation that operates without regard to the claimant's circumstances: it runs against minors and persons under disability without exception, it runs while the claimant is in hospital or in mourning, it runs identically against the literate claimant with a lawyer in the family and the illiterate claimant who learns of the tribunal from a neighbour, and it extinguishes a claim that is, in substance, a claim for the value of a life or a limb. A classification between claimants who file within six months and claimants who do not is said to bear no proportionate relation to the object of compensating accident victims, and to visit the sins of a slow system on the party the system exists to serve. The answer available to the Union is the answer limitation law always gives: that periods of limitation are a legitimate legislative instrument for the finality of claims and the reliability of evidence, that six months is not a trivial period for a step that consists of filing an application, that the police generated report can itself be treated as an application under Section 166(4) so that the state's own machinery starts the claim, and that a claimant defeated by the period loses a statutory remedy and not a fundamental right. The interim direction, under which no petition is to be dismissed as time barred while the challenge is heard, holds the two positions in suspension, and the final judgment will determine not only the fate of the deadline but the terms on which future social welfare legislation may condition its benefits on prompt claiming.

It is worth pausing on why this provision, of everything in the amendment, best fits the pattern this series has traced across statutes: the provision that attracts the public argument is rarely the provision that changes the most lives. The penalty increases were contested in state assemblies, debated on television and moderated by notification, and their practical burden, at the compounded figures most states settled on, is a few thousand rupees at a roadside. The limitation provision was contested by nobody, moderated by nobody, and its practical burden falls, in full and without discount, on the subset of citizens least able to bear it: families in the first six months of grief, in districts where legal advice is a journey rather than a telephone call. The amendment's defenders reply, with some force, that a limitation period serves the integrity of the claims process itself, since evidence decays, vehicles are scrapped, witnesses scatter, and insurers facing claims a decade old cannot investigate fraud; and that six months is generous beside the periods the Limitation Act allows for comparable applications. Both things can be true at once, and the provision is defensible in the abstract. What is not defensible is the allocation of attention: a legislature that argues for weeks about the price of a helmet fine and not at all about the expiry of a widow's claim has ranked spectacle above consequence, and the amendment's trail records the ranking faithfully.

## Third party insurance after the substitution

The substituted Chapter XI restates the obligation that every vehicle used in a public place be covered by a policy of insurance against third party risks, and restates the requirements a policy must satisfy, the extent of the insurer's liability, the duty of insurers to satisfy judgments and awards, and the limited defences open to an insurer that has received proper notice of the proceedings. The definitions were modernised: an authorised insurer is tied to registration with the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, grievous hurt carries its meaning from Section 320 of the Indian Penal Code, a hit and run motor accident is defined by the impossibility of ascertaining the vehicle's identity in spite of reasonable efforts, and third party is expressed to include the Government. The chapter carries forward the prohibition on avoiding liability through restrictive conditions in the policy as against third parties, and the settlement machinery under which an insurer that accepts liability can convert the tribunal proceeding into a consent award.

The restated obligation deserves a sentence of its own, because the compensation edifice stands on it. The chapter requires that no person use, or cause or allow another person to use, a motor vehicle in a public place unless a policy of insurance complying with the chapter is in force, and it specifies the requirements such a policy must meet, the limits of the insurer's liability for third party injury and property damage, and the persons the policy must cover, including, in the case of a transport vehicle, the classes of employee whose risk the employer must insure. The offence of using an uninsured vehicle, priced at two thousand rupees for a first offence under Section 196, is the enforcement tip of this obligation, and the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund is its backstop: between the offence and the fund, the substituted chapter constructs a closed system in which every accident victim's compensation is assigned a payer, the negligent owner, his insurer, or the pooled fund, and the failures of the system are failures to operate the assignment rather than gaps in its design.

Two consequences of the substitution matter to claimants. The first is that the no fault election under Section 164 and the fund under Section 164B sit inside this chapter, so the insurance chapter is no longer only about policies bought by vehicle owners; it also houses the social insurance layer that operates where no policy responds, because the vehicle is uninsured or untraced. The second is that Section 158 and Section 159 machinery, under which the police forward accident information and reports that the tribunal may treat as an application for compensation under Section 166(4), connects the insurance chapter to the police station, so that a claim can be set in motion by the state's own report even where the family has not yet found a lawyer. The interaction with the six month deadline is plain: a system that generates the claim from its own records has less excuse for defeating the claim on the ground that the family generated it late, and that tension is one the courts will have to resolve as the limitation jurisprudence develops. This article is legal information about the structure of the amendment and not advice on any individual claim, and a claimant's position turns on the date of the accident and the state of the authorities in the relevant jurisdiction.

### When can the Central Government order a vehicle to be recalled?

Under Section 110A, where a defect in a motor vehicle of a particular type may cause harm to the environment, or to the driver or occupants, or to other road users, and the defect is reported in the manner the section prescribes, the Central Government may direct the manufacturer to recall the vehicles of that type.


## Guardians, juveniles and the deemed guilty provision

Section 199A, inserted with the new Sections 199A and 199B, is the amendment's most deliberately provocative provision. Where an offence under the Act has been committed by a juvenile, the guardian of the juvenile or the owner of the motor vehicle is deemed to be guilty of the contravention and liable to be proceeded against and punished accordingly. In addition to the penalty for the underlying offence, the guardian or owner is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and with a fine of twenty five thousand rupees. An Explanation directs the court to presume that the use of the vehicle by the juvenile was with the consent of the guardian or owner. The deeming is not irrebuttable: the proviso protects a guardian or owner who proves that the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it. The provision does not apply at all where the juvenile held a learner's licence under Section 8 or a driving licence and was operating a vehicle of the kind the licence authorised. Two further consequences fall on the margins of the offence rather than on the adult: the registration of the vehicle used is cancelled for twelve months, and the juvenile is not eligible for a learner's licence or a driving licence until attaining the age of twenty five years. The juvenile himself is punishable with the fines provided in the Act, while any custodial sentence stands modified by the juvenile justice framework, the [Juvenile Justice Act 2015](/2016/05/15/juvenile-justice-act-2015-explained/) supplying the process through which a child in conflict with the law is dealt with.

The juvenile end of the provision has its own judicial history, which the drafters of Section 199A(6) legislated against. In Shilpa Mittal v. State of NCT of Delhi, a case that arose from a fatal accident caused by a driver below eighteen, the Supreme Court considered whether a juvenile accused of causing death by rash and negligent driving could be tried as an adult under the Juvenile Justice Act 2015, and held that an offence which prescribes no minimum sentence of seven years is not a heinous offence within that Act's definition, so the child could not be transferred to be tried as an adult for it. The judgment exposed the gap the amendment addresses from the other side: the juvenile justice framework, by design, treats the child offender with a reformative hand, and the deterrent weight of the system must therefore fall somewhere else if it is to fall at all. Section 199A places it on the adult who controlled the vehicle, while Section 199A(6) leaves the juvenile's own fines and any custodial consequence to be modified by the juvenile justice framework, preserving the Shilpa Mittal position inside the new liability. The provision is thus a deliberate allocation, and arguments about it that ignore the allocation, treating the guardian's liability as an accident of drafting, misread the section.

The rationale is deterrence aimed at the true decision maker. A fifteen year old on a motorcycle is rarely the person who chose to put the motorcycle within his reach; the keys hang in the house, and the adult who controls them controls the risk. Prosecuting the child alone had produced, in the Ministry's accident reporting, a persistent toll of deaths in accidents involving underage drivers, and the amendment answers by pricing the adult's permission. The objection is equally principled. Criminal liability in the Indian tradition is personal, and a deeming provision that begins by presuming consent and requires the accused adult to prove diligence reverses the ordinary burden in a prosecution that can end in imprisonment. Critics of vicarious criminal liability point out that the provision punishes ownership and guardianship rather than conduct, and that its severity, three years and a cancelled registration, falls hardest on households where a vehicle is the family's livelihood and a teenager's disobedience is a failure of supervision rather than of permission. Both readings are available on the text, and the provision's working will depend on how readily courts accept the diligence defence, which is the safety valve Parliament built into the deeming. What is not available is the reading that the section is a fine on parents of the ordinary kind: twenty five thousand rupees is the fine, but the imprisonment and the twelve month cancellation are the substance, and any summary that reports only the money understates the provision.

### What happens to the registration of a vehicle used in a juvenile's offence?

Section 199A directs that where an offence under the Act is committed by a juvenile, the registration of the motor vehicle used in the commission of the offence is cancelled for a period of twelve months, in addition to the consequences for the guardian or owner.

## The road itself as a defendant: contractor liability

Road safety law in India had, for decades, regulated only the users of roads. Section 198A, inserted by the amendment, regulates the makers of them. A contractor, consultant or concessionaire responsible for the design, construction or maintenance of a road who fails to comply with the standards prescribed for those purposes is liable to a fine which may extend to one lakh rupees, and the explanation to the section provides that contractor includes sub contractors and all persons responsible for any stage in the design, construction and maintenance of a stretch of road. The rule making counterparts inserted at the same time allocate the standard setting: Section 210C empowers the Central Government to make rules for design, construction and maintenance standards for national highways and for the factors a court may take into account under the section, and Section 210D gives the corresponding power to state governments for other roads. The provision belongs to the wider field mapped in [infrastructure laws in India](/2020/06/01/infrastructure-laws-india-guide/), but its insertion in a traffic statute is the novelty: the defective road is treated as a participant in the accident rather than as its scenery.

Two questions will decide the section's real content, and both are left open by the text. The first is the standard of liability: the section penalises a failure to comply with prescribed standards, which makes the prosecution a comparison between the road as built or maintained and the standard as prescribed, and places the prescribed standards, rather than any general duty of care, at the centre of the case. A contractor whose work meets the prescribed standard but whose road remains dangerous in fact has a defence the section respects, which is why the quality of the standards made under Sections 210C and 210D is the true substance of the reform. The second is the relationship between the fine and the victim's claim: the one lakh rupees is a penalty payable to the state, not compensation payable to the injured, and a family whose accident was caused by a defective road must still pursue its compensation through the claims process against the vehicle and its insurer, armed, if the prosecution has done its work, with an official finding that the road failed its standards. The section's gift to claimants is therefore evidentiary rather than monetary, and it is not a small one.

The innovation deserves its innovation's due and its limits' honesty. A pothole that swallows a wheel, a curve banked against its own geometry, a highway that ends its median without warning at speed: these are causes of death of the same order as a drunk driver, and until 2019 the persons paid to prevent them answered to contract terms and departmental enquiries, not to a transport statute. Making them answerable to a fine under the Act changes the conversation in every design review and every maintenance tender. The limit is that the section bites only where prescribed standards exist and are breached, and only to the extent of one lakh rupees, a sum that is trivial against the value of a highway contract. Its effect therefore depends on the standards prescribed under Sections 210C and 210D, on the willingness of authorities to prosecute their own contractors, and on courts developing the factors the rules invite them to weigh. As enacted it is a jurisdiction created and a liability sketched, and its history will be written by enforcement rather than by the text.

## Good Samaritans, recalls and a Board for road safety

Three insertions of the amendment share a single idea, that the system around the driver matters as much as the driver. Section 134A protects the Good Samaritan, defined as a person who, in good faith, voluntarily and without expectation of payment, renders emergency medical or non medical care or assistance at the scene of an accident or transports a victim to hospital. Such a person is not liable for any civil or criminal action for injury or death of the victim resulting from the Good Samaritan's negligence in acting or failing to act while rendering that care, and the rules under the Act protect the helper from being compelled to disclose identity or personal details and from being examined repeatedly by the authorities. The mischief addressed is notorious and well documented: bystanders at Indian accident scenes had learned, over decades, that helping meant police stations, court appearances and suspicion, and the rational bystander walked away while the victim bled inside the golden hour. The Law Commission's 201st Report, on emergency medical care to victims of accidents, had recorded that a large share of road deaths could be prevented by treatment within that hour and had identified the bystander's fear of legal entanglement as a central obstacle to it. The Supreme Court took the next step in SaveLIFE Foundation v. Union of India in 2016, approving a set of Good Samaritan guidelines and directing that they operate as law until legislation filled the field: the helper was not to be detained for want of particulars, not to be examined repeatedly, and not to be made a witness against his will. The amendment answers the same deterrent with immunity drafted at the level of the helper's fear, and Section 134A is therefore best read as the statutory settlement of a reform the Court had already improvised, converting directions that bound through a judgment into a section that binds through the Act.

The recall power in Section 110A, described in the direct answer above, converts vehicle defect from a matter of manufacturers' voluntary codes into a statutory direction, and couples it with consequences for the testing agency that certified the vehicle. The National Road Safety Board, created by Section 215B, completes the institutional group: a Board consisting of a Chairman, representatives of state governments and other members appointed by the Central Government, charged with advising the Central and state governments on every aspect of road safety and traffic management, from the standards of design, construction and maintenance of vehicles and safety equipment, through registration and licensing, to the formulation of standards for road safety, road infrastructure and traffic control, the safety of vulnerable road users and the promotion of new vehicle technology. Sections 215A to 215D were inserted with effect from 1 September 2019, yet the Board itself was constituted only by notification dated 3 September 2021, with its rules notified the same day, a two year gap between the section and the institution that repeats the amendment's larger pattern of text outrunning machinery. The Board is advisory, and its influence will be the influence of a standing expert voice with state governments inside the room; whether that voice disciplines the standards on which Section 198A depends is one of the open questions of the amendment's second decade.

The section's limits are the limits of its form. Immunity protects the helper against liability for the rescue, but the harassments the helper feared were administrative as much as legal: the demand for a name and address at the hospital, the summons to the police station, the repeated calls to testify. Those are governed by the rules made under the Act, which carry forward the protections the Supreme Court's guidelines had articulated, including that a Good Samaritan is not to be compelled to disclose personal particulars and is not to be detained or examined unnecessarily. The amendment's contribution is to give that settlement a statutory floor beneath the rules, so that the protection no longer depends on the continuing force of a judgment's directions.

## Aggregators enter the Act

The 1988 Act licensed agents and canvassers who sold transport services and said nothing about the platforms that, by 2019, dispatched a large share of urban India's hired travel. The amendment named the platform. It inserted a definition of aggregator as a digital intermediary or market place for a passenger to connect with a driver for the purpose of transportation, and it amended Section 93, the licensing provision for agents and canvassers, to require an aggregator to obtain a licence from the state government, providing that in issuing the licence the state government may follow such guidelines as the Central Government issues. The aggregator is also made answerable within the penalty chapter: Section 193, as amended, prescribes a fine of not less than twenty five thousand rupees and extending to one lakh rupees for contravention of the licence conditions applicable to agents, canvassers and aggregators. The design is the amendment's federal pattern in miniature, central guidelines and state licence, and it sits at the boundary with the information technology framework traced in [the technology law guide](/2019/03/01/technology-law-india-complete-guide/), because the regulated intermediary is a software platform and its obligations under the two regimes run side by side.

The Central Government issued the Motor Vehicle Aggregator Guidelines 2020 on 27 November 2020, under Section 93 as amended, as the framework state governments are to follow in licensing. The Guidelines treat the state licence as a mandatory precondition of operation for aggregators with a registered office in India, and they range across the aggregator's eligibility and compliance with the Information Technology Act 2000, the integration and conduct of drivers and vehicles, the functioning of the app, the manner of fare regulation including the sharing of fare between driver and platform, driver welfare, safety obligations toward riders, and pooling and ride sharing. On the two questions riders and drivers ask about most, the Guidelines take positions: the fare is to be divided so that the driver receives not less than eighty per cent of the amount charged to the rider, and dynamic surge pricing is capped at one and a half times the base fare, with a floor below which fares may not be discounted beyond the proportion the Guidelines permit. These are the terms on which the state licence is to be framed, and their presence in the central text, rather than in ninety separate state bargains, is the strongest centralising feature of the aggregator design. The guidelines are guidance and not rules: the legal obligation to hold a licence comes from the Act, the content of the licence from the state that issues it, and litigation in the Bombay High Court, in Savino R. Crasto v. Union of India, tested what aggregators must do in a state whose own rules remain in draft, with the Court requiring the platforms to operate under licence and to conform to the central framework in the interim. The verification position a careful reader should hold is that the Guidelines of 2020 are the operative central text, that licensing remains a state act performed at different speeds in different states, and that the driver's entitlements on any given platform depend on the licence conditions of the state of operation rather than on the Guidelines alone.

## What the amendment did not finish

An amendment trail that records only enactments misleads, because this amendment's defining administrative feature is the distance between its text and its operation. The pattern recurs at every level. The compensation chapters waited thirty one months for commencement while rules and schemes were designed. The National Road Safety Board waited two years for constitution after its section took effect. The golden hour scheme waited from April 2022 until judicial intervention produced first a deadline and then a notified scheme in 2025, with pilot operation preceding wider rollout. The aggregator licence, enacted in 2019 and framed by guidelines in 2020, continued to be constructed state by state, with at least one High Court required to hold the system together in a major state whose rules remained in draft. The standards on which the road contractor's liability under Section 198A depends are prescribed road category by road category and government by government, so the section's coverage map is a patchwork that the text alone does not reveal.

None of this is an argument that the amendment failed. It is an argument about what kind of instrument a large Indian amending Act is. Parliament legislates the duties, the figures and the institutions, and then a second, quieter legislative process of notifications, rules, schemes and state action decides when each piece begins to bind and how hard it bites. The 2019 Act is unusually honest about this in its own structure, since Section 1 wrote staged commencement into the statute and the first notification used the power within weeks. But the honesty has a cost for the citizen reading the Act: the book tells the reader what the law will be, and only the Gazette, consulted provision by provision, tells the reader what the law is on a given date. Every figure in this article has therefore been tied to its commencement, and every institution to the date it actually appeared, because for this amendment the dates are not apparatus. They are the difference between a right and a promise.

The unfinished business also frames the amendment's likely future. The escalator of Section 199B gives the executive a standing power to keep the penalty schedule's real value from decaying, if governments choose to use it year by year. The limitation jurisprudence awaits a final word from the Supreme Court in the pending challenge, which will settle whether six months is a hard gate, a condonable period or an unconstitutional one, and with it the fate of claims arising since April 2022. The Accident Fund, constituted and rule bound, will be tested by the volume of hit and run and uninsured claims laid on it, and its accounts will show whether the compulsory cover for all road users that Section 164B announces is a funded reality or a formula. The Board, once working, either disciplines the standards system or joins the list of advisory bodies whose advice is received with respect and filed without effect. The trail does not end at assent, or at commencement, or at the first scheme; for an amendment of this construction it ends, if it ends, in the notifications and accounts of the decade after enactment.

## The amendment worked through: three situations

Statutes of this kind are best tested against the situations they were written for, and three situations carry most of the amendment's weight.

The first is the overloaded truck on a national highway. Before the amendment, the operator's arithmetic was simple and corrupting: the fine of two thousand rupees plus one thousand for every extra tonne was a predictable cost, lower than the freight earned by the excess load, and it was priced into the rate. After the amendment the arithmetic changes at three points at once. The fine is twenty thousand rupees plus two thousand for every additional tonne, a figure that can exceed the freight for the whole journey. Detection no longer depends on a traffic constable's chance presence, because the state may designate agencies to detect and penalise overloaded vehicles and electronic weighing and camera systems feed the same record. And the compounding figure the operator will actually be offered is the one his state has notified, which is why the same load can be an expensive lesson in one state and a ruinous one in another. The deterrent the amendment promises is real exactly to the extent that the state of the offence has declined to compound the offence back down toward the old arithmetic.

The second is the family scooter taken out by a sixteen year old. The accident he causes injures a pedestrian. Before the amendment, the legal consequences ran against the child through the juvenile process and against the owner through the ordinary provisions on permitting an unlicensed driver, and the compensation claim ran against the vehicle's insurer if the vehicle was insured. After the amendment, Section 199A deems the guardian or the owner guilty of the contravention, exposes that adult to imprisonment of up to three years and a fine of twenty five thousand rupees in addition to the penalty for the underlying offence, cancels the scooter's registration for twelve months, and postpones the young person's eligibility for any licence until the age of twenty five, unless the adult proves the taking was without knowledge or that all due diligence was exercised to prevent it, or unless the young person held a licence for the vehicle he was operating. The compensation claim is untouched by the deeming and proceeds against the insurer in the ordinary way, but the family's own vehicle is off the road for a year and the adult's criminal exposure is the provision's intended pressure on the household's habits. The situation is the one parents ask about, and the answer the amendment gives is deliberately uncomfortable: the law treats the keys as the offence.

The third is the hit and run on an unlit district road. The vehicle is never traced. Before the substitution of 2022, the family's statutory route was the solatium scheme at twenty five thousand rupees for the death. After it, the route is Section 161 at two lakh rupees, claimed through the scheme's enquiry officer rather than through litigation, supported by the hit and run compensation account of the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund, with the police report of the accident starting a record that the claims machinery can use even though there is no owner to serve. If the victim survives the journey to hospital with grievous hurt, the figure is fifty thousand rupees, and the golden hour scheme is designed to have paid for the treatment itself before the compensation question is reached. The same family, had the vehicle been traced and insured, would elect between the fixed five lakh rupees of Section 164 and a fault based claim for a larger sum, and would have six months from the accident to set the claim in motion, the deadline and the generosity arriving, as they do throughout this amendment, in the same envelope.

Read together, the three situations state the amendment's whole theory in miniature. In the first, the state reprices a commercial calculation and builds machinery to detect it. In the second, the state conscripts the family's own authority over its vehicles, because the offender the law most needs to reach is too young for the law's ordinary instruments. In the third, the state abandons deterrence altogether and acts as insurer, because there is no offender to deter. Deterrence, conscription and insurance are three different theories of what a road statute is for, and the 2019 amendment enacts all three at once, which is why single theory accounts of it, whether celebratory or hostile, consistently miss two thirds of what it did.

## For the four readers of this amendment

The brief for this article named four readers, and the trail sorts differently for each. For the claimant family, the operative provisions are two: the six month application period of Section 166(3), running from the date of the accident and subject to the authorities and the interim Supreme Court direction described in this article, and the hit and run compensation of Section 161 at two lakh rupees for a death, claimed through the scheme where the vehicle is untraced. Both are urgent in the plain sense that delay destroys the first and complicates the second, and a family reading this article after an accident should treat the calendar as the first instruction the amendment gives it.

For the driver and the vehicle owner, the operative provisions are the penalty schedule at its 2019 figures, the compounding notification of the state in which the vehicle is driven, which fixes the sum actually demanded to settle most offences, and Section 199A, which makes the household's control of its vehicles a matter of criminal liability where a juvenile is concerned. The owner's exposure is no longer exhausted by lending the vehicle; it extends to failing to prevent the lending, and the diligence defence is the only route out.

For the aggregator and the driver partner, the operative provisions are the definition, the state licence under Section 93 framed on the Motor Vehicle Aggregator Guidelines 2020, the eighty per cent floor for the driver's share of the fare and the surge ceiling the Guidelines prescribe, and the Section 193 penalty band of twenty five thousand to one lakh rupees for breach of licence conditions. The partner's entitlements run through the licence of the state of operation, and the platform's accountability runs through the same instrument.

For the researcher, the amendment is a controlled experiment in Indian federalism and in legislative attention. The penalty schedule shows the Concurrent List working through compounding rather than through repugnancy, the compensation chapters show a social insurance scheme constructed inside a regulatory statute, and the limitation provision shows a consequential change travelling without scrutiny while a symbolic one consumed the scrutiny available. The data for the next decade of that experiment will be generated by the systems the amendment built: the electronic challan record, the accident reporting chain, and the accounts of the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund.

The licensing design also settles, against the platforms, the threshold question of what an aggregator is in law. The platforms' preferred characterisation, across jurisdictions, is that of a technology company that supplies software to independent transport providers and is therefore outside transport licensing altogether. The amendment rejects the characterisation by defining the aggregator inside a transport statute and licensing it under the transport statute's own agent provision, treating the digital intermediary as a participant in the carriage market rather than as a bystander with an app. The practical consequence is that the conditions of market entry, the standards of driver onboarding and the discipline of fares are set by the licensing state, and the Information Technology framework governs the platform's conduct as an intermediary in parallel rather than instead. For the driver partner the licence is double edged in the same way: it creates the welfare and fare share entitlements the Guidelines prescribe, and it makes the partner's access to work depend on a platform whose own right to operate is a revocable state permission.

## Why the fines were always going to be the story

The allocation of attention that this article has criticised was not an accident of journalism; it was built into the amendment's structure, and a trail is incomplete without recording why. A fine is the rare legal quantity that every reader can price against his own wallet in a second, compare with the figure he remembers, and repeat at a traffic signal. The amendment supplied thousands of such quantities at once, several of them ten times their predecessors, and invited the single graphic that every newsroom in the country duly published: the old fine beside the new fine, in a table. The limitation period, by contrast, has no wallet to price itself against. Its victims do not exist at the moment of enactment; they come into existence six months after individual accidents, one family at a time, in districts where no camera is present, and their loss is procedural and therefore difficult to narrate. A change that harms identifiable present readers will always defeat, in public argument, a change that harms statistical future ones, and a legislature debating in public inherits that bias in full.

The states' intervention completed the story's shape. Once Gujarat compounded the fines down, the amendment had a second act with named governments on opposing sides, a plot that could be followed week by week through notifications, and the compensation chapters had no second act at all, because no actor in the system gained by publicising a deadline that would operate against claimants, and the claimants themselves were not yet organised as an interest. The lesson the trail offers for the reading of future amending Acts is mechanical and worth stating flatly: in a large amendment, rank the provisions by the date their consequences first fall due and by the organisation of the people they fall on, and expect public scrutiny to have covered them in almost exactly the inverse order.

## An assessment: the deadline, not the fine

A trail is judged by where it ends. The amendment's public destination was deterrence: a penalty schedule meant to make the Indian driver afraid of the rules for the first time in a generation. Its private destination, where it will actually be felt across decades, is the claims chapter. The fines were negotiated down by the states within weeks, are discounted at the roadside by design, and will be eroded by inflation until the Section 199B escalator or another Parliament restores them. The compensation settlement, by contrast, is structural and self executing: five lakh rupees without proof of fault, two lakh rupees where the vehicle is never traced, a fund standing behind the untraced and the uninsured, cashless treatment in the first hour, and a six month gate in front of all of it. The same Act that made the Indian state louder at the roadside made it, in the claims chapter, something it had never quite been: an insurer of last resort for everyone on the road, with a filing deadline.

The verdict this article defends is the brief's namable claim, earned over the length of the trail. The most important change in the Motor Vehicles Amendment Act 2019 was a deadline, not a penalty. It matters more because it is irreversible in the individual case: a fine can be paid late, compounded, even forgiven by notification, but a claim extinguished by six months of grief does not return, and the interim protection the Supreme Court has ordered exists precisely because the extinguishment, multiplied across thousands of families, looked indefensible while the provision's validity is untested. It matters more, second, because it reverses a considered legislative judgment of 1994 that was itself a response to evidence about who claimants are. And it matters most because it was invisible: the amendment's democratic scrutiny, which functioned vigorously for the fines, did not function for the deadline at all. The series to which this article belongs has found that pattern in statute after statute, the argued provision and the operative provision standing side by side in the same text, and the 2019 amendment is its clearest modern instance. The reader who remembers one sentence should remember the practical one: after an accident, the calendar is the first opponent, and it starts running on the day of the accident itself.

There is a final structural observation the trail supports. The 1988 Act was itself a consolidation meant to modernise a 1939 statute, and by 2019 it had acquired, through this amendment, a compensation code, a social insurance fund, a digital enforcement architecture and a market licensing regime for aggregators, none of which its 1988 scheme contemplated. Each future reform faces a choice the 2019 Parliament made by default: to amend the heavy statute again, adding a fifth decade of layers, or to replace it, reopening every settled position the layers contain. The 2019 experience suggests the choice will keep being made by default, because amendment is cheaper in legislative attention than replacement, and that the 1988 Act's longevity is therefore less a tribute to its drafting than a consequence of the price of starting again.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which provisions did the Motor Vehicles Amendment Act 2019 rewrite?

The amending Act, Act 32 of 2019, worked across the whole parent statute through ninety two sections. It repriced the penalty chapter, recast licensing and registration provisions, inserted Section 136A on electronic monitoring and enforcement, inserted Sections 134A, 198A, 199A and 199B on Good Samaritans, road contractors and juvenile offences, created the recall power in Section 110A and the National Road Safety Board in Section 215B, omitted Chapter X of the parent Act and substituted Chapter XI on third party insurance, sections 145 to 164, together with the claims amendments in Sections 165 to 173. Its provisions commenced in stages, the penalty group on 1 September 2019 and the compensation group on 1 April 2022.

### Q: Why did motor vehicle penalties rise so sharply?

The 1988 figures had been fixed in the money of another era. The general penalty stood at one hundred rupees and the fine for driving without a licence at five hundred rupees, sums that had lost their deterrent value many times over through inflation, so that breaking the rule cost less than obeying it cost in time. The Union government argued in Parliament that penalties which deter nobody license the conduct behind India's road death toll, which the Minister placed at more than one and a half lakh deaths a year. The amendment therefore repriced the schedule at roughly five to ten times the old figures, added an annual revision mechanism in Section 199B, and graduated the serious offences by vehicle class and by repetition.

### Q: Can a state reduce the increased motor vehicle penalties?

A state cannot amend the fines Parliament enacted, and none has purported to. What a state controls is the compounding of offences under Section 200, under which specified offences may be settled, before or after prosecution begins, for such amount as the appropriate government notifies. Gujarat notified reduced compounding amounts within weeks of the September 2019 commencement, other states notified reductions of their own or adopted the schedule as enacted, and some states delayed applying parts of the new regime while reviewing it. The result is that the fine a court may impose is the central figure everywhere, while the sum demanded to compound the same offence at the roadside is a state figure and may be lower.

### Q: What compensation applies in a hit and run case?

Under Section 161, where a death or grievous hurt results from a hit and run motor accident, meaning an accident whose vehicle's identity cannot be ascertained in spite of reasonable efforts, compensation is payable under a scheme framed by the Central Government: two lakh rupees where the accident caused death, and fifty thousand rupees where it caused grievous hurt, or such higher amount as is prescribed. The superseded 1988 figures were twenty five thousand rupees and twelve thousand five hundred rupees. The claim runs through the scheme's enquiry machinery rather than against an identified insurer, the money is supported by the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund, and any sum received is adjusted against a later award for the same accident.

### Q: What time limit now applies to a motor accident claim?

Section 166(3) of the parent Act, inserted by the 2019 substitution and in force from 1 April 2022, provides that no application for compensation shall be entertained unless it is made within six months of the occurrence of the accident. It restored a limitation that the 1994 amendment had deleted, leaving claims without any time bar for twenty five years. The High Courts have held that the bar applies to accidents from 1 April 2022 and not to rights accrued earlier, the Kerala High Court has held late petitions cannot simply be dismissed without trying the limitation question, and the Supreme Court has directed that no petition be dismissed as time barred while the provision's validity is under challenge.

### Q: What protection do Good Samaritans get?

Section 134A protects a person who, in good faith, voluntarily and without expectation of payment or reward, renders emergency medical or non medical care at the scene of an accident or transports the victim to hospital. The Good Samaritan is not liable for any civil or criminal action for injury to or death of the victim resulting from negligence in acting or failing to act while giving that help. The rules made under the Act add procedural protection, shielding the helper from being compelled to disclose identity or personal details and from repeated examination by police or in court, the deterrents that had taught bystanders to leave victims unattended at the roadside.

### Q: Who is liable for a faulty road?

Section 198A makes a contractor, consultant or concessionaire responsible for the design, construction or maintenance of a road liable to a fine which may extend to one lakh rupees where that person fails to comply with the standards prescribed for road design, construction or maintenance. The section's explanation extends contractor to sub contractors and to every person responsible for any stage of the design, construction or maintenance of the stretch of road. The standards themselves are prescribed by the Central Government for national highways under Section 210C and by state governments for other roads under Section 210D, so the liability operates where prescribed standards exist and can be shown to have been breached.

### Q: How are ride hailing aggregators regulated?

The amendment defined an aggregator as a digital intermediary or market place for a passenger to connect with a driver for the purpose of transportation, and amended Section 93 to require an aggregator to hold a licence from the state government. In issuing the licence the state may follow guidelines issued by the Central Government, which arrived as the Motor Vehicle Aggregator Guidelines 2020 on 27 November 2020, covering the platform's eligibility, driver and vehicle integration, the app, fare regulation and the division of fare, driver welfare and rider safety. A platform that contravenes its licence conditions faces the amended Section 193 penalty of twenty five thousand to one lakh rupees.

### Q: From when did the enhanced penalties of the 2019 amendment take effect?

The penalty provisions formed part of the first tranche of sixty three clauses that the Central Government brought into force on 1 September 2019, under the commencement power in Section 1 of the amending Act, which allowed different dates for different provisions. That date governs the 2019 penalty figures, the juvenile and contractor provisions, electronic enforcement and the Good Samaritan protection. The compensation provisions travelled separately: Sections 50 to 57 of the amending Act, omitting Chapter X and substituting the insurance and claims chapters, commenced only on 1 April 2022 by a notification dated 25 February 2022, so accidents before that date are governed by the compensation law as it previously stood.

### Q: What is the penalty for drunken driving under the amended Act?

Section 185 prescribes, for a first offence of driving or attempting to drive with alcohol in the blood exceeding thirty milligrams per hundred millilitres, or under the influence of a drug to an extent that renders the person incapable of exercising proper control, imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months, or a fine of ten thousand rupees, or both. For a second or subsequent offence the ceiling rises to imprisonment for two years, or a fine of fifteen thousand rupees, or both. The corresponding 1988 fine of two thousand rupees is superseded. These are the 2019 figures a court may impose; compounding, where the state notifies it, is a separate route.

### Q: What fine applies to driving without a licence after the 2019 amendment?

Section 181 prescribes a fine of five thousand rupees, with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three months available as an alternative or addition, for driving a motor vehicle in a public place without an effective licence authorising the class of vehicle driven, or in breach of the age requirements of the Act. The superseded 1988 figure was five hundred rupees, so the amendment multiplied the money penalty by ten. The related offence of permitting an unauthorised person to drive one's vehicle, under Section 180, carries the same five thousand rupees figure, and driving while disqualified under Section 182 carries ten thousand rupees.

### Q: What is the penalty for overloading a goods vehicle?

Section 194 prescribes a fine of twenty thousand rupees, together with an additional fine of two thousand rupees for every tonne of excess load beyond the permitted laden weight, and the vehicle is not permitted to proceed until the excess load is removed or transferred as the section directs. The superseded figures were two thousand rupees plus one thousand rupees for every additional tonne, so the base fine rose tenfold. The amendment also strengthened detection by empowering state governments to designate persons or agencies as enforcement agencies for overloaded vehicles under Section 114, and overloading of passengers is dealt with separately under Section 194A at one thousand rupees for every extra passenger.

### Q: What happens when an enforcement officer commits an offence under the Act?

Section 210B provides that any authority empowered to enforce the provisions of the Act which itself commits an offence under the Act is liable for twice the penalty corresponding to that offence. The provision was new in 2019 and has no counterpart in the 1988 text. Its logic is reciprocity: a schedule of penalties raised expressly to restore deterrence would lose its authority if the officials collecting it stood outside it, so the amendment prices an enforcing authority's own offence at double the figure the same conduct would cost an ordinary road user. The provision speaks to authorities empowered to enforce, and its practical force depends on prosecutions actually being brought.

### Q: Can the fines in the Act be increased every year?

Yes, within a statutory cap. Section 199B, inserted by the 2019 amendment, provides that the fines provided in the Act shall be increased on the first day of April every year by such amount, not exceeding ten per cent of the existing fines, as the Central Government may notify. The mechanism is an escalator with a ceiling rather than an automatic indexation: no increase takes effect in a year in which the Central Government issues no notification, and no notification may raise a fine by more than ten per cent at a time. The design answers the decay that destroyed the 1988 schedule, whose figures waited thirty years for a Parliament to reprice them.

### Q: What compensation does Section 164 provide without proof of negligence?

Section 164 fixes compensation payable on the principle of no fault: where death results from an accident arising out of the use of a motor vehicle, the owner of the vehicle or the authorised insurer, as the case may be, is liable to pay five lakh rupees, and where grievous hurt results, two lakh fifty thousand rupees, without the claimant having to plead or prove any wrongful act, neglect or default of the owner, the driver or any other person. The payment is an election, because a claim under the fault based provisions does not survive acceptance of the Section 164 compensation for the same accident, and the section replaced the far smaller no fault sums of the omitted Chapter X from 1 April 2022.

### Q: What is the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund meant to pay for?

Section 164B constitutes the Fund for the purpose of providing compulsory insurance cover to all road users in the territory of India, and directs its use to four heads: the treatment of persons injured in road accidents under the cashless golden hour scheme framed under Section 162; compensation to the representatives of a person who died in a hit and run motor accident under the Section 161 scheme; compensation to a person grievously hurt in such an accident; and compensation to such other persons as the Central Government prescribes. The Fund was constituted through the Central Motor Vehicles (Motor Vehicle Accident Fund) Rules, 2022, and money paid from it for treatment is deducted from any later insurance recovery for the same claim.

### Q: What does Section 162 provide for cashless treatment during the golden hour?

Section 162 obliges general insurance companies to provide for the treatment of road accident victims, including during the golden hour, which the substituted chapter defines as the one hour following a traumatic injury during which prompt medical treatment has the highest likelihood of preventing death. The Central Government is required to make a scheme for cashless treatment of victims during that hour, and the scheme may create a fund for the purpose, a role the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund fills. The duty was slow to mature: the provision commenced on 1 April 2022, and in S. Rajaseekaran v. Union of India the Supreme Court held the scheme to be a statutory obligation protecting the right to life and directed its finalisation by 14 March 2025.

### Q: What penalty applies to an aggregator that violates its licence conditions?

Section 193, as amended, prescribes for a contravention of the licensing conditions governing agents, canvassers and aggregators a fine which shall not be less than twenty five thousand rupees but which may extend to one lakh rupees. The penalty sits alongside the licensing requirement itself: an aggregator must hold a licence from the state government under Section 93, issued in accordance with the framework of the Motor Vehicle Aggregator Guidelines 2020, and the fine is the sanction the Act attaches to operating outside the conditions of that licence. Because licensing is a state function, the conditions whose breach attracts the fine are those of the licence granted by the state of operation.

### Q: What does Section 136A require states to do about electronic enforcement?

Section 136A requires the state government to ensure electronic monitoring and enforcement of road safety on national highways, state highways, roads and in any urban city within the state up to the population limits prescribed by the Central Government. The monitoring and enforcement is carried out by the means prescribed centrally, which in practice covers camera based detection of speed and signal violations, automatic number plate recognition and the electronic challan issued to the registered owner. The Central Government's rule making under Section 137 extends to the limits of urban cities for the section and to the manner of electronic monitoring and enforcement, so the obligation, the technology and the notice are all framed within the Act rather than left to practice.

### Q: What changed in third party insurance under the substituted Chapter XI?

The substituted Chapter XI, sections 145 to 164 in force from 1 April 2022, restated the compulsory insurance of vehicles against third party risks with modernised definitions: an authorised insurer is tied to authorisation and registration with the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, grievous hurt takes its meaning from Section 320 of the Indian Penal Code, a hit and run motor accident is defined by the impossibility of identifying the vehicle despite reasonable efforts, and third party is expressed to include the Government. The chapter retained the insurer's duty to satisfy awards and the limits on the defences an insurer may raise, and it absorbed the new no fault section, the hit and run scheme and the Accident Fund, converting the insurance chapter into the home of both private cover and the social insurance layer.
