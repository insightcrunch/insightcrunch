The claim that revived the planning question is a claim about arithmetic. It runs as follows: whatever prevented central planning from working in the twentieth century, the obstacle was the sheer size of the calculation, and that obstacle has now been removed by the growth of computational capacity, so an economy could in principle be allocated by a plan as a market economy is allocated by prices. The claim deserves a hearing, and part of it deserves concession. But it rests on a conflation that this article exists to separate. Computation and information are two different problems. Computation is what a machine does to data it has been given. Information is what an allocation system must somehow obtain before any computation can begin, and the obtaining is done by people and institutions whose behaviour the system itself shapes. The first problem has changed beyond recognition across three decades. The second has not obviously changed at all, and whether it has changed is a question about incentives and about the formation of human wants, not about processing power. A reader who keeps that separation in view from the first page will find that the familiar question, can planning beat the market, dissolves into a set of narrower questions with different answers, and that the narrower questions are the ones worth arguing about.

![A planning office wall chart with the constraints table, index cards for four allocation domains and a column of unanswered returns, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-56.webp)

The contemporary planning question, stated precisely, asks whether, given the computational capacity and information-gathering reach that now exist, an economy could allocate resources without markets at least as well as markets allocate them, and in which domains that parity or superiority might hold. Every clause of that formulation does work. The comparison is against markets as they actually perform, not against a textbook construction in which prices adjust instantly and costlessly. The standard is at least as well, which places the burden where it belongs, on the challenger. And the closing clause, in which domains, is not a qualification added for caution. It is, this article will argue, where the answer lives. The strongest case for planning turns out to be a case about particular kinds of allocation: needs that can be known in advance, horizons longer than any price signal reaches, purposes that a society states politically rather than discovers through purchase. The weakest case is the general one, planning as a replacement for the price system across the whole field of consumer goods, where wants are numerous, shifting, and partly created by the act of choosing itself. Between those poles sits a shelf of serious models, neither comprehensive plans nor unmodified markets, which the public argument almost never reaches because it is conducted between two slogans. This article works through the argument in the order the logic requires: the computational claim first, then the constraints that survive it, then the models, then the evidence, and only at the end the verdict, which is a verdict about domains.

## The question restated: what central planning is now being asked to do

Begin by noticing how the question has moved. The planning question of the early twentieth century was whether a socialist economy was possible at all: whether allocation without private ownership of the means of production and without market prices for them could be anything other than chaos. That question was argued in the calculation debate, and this article does not re-argue it, because its terms have been superseded by both sides. The historical exchange, its two distinct objections, and the proposed solution that answered one of them are treated in [the socialist calculation debate](/2019/08/15/socialist-calculation-debate/), and the reader who wants the foundations should start there. What matters here is the residue. When the dust of that debate settled, the surviving objection was not that the sums were too many. It was that the knowledge an allocator needs is dispersed among millions of people, much of it impossible to articulate, and that the price system is less a calculating device than a device for bringing that knowledge into play without anyone having to surrender it to a centre.

The new question arises because the ground shifted under one part of that settlement. If the objection had really been about the volume of arithmetic, then the arrival of very fast computation would reopen everything. Advocates of planning noticed this and pressed the point: the arithmetic, they argue, was the binding constraint all along, the constraint is gone, and the case against planning collapses into habit and ideology. Sceptics replied, often too quickly, that the old debate had settled the matter permanently. Both responses assume that the debate was about one thing. It was about at least three: whether the calculation could be performed, whether the data for it could be obtained in accurate form, and whether the wants the calculation serves can be known in advance of the choosing that expresses them. Only the first of those is a computational question. The other two are the subject of the middle of this article, and they are stated as constraints, in a table, so that each proposed model can be graded against them rather than against a mood.

### What changed between the old objection and the new claim?

One element changed and two did not. The arithmetic of allocation, once plausibly described as beyond any conceivable apparatus, became tractable as methods and machines improved. The reporting problem, enterprises supplying the figures on which their own targets depend, persisted untouched. So did the problem of wants that exist only once a chooser confronts actual alternatives.

A further preliminary concerns the standard of success. Planning does not have to be perfect to beat the market in a domain; it has to be better than the market's actual performance there, and markets fail in documented ways: they underprovide goods whose benefits spill beyond the buyer, they discount the distant future at rates few would defend as social judgements, they leave needs unmet where need carries no purchasing power, and they generate the instability of the investment cycle. Any honest grading must hold both systems to the same standard, performance against stated social purposes, and must refuse the rhetorical trick, common on both sides, of comparing one system's theory with the other system's record. That discipline, comparison with domain and criterion always specified, is the comparison basis this article applies throughout, and departures from it are the standard error in the literature this article draws on.

## Two problems wearing one name: computation and information

The distinction can be made concrete by imagining the planning bureau at its most capable. Give it unlimited processing power, perfect algorithms, instantaneous communication with every enterprise in the economy. Now ask what arrives at its terminals. From each enterprise, a report: capacities, stocks, technical coefficients, the inputs each production process requires and the outputs it yields. From the consuming population, somehow, a statement of wants: which goods, in which quantities, in which places, at which times. The bureau's task is then genuinely computational: find the allocation that satisfies the stated wants within the reported possibilities, or report that no such allocation exists and find the nearest approach. That task, as the next sections show, is solvable. But everything in the previous sentences that matters hides inside two words: reported and stated. The reports come from parties whose futures depend on what the bureau does with them. The wants are stated by a procedure that has to be invented, because wants in a market economy are never stated at all; they are enacted, one purchase at a time, and revised in the act.

This is why the computational victory, when it came, changed so much less than its champions announced. The objection it defeated was real but it was the weakest of the three, and in the classic debate it had already been conceded in principle by the critics' own side: the formal problem of solving the equations was granted to be solvable by Enrico Barone as early as 1908, before the debate proper began, and the socialist respondents of the 1930s built their entire answer on trial and error adjustment rather than on solving anything. The knowledge objection, raised in its mature form by Friedrich Hayek in 1945, was aimed at a different joint entirely. It held that the data of the problem do not exist in integrated form anywhere, that they are generated by the process of exchange itself, and that a centre which abolished the process would abolish its own source of knowledge. Nothing about faster arithmetic touches that claim. What touches it, if anything does, is a mechanism for generating the data without the process, and the search for such a mechanism is where the serious models live.

### What does a price produce besides an allocation?

A price produces a public signal that strangers can act on without knowing one another's reasons, and it produces a record against which the signal's accuracy is tested by profit and loss. It also produces the information itself: the bids and refusals that reveal what resources are worth in alternative uses exist only because the exchange was attempted.

Hold the distinction firmly, because the rest of the article hangs on it, and because nearly every popular treatment blurs it at exactly the point where the argument gets hard. When an advocate says that the planning problem has been solved, the claim is almost always about computation: the equations can be solved, the iterations converge, the hardware exists. When a sceptic says that the planning problem is insoluble, the assertion is almost always about information: the data cannot be gathered truthfully, the wants cannot be known in advance, the local knowledge cannot travel. Both can be right simultaneously, about different problems that share a name. The constraints table in this article exists to prevent that equivocation from doing silent work. It separates the constraints, asks of each one whether it binds, whether technical change has loosened it, what would relax it, and which of the serious models addresses it. Graded that way, the question stops being a contest of temperaments and becomes an audit.

## The computational argument in its strongest form

The computational argument, in the form that matters, comes from the computer scientist Paul Cockshott and the economist Allin Cottrell, whose Towards a New Socialism of 1993 remains its fullest statement, supported by papers through the following decade. Their claim is precise and should be stated precisely. An economy's structure can be represented as a vast system of interlocking requirements: each product is made from other products and from labour, so the total requirement for any final basket of goods can be computed by working backwards through the web of inputs. The system is enormous, on the order of millions of distinct products, each with its own row in the table of requirements. A naive method of solution, inverting the whole system at once, would indeed be beyond reach. But the system has a property that changes everything: it is sparse. Almost every entry in the table is zero, because almost no product is a direct input into almost any other. Steel enters the making of a car; yoghurt does not. An iterative method, which starts from a guess and refines it pass by pass, works only with the entries that are not zero, and such methods converge: each pass brings the solution closer, and a tolerable accuracy arrives after a manageable number of passes. The arithmetic that the early critics imagined as an unclimbable mountain turns out, on inspection of its actual structure, to be a long but walkable road.

### Is computation still the obstacle to planning?

no. The matrices involved are sparse and iterative methods converge, so the calculation is tractable at economy scale. The binding constraints are generating accurate data from parties whose targets depend on it, and revealing preferences that are partly formed through choice itself.

The concession should be made without throat-clearing, because it is owed. The tractability claim is substantially correct. It is not a trick of exposition and it does not depend on speculative hardware; it depends on the structure of the object being computed, which is a fact about economies rather than about machines. Input-output tables compiled by statistical offices for market economies display exactly the sparsity the argument requires, since they are built from observed production recipes, and the recipes of real processes each draw on a short list of ingredients. Nor is iteration an exotic device: it is the ordinary method by which large sparse systems are solved wherever they arise, in engineering simulation and in the logistics of large organisations, and its convergence for systems of this kind is a mathematical property, not a hope. A critic who continues to rest the case against planning on the impossibility of the calculation is defending a position that its own side's best authorities surrendered in principle a century ago, and the surrender costs the sceptical case nothing, because the case was never really there. Where the case actually rests is the subject of the sections that follow.

Two clarifications keep the concession honest. First, tractability is a claim about a calculation performed on given data. It says that if the bureau possessed the true table of requirements and a true statement of final demands, the allocation could be computed. It says nothing about how the bureau comes to possess either, and its authors, to their credit, do not pretend otherwise: their book devotes sustained attention to the gathering of information and to the design of incentives for honest reporting, which is an implicit acknowledgement that the hard problems begin where the arithmetic ends. Second, the calculation must be repeated as conditions change, and the economy does not hold still while it is being computed. Harvests fail, machines break, tastes drift, a supplier's furnace goes down for repair. A market handles such changes continuously and locally, at the cost of never solving the whole problem at all. A plan must re-solve, and the iteration that converges so reassuringly on a static table must be run again on a moving one. Even here the computational point largely survives, since re-solving a sparse system is the same kind of task as solving it. What does not survive is any suggestion that solving was ever the whole of the matter.

## Why the table of an economy is mostly empty

Sparsity deserves a section of its own, because it is the load-bearing fact of the computational argument and because understanding it clarifies what kind of object an economy is. A product's recipe, the list of things directly consumed in making it, is short for reasons rooted in the division of labour. Production is organised as a tree of specialisations: the bakery buys flour, yeast, energy, packaging, transport and labour, and it does not buy steel, sulphuric acid, semiconductors or any of the other million things the economy makes. The flour mill in turn buys grain, energy and sacks. Following any single thread backwards, the web widens, since flour's inputs have inputs of their own, and eventually almost everything connects to almost everything else through long chains. But the direct table, the one the calculation works with, records only the first step of each chain, and at that step the connections are few. The density of the table, the fraction of its entries that are not zero, falls as the economy's product list grows, because recipes lengthen slowly while the list of products lengthens fast. Size, which intuition treats as the enemy of computation, is in this respect its ally: the bigger the economy, the emptier its table, and the cheaper each pass of an iterative solution becomes relative to the whole.

There is a second, quieter point inside the sparsity observation. The table's entries are technical coefficients: quantities of input per unit of output, stated for a given technique at a given time. They are the kind of fact that engineering records and statistical surveys can in principle supply, because they describe what a process does when it runs. This is the most favourable terrain the planning idea ever occupies, the terrain of technical information, and it is worth marking how favourable it is before the argument moves to less favourable ground. A blast furnace's appetite for coke and ore does not depend on anyone's opinion, cannot be strategically misreported for long without showing up as a furnace that will not run, and changes slowly enough to be surveyed. Much of what follows turns on the contrast between information of this kind, which exists independently of the allocation process and can be collected by audit, and information of two other kinds, which cannot: reports coloured by the reporter's interest in the outcome, and wants that have not yet taken shape because the choosing that shapes them has not occurred.

### Why are the entries of the table mostly zero?

Because production is specialised, each process draws directly on a short list of inputs, and the list of products grows far faster than any recipe lengthens. A bakery uses flour, yeast, energy and packaging, not steel or semiconductors, so nearly every cell recording a direct requirement between two products is empty.

Iteration, then, is the second element. A direct solution of a vast system treats every equation as entangled with every other and pays for the pretence. An iterative solution begins with any rough allocation, computes the imbalances it implies, product by product, adjusts each product's planned output toward the level its imbalances suggest, and repeats. Products whose production is already consistent barely move; the corrections concentrate where the initial guess was worst. The procedure resembles nothing so much as the adjustment process of a market itself, in which shortages bid activity upward and surpluses let it fall away, except that the iteration runs inside a calculation, at electronic speed, without anyone having to build the wrong goods first in order to discover the error. That resemblance is worth pausing on. The respondents of that decade, Oskar Lange above all, proposed that a planning board imitate the market's trial and error deliberately, announcing prices and adjusting them against observed shortage and surplus. The computational argument can be read as that proposal with the imitation moved inside the machine: the trial and error still happens, but the trials are arithmetic rather than physical, and their cost is time on a processor rather than warehouses of unsold goods.

Convergence, the third element, is where sceptics have traditionally pushed, and it is where the argument's mathematics must be respected. An iterative method is useful only if its successive approximations approach the solution rather than wandering or exploding, and for the class of systems an economy presents, the conditions for convergence are known and are met when the economy is productive in the elementary sense that it can reproduce itself with something left over. A system that consumed more than it produced in the very act of producing would defeat any allocator, human or mechanical, and its defeat would be a fact about the economy, not about the method. Given a viable economy, the iterations close in, and the remaining question is one of tolerance: how close is close enough. Planning does not need the fifteenth decimal place. It needs allocations accurate enough that the errors are smaller than the shocks the system absorbs routinely from weather, breakdown and whim, and that standard is reached early in the iteration's run. The computational argument, taken whole, is a demonstration that the plan's arithmetic belongs to the class of problems that yield to patience and structure. It should be conceded in those terms and then set aside, because the argument's centre of gravity lies elsewhere.

## Where computation genuinely binds

Before leaving the computational terrain, fairness requires marking where computation really is the binding constraint, because the answer developed later depends on the separation being honest in both directions. Inside a single organisation with a defined task, computation binds constantly and its relaxation has been transformative. A freight network deciding which vehicle carries which load, a grid operator balancing generation against demand minute by minute, a warehouse routing goods to shelves and shelves to packers: each of these is an allocation problem performed on data the organisation itself generates as a by-product of operating, for purposes the organisation itself sets. The data arrive whether or not anyone reports them strategically, because the organisation's own sensors, invoices and inventories record them, and the objective is stated in advance by a management that owns the problem. In such settings, faster and cleverer computation has repeatedly converted infeasible allocations into routine ones, and nobody disputes it. The planning advocate is entitled to this evidence, and the sceptic who waves it away is making an error symmetric to the advocate's own.

Notice, though, what the favourable cases share. The deciding body and the reporting body are the same body, or stand in a relation of direct audit: the warehouse cannot lie to its own inventory system about what is on the shelf, because the shelf is checked by the act of picking. The objective is narrow enough to be written down: minimise empty miles, keep the grid's frequency steady, fill the orders that arrived. And the wants being served enter from outside as orders, already formed by customers choosing in a market the organisation does not have to replace. Strip away any one of those conditions and the problem changes character. An economy-wide plan strips away all three at once. The reporters are subordinate but separate, with interests of their own. The objective is the whole of social purpose, which no one has written down and no election fully specifies. The wants must be generated inside the system rather than received from outside it. Computation binds in the small because the information problem has been solved there by structure, by ownership, by audit and by a market at the boundary. It does not follow, and it is the central equivocation of the popular debate to suppose, that computation was the binding constraint in the large.

### Which information does a grid operator already possess?

A grid operator possesses measurements its own instruments record: generation available, load arising, the state of each line and station. Nobody reports these figures in the hope of an easier target, and the objective, balance supply against demand continuously, is fixed by physics and stated in advance by the operator's charter.

## The first remaining constraint: generating accurate data

A plan is only as good as the returns it is built from, and returns are written by people. The planning bureau needs to know, for every enterprise, what it can produce, with what inputs, at what quality, and what it holds in stock. In a market economy this knowledge is nobody's assignment: it lives inside firms, is tested by their survival, and is revealed to others only partially, through the prices at which the firm can profitably sell. A planned economy must extract the knowledge explicitly, in reports, on forms, to deadlines. The extraction changes the knowledge. An enterprise that reports a high capacity will be set a high target; an enterprise that reports generous stocks will see them allocated away; an enterprise that reveals a cheap improvement in its process will find the improvement generalised into next period's norm, its reward for ingenuity being a harder assignment performed with the same resources. Every manager in such a system learns the arithmetic of self-protection quickly, because the system's own rules teach it: understate capacity, overstate requirements, hoard inputs against the day the plan's supply chain fails, and treat every figure sent upward as a move in a negotiation rather than a measurement of the world.

This is not a conjecture about a hypothetical system. It is the documented operating condition of the one large attempt at comprehensive planning, in which the falsification and shading of returns was not a pathology of individual dishonesty but a rational response reproduced at every level, from the workshop inflating its needs to the ministry defending its empire of subordinate enterprises. The practice had its own vocabulary in the literature of that system, and its scale is the reason the record of Soviet planning, examined in [how Soviet planning actually worked](/2019/04/15/soviet-planning-how-it-worked/), reads so differently from the theory of planning: the theory assumes the bureau knows the economy, while the practice was a permanent struggle by the bureau to find out what its own subordinates were doing. The struggle consumed enormous administrative effort, inspectorates, audits, cross-checks between reporting channels, and it never succeeded in making the returns clean, because the incentive to shade them was not an imperfection in the system's personnel. It was the system's design, restated: targets depend on reports, so reports bend toward the targets their authors can meet.

### Who writes the numbers a plan is built from?

The numbers are written by the enterprises that will be judged against them. A manager reporting capacity is simultaneously stating a fact about machinery and labour, and bidding for the target, the resources and the bonuses that the report will bring down. The two acts are inseparable, and every reader of the report knows it.

Computation does not touch any of this. A faster solver fed on shaded returns produces a precisely wrong plan faster, and the precision can make matters worse by lending the output an authority its inputs never earned. Nor does monitoring technology dissolve the problem, although it shifts its margins. Sensors can count what leaves a gate; they cannot count what a process could have produced under better organisation, which is the figure planning most needs and the figure no instrument records, because it describes a counterfactual. Capacity is not a reading. It is a judgement about what a collection of machines, materials and people could do under effort and ingenuity that have not yet been applied, and the only witnesses to that judgement are the people whose rewards the judgement will determine. The advocate's honest options are two: design institutions in which the reporter's reward does not depend on the report, or accept a permanent discount on the data and plan within it. The first option is the real research programme of modern planning advocacy, and the models examined later are best read as attempts at it. The second is a confession that the plan will be built from numbers everyone involved knows to be padded, which is a description of the historical system's ordinary functioning.

## The ratchet: how a good year becomes a harder assignment

One mechanism deserves isolation because it operates even where no one lies outright. Planning from the achieved level, the practice of setting each period's targets by adding an increment to the previous period's results, converts success into punishment with mechanical reliability. An enterprise that exceeds its target teaches the bureau that its capacity was understated, and the lesson is applied: the next target rises by more than the general increment. An enterprise that fails teaches the opposite lesson and is treated gently, its plan adjusted to its demonstrated level. Across a whole economy the dynamic selects for mediocrity with the indifference of a sieve. Managers learn to hold output in reserve, to refuse innovations whose first effect is to raise the base from which future targets grow, and to treat the end of each planning period as a storm to be survived by hoarding rather than a result to be improved. The ratchet is not a failure of socialist morality or of managerial character. It is what any target system does when the target-setter learns from performance and the performer knows it, and versions of it appear inside market economies wherever divisions of a firm are set budgets by a head office that reads last year's figures. Scale and the absence of an outside check are what made it sovereign in the planned case.

The ratchet also poisons the data in a subtler way than padding does. Padding distorts the level of a report; the ratchet distorts its direction over time, compressing the differences between enterprises toward the performance each has learned is safe. A bureau reading such returns sees an economy of uniform, moderate capability, because that is the economy its own target-setting has trained into existence. Genuine differences in technique, in local conditions, in the quality of management, are flattened in the data precisely because expressing them is punished. The plan built on flattened data then allocates as if enterprises were interchangeable, assigning tasks without regard to the real distribution of competence, and the resulting failures are attributed to execution rather than to the information system that made competence invisible. Here the contrast with a market is not that markets are wise. It is that a market does not need to know which firm is competent. It needs only to let the competent firm's lower costs show up as profit and expansion, a signal generated by the firm's own pursuit of gain rather than by a report the firm files against itself.

### Why does a padded return stay rational even for an honest manager?

Because the return's consequences fall on the writer. A truthful report of high capacity brings a target set at that capacity with no allowance for breakdown or shortage of inputs, so honesty converts directly into risk, while a cautious report buys the slack that keeps a plant running through the failures every manager expects and no plan admits.

## Data generation as the incentive problem wearing informational dress

It should now be visible that the data constraint and the incentive problem are not two problems but one problem photographed from two sides. The classic incentive problem asks why anyone in a planned economy should exert effort, innovate, or economise, when the rewards for doing so are weak and the penalties for failure are strong. The informational form asks why anyone should report truthfully, when the report determines the burden. Both arise from the same structural fact: the system's centre must rely on the cooperation of agents whose interests it has not aligned with its own information needs, and it cannot verify their claims from outside because the claims concern their own local circumstances, which is the very knowledge the centre lacks. The full anatomy of the incentive problem, its versions and the replies made to it, belongs to [the incentive problem in socialism](/2019/11/01/incentive-problem-socialism/), and this article does not duplicate that treatment. What matters here is the grading consequence. A model of planning that solves the allocation arithmetic and even the eliciting of consumer wants, but leaves enterprise rewards tied to enterprise reports, has not answered the data constraint; it has assumed it away, and the assumption is the point on which the historical system broke.

The models divide sharply on exactly this line, and the constraints table will grade them by it. Designs that keep firms in a market, buying and selling at prices they do not administer, generate much of their data as a by-product of transactions nobody files for the planner's benefit: the price at which inputs actually change hands, the order book that records what customers actually chose. Designs that allocate by direct instruction must manufacture their data through returns, and inherit the ratchet and the padding with the returns. Designs that decentralise the planning itself, distributing the reporting among councils and federations of producers and consumers, hope to change the reporter's incentives by changing who the reporter is answerable to, substituting peer scrutiny and reputation inside small bodies for the distant bureau's audit. Whether that substitution works is an empirical question on which, as the evidence sections will show, the record is nearly silent. But the grading question is clear, and it is the question the popular debate never asks: not whether the model can compute, but where its numbers come from and what the numbers' authors gain by shading them.

## The second remaining constraint: revealing what people want

Suppose the data problem solved. The bureau holds true coefficients, honest capacities, accurate stocks. It still does not know what to produce, because the economy's purpose is not written in the coefficients. In a market, the question of what to produce is answered continuously and without being posed: consumers spend, producers watch the spending, resources migrate toward what the spending rewards. The answer is often ugly, skewed by the distribution of income, deaf to needs that carry no money, blind to consequences that fall on third parties. But it is an answer, generated by the activity it guides, requiring no one to have stated it in advance. A plan must state it in advance. Somebody, by some procedure, must decide how much of the economy's effort goes to shoes and how much to surgery, to housing and how much to holidays, down through the whole catalogue of final goods in their varieties and qualities and places. That statement of wants is the plan's objective, and obtaining it is the preference revelation problem.

The difficulty is not that people are coy. It is that the wants do not exist in the form the plan requires. A want, as a market encounters it, is not a entry on a list inside the consumer's head, waiting to be transcribed. It is a disposition that reveals itself at a moment of choice, in the presence of actual alternatives at actual relative prices, and it is partly created by that presence. Nobody knows, in the abstract, how much they value a food they have never tasted, a garment in a colour they have not seen, a device whose uses they will discover by owning it. They find out by confronting the thing, comparing it against what it displaces, and choosing. The fashion cycle, the sudden vogue, the product that fails although every survey blessed it and the product that triumphs although no survey predicted it, are not noise around a stable underlying list. They are evidence about what kind of thing a preference is: a relation between a person and a situation, formed in the choosing as much as expressed by it. A procedure that asks people to declare their wants in advance, on a form, for goods many of which do not yet exist, is asking for a document that human beings are not able to supply, not because they will lie, but because the truth it requests has not been made yet.

### Can a preference be collected before it is formed?

Only where the want is stable, recurrent and tied to a need that physiology or circumstance fixes in advance: calories, warmth, shelter from weather, treatment for a diagnosed condition. Where the want concerns variety, novelty or taste, the preference takes shape inside the act of choosing among present alternatives, and a declaration made earlier records a guess.

Markets are often criticised, correctly, for manufacturing wants as well as registering them: advertising creates desires, positional competition makes satisfaction relative, planned obsolescence wastes the wanting it excites. The planning advocate who raises this is pointing at something real, and it cuts in an unexpected direction. If wants are substantially formed by the system that serves them, then the question of which system to choose includes the question of which wants to form, and a planned economy does not escape the burden by abolishing the market's formation mechanism. It replaces commercial formation with political and administrative formation: the wants that the plan can serve are the wants the plan's categories can express, and citizens learn to want in the shapes the catalogue offers, as citizens of the historical planned economies learned to want what the shops intermittently held. Formation is unavoidable. What differs between systems is whether the formation process also functions as a discovery process, registering surprise, dissent and novelty at the margin where individuals confront goods directly. The market's formation is chaotic and manipulative, but it is decentralised, and its surprises arrive whether or not any authority welcomes them. A plan's formation is only as open to surprise as its designers choose to make it, and the history of designed systems suggests that designers choose control.

There is a reply available to the advocate, and it should be given its weight. For a large class of goods, the endogeneity of preference does not matter, because the want is not really a matter of taste at all. Nobody's preference for insulin, for safe water, for a roof that keeps out the rain, for the eradication of a contagious disease, is formed by the act of choosing among brands; these are needs, legible to medicine, engineering and demography, statable in advance by experts and verifiable by outcomes. The advocate is right that for such goods the preference problem dissolves into a technical problem of provision, and this dissolution is the seed of the domain answer that this article eventually defends. But the reply proves its point by retreating from the general case. The general case is the whole catalogue of consumer goods in their endless differentiation, where taste is the substance and novelty is the engine, and there the revelation problem stands. A planning system that confined itself to needs and left tastes to markets would not be a refutation of the objection. It would be a negotiated settlement with it, which is what the serious models, read carefully, turn out to be.

## Technical information and preference-dependent information: the separation that organises everything

The argument so far can be compressed into a distinction that the reader should carry into every later section, because the models and the domains sort themselves by it. Some of the information an allocation system needs is technical: it concerns what can be made from what, in what proportions, with what reliability, and it exists whether or not anyone chooses anything. Technical information is generated by production itself, recorded by engineers and accountants, auditable by inspection, and largely indifferent to the reporter's wishes, since a furnace either smelts at a given rate or visibly does not. Other information is preference-dependent: it concerns what should be made, for whom, in which variety, and it exists only in the choosing. Preference-dependent information is generated by acts of selection among alternatives, cannot be audited against an independent reality, and shifts as the alternatives shift. Allocation systems differ less in their arithmetic than in their handling of these two kinds. Markets are a machine for generating preference-dependent information continuously, at the price of wasting resources on the failures that generation requires and of registering only the preferences that money backs. Plans are a machine for applying technical information coherently across a whole economy at once, at the price of having to import their preference-dependent information from somewhere else: from politics, from surveys, from last period's sales, from the designer's judgement.

### Which kind of information does each system generate for itself?

A market generates the preference kind as a by-product of exchange, while taking its technical information from inside firms, where it is produced and tested privately. A plan generates neither: it must collect technical data by return and audit, and obtain knowledge of wants from a political or statistical procedure outside production.

Once the separation is drawn, several familiar arguments change shape. The claim that large organisations already plan successfully, examined in its own section below, resolves into the observation that organisations plan on technical information inside a boundary, while the preference-dependent information arrives at the boundary from a market they did not have to replace. The claim that computation has solved the planning problem resolves into a claim about the processing of technical information, true as far as it goes, which never reaches the other kind. And the domain answer comes into view as a sorting rule: assign a decision to planning when the knowledge it needs is of the technical kind and its purpose arrives from need or from politics, and assign it to exchange when the knowledge it needs concerns wanting, and must be coaxed out of choosers in the very act of choosing. The rule does not flatter either camp's general claim. It is meant not to. It is meant to fit the structure of the problem, which is not one problem.

A student meeting this material in an examination should treat the separation as the highest-yield distinction the topic offers, ahead of any model or any date. Questions on planning are usually lost by candidates who argue about allocation in general, praising coherence or damning rigidity in the abstract, when the marking scheme rewards the candidate who asks, of any proposal, two questions in order: where does its technical information come from, and where does its preference information come from. A proposal with a good answer to the first and no answer to the second is half a proposal. A proposal with answers to both, and a stated view of what happens when the answers conflict, is the thing itself. The remainder of this article can be read as an extended application of those two questions: to the constraints, to the four serious models, to the three bodies of evidence, and to the domains in which the final verdict divides.

## Why the required information does not exist before the process that generates it

The strongest form of the sceptical case is not about dishonesty, capacity or even taste. It is about existence. The information a plan requires, the objection runs, does not exist prior to the process that generates it, and the process that generates it is the market process the plan proposes to replace. Consider what a price actually is in this light. A price for copper is not a measurement of a pre-existing fact called copper's value, recorded by the market the way a thermometer records a temperature that was there before the measuring. It is the residue of a contest: users of copper bidding against one another, each bringing knowledge of their own processes, their own order books, their own expectations, most of it tacit and none of it written down. The contest produces the number, and the number then guides people who know nothing of the underlying circumstances. Abolish the contest in favour of a bureau that sets copper's accounting price by calculation, and the bureau must obtain, by some other route, the knowledge that the contest was manufacturing as it went. The calculation can be as fast as physics allows. The knowledge is not there to be calculated from, because it was never a stock. It was a flow, produced by the activity of rivalrous choosing, and the tap has been turned off.

This objection, associated above all with Hayek's 1945 essay on the use of knowledge in society and developed by his successors, is the one the verdict defeater of this article is built around, and it should be distinguished carefully from weaker cousins with which it is often confused. It is not the claim that the centre would be slow. Nor does it rest on the centre being corrupt, or stupid, or overwhelmed, although all of those have been claimed elsewhere. It is the claim that a specific epistemic function, the generation of knowledge about relative scarcity and about wants, is performed by the process of exchange itself and by nothing else yet proposed, so that a system which replaces exchange must either replace the function or operate without the knowledge. The models divide, on inspection, into those that accept the objection and design around it, keeping exchange alive in the domains where the knowledge matters, and those that deny it and propose a substitute generator: democratic deliberation, iterative consultation, statistical inference from behaviour. The substitutes are the interesting ground, and they are examined on their merits in the model sections. What can be said in advance is that the burden of proof sits with the substitute, because the function is not optional. An economy ignorant of what its resources could earn in other uses, and of what its people want at the margin between two goods, does not merely allocate badly in some average sense. It allocates blind in the specific places where the knowledge was supposed to be, and the blindness shows up as the simultaneous shortage and surplus, the queue beside the warehouse, that the historical record displays so monotonously.

### What would count as a substitute generator of the knowledge?

A substitute would have to elicit, from the people who hold the knowledge in tacit and local form, statements accurate enough to allocate by, as fast as circumstances change, without paying them in easier targets for what they reveal, and without requiring them to know in advance what they would choose among goods not yet offered.

One more refinement protects the objection from a cheap rebuttal. The rebuttal says that markets also fail to know the future, that they generate bubbles, manias and crashes, that their knowledge is myopic and their signals noisy. All true, and all beside the point, because the objection is comparative. It does not claim that the market's knowledge is good. It claims that the knowledge exists in a market system, in distributed and actionable form, and that the planner who abolishes the generating process must show where the equivalent knowledge will come from. Pointing at the market's epistemic failures establishes that the standard is low. It does not establish that the challenger clears it. The grading of both systems against stated purposes, in specified domains, remains the method, and on that method the knowledge objection does not deliver a general verdict for markets. It delivers a specific challenge, domain by domain: here is a kind of knowledge this domain needs; show the mechanism that produces it under the proposed arrangement. Domains differ in how hard that challenge is to meet, which is the entire substance of the answer this article eventually gives.

## The constraints table

Everything argued so far can be set out in a single exhibit, and the exhibit is the instrument the rest of the article uses. The constraints table lists the four constraints that any allocation system must satisfy, and asks of each the same four questions. Does it bind, meaning does it actually restrict what the system can achieve, or is it a formality that dissolves on inspection? Has technical change altered it, meaning has the growth of computational and communications capacity loosened it, left it where it stood, or tightened it? What would relax it, meaning what institutional or epistemic development, if it arrived, would ease the constraint? And which of the serious models addresses it, meaning which proposal takes this constraint as a design problem rather than assuming it away? The table's discipline is that a model earns no credit for constraints it ignores. A proposal that solves the first row and is silent on the second and third has not been refuted; it has been located. Location, in a debate addicted to total verdicts, is most of the value an audit can add.

| Constraint | Does it bind? | Has technology changed it? | What would relax it? | Which model addresses it? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Computation | No longer binds at economy scale | Transformed it: sparsity and iteration made the arithmetic tractable | Little left to relax; faster re-solving as conditions move | The computational planning proposal of Cockshott and Cottrell takes this row as its whole case |
| Data generation | Binds wherever rewards depend on reports | Barely: monitoring counts outputs, not capacities or counterfactuals | Rewards detached from the content of returns, or data generated by transactions rather than filed | Market-keeping models inherit transaction data; participatory designs substitute peer scrutiny |
| Preference revelation | Binds wherever wants are taste-like and novel | No: wants formed in choosing are not collected faster by being collected digitally | A generator of wants-knowledge that does not rely on purchase, or domains where wants are needs | Models retaining consumer markets leave this row to the market; deliberative models propose consultation rounds |
| Incentive compatibility | Binds wherever effort and honesty are unrewarded | No: the ratchet is a property of target systems, not of their hardware | Careers, incomes and standing tied to outcomes the agent does not self-report | Worker-managed and market socialist models rebuild the reward structure; comprehensive plans assume it |

Read the table column by column and a pattern emerges that neither camp advertises. Technical change has moved exactly one row. The first constraint, the only one that was ever about machinery, has been relaxed by machinery, and the relaxation is genuine and permanent. The other three rows describe relations between people: between a reporter and the authority that will judge him by his report, between a want and the act that brings it into being, between an agent's effort and an agent's reward. Relations between people are not loosened by faster processing, because their tightness was never a processing problem. A bureau that could solve its equations in a microsecond would still be solving them over returns written by managers bidding for survivable targets, in service of wants that nobody had yet formed, executed by workers whose reward bore no relation to the plan's success. This is the analytical heart of the article, and it is why the computational victory, real as it is, settles so little. The debate's centre of gravity was never in the first row. The first row was simply the only row the sceptics of an earlier generation could prove, and the only row the advocates of this generation can disprove, which is why the public argument keeps returning to it while the argument that matters waits in the rows below.

### How should a new proposal be graded against the table?

By the highest row it leaves unaddressed. A model that keeps markets for consumer goods never faces the preference row at full strength; a model that allocates investment politically faces the data row in its hardest form, since the returns concern projects that do not yet exist and cannot be audited against any operating record.

The table also disciplines the advocates' favourite word, feasible. Feasibility is not a property of a model; it is a property of a model relative to a constraint. To call a proposal feasible because its arithmetic can be performed is to grade it on the first row alone, the row that no longer binds, which is rather like certifying a bridge by the quality of its paint. The serious question for each model is which row stops it, and the model sections that follow are organised to answer exactly that, model by model, in the proponents' own terms first and under audit second. The reader will find that the models are not equally vulnerable. The ones that concede the most territory to markets are exposed on the fewest rows, which is not a coincidence but a trade: every domain surrendered to exchange is a constraint outsourced to the mechanism that generates the relevant knowledge as a by-product. The ones that claim the whole economy are exposed on every row at once. Between those poles, the interesting argument is about the exchange rate of the trade, how much of the socialist purpose survives each surrender, and that argument, unlike the computational one, has no technical resolution. It is a political and philosophical argument conducted in economic vocabulary, and the article treats it as such when the models are done.

## The middle ground: why the serious discussion lives between the poles

The public argument about planning is a duel between two positions that almost no serious writer holds: comprehensive central allocation of everything, and the night-watchman market that allocates everything by price. The literature that matters occupies the ground between, and it got there by a route worth tracing. The collapse of the state socialist economies at the end of the 1980s discredited the comprehensive pole beyond rehabilitation among its own former sympathisers, while the financial instability and inequality of the unmodified market kept the other pole from inheriting the earth by default. What followed, in the decade after, was a flowering of model-building by writers who accepted large parts of the sceptical case and tried to design systems that answered it: systems that kept markets where markets generate knowledge, socialised the ownership that markets left concentrated, democratised the firm, and reserved collective decision for the choices, above all investment, where they argued the market's own performance was least defensible. These are the middle models, and the brief shelf of them, four books across fourteen years, constitutes the actual contemporary discussion of planning, as opposed to its journalistic shadow.

The four differ profoundly, and treating them as a single position called market socialism is the first error to avoid. One of them abolishes the capital market while keeping the market for goods almost intact, changing who owns firms rather than how goods are allocated. One keeps goods markets and adds workplace democracy and a socialised investment fund. One attempts the boldest construction, replacing market allocation itself with an iterative negotiation between producers and consumers, and pays for the boldness in the currency of citizens' time. One distributes the planning function among representative bodies of affected interests, negotiating coordination where a ministry would otherwise command. What unites them is not a mechanism but a concession: each accepts that the knowledge and incentive constraints are real, and each can be read as a device for routing around a specific row of the constraints table. The survey of such models in the series' treatment of [real utopias](/2020/04/15/real-utopias-explained/) places them in the wider family of institutional designs for a post-capitalist economy; the treatment here is narrower and more adversarial, grading each against the constraints it was built to survive.

### What is the common concession underneath the four models?

Each model concedes that some knowledge an allocator needs is generated only by decentralised choosing, and each therefore preserves a sphere of choosing it does not attempt to replace. They differ in where they draw the sphere's boundary: at consumer goods, at the firm, at investment, or at the plan itself.

Before grading them, take each in its author's own terms, because these writers are routinely summarised by their enemies. The summaries share a method: describe the model as either capitalism with flags on it, if it keeps markets, or as the old planning with better public relations, if it does not. Both descriptions are evasions. A model that socialises investment while keeping consumer markets is not capitalism in any sense its critics elsewhere use, since the allocation of the surplus, the decision about the future's shape, has been removed from private hands; and a model of negotiated coordination is not Gosplan with a smile, since no centre in it issues binding commands. The grading that follows applies the constraints table without the slogans. Where a model is exposed, the exposure is stated. Where a model answers a constraint that its rivals assume away, the answer is credited. The aim is the one the whole cluster of critiques in this series sets itself: a scorecard a hostile reader can trust because its concessions are real.

## Coupon socialism: Roemer and the socialisation of ownership without the planning of goods

John Roemer's A Future for Socialism, published in 1994, is the most austere of the four models and the one that surrenders least to the planner's ambition, which is precisely its interest. Roemer's diagnosis begins from the failures he takes to be established: comprehensive planning founders on information and incentives, and public ownership in its state form produced managers answerable to ministries rather than to any discipline that made them efficient or honest. His remedy relocates the socialist intervention. Citizens receive an equal endowment of coupons, a second currency that cannot be converted into money, cannot be transferred, and dies with its holder. With coupons, and only with coupons, shares in firms can be bought. Firms operate in ordinary markets, selling goods for money, competing, failing, hiring, exactly as firms do, except that their owners are the citizenry holding coupon portfolios, often through mutual funds that monitor management with the vigilance of any large shareholder. The profit income that capitalism concentrates flows instead across the whole adult population, equalised at the source rather than redistributed after the fact by a tax state chasing mobile wealth.

Grade the model against the table and its strategy becomes transparent. The preference row is never faced, because consumer goods are allocated by money prices and uncoerced choice, generating the knowledge of wants exactly as a market does. The data row is largely bypassed, because the figures that matter, costs, sales, profits, arise from transactions and audited accounts rather than from returns filed against targets. The incentive row is addressed in its managerial form: fund managers and firm managers face something like the disciplines of a capital market, since coupon holders can move their holdings and the funds compete for citizens' coupons, although the discipline is admittedly softer than one in which the monitor's own wealth is at stake in money. And the computation row is simply absent, since nothing economy-wide is computed. What the model gives up, it gives up openly: the direction of investment is still set by profit-seeking under coupon ownership, so the socialisation is of income from capital, not of the economy's trajectory. Roemer's reply is that the trajectory was never governable by the means his critics propose, and that equality of the profit stream plus ordinary democratic politics, taxing and spending and regulating, is the feasible residue of the socialist ambition. Whether that residue deserves the name is the argument his book invites, and it is an argument about purposes, not about feasibility. On feasibility, graded by constraints, the model is the strongest of the four precisely because it attempts the least allocation.

### Which constraint does coupon socialism refuse to face, and why is the refusal defensible?

It refuses the preference row by leaving consumer choice entirely to money markets, on the ground that no proposed substitute generates knowledge of wants as reliably as choosing does. The refusal is defensible as engineering: a system is not obliged to solve every problem, only to avoid claiming solutions it does not have.

## Economic democracy: Schweickart and the socialisation of investment

David Schweickart's After Capitalism, published in 2002 and developing his earlier Against Capitalism, draws the boundary one step further in. His economic democracy keeps the market for goods, as Roemer does, and adds two transformations Roemer does not attempt. Within the firm, managerial authority is replaced by workplace democracy: enterprises are controlled by those who work in them, who elect their managers and decide the disposition of the surplus after obligations, on the model of the successful cooperative sector that market economies already contain. Between firms, the capital market is abolished and replaced by a social investment fund, raised by a flat tax on the capital assets of enterprises and distributed back to regions and communities by democratic decision, per capita, with banks as public institutions lending the fund's resources to worker-controlled firms whose plans they judge. The market thus survives as the allocator of goods and of labour's daily deployment, while the two decisions Schweickart takes to define capitalism, the subordination of the worker inside the firm and the private direction of society's investment, are both collectivised.

The audit finds this model exposed on a different row than Roemer's. Consumer preference remains the market's business, and the data of current production still arise from transactions, so the second and third rows press lightly. The pressure concentrates on investment, which is preference-dependent information of the hardest kind: knowledge about futures that do not exist, embodied in projects that have never operated, proposed by parties whose standing and resources depend on the proposals being accepted. A public bank judging a worker-managed firm's expansion plan is in the epistemic position of the planning bureau judging a capacity return, with the aggravation that there is no operating record to audit, only a forecast. Schweickart's answer is that this is exactly the position of a capitalist bank or venture fund, which also judges forecasts by interested parties, and that the comparison must be with that fallible process and its documented wastes, duplicated capacity, speculative manias, abandoned regions, rather than with an ideal. The answer has force. It concedes, in effect, that investment allocation under any system is judgement under uncertainty by interested parties, and claims only that democratic judgement with social criteria will distribute its errors more acceptably than private judgement with profit criteria. That is a claim about values and about the distribution of error, not about knowledge, and it should be argued as such. On the incentive row the model is genuinely inventive: workers who control their firm and share its surplus have reasons to report honestly to themselves that no ministry could manufacture, and the data a self-governing firm produces for its own decisions are produced for the one audience that cannot be fooled by them for long.

### Who decides what gets built in economic democracy?

Investment is decided neither by a ministry nor by private wealth but by public banks allocating a socially raised fund to worker-controlled firms, under criteria set democratically and distributed across regions by population. The deciders judge proposals and forecasts, which places the model's weight on the hardest information to verify.

## Participatory economics: Albert, Hahnel, and the plan built from proposals

The boldest of the four models refuses the settlement the previous two accept. Participatory economics, set out by Michael Albert and Robin Hahnel in The Political Economy of Participatory Economics in 1991 and in companion volumes of the same period, proposes to allocate without markets at all, and to do it by a procedure that manufactures the missing information through structured consultation. Workplaces are governed by workers' councils and organised into federations by industry and region; consumers are organised in parallel councils, from the neighbourhood upward. Planning proceeds in rounds. Councils propose: workplaces state what they could produce and what they would need, consumers state what they would wish to consume, and an iteration facilitation board publishes indicative prices reflecting the social costs and scarcities implied by the aggregate of proposals. Each side revises in light of the prices and of the visible excess demands and supplies, and the rounds continue until proposals converge on a feasible, mutually consistent plan. Remuneration follows effort and sacrifice rather than output or bargaining power, and jobs are balanced so that rote and empowering tasks are shared, attacking the division between those who decide and those who execute that the authors identify as a class division in its own right.

This is the only model of the four that takes the preference row head-on, and its method deserves respect: it treats wants as things to be formed through deliberation rather than merely registered, which answers the endogeneity objection by embracing it. If preferences are made in the choosing, then let the choosing be a social process of proposal, costing and revision, in which a neighbourhood can discover, by seeing the price its wishes imply, that it prefers the swimming pool to the second car park. The indicative prices of each round are meant to do the epistemic work of market prices, confronting every proposer with the opportunity cost of the proposal, without the market's dependence on purchasing power. Whether they can do that work is the model's crux. An indicative price disciplines only if the proposer believes the eventual allocation will honour it, and the rounds generate information only if participants report their true wishes and capacities rather than gaming the iteration, claiming extra to bargain down from, understating capacity to ease the eventual assignment. The gaming problem is the data row returning through the deliberative door, in a forum where the returns are proposals and the audience is one's neighbours. The authors reply that transparency and peer knowledge inside small councils make gaming visible and shameful in a way a ministry's forms never could. The reply is not absurd. It is also not evidence, and the model's defenders and critics divide, in the end, over a psychological conjecture about how people behave in iterated public proposal that no existing society has tested at scale.

The standing objection, though, is cruder and has never been answered in the model's own terms: time. A plan built from rounds of proposal and revision, conducted for every significant category of production and consumption, at every level from the workplace to the national federation, consumes the scarcest resource citizens have. The critics' arithmetic is not about matrix sizes, which the figures protocol of this article forbids rehearsing in any case; it is about evenings. Participation in the model is not a garnish on citizenship but a second job, and the suspicion follows that attendance would decay, that the rounds would be dominated by the leisured, the confident and the professionally articulate, and that a de facto planning stratum would re-emerge from the facilitation boards and the federation staffs, reconstituting inside a participatory shell the separation between deciders and decided that the model exists to abolish. The authors answer that market citizenship also consumes time, in commuting, in second jobs taken from necessity, in the anxieties of insecurity, and that balanced job complexes would return time by abolishing useless labour. The exchange is honest on both sides and resolvable only by experiment. What the audit can say is that participatory economics is the model that faces the most rows of the table simultaneously, because it declines every available outsourcing, and that its fate hangs on rows two and three, on whether deliberation can generate truthful data and formed preferences at a tolerable cost in time. No other model asks so much of its citizens. None, if it worked, would deliver so much of the original ambition.

## Negotiated coordination: Devine and the planning of the framework

Pat Devine's Democracy and Economic Planning, published in 1988, predates the other three and differs from all of them in locating planning neither in a centre nor in a market but in negotiation among the affected. Devine distinguishes three kinds of economic decision. The allocation of existing resources among current uses he is content to leave largely to markets, whose signals he accepts for that purpose. The coordination of interdependent investment, where one industry's expansion is worthless without another's, and the determination of the economy's overall direction, he assigns to negotiated coordination: bodies composed of representatives of all the interests a decision touches, producers, consumers, communities, the relevant levels of government, bargaining toward an agreed framework within which market processes then operate. Ownership is social in the specific sense that the surplus belongs to the society whose representatives negotiate, and enterprises are self-managed by their workforces within the negotiated framework. The model is best understood as an attempt to plan the parameters rather than the particulars: the rate and direction of investment, the fate of declining industries, the development of regions, settled by deliberation among those who will live with the results, while the particulars of production and exchange are left to the mechanisms that generate the relevant knowledge daily.

Graded against the table, negotiated coordination is the most self-consciously epistemic of the four designs. It does not claim that any body can know the economy; it claims that the knowledge relevant to framework decisions is held, in fragments, by the affected parties themselves, and that a negotiation among them is a machine for pooling the fragments, cruder than a price but richer, since parties can bring reasons, forecasts and values that no price encodes. Its exposure lies in the familiar pathologies of representative bargaining: the organised out-argue the diffuse, the present generation's representatives discount the unborn, negotiation rewards veto and delay, and the framework that emerges may be the sum of the participants' powers rather than of their knowledge. Devine's answer is institutional: representation designed to include the affected in proportion to their affectedness, decisions taken at the lowest level that internalises the consequences. The answer states a design principle rather than a guarantee, and the model's author is more candid than most that the principle's realisation is a political achievement, not a technical one. On the data row the model is moderately placed, since the framework decisions it plans are few and large, and few and large decisions can be researched, contested and audited in a way that millions of daily allocations cannot. On the preference row it takes a distinctive position: the wants that matter for framework decisions are collective wants, about the kind of society to inhabit, and those, it argues, are properly formed in political deliberation rather than discovered in shops. The claim is the mirror image of the participatory one, narrower and therefore harder to refute: nobody supposes that a society's decision to decarbonise its energy supply, or to privilege housing over offices in its cities, is a preference that a market could have revealed, since markets reveal only the preferences that existing prices and incomes make expressible.

### Which models generate their knowledge, and which merely inherit it?

An eliciting model builds a procedure whose operation produces the knowledge the allocation needs: transactions that reveal costs, rounds that confront proposers with the price of their wishes, negotiations that pool the fragments held by affected parties. An assuming model begins after the knowledge, taking wants and capacities as inputs it never explains.

With the four models stated, the separation in that answer can be applied as a sieve. Roemer elicits through markets and assumes nothing he cannot point to, at the price of planning almost nothing. Schweickart elicits current production through markets and assumes that democratic judgement can allocate investment as well as private judgement does, a comparison he is entitled to press. Devine elicits framework knowledge through negotiation and assumes the representation problem is soluble by design. Albert and Hahnel elicit through rounds and assume the rounds will be attended, honest and worth their cost. Cockshott and Cottrell, whose computational proposal stands behind the whole discussion, elicit consumer demand through a market-like mechanism of their own, prices adjusted against sales of consumer goods, while assuming the enterprise returns can be made truthful by audit and by the direct recording of production. No model assumes everything; no model elicits everything. The debate among them is a debate about which assumption is cheapest, and it cannot be settled by the constraints table, which can only make the assumptions visible and identically priced. Visibility, though, is not nothing. The popular version of this debate proceeds as if the choice were between a solved problem and an impossible one. The table shows a row of engineering trade-offs under genuine uncertainty, which is a less exciting picture and a true one.

## The evidence problem: three bodies of fact, none of them decisive

Models are cheap; the debate's scarcity is evidence. A proposed allocation system can be tested only by being run, at scale, over time, against a comparison, and societies are not laboratories in which rival systems are assigned at random to matched populations. What the argument actually possesses is three bodies of material, each real, each routinely overclaimed by one side or the other, and each probative about a narrower question than the one the debaters are asking. The first is the record of the state socialist economies, which operated comprehensive planning for decades across a large share of the world's population. The second is the record of wartime planning inside market economies, which suspended the price mechanism across wide sectors for defined emergencies. The third is a single short-lived experiment in cybernetic economic management in Chile in the early 1970s, which has acquired in the retelling an evidentiary glow entirely disproportionate to its duration. Take each in turn, and be precise about what it is evidence of.

The discipline required is the one this series applies to every contested record: a body of evidence tests the system that was actually operated, under the conditions that actually held, and not the system a theorist wishes had been operated instead. Advocates of the middle models are entitled to insist that their designs were never tried in the state socialist world, and the insistence is factually correct. Sceptics are entitled to reply that the comprehensive system that was tried embodied the same constraints the models claim to route around, and that its failure modes, padding, ratchet, shortage, the queue beside the warehouse, are data about those constraints and not merely about one regime's brutality or backwardness. Both entitlements can be honoured at once, and the sections below try to honour them: the record is treated as evidence about constraints, not as a verdict on models it never implemented.

## The state socialist record: a test of the comprehensive design, not of these models

No middle model was implemented in the Soviet Union or its allied economies. Roemer's coupon ownership, Schweickart's workplace democracy, Albert and Hahnel's councils, Devine's negotiated coordination: none of these describes a system that existed there, and the distance is not a detail. The state socialist firm was not worker-controlled; its manager was an appointee executing instructions. Its consumers did not deliberate; they queued. Its investment was not negotiated among affected interests; it was decreed through a hierarchy that treated information as a command resource. To cite the Soviet collapse as a refutation of participatory economics is therefore a straightforward category error, and advocates are right to protest it every time it occurs, which is often.

### Does the Soviet record test the middle models?

It does not, since none of the four designs was operated there: no coupon ownership, no workplace control of enterprises, no council iteration, no negotiated framework. The record tests the comprehensive command design, and it tests the constraints directly, by displaying what reporting, target-setting and shortage do when no rival mechanism relieves them.

But the protest, valid as far as it goes, is then routinely stretched past its warrant. The record may not test the models, yet it tests the constraints, and the constraints are the models' own acknowledged enemies. When the historical system exhibits the ratchet, the padded return, the hoarded input and the falsified fulfilment report, it is exhibiting the data row in operation, under the only conditions in which that row has ever been observed at national scale. A model whose answer to the data row is a conjecture about peer scrutiny or about redesigned rewards is making a claim that the record neither confirms nor refutes, and honesty requires the claim to be labelled as conjecture, not smuggled into the ledger as experience. The same discipline applies in reverse to the sceptics. The record shows that a command system, operated by a party-state with no independent audit and no political feedback, generated corrupted data and chronic shortage. It does not show that every departure from market allocation corrupts data to the same degree, and the sceptic who treats a hospital's capacity plan or a grid's dispatch schedule as Gosplan in miniature has abandoned the domain discipline that this article exists to enforce. The record's real lesson is narrower and more useful than either camp's slogan: the constraints bind in proportion to the share of the economy a single mechanism attempts to govern, and they bind hardest where the mechanism must manufacture its own information. That is a lesson about dosage, and dosage is the language of the domain answer.

## Wartime planning: the fair test of a narrow claim

The second body of evidence is the most favourable to planning that exists, and it should be given without grudging. In the Second World War, the major market economies suspended large parts of the price mechanism and allocated by administrative decision: raw materials by priority boards, production by contract and direction, labour by conscription and reserved occupations, consumption by rationing. The results, by the criterion the planners set themselves, were formidable. Output of the designated goods rose at speeds no peacetime market had approached, entire industries were created or converted in months, and the mobilisation was achieved with a degree of social consent that rationing's fairness underwrote. Advocates cite this record constantly, and they are entitled to. It demonstrates that administrative allocation is not a fantasy, that a modern state can direct an economy's commanding heights toward stated objectives and hit them.

Precision about what was demonstrated is the whole value of the case. The wartime planners operated under conditions that read like a checklist of the domain answer's favourable column. The objectives were few and known: ships, aircraft, munitions, fuel, food at stated nutritional standards. The wants that mattered were not preferences to be discovered but requirements to be met, set by strategy and physiology. The horizon was the war's duration, long enough to justify building plant, short enough that the capital stock's eventual fate could be ignored. The data were substantially technical: bills of materials, shipping tonnage, calorie requirements, generated by engineers and quartermasters rather than elicited from choosers. The preference row was not solved but suspended, by rationing, which is to say by the political decision that private wants would not be consulted beyond a guaranteed floor. And the whole apparatus floated on a market economy's accumulated capital, skills and supplier networks, which it directed but did not have to create. Wartime planning is thus a fair test of exactly one claim: that where objectives are few, known and urgent, and preference is legitimately overridden, administrative allocation mobilises superbly. It is a test of nothing else, and the advocate who cites it for the general case is committing the mirror image of the sceptic's Soviet error, generalising from a domain to the whole economy. The symmetry is instructive. Each camp owns one body of evidence, gathered in the domain where its preferred mechanism shines, and each camp's favourite argumentative move is to exhibit its own domain and call it the world.

### What does wartime mobilisation actually prove?

It proves that administrative allocation excels when the objectives are few, stated in advance, and measurable in physical terms, when wants beyond a rationed floor are legitimately set aside, and when the horizon is a defined emergency. It proves nothing about allocating a peacetime consumer economy, where the objectives are the wants themselves.

## The Chilean cybernetic project: an attempt, and evidence about nothing else

The third body of evidence is the slightest and the most mythologised. In the early 1970s, the Chilean government of Salvador Allende, pursuing a democratic road to socialism whose political history is told in [the article on Allende's Chile](/2018/12/01/allende-chile-democratic-road/), commissioned the British cybernetician Stafford Beer to design a system for the real-time management of the nationalised sector. Factories were to report production data daily through a network of telex machines to a centre in Santiago, where statistical filtering would flag the exceptions needing attention, algedonic signals in Beer's vocabulary, leaving routine management autonomous and reserving intervention for genuine deviation. An operations room, famously futuristic in its design, was built to display the state of the economy to its managers. The system was partial, improvised under siege conditions, and used most seriously during a crisis of distribution when it helped coordinate the movement of goods around a strike of transport owners. In September 1973 the government was overthrown by military coup, and the project was destroyed with it, its room dismantled, its personnel scattered into exile and prison.

The episode is irresistible to both camps, and both should resist. For advocates, it gleams as proof that the idea was tried and was working when violence stopped it. For sceptics, it serves as a parable of technocratic fantasy. The record supports neither use. The system managed a flow of production reports from a subset of enterprises for a brief period under emergency conditions; it never allocated an economy, never faced the preference problem at all, since consumer allocation continued through ordinary and increasingly chaotic channels, and never ran long enough for its data quality to be tested by the strategic adaptation of its reporters, which takes longer than the interval allowed. It is evidence that a reporting and exception-flagging apparatus could be built with the communications technology of its period and operated by committed staff, which is a fact about engineering and morale. About the question this article is asking, whether such a system could allocate as well as markets across domains and years, it is evidence about nothing, because the experiment was terminated by an army before it reached the stage at which evidence accumulates. The honest sentence is the brief one: the attempt was made, it was ended by force, and no conclusion about feasibility follows in either direction. Any writer who recruits Chile for a verdict, for or against, is telling the reader about the writer's prior commitments and about nothing in the Chilean record.

### Why does the Chilean attempt count as evidence about nothing?

Because it ended before it could accumulate the only evidence that matters: performance over years, against changing conditions, with reporters who had learned to game it and consumers whose wants it had to serve. A reporting apparatus demonstrated under siege for a short interval shows that reporting is buildable, which nobody disputed.

There is a coda to the evidence survey, and it concerns the direction of the burden of proof. After three bodies of material, the ledger stands as follows. The comprehensive design has been tested and has failed on the constraints this article identifies, whatever else also contributed to its failure. The narrow domain of mobilisation has been tested and has succeeded under its special conditions. The middle models have not been tested at all. In that situation, the burden does not sit symmetrically. The advocate of an untested model is asking for a society to be reorganised on the strength of an argument, and the sceptic is entitled to demand that the argument specify, row by row, where its information comes from. But the sceptic who slides from that demand into the claim that the models have been refuted is claiming a test that never occurred, and the slide should be named whenever it happens, including in the verdict section of this article, which will grade the models as unproven rather than as disproven. Unproven is not a euphemism. It is a precise epistemic status, and in a debate where both camps habitually counterfeit their evidence, precision about status is the beginning of honesty.

## The argument from the large firm: planning inside a market is not planning instead of one

No argument in this debate is repeated more often, in speeches and on forums, than the argument from scale. Very large firms, it is said, already plan internally on a scale comparable to the national economies of the mid twentieth century: they coordinate supply chains across continents, schedule production in detail, allocate capital among divisions by committee, and move goods within their boundaries at administered transfer prices rather than by internal markets. If planning at that scale works inside a firm, the argument runs, then planning at the scale of an economy is a difference of degree, not of kind, and the sceptics' impossibility claims stand refuted by the existence of every large retailer and manufacturer. The argument has a distinguished ancestry: it is a descendant of the observation, made by Ronald Coase in 1937, that firms exist precisely because organising some transactions by direction is cheaper than organising them by market contract, so that every market economy is an archipelago of planned islands. The islands are real. The inference drawn from them is not.

The reply turns on what surrounds the island. A firm plans inside a market that performs, for free and without being asked, the two services this article has identified as the hard ones. First, the market supplies the firm with input prices formed by the bids of all other users, so the firm's planners allocate against genuine scarcity signals they did not have to manufacture: the cost of steel to the firm is a fact delivered by the world, not a return filed by a subordinate. Second, the market supplies the test of the firm's output: customers who can refuse. The firm's internal plan is continuously audited by an external reality it does not control, the willingness of strangers to pay, and the audit is what keeps the internal numbers honest, since a division that pads its costs or inflates its forecasts eventually shows up in results that shareholders, lenders and rivals can read. Remove the surrounding market and both services vanish together. The economy-wide planner has no outside from which prices arrive and no outside by which results are judged; it must generate its signals and its tests internally, which is the data and preference problem restated. Internal planning is therefore not a small-scale test of allocation without markets. It is a demonstration of how well planning works when markets do its hardest work for it, and as evidence for the general case it counts, strictly, for nothing.

### Does a large firm's internal plan test planning without markets?

It tests planning with markets: the firm receives its input prices from the exchanges of others and its verdict from customers free to refuse its goods. The two services an economy-wide plan must perform for itself are performed for the firm by the surrounding system, so the firm's success measures the combination, not the planning alone.

The point deserves one more turn, because a subtler version of the argument survives the reply and should be credited. The subtle version observes that the boundary between firm and market is itself chosen, transaction by transaction, and has moved dramatically: activities once coordinated across markets are now coordinated inside platforms and supply networks, and the trend of organisational technology has been toward larger spans of deliberate coordination. If the efficient boundary keeps moving toward planning, perhaps it has no natural stopping point short of the economy. The observation is correct about the boundary's mobility and wrong about what moves it. Firms extend their span exactly as far as internal direction remains cheaper than contracting, and the calculation of cheaper is performed in prices the market supplies, including the price of the firm's own failure, which is acquisition or bankruptcy. The boundary is patrolled by the market at every point. An economy-wide plan abolishes the patrol, and with it the instrument that was measuring how far planning should extend. The subtle version thus ends in the same place as the crude one: the firm's planning is evidence about planning under market discipline, and the discipline is not a detail of the arrangement. It is the arrangement's epistemic foundation.

## The trial-and-error ancestor: what the older answer still teaches

Behind the computational proposal stands an older answer to the calculation problem whose logic repays attention, because it shows the debate's participants discovering the information problem in real time. Oskar Lange, writing in 1936 and 1937, proposed that a central board set prices as an auctioneer does, announce them, watch where shortages and surpluses appeared, and adjust: raise the price of what was short, lower the price of what accumulated, until the economy cleared. Managers of socialised firms would follow two rules, produce at minimum cost and expand output until cost met price, mimicking the behaviour competition enforces. The proposal was frankly imitative: Lange presented the market as a computing device of great antiquity and proposed to keep its procedure while changing its ownership. His famous courtesy to his opponent, the suggestion that a statue of Ludwig von Mises be placed in the planning ministry's hall in gratitude for having forced socialists to think clearly, acknowledged that the objection had improved the answer.

The trial-and-error design is the ancestor of every eliciting model in this article, and its weakness is the one its critics identified at the time and the constraints table formalises. The board's adjustments are only as good as the shortage and surplus signals reaching it, and those signals are generated by managers whose compliance with the two rules cannot be verified from the centre: a manager who prefers a quiet life to minimum cost produces the shortage the board then reads as a reason to raise his price and ease his position. The design elicits preference information from consumers tolerably well, since consumers spending money incomes in shops reveal wants in the ordinary way, and Lange kept that market intact. It elicits cost information from managers hardly at all, because the manager's report of his costs is precisely a return on which his comfort depends. The pattern is the one this article has generalised: in every design, ask whose report the system cannot check, and there the data row will bind. Lange, to his credit, saw the consumer side clearly. The producer side he handled by assumption, instructing managers to behave as the rules required, and an instruction is not a mechanism. The distance between Lange's board and Cockshott and Cottrell's iteration is real but narrower than the rhetoric suggests: both designs solve the arithmetic, both keep a consumer market to generate wants, and both meet their hardest problem at the enterprise's door, where the figures are authored by the very people the figures will judge.

## The sceptical synthesis: Nove and the economics of the feasible

Between the models and their critics stands one figure who belongs to neither camp and whose work disciplines both. Alec Nove, whose The Economics of Feasible Socialism appeared in 1983, spent a career inside the Sovietological evidence and emerged with a position that the constraints table would recognise as its own. Nove accepted that the comprehensive command system was unreformable in its own terms, and he accepted, against the market's triumphalists, that a socialist economy remained a coherent ambition. His feasible socialism was a deliberate mixture: a sector of state enterprises for the commanding heights, a large cooperative sector, small private enterprise at the margins, markets for consumer goods and labour, and planning reserved for the structural decisions, the balance between consumption and investment, the fate of regions and industries, where he judged market signals to be systematically misleading guides. The mixture was not a compromise struck for comfort. It followed from his central analytical claim, which was about scale and information: beyond a certain size, no centre can possess the knowledge that detailed allocation requires, and below a certain size, no market can be trusted with decisions whose consequences are irreversible and collective. Feasibility, in Nove's usage, is the discipline of assigning each decision to the mechanism whose information fits it.

### What makes a socialism feasible in Nove's sense?

Feasibility is a matching exercise: each class of decision assigned to the mechanism that can know what the decision requires. Detailed allocation goes to markets and firms, which generate the knowledge in acting; structural choice goes to politics and planning, where the knowledge is about purposes and long consequences rather than about capacities and tastes.

Nove's synthesis matters to this article for two reasons beyond its content. The first is that it demonstrates the domain pattern operating inside the socialist tradition itself, a generation before the computational revival: the sorting of decisions by the information they need was not invented by planning's enemies but by one of its most knowledgeable friends, working from the record rather than from theory. The second is that Nove states the standing challenge to every general verdict in this debate. If the correct unit of analysis is the class of decision, then both the advocate's claim that planning works and the sceptic's claim that it cannot are category errors of the same shape, answers to a question whose grammar is wrong. The remaining sections of this article take up the challenge directly, walking the domains one by one: where knowledge is technical and purposes arrive ready-made, where knowledge concerns wants and wants must be found by choosing, and where the two kinds mix, which is most of the interesting territory. The verdict, when it comes, will be Nove's method with the constraints table's bookkeeping: a sorting, defended domain by domain, with the defeater stated for the whole.

## Where planning is most defensible: needs known in advance

The domain answer begins with the column in which planning's conditions are met. A domain favours administrative allocation when four features coincide. The want to be served is a need, statable in advance by a technical or political procedure rather than discovered by choosing. The information required is predominantly technical: capacities, flows, physical requirements, of the kind that audit can verify. The horizon is long, so that the slowness of deliberate decision is not a handicap and the myopia of price signals is. And the costs of error are asymmetric in planning's favour: underprovision is catastrophic or irreversible, while overprovision is merely wasteful. Where those four features coincide, the market's celebrated information machine has little to feed on, because there is no dispersed preference knowledge to gather, and its known vices, the discounting of the future, the deafness to unpriced consequences, the underprovision of goods whose benefits are diffuse, operate at full strength. Such domains exist, they are large, and every functioning market economy already allocates within them substantially by plan, through public provision, regulated monopoly and long-term contracting, which is a fact the sceptical tradition prefers not to dwell on: the argument in those domains was settled by practice long ago, in planning's favour, and the only live question is the quality of the planning.

Infrastructure is the type case. A water network, a road system, a rail network, an electricity grid: each is a physical web whose parts are worthless except in combination, whose capacity must be built ahead of the demand it will serve, and whose services are consumed under conditions of natural monopoly in which competing parallel networks would be an absurdity. The wants are legible without any market's assistance: populations need water at pressures and purities that engineering states, freight and passengers need to move between the places demography and geography fix, and the network's required shape follows from maps and load forecasts rather than from taste. The information is technical and auditable: flows, pressures, failure rates, the physics of the web. The horizon is measured in decades, across which a price signal formed by this quarter's scarcity is nearly meaningless as a guide to what to build. And the errors are asymmetric: a grid built too small fails publicly and dangerously, while capacity built ahead of need merely waits. None of this implies that infrastructure planning is done well wherever it is done; the record of megaproject overruns and politically routed railways is its own literature of failure. It implies that the alternative mechanism, allocation by price among competing providers, is not available in any form that survives inspection, so the comparison the sceptic invites cannot be run. In such a domain the question is never plan or market. It is competent planning or corrupt planning, and the constraints that bind are the ordinary constraints of public administration rather than the epistemic ones of this article.

### Which features of a domain argue for allocating it by plan?

A want that is a need, statable before any choosing occurs; information that is technical and open to audit; a horizon long enough that price signals formed in the present mislead; and errors whose costs fall far more heavily on underprovision than on surplus. Where the four coincide, planning is not a concession but the natural instrument.

Public health provision extends the same logic into services. The need for vaccination, for sanitation, for the treatment of diagnosed illness, is stated by epidemiology and clinical knowledge rather than revealed by purchase, and it is stated for populations, including the populations who would not or could not express it as demand: children, the destitute, the carriers of infections whose treatment protects others more than themselves. The information is technical in the strong sense: incidence, transmission, dosage, the logistics of cold chains and clinic networks, all of it auditable against outcomes that declare themselves regardless of what any return says, since the disease files none. The horizon is the horizon of prevention, whose benefits accrue across decades and accrue largely to people other than the payer, a structure that market demand registers not weakly but perversely, since the beneficiary of a stranger's vaccination cannot be charged for it. And the asymmetry of error is extreme: an underprovided immunisation programme fails in epidemics, while an overprovided one fails in unused capacity and wasted doses, and no society that has experienced both regards the failures as equivalent. Health systems in market economies are accordingly planned in their core, financed collectively, allocated by need as assessed by professionals, with markets admitted at the margins for amenities and electives. The arrangement is so familiar that its nature goes unremarked: it is a standing demonstration that where the domain's features align, allocation by plan is not a utopian proposal but the ordinary practice of societies that consider themselves market economies, defended by electorates that would reject the label while keeping the institution.

## Long horizons: the far future as planning's home ground

A special case of the favourable domain deserves its own treatment, because it is growing in importance and because the market's defect there is structural rather than incidental. Prices are formed by the present's scarcities and the present's expectations, discounted at rates that reflect private impatience and private risk. For decisions whose consequences unfold over generations, the discounting is not a neutral technique but a judgement, and a judgement few would defend if it were stated plainly: that a benefit arriving in sixty years counts for a small fraction of the same benefit arriving now, and a catastrophe at the same distance counts for almost nothing. The long-run transformation of an energy system is the standing example. The decision to rebuild a society's generation, networks and building stock around new sources is a decision about capital with lifetimes measured in decades, taken under uncertainty about technologies still maturing, whose benefits, a stabilised climate and an independent supply, are collective, diffuse and largely bequeathed to people not yet born. There is no preference information for a market to gather here, because the relevant preferences belong to the unborn and to a public that can express them only politically. There is no technical shortcut either: the information required is about costs and feasibilities along paths that must be explored to be known. What the domain requires is exactly what planning, at its best, is: a society stating a destination, committing resources across electoral cycles, coordinating the complementary investments without which each is wasted, a grid without generation, generation without storage, vehicles without charging networks, and holding the course through the interval in which the prices of the old system still signal that nothing need change.

The advocate who presses this case is on strong ground, and the sceptic's standard replies are weaker here than anywhere else. That governments plan energy transitions badly in practice is true and insufficient, since the comparison is with the market's unaided performance, which is not bad but absent: left to price signals formed under the old system's costs, the transition does not happen at the required pace at all, a point conceded across most of the serious literature by the universal resort to subsidy, mandate and public investment, which are planning instruments whatever their sponsors call them. That the destination might be wrongly chosen is also true, and it is the standing risk of all collective choice, to be managed by the ordinary disciplines of politics: stated goals, published reasoning, revisable commitments, elections. What the domain shows, for this article's purposes, is the shape of a fair fight. Where the want is collective and future, where the information is about purposes and technical paths rather than about tastes, where coordination across complementary investments is the binding problem, planning does not merely hold its own. It is the only mechanism on the field, and the market's role, a real one, is subordinate: to execute within the framework, to discover the cheapest means, to discipline the contractors. Framework by decision, execution by price: that division of labour, which Devine's model generalises, is the form planning's strongest case actually takes in the world, and it is a long way from the comprehensive allocation that the popular debate argues about.

### What does a long horizon do to the authority of a price?

A price speaks for the present's scarcities, discounted at the present's rates. Across a horizon of decades the discounting silently converts a social judgement about the future's weight into an arithmetic fact, and the signal's authority drains away: the price is accurate about a question nobody with a long purpose is asking.

The favourable column can now be summarised without remainder. Planning is most defensible where needs are known, horizons are long, and preferences are not the relevant input, which describes infrastructure, public health provision and the long transformation of energy systems, together with the mobilisation case examined earlier, which is the same column under emergency conditions. In those domains the constraints table's hard rows press lightly: the data are technical and auditable, the wants are given by need or by political decision, and the incentive problem shrinks to the ordinary problem of administering public bodies honestly, which is hard enough but is a problem market societies also face in their firms and agencies. It is worth stating plainly what this concession costs the sceptical tradition. If the domain answer is right, then the proposition that planning cannot work is false, refuted by the standing practice of every developed economy in the domains named, and the sceptic must retreat to the weaker claim that it cannot work everywhere, which the advocate of the middle models never disputed. The argument's real frontier lies elsewhere: in the vast domain of ordinary consumer goods, where the features reverse, and it is to that domain, the market's home ground, that the article now turns with the same discipline.

## Where markets are most defensible: variety, novelty and taste

Reverse every feature of the favourable column and the market's home ground appears. A domain favours allocation by price when the wants are preferences rather than needs, heterogeneous across persons, unstable across time, and partly created by the availability of the goods themselves. When the information that matters is preference-dependent, generated only as people choose among the alternatives in front of them. When the horizon is short and the cost of error is a disappointed purchase rather than a failed harvest. And when the variety on offer is not a luxury layered over a standard satisfaction but the substance of what is being provided, as it is in clothing, food beyond subsistence, entertainment, personal services and the whole penumbra of goods by which people express identity and pursue projects nobody else can state for them. In such a domain a plan meets the preference constraint undiluted, with no political shortcut available, because the wants cannot be legislated: a ministry can decree that citizens shall be fed, but it cannot decree what they shall enjoy, and the attempt to supply enjoyment by catalogue produces the historical planned economy's most intimate failure, the shop stocked with what nobody chose, in sizes nobody wore, beside the queue for what everybody wanted.

The point is not merely that tastes are diverse. It is that they are diverse in a high-dimensional space that no return can enumerate. A consumer good is a bundle of characteristics: flavour, texture, colour, cut, weight, scent, association, novelty, the company it keeps on the shelf. Preferences run over the bundles, not over the categories, and the bundles multiply combinatorially as producers differentiate. A plan must choose the bundles in advance, from reports and surveys, which is to say it must guess the shape of a space it cannot observe, and then commit production to the guesses, so that its errors arrive as physical stock, unsold, while its omissions arrive as absence, unrecorded anywhere in its own statistics, since a plan's data describe what was made and distributed, not what would have been wanted. The market's method is the opposite in every particular: it proposes bundles speculatively, in small runs by many rival producers, lets the choosing happen, and reads the result in sales, expanding what was chosen and extinguishing what was not. The method is wasteful in a way the advocate rightly deplores: most new products fail, and the resources in the failures are real. But the waste is the price of the discovery procedure, and no cheaper procedure for discovering the contents of other people's tastes has ever been demonstrated. The plan's apparent economy, producing only what was ordered, is achieved by ordering only what was already known, which is to say by declining to discover anything.

### Why does novelty defeat a plan built from returns?

A return describes what was produced and taken up under the old assortment. A novel good has no return: it appears in no category, its demand exists nowhere as data, and the evidence for it can be generated by offering it and watching the choosing. A system that allocates from its records starves the goods its records cannot describe.

Novelty deserves the emphasis, because it converts the preference problem from a difficulty about measurement into a difficulty about time. Even a perfect census of wants, taken at a single instant with complete honesty from every citizen, would describe only the wants addressed to the goods that exist. The economy's growth in welfare, however, comes substantially from goods that do not yet exist: new medicines in the favourable column, but also new foods, new media, new forms of leisure and convenience whose value nobody could have stated before encountering them. Markets handle novelty by a division of labour between imagination and test: anyone may imagine a good, risk resources on offering it, and submit the imagination to the choosing of strangers, with failure cheap at the level of the system because failures are small and parallel. The entrepreneur in this story, celebrated by the Austrian tradition in the figure of the alert discoverer associated with Israel Kirzner's work, is less a hero than a sensor: the system's way of proposing hypotheses about other people's wants. A comprehensive plan has no equivalent organ. Its proposals come from the same centre that must approve them, its tests are the production runs themselves, large and serial, and its failures are correspondingly public and defended rather than extinguished. The middle models that keep consumer markets are, on this point, simply conceding the organ's necessity. The models that do not keep them owe an account of what replaces it, and the participatory answer, that councils will propose new goods in their rounds, substitutes the imagination of a committee in session for the imagination of anyone at all, a substitution whose poverty the history of designed assortments does nothing to reassure.

None of this requires the market's performance in its home domain to be idealised, and the article's comparison basis forbids it. The market registers wants weighted by money, so the tastes of the rich are discovered lavishly and the tastes of the poor thinly, a distortion the advocate is right to call a moral fact and not merely an informational one. It manufactures wants for profit, degrades attention, rewards the addictive and the positional, and its discovery procedure burns resources on duelling varieties of near-identical goods while leaving unglamorous needs unmet at the domain's edge. All of that stands. The grading question is comparative: whether a plan, facing the same domain, would discover wants better, serve them more equally, and waste less. On discovery, the plan has no mechanism. On equality of service, it has the ration and the queue, which equalise access to a narrower assortment, an equality of a kind, purchased by suppressing the variety in which inequality expressed itself. On waste, it substitutes the waste of the unwanted stockpile and the unmet want for the waste of the failed launch. The verdict for this domain is therefore not that markets are good. It is that the specific epistemic function the domain requires, the continuous discovery of formed-in-choosing wants, is performed by exchange and by nothing else on offer, and a system that abolishes exchange in this domain allocates without the knowledge the domain runs on. That is the strongest sentence the sceptical case can support, and it is a sentence about a domain.

## Standardised bulk goods: the contested middle

Between the columns lies a domain that partisans of both sides claim and that the domain answer assigns to neither outright: standardised bulk goods, the grains, fuels, basic metals, fertilisers, cement and simple chemicals of the industrial base, together with the staple foods whose characteristics vary within narrow bands. Here the wants are needs at one remove, known in aggregate from demography and industrial structure rather than divined from anyone's palate, and the information they run on is largely technical: a tonne of a specified grade is a tonne, whoever produces or consumes it, and quality can be policed by assay rather than by the chooser's delight. Variety is minimal, novelty slow, and the horizon of the capital involved is long. By the features test, the domain leans toward planning, and the historical record leans with it: bulk sectors were the planned economies' least unsuccessful territory, the place where tonnage targets bore some relation to reality and where the system's output, whatever its other failings, was not a fiction. Market economies, for their part, organise these sectors through a handful of large firms and long contracts that resemble private planning more than they resemble the textbook's swarm of competitors, and the prices that do form are formed on exchanges whose function is closer to a clearing house for standardised lots than to a discovery procedure for wants.

### What separates bulk goods from consumer goods in this argument?

A bulk good's identity is exhausted by its specification: grade, purity, dimension, delivered quantity. Whoever can verify the specification can allocate the good, and verification is a technical act. A consumer good's identity includes its appeal, which no specification captures and only choosing verifies, so the allocating knowledge differs in kind, not merely in amount.

Yet the middle domain is not planning's free gift, because the data row operates there in its classic form. The bulk plan is exactly where the ratchet and the padded return did their historical work: capacities understated, requirements overstated, quality shaded within the tolerance of the assay, tonnage achieved by weight rather than by usefulness, the nail produced by the tonne that the literature of the old system made into a parable. The domain's lesson is that technical information is necessary but not sufficient: the coefficients may be honest facts of engineering while the reports of them remain strategic acts, because the reporter's reward still hangs on the report. Bulk allocation by plan works tolerably where an independent audit can check the physical flows against the paper, where the assortment is too narrow for quality shading to hide in, and where the plan's targets are set with slack for the shocks the reporters fear. It works badly where the audit is captured, the assortment creeps, or the centre treats the returns as measurements rather than as moves. The middle domain thus grades out as genuinely contested: allocable by either mechanism with characteristic failures, plan failing by corrupted information and rigidity, market failing by concentration, speculation and the cycle. A verdict that assigned it wholly to either camp would be a slogan. The domain answer assigns it conditionally, and the conditions are institutional, about audit and slack, which is to say about the unglamorous machinery that neither camp's rhetoric mentions.

## The strongest objection: the data are generated by the process the planner replaces

Every argument so far converges on one objection, and the cluster to which this article belongs requires that it be stated at full strength, attributed, and answered without evasion. The objection, in the form developed by Hayek and sharpened by the critics of the computational revival, runs as follows. The allocation problem is not a computation problem and never was, because computation operates on data, and the data an allocation system needs are generated by the very process the planner proposes to replace. Relative scarcities are not facts lying in the world awaiting collection; they are precipitates of rivalrous bidding, in which each user's local knowledge of circumstances, much of it tacit, is brought to bear and summarised in a price. Wants are not entries in a registry; they precipitate out of choosing among priced alternatives. Abolish the process and you do not inherit its data in a filing cabinet. You inherit silence, and every mechanism proposed for breaking the silence either reintroduces the incentive problem, by asking parties to report data on which their rewards depend, or reintroduces the market, by using prices to elicit what the plan was supposed to supersede. Faster computation of absent data is not an answer to this objection. It is a restatement of the problem in more expensive terms: a quicker route from premises nobody possesses to conclusions nobody can check.

The objection should be felt at its full weight before any answer is attempted, because the answers available are partial and the article's honesty depends on not inflating them. Notice first that the objection is not empirical in the simple sense. It does not rest on the Soviet record, although the record illustrates it lavishly; it would stand even if the record were kinder, because it is a claim about where knowledge comes from. Notice second that it is not answered by pointing to the genuine successes of planning in the favourable domains, since its proponents can grant those domains and do: the objection is about the general case, the allocation of a whole economy's resources among competing uses and competing wants, and it claims that in the general case the knowledge does not pre-exist the process. Notice third that the objection's either-or, incentives or markets, has the structure of a trap with real teeth. The participatory rounds elicit by consultation, and consultation is a reporting procedure, so the incentive row applies to it. The market socialist models elicit by prices, and prices are the market, so the objection's second horn applies to them, and their authors, to their credit, mostly accept the description and deny that it wounds them: yes, they say, we keep the market for goods, because the knowledge argument is sound there, and we socialise what the knowledge argument does not protect, ownership and investment. The objection thus functions in the serious literature less as a refutation than as a border guard, marking the line beyond which proposals must either pay the incentive toll or confess the market's retention. Its full-strength statement is the one this section has given. Its answer, such as it is, comes next, and it begins by separating the constraints the objection fuses.

## The answer: separating the constraints, and locating where computation binds

The answer has three movements, and only the third is a concession, although the concession is the one that matters most. The first movement is separation. The objection treats the data problem as one problem, but the constraints table shows it is three, and they do not stand or fall together. Computation was a real constraint once, and the objection's classical form, which relied on the practical impossibility of the calculation, has been overtaken: the arithmetic binds no longer, and where the required information is technical and given, computation is exactly the constraint that technical progress relaxed, as the grid operator and the logistics network demonstrate daily. An objection that was framed when calculation itself was dubious must be re-based on its surviving rows, and its defenders, Hayek foremost among them in his later work, did re-base it, on knowledge rather than arithmetic. The re-based objection is the strong one stated above. But the re-basing matters, because it confines the objection's force to the rows where knowledge is generated by process: preference-dependent information above all, and strategically reported information wherever rewards hang on reports. Technical information, the coefficients and capacities of the production web, is not generated by exchange; it is generated by engineering and recorded by accounting, and exchange merely prices it. A plan that confines itself to domains where technical information dominates therefore does not meet the objection at all, which is why the objection's proponents, pressed on infrastructure or immunisation, retreat to complaints about bureaucratic quality rather than impossibility. The objection, properly scoped, is a claim about kinds of knowledge, and kinds of knowledge sort by domain.

The second movement concerns the substitute generators, where the answer is genuinely open. Deliberation, consultation rounds, negotiation among affected parties, democratic priority-setting: these are not nothing. They generate a kind of knowledge that markets cannot generate at all, knowledge about collective purposes, about what a society is willing to sacrifice for what, about the distribution of burdens that citizens will accept as fair. A referendum cannot price a tonne of steel, but neither can a price express a society's judgement that its coastline matters more than its convenience, and the second kind of ignorance is as real as the first. The models that build on deliberation are therefore not merely evading the objection; they are answering a different ignorance, one the market suffers from constitutionally. Where the two ignorances overlap, in investment, in the framework decisions, the contest between the mechanisms is a genuine contest, and the article has graded it as such. Where they do not overlap, in the daily discovery of consumer wants, deliberation is not a substitute generator but a category error, a town meeting asked to do the work of a million acts of choosing, and the participatory model's exposure on exactly that point has been recorded.

Then the concession, made cleanly as this cluster's method requires. For the general case of a whole economy, the objection stands. No proposed model has answered it: the ones that keep markets have answered it by surrendering the territory it defends, which is a settlement and not a rebuttal, and the one that contests the territory, participatory economics, rests its answer on a conjecture about deliberative behaviour that has never been tested at the scale the model requires. Faster computation does not answer it, for the reason the objection gives: the computation was never the site of the difficulty, and a demonstration that the sums can be done, however rigorous, leaves the question of the data exactly where it was. The concession is not the end of the argument, because the general case is not the whole of the argument. It is the end of one claim, the claim that the planning question has been solved in general by technical progress. What survives the concession is everything else in this article: the domain verdict, the models as settlements of varying depth, and the standing challenge to the sceptic to defend the market in the domains where its own information runs out. A debate in which the strongest objection stands against the general case and fails against the specified domains is a debate the domain answer has already described. The objection and the answer are the same shape, seen from opposite ends.

### Where does computation bind, if not at the economy's scale?

It binds wherever the data are already in the room: inside firms and networks whose operations generate their own records, in grids and logistics and scheduling, where the objective is stated and the information is technical. There its relaxation has been real and large. The economy-wide case differs because the data are not in the room.

## The verdict defeater: what would reverse the domain verdict

This cluster requires every verdict to carry the condition of its own reversal, and the requirement is not decorative. A verdict that cannot state what would defeat it is a posture, and the planning debate has suffered from postures on both sides for a century. The verdict of this article is the domain verdict: that each territory has its own binding constraint, that planning outperforms markets where needs are known, horizons long and preferences not the relevant input, that markets outperform planning where wants are taste-like, novel and formed in choosing, and that the general question therefore has no general answer. That verdict would be defeated, first, by a demonstration that the constraints bind uniformly across domains: evidence, that is, that the data and preference problems press as hard on an immunisation programme or a grid as they press on a consumer catalogue, or as lightly on the catalogue as on the grid, so that the sorting this article performs collapses into a single grade for a single mechanism. It would be defeated, second, by construction: a planning implementation at national scale in a high-variety consumer economy, sustaining over years a responsiveness to changing taste at the level markets achieve, with assortments that turn over as market assortments do and shortages no worse than the market's own failures. Either exhibit would do the work. The first would dissolve the domain structure analytically; the second would refute it in the only court this subject finally recognises, operation.

The brief for this article states the defeater from the constraints side, and it should be recorded alongside, since the two forms are the same challenge read from opposite ends. The verdict that data generation and preference revelation remain binding would be defeated by a working implementation, at national scale and over several years, in which the information required for allocation was produced by parties whose rewards did not depend on it, or by evidence that preference formation does not depend on the choice mechanism. Neither exhibit, in either form, has been produced. The implementations that exist are the comprehensive one, whose reporters' rewards depended entirely on their reports, and the domain-limited ones inside market economies, whose information arrives from the surrounding process. The evidence about preference formation runs, if anything, the other way: each decade's study of consumption finds wants more entangled with the choosing environment, not less. The verdict therefore stands, and it stands in its limited, sorted form, which is the only form this article has ever claimed for it. What the defeater's statement adds is a discipline for the reader: when a future system is exhibited as the answer to this debate, the questions to ask of it are already written. Whose rewards depended on its data. How it learned what people wanted. In which domains it was run, and for how long. A system that survives those questions will have earned the reversal. Until one does, the domain answer is where the argument rests.

## The computational proposal whole: what its authors actually build

The computational argument is usually debated as a claim about arithmetic, but its authors built a whole system, and the system's other parts reveal where its designers felt the pressure. In Cockshott and Cottrell's design, consumer goods are not allocated by the plan at all in the first instance. They are sold, in shops, for labour tokens earned by work and cancelled on use, at prices the planning authority adjusts against observed sales: a good that accumulates is marked down, a good that vanishes from shelves is marked up, and the detailed plan for the next period is drawn toward what the adjusted prices and the sales record indicate. The mechanism will be recognised at once. It is Lange's trial and error, automated: prices as instruments for eliciting consumer wants, revised by shortage and surplus, with the plan following the signals rather than replacing them. The design's honesty is instructive. Confronted with the preference row, the computational proposal does not propose to compute wants. It proposes to keep a market-like eliciting device for consumer goods, differing from an ordinary market in the token's non-transferability and in the plan's ownership of the producing enterprises, and to reserve direct planning for the structure beneath: the allocation of investment and of intermediate goods, where the input-output machinery does its work.

That division locates the proposal on the article's map with precision, and it clarifies what the computational revival actually claims once its rhetoric is discounted. The claim is not that the whole economy can be allocated from returns. The claim is that the intermediate web, the vast inter-industry structure that no consumer ever sees, can be computed directly once final demand is known, and that final demand can be elicited by an administered price mechanism rather than by private exchange. The enterprise data problem remains inside the design, addressed by its authors with proposals for direct recording of production and for democratic accountability of managers, proposals whose adequacy the record of every reporting system invites the reader to doubt. And the investment decision, who decides the growth and direction of the capital stock, is assigned to democratic politics operating through the plan, which returns the design to the hardest information of all, forecasts by interested parties, at exactly the point where its arithmetic is most impressive. None of this diminishes the design's seriousness. It is the most fully worked proposal in the field, and its authors engage their critics in detail. But its structure confirms the article's thesis from the advocate's side: even the flagship of comprehensive planning, rebuilt with modern computation, elicits its wants through prices and reserves its computation for the technical web. The general case, allocation of everything from data that the centre gathers for itself, is not defended even by the writers most often cited as having defended it.

### What does the flagship computational design do about consumer wants?

It declines to compute them. Consumer goods are sold for labour tokens at prices adjusted against sales, so wants are elicited by a market-like device and the plan follows the signals. Direct computation is reserved for the inter-industry web and for investment, where the design meets its hardest problems in other forms.

## Scale: does size change the constraints, or only their weight?

A recurring move in the debate treats scale as the master variable: planning failed, says one side, because economies outgrew the centre's span, and computation restores the span; planning is unnecessary, says the other, because markets scale without limit while administrations choke on their own paperwork. The constraints table suggests a more differentiated answer. Computation is the one constraint whose tightness genuinely varies with scale, and in the counterintuitive direction established earlier: the larger the economy, the sparser its table, the cheaper the iteration relative to the whole. Data generation tightens with scale for a different reason: each additional layer between the centre and the enterprise is another reporter with an interest and another translation in which local knowledge is flattened into categories, and the flattening is not a failure of diligence but of representation, since the categories must be fixed in advance by the centre that designed the form. Preference revelation is nearly scale-invariant in structure, since a want is formed at a shelf whether the shelf is in a village or a metropolis, but its aggregate weight grows with the variety that scale makes possible: a small economy's consumers want fewer kinds of thing, and a plan that starves variety starves less where variety was scarce to begin with. This is one reason the historical planned economies performed least badly in their early, poorer decades and worst as their citizens' wants differentiated, a pattern the domain answer predicts and a pure scale story cannot explain.

The scale question has a second face, on the market's side, which even-handedness requires stating. Markets also degrade with scale in specific ways: the price of a complex financial instrument in a vast and interconnected system may encode less knowledge and more imitation than the price of wheat in a local market, and the discovery procedure for wants works worst exactly where products are engineered to be incomparable and choices are mediated by platforms whose own incentives distort the choosing. The domain answer absorbs these observations without strain, since it never claimed that markets perform uniformly; it claimed that which constraint binds is a property of the territory, and a domain in which market signals have been corrupted by the architecture of choosing is a domain where the comparison must be run again on the facts. What the scale discussion rules out is the lazy inference in both directions: from the failure of planning at Soviet scale to its failure at the scale of a water network, and from the success of coordination inside a vast firm to its success at the scale of a nation. Scale changes the weight of the constraints, differently for each row, and a writer who treats it as a single dial has not understood the table the scale is supposed to be turning.

## The mathematics that planning already had: Kantorovich and the shadow price

One episode from inside the old system deserves record because it refutes a lazy assumption shared by both camps, that the planned economy failed for want of mathematical sophistication. In 1939 the Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich published the founding work of linear programming, a method for computing optimal allocations under constraints, developed in response to a practical problem of organising production in a plywood trust. The method yields, as a by-product of its solution, a set of valuations for the scarce resources involved, one for each constraint, measuring what an additional unit of each resource would be worth to the plan's fulfilment. These resolving multipliers, as Kantorovich called them, are shadow prices in everything but name: the plan's own arithmetic generates price-like signals from within, without any market. The discovery was neglected for years in its home country, partly because its implications sat awkwardly with the doctrine that prices under socialism were mere accounting conventions, and Kantorovich's eventual recognition, including a Nobel Prize shared with Tjalling Koopmans in 1975, came when the system that might have used his method was least able to reform itself around it.

The episode's bearing on the contemporary argument is double-edged, which is why it belongs in an honest audit. Against the sceptic, it shows that the computational tradition inside planning was deep and early, and that the mature form of the computational claim, the plan computing its own scarcity valuations, was available in principle generations before the hardware. The binding constraint on the Soviet economy was never the absence of Kantorovich's method; the method existed, was understood by the country's best economists, and was applied at the margins in transport and scheduling where the data were physical. Against the advocate, the episode shows with unusual clarity what the binding constraint was. Shadow prices are computed from the plan's data, and they inherit the data's corruption: a resolving multiplier derived from padded returns and falsified capacities is a precise valuation of a fictitious economy. Worse, the multipliers answer the question the plan poses, the fulfilment of its stated targets, and the targets themselves embody the centre's wants, not the population's, so the elegance of the internal pricing coexists with the external absurdity of the assortment. The mathematics was never the missing organ. The missing organs were truthful data and a want-registering process, and Kantorovich's fate, honoured abroad and marginal at home, is the historical emblem of the article's central distinction: a system can possess the computation and starve for the information.

### What do a plan's shadow prices actually measure?

They measure the value of relaxing each constraint within the plan as stated: what one more unit of a scarce input would contribute to fulfilling the targets the centre has set. They are genuine scarcity signals internal to the calculation, and they inherit both the corruption of the returns beneath them and the purposes of the targets above them.

## Investment: the allocation where every constraint meets

If one class of decision concentrates all the rows of the table at once, it is investment: the commitment of resources to capacities that will operate in a future nobody can inspect. Investment data are forecasts, which is to say reports about counterfactuals, filed by parties whose projects live or die by the filing. Investment wants are collective and contested, since the future being built is a future for everyone, including those who would have built a different one. Investment errors are the least reversible in economics, embodied in concrete and steel that will stand for decades as either an asset or a monument. And investment is the decision on which the socialist tradition has always staked its claim, since the market's direction of investment by expected private profit is, for that tradition, the mechanism by which the future is mortgaged to the past's distribution of wealth. Any model of planning that cannot say how investment is allocated has declined the tradition's central question, and any model that can say it has volunteered for the hardest examination in the subject.

The market's own record under that examination is mixed enough to keep the contest honest. Private investment discovers new industries with unmatched fertility, because the discovery procedure of parallel speculative bets, most of them failing, is the same procedure that discovers new goods, applied to the future. It also herds: into property booms whose collapse idles the capacity it built, into duplicating the fashionable sector while unglamorous maintenance goes unfunded, into horizons shortened by the discount rate and by the career length of the fund manager. The planned alternative substitutes a single portfolio for the parallel bets, chosen by deliberation, which abolishes the herding and the duplication at the price of abolishing the parallelism: when the single portfolio is wrong, it is wrong at national scale and with no surviving rival approach from which to learn. Schweickart's public banks and Devine's negotiated framework are attempts to reintroduce pluralism inside the socialised decision, many lenders, many negotiating tables, precisely because their authors saw that the epistemic virtue of the market's investment process is not profit but multiplicity. The grading that follows from the table is therefore not a verdict for either mechanism but a specification of what a good one would look like: multiple independent judges of forecasts, each with something real at stake in being right, a social statement of the purposes the portfolio must serve, and a procedure by which failed directions are abandoned rather than defended. Neither the unaided market nor the comprehensive plan matches that specification. The market lacks the statement of purposes; the plan lacks the independent judges and the abandonment. The models that grope toward the specification are the debate's live frontier, and they are frontier precisely because the information investment needs, knowledge of futures, is generated by no existing process in adequate form. On investment, uniquely, both systems are improvisations, and the honest article says so.

## Quality: the dimension neither mechanism measures well

Quantity is the native language of plans and the first language of prices, and quality is the dimension on which both mechanisms stumble, in instructively different ways. A plan specifies quality in standards and inspects against them, and the history of that method is a history of the specification being gamed: the measurable dimensions of the product swell to meet the standard while the unmeasured ones, finish, durability, fit, flavour, the courtesy of the service wrapped around the good, drain away, because the inspectorate can only police what the standard names and the standard can only name what can be written down. The planned economies' goods were not merely scarce; they were, in the dimensions nobody could specify, bad, and the badness was systematic rather than accidental, the predictable output of an information system that received quantities faithfully and qualities barely at all. A market handles quality by the chooser's inspection and the seller's reputation, which works where quality reveals itself at or before purchase and fails where it reveals itself late, in the medicine that does not cure, the repair that does not hold, the financial product whose risk matures in a decade. The failures differ in timing and the successes differ in mechanism, but the shared lesson matters for the domain audit: quality is preference-dependent information of a particularly elusive kind, since the chooser often cannot state in advance which dimensions will matter, and any allocation system must find a channel for it or watch it evaporate. Markets channel it through repeat purchase and brand, imperfectly and at the cost of rewarding the appearance of quality where appearance is cheaper than the thing. Plans channel it through standards, with the opposite imperfection, freezing the dimensions at the moment the standard was drafted. A model that does not say where quality information enters has a hole in it that no amount of computation covers, and the hole is one more reason the comprehensive design, whose standards must be drafted for everything at once, bleeds quality across the whole catalogue while a market bleeds it only where inspection fails.

## The ecological case: a want that only politics can state

The long-horizon argument of the energy section generalises, and its general form deserves statement because it is the favourable column's deepest instance. The natural environment presents an allocation problem in which every feature that defeats markets is present at maximum strength. The wants at stake belong substantially to future persons who cannot bid. The damages fall on third parties who were not consulted by the transaction that caused them. The timescales exceed every discounting convention in private use. And the information required is not preference-dependent in the market's sense at all: the carrying capacity of a fishery, the absorptive capacity of an atmosphere, the recharge rate of an aquifer are technical facts, knowable by science, statable in advance, indifferent to anyone's taste. A society that leaves such allocation to prices alone has not declined to plan; it has adopted, by default, the plan implicit in the existing distribution of property and the existing schedule of prices, which allocates the commons to whoever can take from it first and charges the cost to whoever arrives later. The explicit alternative, a politically stated budget for the use of the commons, allocated by permit, standard or public provision, is planning in the strict sense of this article, and it is practised, in fragments, by every state that regulates a fishery or caps an emission. The fragments' incompleteness is a political fact, not an epistemic one: nobody argues that the information for a fuller version is unknowable in the way consumer wants are unknowable. The ecological case therefore sharpens the domain answer's edge. Where the constraint on good allocation is that the want must be stated politically because no choosing process can state it, planning is not one option among others. It is the form the decision takes when it is taken deliberately at all, and the market's role is again the subordinate one of finding cheap means inside a stated budget.

### Why can a market not reveal a society's environmental wants?

Because the parties to the relevant wants are absent from every transaction: the unborn cannot bid, the damaged third party is not at the counter, and the resource itself, a fishery or an atmosphere, has no owner to refuse a bad price. The want exists, but the only process that can state it is a political one.

## Housing: a mixed domain, and what its mixture teaches

Housing is the domain where the two columns interpenetrate most completely, and for that reason it is the best training case for the domain method. Shelter is a need, as basic as any the favourable column contains: its absence is destitution, its minimum standard is statable in space, warmth, sanitation and security of tenure, and a society can state the floor politically and verify provision by inspection. But a dwelling is also a bundle of taste and location and association, saturated with preference: the street, the light, the neighbours, the commute, the feel of a district, dimensions no specification captures and no two households weight alike. And land, housing's essential input, is fixed in the places people need to be, so the market for it allocates not only shelter but access to cities, schools and work, generating the exclusion and speculation that make housing the standing scandal of market economies. A comprehensive plan for housing produces the floor reliably and the rest badly: the historical record of planned housing is vast quantities of standardised dwelling, allocated by queue and category, in districts whose monotony their residents spent decades escaping, alongside waiting lists that rationed by patience what prices would have rationed by wealth. A pure market produces the variety and the responsiveness and fails the floor entirely, leaving the need unmet where need has no money and converting the city's land into an asset market whose returns depend on keeping shelter scarce.

The domain method does not split this difference lazily. It sorts the decisions. The floor, the guarantee that every household reaches the stated minimum, is a need and belongs to public provision or to entitlement, planned and audited. The land, whose supply is fixed and whose value is created by the surrounding society, is a framework decision of the Devine type, where the community's claim on the increment it creates is a political judgement no price can make. The variety above the floor, the endless differentiation of dwelling by taste and project, is preference-dependent and belongs to choosing, in a market disciplined by the floor beneath it and the framework around it. Each layer is governed by the instrument suited to the knowledge that layer runs on, and the failures of real housing systems can usually be traced to a layer governed by the wrong instrument: floors left to markets, variety dictated by ministries, land increments left to whoever fenced them first. The case generalises, and it is the generalisation the domain answer finally rests on. Most real domains are mixtures. The skill the debate requires is not allegiance to a mechanism but the sorting of a domain's decisions by the kind of knowledge each decision needs, and the refusal, which both camps find uncongenial, to let one mechanism's triumph in its layer colonise the others.

## Education: needs, tastes and the formation of the chooser

Education presses the mixture one level deeper, because it is the domain where the chooser's preferences are not merely formed by the process, as with consumer goods, but are the process's intended product. A society does not consult the existing preferences of children to decide whether literacy shall be provided; it provides literacy in order to create persons whose preferences will be worth consulting. The need layer is therefore unusually thick: the capabilities a citizen requires, reading, numeracy, the elements of science and of the society's own history, are statable in advance by a political and professional judgement, and their provision is planned in every developed society, financed collectively and allocated without price, with the market admitted, where it is admitted, at the edges. Above the need layer, the domain differentiates: aptitudes diverge, vocations beckon, tastes for kinds of knowledge form in the encountering, and a system that planned the whole of education as it plans the floor would flatten exactly the diversity that the floor exists to make possible. The parallel with the consumer domain is exact in structure and reversed in moral weight. There, the formation of wants by the serving system was a reason for suspicion of the former. Here, formation is the purpose, openly avowed, and the argument is about who forms: the state through a curriculum, the market through the price of tuition, the family through its resources, the student through choosing among offerings whose existence somebody else decided. No allocation escapes the question. The domain answer's contribution is to insist that the question be asked layer by layer, floor by plan, superstructure by choosing within a framework, framework by politics, rather than by the wholesale slogans, public good or parental choice, in which the education debate is usually conducted. A reader who has followed the argument this far will recognise the method operating on new material without assistance, which is the test of whether the article's instrument works: the constraints table and the domain sorting are meant to be carried away and used on domains the article never mentions.

## The boundary problem: every model keeps a market somewhere

Stand back from the four models and a structural fact comes into view that the debate's rhetoric obscures. Every serious design keeps a market somewhere, and every serious market economy plans somewhere, so the actual argument is never between systems but between boundaries: where the line between allocation by decision and allocation by price is drawn, who draws it, and what patrols it. Roemer draws it at ownership. Schweickart draws it at the firm's door and the investment fund. Devine draws it between framework and particular. Albert and Hahnel draw it furthest out, at the boundary of the planning procedure itself, and even their design retains money-like instruments, effort ratings and indicative prices, that perform eliciting work. On the other side, every state that calls itself a market economy draws lines constantly: the central bank sets the price of money by committee, the grid is dispatched by instruction, the hospital's beds are allocated by triage, the army is not recruited by auction. The boundary is the politics. A debate conducted as if one side proposed a world without prices and the other a world without plans is a debate about two places that have never existed, and its interminability is explained by that fact: positions about imaginary worlds cannot be refuted by evidence from the real one.

### Who patrols the boundary between plan and market in a mixed system?

In practice, politics does, badly and continuously: legislatures move activities across the line in both directions, courts police the crossings, and the line's location in any decade records the balance of interests and ideas rather than any theorem. The article's claim is only that the line should be drawn by the information test, decision by decision.

Drawing the boundary by the information test is a more demanding discipline than drawing it by ownership, by tradition or by the relative lobbying strength of the affected industries, which are the methods actually in use. The test asks, of each class of decision, the twin questions the student section commended: from where does a decision draw its technical knowledge, and from where its knowledge of wants. Where both arise inside a choosing process that already exists, leave the decision to the process and spend public effort on the process's fairness, the distribution of the money that weights the choosing. Where the preference information does not exist because the want is collective, future or a need, take the decision into politics and planning, and spend the effort on the quality of the deliberation and the audit of the execution. Where the technical information is corrupted by the reporting relation, fix the relation before extending the plan that feeds on it. The test does not mechanise judgement; hard cases, housing and education among them, remain hard, and reasonable auditors will sort some decisions differently. But it converts an ideological confrontation into an audit with stated criteria, which is the most that method can ever do for a political argument, and it explains, retrospectively, why the practice of actual societies converges on mixtures that their rhetoric disowns: the mixtures are the information test's output, reached by trial and error rather than by theory, and the theory's contribution is to make the output examinable and the errors correctable in the open.

## The concessions, audited: what each side has already granted

The debate's real state can be read off the concessions each side has made when arguing carefully, as opposed to when campaigning. The advocates of planning have conceded, in their serious models, that consumer wants are elicited by price-like mechanisms or not elicited at all; that enterprise data cannot be taken on trust from interested reporters; that the arithmetic was never their strongest ground and the ground has now been taken by the other side's concession; and that the comprehensive design their tradition once defended is not being re-proposed by anyone whose work this article has had to grade. The sceptics have conceded, in their serious literature, that the calculation is performable and was conceded to be performable in principle from the debate's first decade; that whole domains of modern economies are allocated by plan with results they do not propose to reverse; that the market's information is weighted by wealth, deaf to the unborn and to third parties, and formed partly by the sellers it is supposed to discipline; and that the strongest objection, the generation of knowledge by process, is a domain-limited instrument that does not touch needs, frameworks or futures. Set the two lists side by side and the remaining disagreement is visible at its true size. It concerns the sorting of the contested middle: investment, bulk production, the framework decisions, and the pace at which deliberative substitutes for price might earn promotion out of the unproven column. That is a real disagreement, worth the books it fills. It is not the disagreement the public debate performs, and a reader who can state the two concession lists has understood the contemporary planning question better than most of its performers.

## The domain answer: the claim this article is built on

The namable claim can now be stated in its full form, with the grading behind it. The domain answer holds that planning already outperforms markets in specified domains, that markets outperform planning in others, and that the general question has no general answer, because the binding constraint differs by domain. In the domains of known need, long horizon and collective purpose, infrastructure, public health, the transformation of energy systems, the protection of commons, mobilisation in emergency, the market's information machine has nothing to gather and its vices operate unchecked, and planning, for all its administrative frailty, is the stronger instrument and the one actually in use. In the domains of taste, variety and novelty, the consumer catalogue in its endless differentiation, the plan's information does not exist until a choosing process generates it, and markets, for all their weighting by wealth and their manufacture of the wants they serve, are the only generator on offer. In the contested middle, bulk production and investment above all, the outcome depends on institutions: on whether audit can keep reported data honest, on whether investment judgement is plural and corrigible, on whether the framework within which prices work has been set by a politics that knows what it is doing. The answer is not a compromise in the sense of a splitting of differences. It is a refusal of the question's grammar, on stated grounds, with a replacement question offered in its place: not whether planning works, but where, under what informational conditions, with what defeater standing over the verdict.

### How should the general question be graded when it is asked?

As a category error with an instructive structure. The question assumes a single contest with a single result, while the evidence describes several contests with different results, decided by which constraint binds in each. A strong answer declines the general verdict, performs the sorting in the open, and states what evidence would overturn the sorting.

The domain answer also explains a pattern in the debate's history that neither camp's narrative accommodates: the argument never ends, and its terms keep migrating. It never ends because each side's evidence is genuine in its own domain, so each refutation, carried across the boundary into the other side's territory, fails, and the failure is misread as the other side's bad faith. Its terms migrate because the boundary itself moves with technology and institutions: computation moved the first row of the table in a generation, and other movements, in the recording of transactions, in the design of deliberative bodies, in the audit of organisations, may move others, which is why this article has stated its verdict with a defeater rather than as a law. The verdict is a snapshot of where the constraints bind under the mechanisms so far proposed, taken by an auditor who expects to be superseded and has said by what. That expectation is not modesty. It is the difference between an assessment and a creed, and it is the difference this cluster of critiques exists to teach, on this question as on every other it examines: hold the verdict, name its defeater, and let the next evidence do its work.

## What would settle the planning question

A debate that cannot say what would settle it is not a debate but a liturgy, and this one can say it, at three levels of strength. At the weakest level, the question would be advanced by honest domain experiments: allocation systems of the middle type, operated at the scale of a city, a sector or a public service, over years, with their data practices and their failures published, so that the conjectures about peer scrutiny, deliberative elicitation and socialised investment could graduate from argument to evidence. Nothing in principle prevents such experiments except the political difficulty of running them and the scholarly difficulty of comparing them, and their absence, decades into the models' existence, is itself a datum about the debate's seriousness on both sides: camps that preferred verdicts to knowledge have not demanded the trials that might have supplied it. At the middle level, the defeater's exhibits would advance it in either direction: proof that the constraints press equally in every territory, dissolving the domain structure, or a deliberative eliciting mechanism shown to work under audit at scale. At the strongest level, the question would be settled in the only way large social questions ever are, by a society running a model long enough for its information practices to mature and its record to be read against a neighbour's, the divided-country comparison that settled so much else in this series' territory. None of the three has occurred for the middle models. The question therefore remains, in the precise sense, open: not undecidable, not a matter of taste, but awaiting evidence that identifiable events would supply. An article that ends by specifying the missing evidence, rather than by pretending to possess it, has done the subject the only service print can do it.

### What is the cheapest experiment the debate has not run?

A middle model operated at municipal or sectoral scale for a run of years, with its reporting practices open to inspection and its assortment, shortage and satisfaction outcomes published against a comparable market-run service. The cost is small beside the debate's volume; the yield would be the first direct evidence about deliberative data generation.

## Misreadings to correct

Five misreadings organise most of the confusion in the coverage of this question, and each has been corrected in place in the body of the article; they are gathered here because a reader who can recite the corrections owns the topic's defensive perimeter. The first misreading holds that computation was ever the whole objection, a belief that requires forgetting Barone's early concession and Hayek's explicit migration of the argument from arithmetic to knowledge. The second holds that computational tractability answers the knowledge argument, which confuses the processing of data with the generation of it, the article's founding distinction. The third holds that the Soviet record tests the proposed models, a category error, since none of the models was operated there, although the record tests the constraints the models must survive. The fourth holds that internal corporate planning is planning without markets, when it is planning inside markets, fed by their prices and judged by their customers. The fifth holds that the question has one answer across all domains, the assumption the domain answer exists to retire, and the only one of the five that both camps share, since each camp's general verdict requires it. A treatment of the planning question that avoids all five is already ahead of most of what is published on it, whatever verdict it then defends.

## Precision work: the terms that must not blur

Four words carry this argument, and the debate's confusion is substantially the history of their blurring. Calculation is the manipulation of given symbols according to rules: it is what the iterative method does to the table, and it is cheap, exact and indifferent to the symbols' truth. Data are recorded observations, the returns, the sales figures, the coefficients, and they have the property calculation lacks: they can be wrong, stale, shaded or fabricated, and their quality is a social achievement rather than a mathematical one. Information is data that reduces someone's uncertainty about a decision, a relational property: the same return is information to the bureau that lacked it and noise to the manager who wrote it. Knowledge is the deepest term and the one Hayek's argument turns on: the capacity to act appropriately in circumstances, much of it tacit, embodied in skill and local acquaintance, incapable of being fully stated by its holder, and therefore incapable of being fully transmitted to any centre by any reporting procedure, however honest. The planning problem, stated with the terms kept apart, is that calculation has become cheap, data remain corruptible, information must be generated by procedures with their own incentives, and knowledge, in its tacit mass, cannot travel at all, only be used where it sits. Systems differ in how much of each they need. A market needs little knowledge to travel, since it uses knowledge in place and transmits only its precipitate, the price. A comprehensive plan needs knowledge to travel in bulk, and the need is its wound. The models can each be redescribed in this vocabulary in a sentence, and the redescription is a fair examination exercise: Roemer minimises travel, Schweickart localises it in the firm and socialises only the forecast, Devine pools it at the framework table, Albert and Hahnel attempt to make it travel by deliberation, and the computational design replaces its travel with recording wherever recording is possible. The blurring of the four terms is not a pedant's concern. Every popular proof that planning has been solved trades on sliding from calculation to knowledge mid-sentence, and every popular proof that it is impossible trades on sliding back.

## The reader's instrument: grading claims that arrive wearing this debate's clothes

The claims a reader will actually meet arrive as journalism and as posts: a new computational feat announced as the solution to the planning problem, a corporate logistics triumph exhibited as the economy in miniature, a historical anniversary recruited for a verdict. The article's instrument grades them with four questions, in order. Which constraint does the claim address: if the answer is computation, the claim concerns a row already relaxed, and its news value is confined there. Where does the system's data come from, and what do the data's authors gain by the content of their reports: if the claim is silent, the silence is the finding. How are wants registered, and what happens to a want for a good that does not yet exist: if the mechanism registers wants only for the assortment already on offer, the claim's economy is a museum of the present. And in which domain was the performance observed, and over how long: if the territory is narrow, the purposes handed to the system and the run short, the claim's generality is borrowed. A claim that survives the four questions with its scope intact is a contribution. A claim that shrinks under them to its proper domain has been graded, not refuted, and grading, this article has argued throughout, is the operation the subject needs and the one its loudest voices omit. The instrument is offered to the reader as the article's portable residue, usable on material the article never anticipated, which is the only test of a method that matters.

## Sources and their standing: what the assessment rests on

The assessment rests on a small shelf, and its standing should be visible. For the computational claim, the founding text is the 1993 book by Cockshott and Cottrell, Towards a New Socialism, with the authors' later papers refining the method and answering critics; it is a primary source, written by the proposal's designers, and it has been read here as designers' evidence, credited for its concessions as much as for its claims. For the knowledge objection, the primary text is Hayek's essay of 1945, supplemented by the later Austrian development in the work of Israel Kirzner on discovery; these are the objection's own statements, graded as arguments rather than as authorities. For the models, the primary texts are the four books themselves: Roemer in 1994, Schweickart in 2002, Albert and Hahnel in 1991, Devine in 1988, each summarised in its author's terms before audit. For the sceptical synthesis, Nove's The Economics of Feasible Socialism of 1983 represents the position of a scholar working from the record of the planned economies rather than from either camp's theory. For the Chilean episode, the article relies on the standard historical accounts of Stafford Beer's project and treats all retrospective verdicts on it, favourable and hostile, as advocacy rather than evidence, for the reasons given in its section. The replies to the standard critiques, as a body, are assessed in [the series' article on the Marxist replies](/2019/12/01/marxist-replies-to-critiques/), where several of the arguments handled here reappear in their defensive form; the present article's independence of that assessment is deliberate, since a critique graded by its targets' replies alone would inherit their framing.

## What this article has deliberately not done

The omissions are principled and should be stated, so that a reader does not mistake a boundary for a gap. The historical calculation debate has not been re-argued; it is linked, because its two objections and their separation are the foundation this article builds on and because re-arguing it would double the length without changing the contemporary question. Soviet planning practice has not been retold; it is linked, because the record matters here for what it shows about the constraints, not as a story to be narrated again. The incentive problem has not been given its full anatomy; it is linked, because its informational form, the part this article needed, could be stated without the whole. No contemporary technology, firm, policy or government has been named anywhere in the assessment, by design: the question's decay rate is high, its journalism faster than its scholarship, and an article that tied its verdict to the capabilities of a season would expire with the season. The constraints, by contrast, are stated structurally, in terms of reporting relations and the formation of wants, and they will still be the constraints when the machinery is unrecognisable. That is the sense in which the article aims to be evergreen: not by avoiding the contemporary question, but by answering it at the level that does not date.

## What a price cannot say: the silences on the other side

The objection section stated the market's epistemic strength; the audit's symmetry requires its epistemic silences to be stated with the same care, because a domain verdict built on only one side's silences would be advocacy. A price is a compression: it squeezes the circumstances of everyone who bid, offered, refused and abstained into a single number, and the squeezing is its genius and its limit. What the number cannot carry is everything that was true of the circumstances but irrelevant to the transaction. It cannot say who was excluded from bidding by poverty, so the wants of the moneyless leave no trace in the signal that allocates to the wants of the moneyed, and the system's celebrated sensitivity to preference is sensitivity to a weighted sample whose weights it never displays. It cannot say what the transaction cost third parties, since the polluter and the downstream village are not at the counter, so the signal systematically certifies allocations whose full costs it has not registered. It cannot say anything about the future beyond the horizon of the contracts actually being written, which is why the far future arrives in a price system as a rumour rather than a datum. And it cannot say whether the preferences it registers are ones the preferrers would endorse on reflection, since the choosing it records is the choosing of people as the system's own advertising, addiction engineering and positional competition have formed them. None of these silences refutes the knowledge argument, which is about the existence of knowledge, not its moral quality. But they mark the boundary of what the knowledge is knowledge of: a price system knows, with unmatched granularity, what people with money will pay for, under the formation the system itself has worked on them, for consequences that fall inside the transaction. A society that needs to know anything else, what its people need, what its future requires, what its transactions cost the absent, must build other instruments, and the building of other instruments, for those purposes, is planning by another route. The domain answer is, at bottom, the refusal to let either instrument's competence be mistaken for omniscience.

## Shortage and surplus: the feedback vocabulary of a plan

Every allocation system speaks to itself in a feedback vocabulary, and the vocabularies differ in what they make easy to say. A market's vocabulary is price movement and inventory: the rising price announces scarcity in a form that simultaneously recruits remedy, since the same signal that reports the shortage rewards whoever relieves it. A plan's vocabulary is the fulfilment report and the shortage list: enterprises report plan completion in percentage terms, and the centre learns of scarcity from complaints, queues and the petitions of ministries, channels in which the signal and the remedy are divorced, since the enterprise that reports a shortage caused by its supplier's failure is reporting on a party it cannot discipline and may not wish to embarrass. The divorce matters more than the delay. In a market, the party who discovers the shortage is typically a party positioned to act on the discovery, a buyer who can substitute, a producer who can expand, a trader who can import, and the signal pays them for the acting. In a plan, the discovery accumulates at the centre, the one place that cannot act on it directly but must act through the same hierarchy whose reports concealed it, and the centre's instruments, reallocation decrees, priority ratings, emergency commissions, are slow because they are political: each reallocation creates a loser with a ministry to plead through. The historical systems developed an informal repair mechanism for exactly this divorce, the expediter, the fixer, the personal network through which managers obtained what the plan had failed to deliver, and the fixer's ubiquity is diagnostic evidence of the highest value: a system whose official feedback worked would not breed a profession devoted to circumventing it. The middle models can be read, once again, through this lens. Each is an attempt to rejoin signal and remedy: the market models by keeping the price vocabulary for everything below the framework, the deliberative models by putting the parties who feel the shortage in the room where the revision happens. Whether the rejoinder works is the unproven question. That it is the right question is the article's recurring finding.

### What does a queue know that a price does not?

A queue knows the intensity of a want among people whose money cannot speak: its length registers demand unweighted by wealth, which is information a price destroys. But the queue cannot transmit its knowledge to a remedy, reward a reliever, or distinguish the want's urgency from the queuers' patience, so the knowledge accumulates without acting.

## Foreign trade: the plan at the border

One structural feature of any real planning proposal is so routinely omitted that its omission deserves a section. No economy of any size is self-sufficient, and a planned economy must trade with market economies or with other planned ones, which means that at its border it meets prices it does not control and cannot compute from its own table: the world prices of oil, grain, machinery and credit, formed by the very processes the plan replaces at home. The border prices then leak inward. Every planner deciding whether to produce a good or import it must compare domestic cost against the world price, and the comparison, once admitted for one good, becomes the natural yardstick for all of them, so that the plan's internal valuations are silently disciplined by the market outside, exactly as the firm's internal plan is disciplined by the market around it. The comprehensive design therefore faces a dilemma with no comfortable horn. Trade extensively, and the plan becomes a framework inside a world market, its autonomy confined to the distribution of the gains; or restrict trade toward autarky, and pay for autonomy in forgone specialisation, a cost the historical record itemised in the technological lag of the economies that chose insulation. The middle models mostly choose the first horn without advertising the choice: Roemer's firms trade at world prices like any others, Schweickart's economy likewise, and only the most ambitious designs imagine the border away. The domain method notes the fact without triumph. A national plan in a trading world is a domain-limited instrument by construction, whatever its designers intend, and the world market's existence is one more reason the general question dissolves on inspection: there is no general case, because there is no closed economy for it to be the case about.

## Democracy as an information system: the feedback a plan cannot switch off

Allocation systems need error signals, and political systems are among the channels through which error signals travel. This banal observation has a sharp edge for the planning question. A market economy's allocation errors announce themselves in prices, bankruptcies and unemployment, signals no government can suppress for long because they are generated by millions of transactions it does not intermediate. A comprehensive plan intermediates nearly everything, which gives its governors a power no market government possesses: the power to suppress the error signals themselves, by classifying the statistics, punishing the bearers of bad figures, and treating shortage as a rumour spread by enemies. The historical systems used the power, predictably, since the plan's fulfilment was the regime's legitimacy and a signal of failure was a political act. The result was an information pathology worse than padding: the centre blinded itself deliberately, at exactly the moments when sight mattered most, and learned of its economy's condition from the channels it could not close, the queue, the joke, the defector, the foreign estimate. The lesson for model design is constitutional rather than economic. Any planning system that cannot protect its own error signals from its own governors will degrade its data by a route no audit design can prevent, because the auditor's appointment is itself a governor's act. The models that embed independent statistics, a free press and electoral removal are not decorating their economics with liberal garnish; they are specifying the immune system of their information supply. Schweickart and Devine both build political democracy into their designs as a load-bearing element, and the computational design's authors likewise insist on democratic control of the planning authority. The insistence should be read as an information-theoretic claim: a plan that cannot be voted against will eventually be unable to hear itself think. It is the strongest argument in the entire debate that the economic and the political questions cannot be separated, and it comes from inside the planning tradition's own reconstruction.

## Shocks: which system absorbs a surprise better

Allocation systems are tested less by their steady states than by their surprises: the failed harvest, the embargo, the sudden fashion, the technology that obsoletes a capital stock overnight. The two mechanisms absorb shocks by opposite methods, and the domain sorting predicts which method a given shock rewards. A market absorbs by dispersion: the shock's cost is distributed across every holder of the affected assets and every consumer of the affected good, through price movements nobody has to authorise, and the adjustment begins everywhere at once, wastefully, unjustly in its incidence, but without waiting for a decision. A plan absorbs by concentration: the shock is reported upward, assessed, and answered by a deliberate reallocation that can protect priorities the market would sacrifice, the hospital's supply secured while the boutique's is cut, at the cost of the reporting delay and of the centre's inevitable ignorance about where the shock's edges truly run. Emergencies favour concentration, which is the mobilisation finding restated. Slow, diffuse, preference-laden shocks, a drift in taste, a gradual shift in the skill the economy rewards, favour dispersion, because no centre can perceive a drift that exists only as a million small changes in what people choose. The interesting shocks are the compound ones, a supply failure in a good that is also a need, an energy shock, a pandemic's demand for a specific capacity, where the need layer argues for command and the information layer argues for dispersion, and real systems respond, sensibly, with hybrids: commandeer the capacity, then let prices and queues fight over the remainder. The hybrid is not a failure of theoretical purity. It is the domain method operating under time pressure, and its ubiquity in genuine emergencies is some of the best evidence that the sorting this article defends describes how allocation actually succeeds, as opposed to how the camps describe it.

## Innovation inside the firm: who proposes, who disposes

The novelty section treated the new good; a parallel question concerns the new process, the cheaper method, the reorganised line, the innovation that does not announce itself to any consumer because it changes only how an existing good is made. Process innovation is where the incentive row does its quietest damage, because the innovator inside a planned enterprise volunteers for a specific punishment: a successful improvement raises the demonstrated capacity of the plant, and the ratchet converts the demonstration into next period's norm, so the innovator's reward for ingenuity is a permanently stiffer assignment to meet with the same means, while the failed experiment's cost, the missed target during the trial, is borne in full. Rational managers in such a system innovate the way rational taxpayers volunteer for audits. The historical systems knew the problem and fought it with campaigns, prizes and innovation funds, weapons that fired in bursts and rusted between them, because the underlying arithmetic of reward was untouched. Market firms face no ratchet of that form, since a firm's improved method lowers its costs against rivals whose methods did not improve, and the gain is kept, for a while, as profit and market share; the system's cruelty lies elsewhere, in its willingness to let the unimproved die. The middle models divide instructively here. Worker-controlled firms keep their process gains internally, as income or as leisure, which repairs the incentive at the firm's boundary; whether the gains diffuse across the economy as market competition forces them to diffuse, by imitation under threat of extinction, is a harder question, since a comfortable cooperative has reasons to enjoy its improvement quietly that a threatened competitor does not. The point generalises the article's method once more: innovation is not a single problem but a chain, proposal, trial, reward, diffusion, and each link has its own information and its own incentive, so a model can repair one link and break another while its advocates celebrate the repair. Grading the chain link by link is slower than grading the slogan. It is also the only grading that predicts anything.

### Why does a planned enterprise hide its own improvement?

Because the improvement is evidence, and the evidence will be used to set its future burden. A method that lowers cost per unit teaches the centre that the unit was over-resourced, and the lesson arrives as a higher norm with the same inputs, so concealment is not sabotage but the enterprise's rational custody of its own slack.

## Money under a plan: the unit of account and its temptations

Every allocation system needs a unit in which unlike things are made comparable, and the plan's relationship to money is more troubled than its relationship to computation, because money is never only a unit. In the comprehensive design, money survives as an accounting device and as the population's wage and shopping medium, while being denied its role between enterprises, where transfers are bookkeeping entries against the plan's physical assignments. The denial creates a standing temptation that the historical record documents exhaustively: since the accounting prices between enterprises do not have to clear anything, they drift into arbitrariness, set by cost-plus conventions, by bargaining between ministry and enterprise, by the political weight of the product's consumers, until the plan's own value aggregates, its measures of growth and efficiency, are computed in prices that certify whatever the physical plan already decided. The system then possesses two incommensurable descriptions of itself, a physical one of tonnes and units and a monetary one of impressive totals, and can quote whichever flatters the occasion. The computational design's labour tokens are an attempt to rebuild the unit honestly, tying the measure to labour time embodied, a magnitude with a production meaning independent of any bureau's convention. The attempt deserves its reputation as the most serious effort to give planning a non-arbitrary numeraire, and its difficulties are equally instructive: labour time prices the reproducible and the routine well, and prices badly everything whose value lies in scarcity of a non-labour kind, land, location, the unique and the unrepeatable, which must then be handled by supplements and exceptions that reintroduce discretion through the side door. The market's numeraire has the opposite profile: formed by exchange, it prices scarcity of every kind in one dimension and is silent, as established, about need, desert and the future. Neither unit says everything. The choice between them is a choice about which silences a society can administer around, and like every other choice in this article it sorts by domain more readily than it crowns a winner.

## A worked comparison: one city's buses

Abstract sorting earns its keep on a concrete case, and urban transport is small enough to hold in the hand. A city's bus network presents, in miniature, every layer this article has distinguished. The existence of the network is a framework decision: whether the city moves by collective transport or by private car is a judgement about land, air, access and the shape of the city itself, statable only politically, since the preferences it overrides and creates are exactly the ones a market in transport would take as given. The network's shape, its routes and frequencies, is a planning problem on technical information: populations, employment centres, flows measurable by counting, with needs legible in the geography of who must reach what. The service's quality, its reliability at the margin, the driver's conduct, the cleanliness of the vehicle, is quality information of the elusive kind, channelled in a public system by complaint, inspection and electoral pressure, and in a private one by the passenger's exit to a rival, where a rival exists. The vehicle's manufacture is bulk and consumer production at once, chassis by specification, fittings by taste. And the fare is a price doing several jobs badly or well: rationing space, signalling demand, raising revenue, expressing a social judgement about whether mobility is a right or a purchase. Run the case through the constraints table and no single mechanism takes the whole. The framework is politics, the network is planning, the quality is feedback, the manufacture is industry, the fare is a hybrid instrument tuned by all of them. A writer who can sort the bus case can sort the economy's larger cases by the same operations, and a writer who insists the buses must be all plan or all market will insist the same of everything, with the same warrant. The case is offered as the article's pocket demonstration: the domain answer is not a refusal to judge but a method of judging, visible entire in a system the reader rode to work.

## How the computational claim was received: the profession's two mistakes

The reception of the computational argument, from its publication in 1993 onward, is a case study in the debate's characteristic failures, because both camps misread it in mirror image. Sympathetic readers received it as a vindication: the calculation problem had been the objection, the calculation was now feasible, therefore the objection had fallen and planning stood acquitted. Critical readers received it as a curiosity: a demonstration, by two academics, that an imaginary bureau could solve equations nobody had doubted it could solve since Barone, dressed as a breakthrough. The first reception inflated the claim past its authors' own statement of it, since the book itself spends its substance on information, incentives and democracy, the rows its popularisers skipped. The second reception deflated it past fairness, since the sparsity and iteration results were genuine contributions that retired a lazy version of the sceptical case which many sceptics were still using. The profession of economics, in the main, did neither: it ignored the book, as it ignores most model-building outside its own journals, and the argument migrated to the computer science margins and the political press, where the inflated version flourished. The episode generalises into a warning the reader can apply to every future round of this debate. A technical result enters public argument through two distorting filters, the advocate's headline and the sceptic's shrug, and emerges as a claim its authors would not recognise. The remedy is the practice followed throughout this article: return to the primary text, extract the precise claim, concede what it proves, and locate what it does not address. Applied to the computational claim, the remedy yields the article's verdict in miniature: proved, the tractability of the arithmetic; not addressed, the generation of the data and the wants; and the distance between those two clauses is the distance between the headline and the book.

## The consumer at the counter: what a purchase knows

It is worth slowing down over a single purchase, because the preference row's whole force is visible in it at life size. A shopper stands before a shelf of near-identical goods, chooses one, and pays. In that small act, an extraordinary amount of information has been generated and transmitted. The choice reveals a ranking among the offered bundles at the offered prices, by a person with knowledge of her own household's stocks, tastes, plans and whims that no survey has ever extracted, including knowledge she could not have articulated if asked: the meal half-planned for Thursday, the child's new aversion, the memory of last time's disappointment with the other brand. The refusal of the neighbouring goods is equally informative and equally tacit. Aggregated across millions of such acts, the choosing constitutes a continuous census of wants in their only fully honest form, honest because the chooser pays for her answer with money she could have spent otherwise, so the statement is backed by sacrifice in a way no questionnaire response is. Every eliciting device proposed as the market's replacement must be measured against this act and its properties: continuous rather than periodic, backed rather than costless, tacit-knowledge-bearing rather than form-filling, and self-financing, since the census is conducted by the censused in the course of their own business. Consultation rounds are periodic, costless to game, confined to what can be said in a meeting, and financed in the scarcest currency of all, the participants' evenings. The comparison does not make the rounds worthless; for collective wants they remain the only instrument. It establishes what they would have to become to replace the counter, and how far short of that they stand. The shopper, meanwhile, knows nothing of having performed an epistemic function for a system. That too is a property of the market's census: it requires no civic virtue, no attendance, no literacy in the system's theory. A planning design whose information supply requires virtue at scale is betting the economy on the scarcest input in politics, and the bet should be recorded as such in its prospectus.

### What makes a purchase a more honest signal than a survey response?

The purchase costs the chooser something she values, the money's alternative uses, so the ranking it reveals is backed by sacrifice and cannot be costlessly inflated. It also carries tacit knowledge the chooser could not state on a form, since the choosing draws on circumstances she knows without knowing that she knows them.

## Exit and voice: the two channels through which allocation learns

The political scientist Albert Hirschman distinguished two responses to decline in any organisation: exit, the customer's departure to a rival, and voice, the member's complaint, petition and vote. Allocation systems can be classified by which channel they keep open, and the classification cuts across the plan and market labels in a way that illuminates both. A market is an exit machine: its primary feedback is departure, silent, individual, unanswerable, and its weakness is that exit carries no reasons, so the abandoned producer learns that he was left but not why, and the community that needed the service learns of its loss only in the loss itself. A plan is a voice machine, or aspires to be: its feedback is the petition, the complaint, the plan discussion, the election, channels that carry reasons in abundance and suffer from the opposite defect, that voice without exit behind it decays into ritual, since the authority that cannot be abandoned can afford to hear everything and change nothing. The historical planned economies demonstrated the decay at scale: complaint channels of Byzantine elaboration coexisted with an absence of remedy that every citizen understood, and the understanding converted voice into its counterfeit, the ritual affirmation. The middle models can be graded on this axis too, and the grading is not redundant with the constraints table, because it concerns the politics of feedback rather than the economics of data. Designs that keep exit alive beneath a planned framework, the consumer's freedom to refuse, the citizen's freedom to leave a cooperative for a rival, preserve the one signal no authority can counterfeit, the signal of departure. Designs that rely on voice alone must explain why their voice will not ritualise, and the explanation, where it is offered, runs through democracy's genuine sanctions: the meeting that can dismiss, the election that can remove. The reader will notice that the argument has arrived, by an economic road, at the constitutional conclusion of the earlier section: the information supply of any ambitious allocation system depends on feedback its governors cannot switch off, and the name for unswitchable feedback, in politics, is freedom.

## Needs: who states them, and the politics of the list

The favourable column rests on the category of need, and the category deserves the same sceptical pressure the article has applied to preference, because a need is not a fact of nature wearing a label. Some needs approach that status: the body's requirements for water, calories, shelter from exposure, the treatment of sepsis, are set by physiology and verified by mortality, and a list confined to them is short, grim and uncontroversial. Every actual list of needs runs far beyond physiology, into housing of a stated standard, education of a stated length, connectivity, mobility, care in old age, and at each extension the statement of the need is a political act performed by somebody: a ministry, a profession, a parliament, a movement. The history of such lists is a history of their expansion under pressure from below and their contestation from above, and the expansion is not corruption of the category but its normal politics, since what a society counts as a need is one of the deepest statements of what it thinks a person is owed. Planning's favourable column therefore inherits a question it cannot evade: who writes the list, and by what right, and with what appeal against it. The market's evasion of the same question, letting purchasing power write the list silently, is not an answer but an abdication with the authorship hidden. Between the visible politics of the stated list and the invisible politics of the weighted market, the article's preference is methodological rather than sentimental: a list that can be argued with is an information channel, and a list that cannot even be seen is none. But the preference comes with the standing warning of the democracy section. A stated list, in a system whose governors control the stating, freezes at the governors' convenience, and the needs of the unfashionable, the unorganised and the unborn are as easily omitted from a ministry's schedule as from a market's signal. The need layer of any domain must therefore be audited like any other: authorship named, revision procedure stated, omissions priced. Needs are planning's home ground, and home ground is where complacency does its worst work.

## Labour: the allocation both systems prefer not to discuss

Goods dominate this debate, but an economy allocates people before it allocates things, and the allocation of labour exposes values in both systems that their advocates prefer to leave unexamined. A comprehensive plan must assign labour or induce it, and the historical systems did both by turns: direction of graduates, residence permits binding workers to places, criminal penalties for unauthorised job-changing in the harshest periods, softening later into a labour market in all but name, in which enterprises competed for scarce workers with wages and amenities the plan pretended not to see. The pretence is instructive: labour was the input the plan could least afford to allocate honestly, because honest allocation would have required admitting that workers have preferences about their own lives, enforceable by refusal, which is to say a market in the one factor whose commodification the tradition exists to abolish. The middle models mostly keep the labour market and accept the consequence, that a worker-managed economy still sorts people into jobs by wage and inclination, with the firm's democracy beginning after the sorting. The participatory design alone attempts the deeper change, balancing jobs so that the division between directing and directed labour dissolves, and pays for the attempt in administrative complexity of a high order, since balancing is an assignment problem over the whole workforce's tasks, revisited whenever anyone moves. The market's own labour allocation, meanwhile, performs the preference miracle and the preference scandal in the same act: it discovers, continuously, what skills are wanted where, and it prices the discovery without reference to the worth of the work to anyone but the buyer, so that the care of the dying can be priced below the persuasion of the living, a result the price system reports with perfect accuracy and no embarrassment. Labour is the domain where the article's two silences meet: the plan cannot hear the worker's preference without ceasing to be a plan for labour, and the price cannot hear the work's value beyond the buyer's purse. Sorting labour's decisions, the floor of security and the ban on direction by plan, the framework of training and credentialing by politics, the matching of person to post by choosing, is among the domain method's most consequential applications, and the debate's neglect of it, in favour of goods, is a measure of how far the debate's categories lag its subject.

## Administered prices: the plan's own price problem

A plan that retains prices as instruments inherits a problem its advocates discuss too little: somebody must set them, and the setting is a decision with all the pathologies of the other decisions. An administered price must be revised as costs and scarcities change, and each revision is a small political act, since every price is somebody's income and somebody else's cost, so revisions lag, accumulate pressure, and arrive in jumps that falsify the accounts on both sides of the jump. An administered price must also decide what it is for: to clear the market it faces, to express a social valuation that differs from clearing, to subsidise a need or tax a luxury, purposes that conflict, since a price held below clearing for reasons of equity generates the shortage and the queue that then require a second apparatus of rationing to manage. None of this is an argument against administered prices, which every society uses somewhere, in the regulated utility, the controlled rent, the subsidised staple. It is an argument for counting their cost honestly in the models that rely on them. Lange's board revising prices against shortage was, in this light, proposing that the centre perform, continuously and for everything, the act that real governments perform occasionally and for a few goods, with visible strain. The computational version inherits the strain in a subtler form: its adjusted prices for consumer goods must be revised at the speed of taste, and the revision rule, mark down what accumulates, mark up what vanishes, is the market's rule executed by a committee's software, which raises the question the boundary section posed in general form. If the prices are set by the market's rule against the market's signals, the plan has retained the eliciting mechanism and changed its ownership, a perfectly coherent design, and one whose description as the abolition of the market is a courtesy title. The audit's interest is not in the title but in the mechanism, and the mechanism, once again, is elicitation by price, filed under new management.

### What is an administered price asked to do that a market price is not?

A market price is asked only to clear and to signal. An administered price is asked, in addition, to be fair, to be stable, to subsidise the needful and discourage the harmful, and to do all of these in one number, so its failures are usually the visible form of an impossible job description rather than of administrative incompetence.

## The public budget: the planning every market economy already does

There is a planning exercise so familiar inside market economies that its nature escapes notice, and noticing it reframes the debate's scale. The annual public budget is a plan: a statement, in money, of what a society will collectively do, allocating among defence, health, infrastructure, education and transfers by deliberation and vote, executed through administrative hierarchies, audited after the fact, and revised in the light of outcomes and elections. Its information problems are the article's problems in recognisable dress. Departments bid for funds with forecasts whose optimism is strategic, the treasury's version of the padded return. Outcomes resist measurement in the unit the budget counts, since a school's product is not its expenditure. Wants are stated politically, through the manifesto and the lobby, with the unorganised underheard exactly as the needs section predicted. And the budget's share of the economy in developed societies is not a margin but a mass, routinely the largest single allocation exercise in the nation, dwarfing any firm's internal plan. The sceptic who holds that planning cannot work is therefore committed, whether he knows it or not, to the view that the state's existing planning is a failure across the board, a view some hold consistently and most do not. The advocate who holds that planning works because budgets exist is committing the firm's fallacy at national scale, since the budget plans inside a market that supplies its prices, its contractors and its verdicts at the ballot box and the bond market. The budget's true lesson is the domain lesson again, at the largest scale available: a society already allocates a vast share of its resources by deliberation and hierarchy, tolerably or badly according to the quality of its information practices and its feedback protections, and the question of extending or contracting that share is a question about particular functions and their informational character, not about the possibility of the exercise. The exercise is not in doubt. Only its sorting is.

## Why the debate restarts with every generation

Close the analytical part of the article with an observation about its subject's strange immortality. The planning question has been declared settled, in both directions, at intervals for a century, and it restarts each time with undiminished confidence on both sides, because the conditions that generate it are permanent. It restarts when computation leaps, and the table's opening row moves. It restarts when a market fails conspicuously, and the silences of price become the salient fact of public life. It restarts when a new cohort encounters the historical record fresh and finds, correctly, that neither the comprehensive design's failure nor the market's cruelties answer the question they actually have, which is what to build. And it restarts because the question is finally not technical at all, although it has technical components this article has been careful to grade. Underneath the argument about information sits an argument about the kind of knowledge a society trusts: the knowledge that precipitates from millions of uncoordinated acts, or the knowledge that assembles in deliberation and answers to reasons. Each kind has its domain, its characteristic blindness and its characteristic corruption, and every generation must learn the sorting for itself because the sorting cannot be inherited as a slogan without becoming the thing it replaced. The article's ambition is confined to making this generation's sorting examinable: constraints named, models graded, evidence weighed at its true value, verdict stated with its defeater. The next restart will amend the table. It will not repeal the need for one.

## Risk: who bears the cost of being wrong

Every allocation is a bet, and systems differ as much in where they put the loss when the bet fails as in how they place the bet. A market socialises loss by a brutal and effective routine: the failing firm's owners lose their capital, its workers lose their jobs, its creditors lose their loans, and the loss is final, which is precisely why the system's information can be trusted to accumulate, since errors are paid for and therefore counted. The routine's vice is its incidence: the loss falls hardest on those least responsible for the bet and least able to bear it, the dismissed worker rather than the diversified shareholder, and it falls procyclically, in the downturn when alternative employment has vanished. A plan socialises loss by absorption: the failing enterprise is carried, its workers kept, its output re-planned, and the loss is spread invisibly across the whole economy as a slightly lower general standard, paid by everyone and noticed by no one. Absorption's vice is the mirror of the market's: because no error is ever final, no error is ever fully counted, and the system accumulates commitments to activities whose failure it has agreed not to register, the soft budget that the literature on the planned economies made famous. The middle designs lend themselves to reading as attempts to harden the budget without restoring the brutality: Schweickart's firms can fail, and their workers bear a loss their democracy voted for, which changes the loss's legitimacy without abolishing it; the participatory councils must decide, in their rounds, which proposals to starve, a decision whose visibility is the model's honesty and whose pain is its price. The domain sorting applies here as everywhere. Where the loss from failure is catastrophic and irreversible, the dam, the reactor, the immunisation programme, absorption is the right instinct and redundancy the right extravagance. Where the loss is the ordinary tuition of discovery, the failed product, the superseded method, finality is the right instinct, and a system that cannot let anything die will eventually be governed by its undead. Risk is the constraint the table folds into its rows rather than listing separately, because it is not a fifth problem but the price tag attached to the other four: every information failure, under either mechanism, is paid for by somebody, and the argument about systems is, more often than the debaters admit, an argument about who.

## Agriculture: the domain where the two systems ran side by side

One domain offers something close to a controlled comparison, and it has been cited so often, by both sides, that its precise lesson is worth recovering. In the collectivised agricultures of the planned economies, the same households typically worked two systems in parallel: the collective fields, allocated by plan, worked for wages and shares, and the small private plots, allocated by the household, worked for itself and for sale in the local market. The plots were a small fraction of the land and produced a strikingly disproportionate share of the output, especially of the labour-intensive and quality-sensitive products, vegetables, fruit, meat, dairy, that the collective sector chronically failed to supply. The comparison is not clean, and honesty requires its confounders to be named: the plots received the household's best hours and its manure and its care, the collective fields received the residue of everyone's energy, and the products differ in kind. But the direction of the evidence survived every attempt to explain it away, and its lesson is precise. The difference between the two sectors was not computation, not technique, not even ownership in the abstract. It was the reporting and reward relation: on the plot, the person who decided what to plant was the person who knew the soil and ate the result, so information and incentive were the same fact; in the collective, the decider was elsewhere, the knowledge was local, and the reward was disconnected from both. Agriculture is the domain where the knowledge at stake is most irreducibly local, varying by field, by week, by the weather of a single valley, and where the product's quality is least reducible to specification, which is why it punished the comprehensive design more instructively than any other sector and why its lesson generalises exactly as far as those features generalise. Where knowledge is local and quality is tacit, allocation must live where the knowledge is. Where knowledge is centralisable and quality is assayable, the plot's lesson falls silent, and the bulk domain's different verdict takes over. The comparison's gift to the debate is not a slogan about private plots. It is a demonstration, run involuntarily at continental scale, of the article's central sorting variable.

### What did the private plots actually demonstrate?

That the same people, with the same tools, on similar land, produced disproportionately more of the knowledge-intensive and quality-sensitive goods when the decider, the knower and the beneficiary of the decision were the same person. What the plots isolated was the relation between reporting and reward, not effort in the abstract.

## The market's coordination failures: the empty centre

Symmetry requires a section on the failures that arise not from the market's information but from its absence of a centre, because there are decisions whose structure defeats decentralised choosing even when every chooser is perfectly informed. The type case is complementary investment: a railway is worth building if the factories come, the factories are worth building if the railway comes, and each private investor, reasoning correctly from the world as it is, declines, so the world stays as it is. Development economics built a literature on this trap, the big push, the coordination failure, and its lesson survives the literature's fashions: there exist configurations in which every actor's information is accurate, every price is right, and the outcome is a bad equilibrium that only a coordinated move escapes. A second case is the standard: the technical convention, the gauge, the plug, the protocol, whose value lies in universality and whose adoption no sequence of individual choices reliably converges on, since each chooser waits for the others to move first. A third is the public good proper, the lighthouse and the flood defence, where the information problem is inverted: everyone knows the want, and the mechanism fails anyway, because each person's rational contribution is zero. These are the cases where the sceptic's tradition, at its most honest, concedes the need for a deciding centre and argues about its scope, and they belong in the audit because they mark the points where the domain answer's favourable column has a theoretical foundation and not merely a practical one. The market's information machine processes the knowledge that transactions generate. Coordination problems are defined by the absence of the transactions that would generate it, the unbuilt railway carrying no passengers whose fares could signal its worth. A plan, or a state acting as one, can break the symmetry by deciding, and its decision manufactures the fact that the market was waiting to observe. Deciding is not knowing, and the centre that breaks one deadlock will blunder into others. But a mechanism that can only respond to knowledge and never create the occasion for it has a hole at its centre shaped exactly like the future, and the hole is the planning idea's permanent opening.

## Periodicity: the plan's calendar against the market's continuity

A plan has a period, and the period is a design flaw hiding in plain sight. Allocation by decision happens in rounds: the annual plan, the five-year framework, the quarterly revision, and between the rounds the economy's circumstances move continuously while the allocation stands still, so that the system's responsiveness is governed by its calendar rather than by the speed of its computers. The pathology this breeds is documented in every account of the historical systems: the storming at the period's end, when enterprises convulsed to meet the target the period would judge; the hoarding at its start, against the uncertainties the period would bring; the systematic falsity of any figure whose meaning depended on the period's boundary. Markets have no period. Their allocation revises with every transaction, which is wasteful in its own way, churning, overshooting, mistaking noise for signal, but immune to the calendar's specific corruptions. The computational revival mitigates the plan's periodicity in principle, since a calculation that can be re-run cheaply can be re-run continuously, and the design's defenders are entitled to the point. What they are not entitled to is the assumption that the institutions around the calculation can revise at the calculation's speed. Targets are set by bodies that meet; bonuses are assessed by periods that statutes define; ministries defend the plan they signed. The period lives in the incentives, not in the arithmetic, and it will survive any processor. The middle models each handle the calendar differently, the market models by abolishing the period for everything below the framework, the participatory model by making the rounds themselves the constitution, at the time cost already recorded. A reader grading a new proposal should ask after its calendar as instinctively as after its data: how often may the allocation change its mind, and what happens to the people whose rewards are assessed in the interval. The answer locates the proposal on the map as reliably as any other single question.

## The criteria problem: what would count as beating

One question has been deferred throughout, because the article's method required the domains to be sorted before it could be answered honestly: what does beating mean. The title's verb smuggles in a single criterion, and real comparisons run on at least five, which do not always agree. Efficiency asks which mechanism extracts more of what is wanted from what is available. Equity asks how the result is shared, and in particular whether the mechanism's operation depends on an antecedent distribution it then ratifies. Stability asks which mechanism's failures are gentler and less correlated. Freedom asks what the mechanism requires of the people inside it, what they must attend, declare, obey and endure. Growth asks which mechanism better serves a future that neither can inspect. A verdict that does not state its criterion is a mood, and much of the debate's sterility is the sound of five criteria arguing under one verb. The domain answer can now be restated in the criteria's terms, and the restatement sharpens it. On efficiency, the sorting by information decides, domain by domain, as argued. On equity, the advantage lies with whichever mechanism a society can bend toward its stated distribution, which is a political property rather than an informational one, although the market's dependence on the antecedent distribution is a standing count against it. On stability, the plan's failures are slower and more correlated, the market's faster and more contagious, and the choice is among pathologies. On freedom, the comprehensive plan is the most demanding mechanism ever proposed, requiring attendance, declaration and obedience as the price of its information, while the market's demands, though real, are the demands of necessity rather than of a bureau. On growth, the verdict belongs to the investment section's honest stalemate. Stated this way, the comparison loses its false promise of a single winner and gains what the article has sought throughout: a set of judgements a reader can dispute one by one, on evidence, which is the only kind of conclusion this subject has ever honestly supported.

## The case for the centre, stated fairly

An audit that has spent its length on the centre's disabilities owes the centre its strongest honest statement, because the disabilities were never the whole of the matter and the tradition that built the case was not stupid. Central direction has genuine economies that decentralised choosing cannot replicate. It can move first: where a configuration of investments is worthless unless made together, a centre can make them together, and the history of industrialisation is substantially the history of such coordinated lunges, railways and steel and power raised as one gesture by states that treated the sequence as a single decision. It can standardise: a single authority can impose the gauge, the voltage, the protocol, without waiting for a standards war to exhaust the combatants, and the savings from a settled convention compound for a century. It can stabilise: an economy steered by a centre that commands investment directly does not suffer the private investment cycle's alternation of mania and paralysis, whatever other cycles it invents, and for societies whose memory is of collapse, that steadiness has been worth a high price in other goods. And it can mobilise savings at a rate no voluntary system matches, holding consumption down by decision and converting the sacrifice into plant, which is the brutal arithmetic by which poor countries have repeatedly become less poor. These are not apologies for a record. They are capacities, and any verdict that cannot explain why intelligent people reached for them, in country after country, has mistaken a pathology for the whole physiology.

### What is the strongest honest case for a centre?

Coordination, standardisation, steadiness and mobilisation. A centre can make complementary investments together when no private actor can move first, settle conventions without a standards war, damp the investment cycle by commanding its largest component, and convert present consumption into future capacity at a rate voluntary saving rarely reaches.

The capacities have a common structure, and naming it sharpens rather than blunts the article's thesis. Each is a case where the relevant knowledge is about purposes and sequences rather than about tastes and capacities: what to build first, which convention to bless, how much of the present to mortgage to the future. These are framework judgements, few in number, large in scale, and legible to deliberation, which is to say they sit in the territory this article has already assigned to decision rather than to price. The centre's tragedy, historically, was never that it could do nothing. It was that a competence genuinely earned at the framework was promiscuously extended downward, into the assortment of a shoe shop and the cropping plan of a valley, where the knowledge required changed kind and the competence evaporated. The case for the centre, stated fairly, is therefore not a rival to the domain answer. It is the domain answer's favourable column with its chest out, and the reader who meets the case in its strong form, in the literature of development and of wartime, will find that its best exponents state it in exactly the bounded form given here, while its worst reach for the whole economy and lose the argument their own evidence was winning.

## Categories: how a plan's language freezes its world

A subtler disability of central allocation deserves record, because it operates even when every reporter is honest and every want is known. A plan must classify before it can count, and its classifications, fixed in the forms and the product codes, decide in advance what distinctions the system is capable of noticing. Output is recorded by category, and the category, once fixed, becomes the reality the centre administers: goods that differ in every way a user cares about are one line if they share a code, and a producer rewarded by the line will rationally let the unrecorded differences rot. The historical systems' assortments froze in this way, not because anyone forbade variety but because variety that the classification could not see could not be planned, credited or rewarded, and therefore, in the only sense the system recognised, did not exist. Nor is the freezing a remediable oversight, a matter of finer codes. Finer codes multiply the reporting burden and the gaming surface in proportion, and the classification must in any case be fixed before the period it governs, while the world's variety is generated during it, by the same process of differentiation that markets ride and plans must pre-empt. The market's classification, by contrast, is written continuously by the choosing itself: a distinction exists in the system exactly when some buyer will pay for it, and dies when none will, so the catalogue of economically real differences is revised at the speed of taste. This is a deep and rarely stated form of the knowledge argument. The plan does not merely lack information about the world. It lacks, in its language, the words for the parts of the world that emerged after its dictionary was printed, and it allocates fluently in a vocabulary that the economy has already outgrown.

### Why does a classification freeze the assortment it describes?

Because a plan can only reward, credit and correct what its categories record, and its categories are fixed before the period begins. Differences the code cannot express earn nothing and are therefore not produced, while the market's catalogue of real differences is rewritten continuously by buyers paying for distinctions as they emerge.

The point has a modern echo that the evergreen discipline of this article permits, since it names no product and no firm. Any large administrative system, including the internal systems of market economies' own bureaucracies and corporations, allocates through classifications, and all of them exhibit the freezing in miniature: the budget line that cannot see the new need, the performance indicator that trains its subjects to abandon the unmeasured dimension, the procurement specification that purchases the past at a discount. The plan's disability is the general disability of administration, raised to the scale of a whole economy and deprived of the surrounding market that, in mixed systems, quietly supplies the goods the classification missed. That is why the disability matters most at the comprehensive margin. A society can afford a frozen classification in its vehicle fleet, because the unfrozen world outside the fleet office corrects the assortment. It cannot afford one in its food supply, where the classification is the assortment and there is no outside left to correct it.

## The archive problem: reading a record its keepers falsified

The evidence sections graded the historical record; a methodological postscript is owed on how such a record can be read at all, because the planned economies present the historian with a peculiar object: an archive produced by a system that falsified its own statistics as a condition of governing. The naive readings are two, and both are worthless. The first takes the published figures at face value, inheriting the padding as fact. The second discards the archive wholesale, treating every number as propaganda, and thereby discards the genuine information the system recorded for its own use, since a regime that lies to its public must still, in its internal channels, approximate the truth it needs to rule. The working method of the serious scholarship is triangulation: the internal series read against the physical series, tonnages against freight movements, reported harvests against procurement and ration data, the whole checked against the testimony of participants and the estimates of foreign observers who had their own reasons to err in the other direction. Where the triangulations converge, the record can be used, with its error bars stated. Where they diverge, the honest output is a range and an admission. This article's use of the record has followed that method and drawn only the conclusions that survive it, which is why the conclusions concern behaviours, padding, hoarding, the ratchet, attested by every channel including the system's own internal complaints, rather than magnitudes, which remain contested in detail. The reader should apply the same suspicion symmetrically. Market economies' statistics are produced by institutions with their own incentives, milder in degree, different in kind only where the feedback protections differ, and the national accounts of a boom have certified fictions before now. The archive problem is a special case of the article's master problem: every allocation system generates the record by which it will be judged, and the judge's first task is to ask what the record's authors gained by its contents.

### How should a falsified archive be read?

Neither at face value nor as waste paper. Its internal series should be triangulated against physical magnitudes the falsifiers could not easily bend, freight, energy, mortality, and against testimony and foreign estimate, with conclusions confined to what the converging channels support and the error stated where they diverge.

## What would move the other rows

The verdict defeater stated what would reverse the verdict from outside, by exhibit. A companion question deserves its own section: what developments, short of a full exhibit, would loosen the rows that bind, and are any of them visible on the horizon the article is permitted to inspect. The data row would loosen if the recording of production became a by-product of producing rather than a report about it, so that the figures a plan consumes were generated by the activity itself, in the course of operations their authors could not retrospectively shade, and if the assessment of managers were detached from the content of those figures, so that honesty cost nothing. Fragments of that condition exist wherever operations are instrumented for other purposes, inside firms and networks, which is why the data row binds least exactly there. Its general form, an economy whose every process reports itself as a side effect, would still leave the counterfactual untouched: no instrumentation records what a plant could have produced, and capacity, the plan's most needed figure, would remain a judgement filed by the judged. The preference row would loosen if wants became more stable, more legible or less endogenous to the serving system, and every tendency of developed consumption runs the other way, toward differentiation, novelty and the manufacture of desire, so the row is likelier to tighten than to slacken as economies grow richer. The incentive row would loosen if a society discovered how to make standing, income and self-respect follow outcomes that agents do not self-report, at national scale and across generations, a discovery that has eluded every system including the market's own firms, which struggle with it in miniature. The audit's conclusion is therefore asymmetrical in a way the debate rarely notes. The row that technology moved was the row about machinery. The rows that remain are about the relations between reporters and judges, choosers and goods, agents and rewards, and they will move, if they move, at the speed of institutional invention, which is the slowest speed in social life. A reader approached by the next announcement that the planning problem has been solved should ask which row the announcement addresses. The answer, on the record of a century, will be the first one.

## A short history of a word: planning

The word itself carries the debate's archaeology, and a brief excavation explains some of its heat. Planning entered economic language as a term of pride, associated in the early twentieth century with rationality itself, with the promise that the evident order of the factory and the wartime ministry could be extended to the chaos of the market, and for a generation the burden of proof lay on anyone who doubted it. The word's prestige survived the first news from the economies that adopted it whole, because prestige is lagged, and it powered the mid-century settlement in the market economies themselves, where indicative plans, national plans and planning ministries multiplied without embarrassment. The reversal, when it came, was semantic as much as economic: by the century's last decades the word had become, in the mouths of its opponents, a synonym for the queue and the empty shelf, and its surviving uses fled into euphemism, industrial strategy, coordination, framework, anything but the noun itself. The models this article has graded were all written under the shadow of that reversal, and their titles perform it: futures, democracies, utopias, everything except a plan. The excavation matters because arguments inherit their words' charges. A proposal described as planning must still pay the word's accumulated debt in the reader's ear, while the identical proposal described as coordination is heard fresh, and a debate conducted in loaded vocabulary will misgrade its objects in both directions, crediting the euphemism and fining the honest noun. The article has used the plain word throughout, in its plain sense of allocation by decision, and has tried to grade mechanisms rather than their names. If the sorting it defends is right, the word's next career is predictable: it will be rehabilitated domain by domain, as the favourable column's practice accumulates, and ruined again each time it is promiscuously extended past the territory where its information can follow. Words, like mechanisms, work where their conditions hold.

The excavation yields one practical instruction for readers of the debate's journalism. When a proposal is praised or damned, substitute the mechanism for the label before judging: ask not whether the thing is called planning, but who decides, from what information, with what feedback, over which territory. The substitution takes a sentence and dissolves most of the charge the vocabulary was carrying, leaving the argument's actual bones visible. Bones, unlike banners, can be examined, and examination is the whole of the method this article has tried to practise and to teach.

## Who staffs the centre: the planner as a problem in the planner's own terms

Every design in this article has been graded by the information it can gather and the incentives it creates for producers and consumers. One population has escaped the grading, and it is the population the whole apparatus depends on: the planners themselves. A planning system is staffed by a profession, recruited, trained, promoted and pensioned like any other, and the profession's own information and incentives obey the article's laws with the rest. Its members know the economy through the returns whose pathologies have filled these pages, and they know their own standing through the fulfilment of the plans they wrote, so the centre's picture of the world and the centre's interest in that picture are fused at the source. A planner who reports that the plan's targets were misconceived reports on his own work; a ministry that discovers slack in its sector discovers its own redundancy. The historical systems answered this with rotation, purges and parallel reporting channels, remedies that treated the symptom by terrorising the staff and thereby worsening the information, since frightened statisticians are no more truthful than frightened managers. The gentler systems of the market economies answer it with the audit office, the independent statistics authority and the opposition's question time, the immune system described earlier, and the answers work exactly to the extent that the careers inside them are insulated from the findings they produce.

### Who plans the planners' incentives?

Nobody, inside a closed design, which is the difficulty. The centre's staff are judged by the success of allocations they themselves assessed, so their interest in the assessment's kindness is structural. Insulation, independent audit, protected statistics and removable governments are the known partial remedies, all of them political.

The models divide on this population as they divide on every other. The market designs shrink it: Roemer's state employs fund monitors and regulators in numbers a market economy would recognise, and the profession's temptations shrink with its jurisdiction. The framework designs professionalise it differently, staffing the negotiating tables with representatives whose knowledge is admitted to be interested and whose interests are meant to cancel in the open. The comprehensive and participatory designs enlarge it or dissolve it into the citizenry, and each choice has its price: enlargement breeds the stratum whose emergence the critics of bureaucracy predicted from the beginning, while dissolution asks the citizen to be the profession in her spare time, at the cost the time objection records. There is no design in which the question disappears, because allocation by decision requires deciders, and deciders are people with careers. The most a design can do is arrange for the deciders' errors to be somebody else's news, delivered through channels the deciders do not control. That arrangement has a name outside economics. It is called accountability, and its presence or absence predicts the quality of a planning system's information better than any feature of its mathematics.

## The market as a designed thing: the framework beneath the signals

A final symmetry completes the audit. The sceptical tradition speaks of the market as the natural condition and of planning as the artificial imposition, and the article's grading has no use for the distinction, because a market is as much a designed institution as a plan is. Property rights are drafted. Contract is enforced by courts that somebody funds and staffs. The corporation is a legal artefact, limited liability a legislative gift, money itself an issue of the state, and the rules against fraud, collusion and insider dealing are a standing administrative apparatus without which the price system degenerates into the strong preying on the uninformed. Every functioning market economy therefore rests on a planned framework, and the argument between the camps was never between design and its absence. It is between designs that allocate by decision inside a framework of prices, and designs that allocate by price inside a framework of decisions, and the practical question is always the mixture's proportions at each layer. This is not a debating trick. It dissolves the last refuge of the general verdict, the appeal to nature, and it explains the otherwise puzzling convergence of real societies: all of them, whatever their banners, operate a designed framework, a priced interior and a planned set of exceptions, and differ in where the layers sit. The information test remains the only principled instrument for arguing about the sitting. A decision belongs inside the framework, taken deliberately, when its knowledge is of purposes, sequences and floors. It belongs in the priced interior when its knowledge precipitates from choosing. And the exceptions, the domains where the interior's signals fail by structure rather than by accident, belong to the plan that every society already keeps, whether or not it keeps the word.

### Why is the appeal to the market's naturalness a mistake?

Because the market's preconditions are artefacts: property drafted by legislatures, contracts enforced by courts, money issued by states, fraud and collusion policed by standing authorities. The choice is never between design and nature but between designs, differing in which layer decides and which layer discovers.

## Two ways of saying no: the price tag and the waiting line

Every allocation system must refuse most of the wants presented to it, because scarcity is the condition the whole subject exists to manage, and the manner of the refusal is where the systems' characters show most plainly. A price refuses by wealth: the good goes to whoever can pay, and the refused are refused silently, in the privacy of their own arithmetic, with no official ever pronouncing their exclusion. A queue refuses by time: the good goes to whoever arrives and waits, and the refusal is public, embodied, and equal in the single dimension of hours, which the rich and the poor possess in the same daily quantity and value at wildly different rates. A ration refuses by category: the good goes to whoever the schedule names, children before adults, soldiers before civilians, the registered before the stranger, and the refusal is a political statement about whose need counts, arguable in the open because it is written down. None of the three refusals is humane in itself. Each merely chooses the currency in which disappointment is paid, money, time or status, and each currency distributes its burden across a different population, which is why arguments about allocation so often turn out, on inspection, to be arguments about whose disappointment a society prefers not to see. The price's refusal is the least visible and therefore the easiest for a prosperous society to live with; the queue's refusal is the most visible and therefore the first to be photographed; the ration's refusal is the most arguable and therefore the one wartime publics have repeatedly accepted as fair, because a written schedule can be defended, amended and shamed in a way that a price, which answers to no one, cannot.

### What does the manner of refusal reveal about a system?

It reveals the currency in which the system denominates disappointment: money, where exclusion is private and weighted by wealth; time, where exclusion is public and levied in hours; or category, where exclusion follows a written schedule of desert that politics must defend in the open.

The planning debate's emotional register is largely set by these refusals, and the audit profits from noticing it. The planned economies are remembered by their queues, which were the visible form of a refusal the system had chosen, or fallen into, without admitting the choice; the market economies are forgiven their refusals because the refused simply do not appear, there being no line to photograph, only a absence where a purchase would have been. A fair comparison must count both: the hours burned in the line and the wants silently unformed because the price was known to be out of reach before the wanting began. Neither ledger is kept by the system it describes, since prices do not record the desires they pre-empt and queues do not record the goods that were never stocked to be queued for. The domain method offers its usual discipline. Where the good is a need and the society's judgement is that access should not track wealth, refusal by category or by queue, for all their waste, denominate disappointment in a currency the society has chosen on purpose, and the choice can be defended as policy. Where the good is a matter of taste among strangers, refusal by price performs the additional service of generating the information the next allocation needs, and the queue in that territory is pure loss, time burned to produce a signal nobody reads. The manner of refusal, like every other feature examined in this article, sorts by domain, and a writer who treats one manner as the emblem of a whole system has mistaken a currency for an economy.

There is a fourth refusal, easily overlooked because it operates before any of the other three: refusal by assortment, in which the unwanted want is never presented with its object at all. A market performs it whenever producers, anticipating poor returns, decline to make the good a minority craves; a plan performs it whenever the catalogue omits the item, the size, the district, the hour. Assortment refusal is the quietest of the four and in some ways the cruellest, since the refused cannot identify their refuser, cannot queue, cannot save, cannot petition a schedule that nowhere mentions them. It is also the refusal most sensitive to the discovery procedures examined earlier in this article. A system with many independent producers, each free to gamble on an unmet craving, converts assortment refusal into a standing invitation, since the unserved niche is precisely where a newcomer's profit hides. A system with a single assortment committee converts it into a standing temptation, since every omission is invisible in the committee's own statistics, which record the distribution of what was offered and are silent, necessarily, about what was not. The historical planned economies' citizens learned to read the assortment as a text, treasuring the accidental import, the anomalous line in a provincial shop, as evidence of what the catalogue elsewhere forbade. Market citizens read prices the same way. Both are literacies of refusal, acquired by living inside a manner of saying no, and the comparison of systems is incomplete until it counts, among its costs, the wants each system teaches its people not to have.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The single clearest correction this topic offers a classroom is the distinction between computation and data generation, and it is worth building a whole session around the moment students conflate them. Set the computational claim first, in Cockshott and Cottrell's own terms, and let the room concede it fully; the concession is genuine and the concession is the lesson's hinge. Then ask the room a question with no technical content at all: the bureau has solved its equations, so where did the numbers in them come from, and what did the people who supplied those numbers want. The silence that follows is the seminar's real curriculum. From it, the constraints table builds itself, row by row, with the students supplying the rows from their own reasoning about reporters, wants and rewards, and the four models can then be introduced as four wagers about which row matters least. A productive exercise is the grading of a single claim, drawn from journalism, by the reader's instrument of four questions, in groups that must agree a scope for the claim before they may agree a verdict on it. The topic's examination traps, the Soviet record used against untested models, the large firm used as evidence for allocation without prices, make reliable discussion starters, since every cohort contains students who arrive holding one of them sincerely. Close the session on the defeater: ask each student to state, in one sentence, what evidence would change the verdict they have reached, and treat any student who cannot as not yet finished, whatever their verdict is.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this question is not theoretical but evidentiary, and it is thinner than the debate's volume suggests. The middle models have now existed as worked designs for three decades and more, and the direct evidence about their operating characteristics remains close to nil, because no polity has run one at a scale or for a duration that would test its information practices. What exists instead is adjacent evidence: the performance of cooperative sectors inside market economies, the behaviour of deliberative bodies in participatory budgeting at municipal scale, the data practices of large organisations whose internal planning is audited by external markets. Each adjacent body bears on a row of the constraints table, and a literature that mapped them row by row, with the same discipline this article applies to the historical record, would advance the question further than another decade of model refinement. For historians, the open archive problem is the data row's own history: the reporting practices of the planned economies are documented largely by the systems' critics and by post hoc memoir, and the internal statistics' rehabilitation by later scholarship remains incomplete. For journalists, the standing request of this article is modest and specific: when a computational result is announced as settling the planning question, ask which constraint it addresses, and print the answer. The claim that survives that question is news. The claim that does not was a headline in search of a constraint, and the distinction, applied consistently, would improve the coverage of this question more than any verdict a newspaper could reach.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Can central planning work today?

It can work in the domains where it already works, and the general form of the question is the obstacle to seeing why. Where needs are known in advance, horizons are long and the information required is technical, as with water and power networks, the public provision of health, and the rebuilding of an energy system, administrative allocation performs at least as well as allocation by price and is the instrument actually in use. Where wants are matters of taste, variety and novelty, no proposed mechanism generates the knowledge that choosing generates, and planning in that territory allocates without its essential input.

### Q: Is computation still the obstacle to planning?

No, and it has not been the real obstacle for a long time. The arithmetic of allocation is tractable because the economy's table of requirements is sparse and iterative methods converge on it, a result the critics' side had granted in principle within the debate's first decade. The obstacles that remain are of a different kind: obtaining accurate data from enterprises whose targets and rewards depend on the figures they report, and discovering what people want in domains where the wanting takes shape inside the choosing. Neither obstacle is eased by faster processing.

### Q: What are the remaining constraints on planning?

Four constraints organise the whole question, and only one of them concerns machinery. The arithmetic of allocation can be done, so computation is off the list of live obstacles. What remains is, first, the problem of the returns: a plan is calculated over figures supplied by the very enterprises the figures will govern, and self-protection bends them. Second, the problem of wants still unformed, because a taste for something untried cannot be declared before the encountering. Third, the problem of reward: a system that taxes success with harder assignments and pays nothing for honesty will be supplied with neither. The table in this article scores each serious design against all four tests.

### Q: What are the main market socialist models?

Four designs define the serious shelf. John Roemer's coupon socialism of 1994 socialises ownership through non-transferable share entitlements while firms trade in ordinary markets. David Schweickart's economic democracy of 2002 adds workplace control of firms and a socially directed investment fund. Michael Albert and Robin Hahnel's participatory economics of 1991 replaces market allocation with iterative rounds of proposal and revision between councils of producers and consumers. Pat Devine's negotiated coordination of 1988 plans the framework of interdependent investment through bargaining among affected interests, leaving current allocation largely to markets.

### Q: Does internal corporate planning count as evidence?

Strictly, it does not, because the firm's feat is performed with borrowed equipment. Its planners work from prices for steel, energy and transport that other people's bidding has already formed, and they learn whether the plan was good from buyers who can walk away. A national plan enjoys neither gift: it must manufacture its own scarcity signals and submit to a judgement it has itself arranged. So the giant firm's smooth internal coordination shows that direction works when a market feeds and marks it. Exhibiting that as proof that direction could replace the feeder is the debate's most durable equivocation.

### Q: What was the Chilean cybernetic project?

It was an attempt, in the early 1970s under the government of Salvador Allende, to manage a nationalised sector in something close to real time. Designed with the British cybernetician Stafford Beer, it gathered daily production reports from factories through a telex network to a centre in Santiago, filtered the flow statistically so that only significant deviations demanded attention, and left routine decisions with the enterprises themselves. It operated partially, under siege conditions, and was used most seriously to coordinate distribution during a transport strike. The military coup of 1973 destroyed it before it could be assessed as an allocation system.

### Q: Where is planning most defensible?

It is most defensible where the requirement can be written down before anyone goes shopping: so many dwellings of a stated standard, a population immunised, a network that reaches every settlement, an energy system rebuilt over decades. In such territory the knowledge involved is about loads, flows and dosages, matters an engineer can check, and the penalty for providing too little dwarfs the waste of providing too much. Developed societies already run these territories administratively, whatever their official philosophy. The defence weakens as the requirement dissolves into taste, until at the far end, the world of fashion and novelty, it disappears altogether.

### Q: What is participatory economics?

Participatory economics, which Robin Hahnel and Michael Albert proposed in 1991, is a design for allocating without markets through deliberation. Workers and consumers are organised in councils and federations, and planning proceeds in rounds: workplaces propose what they could produce and would need, consumer councils propose what they would wish to have, and indicative prices reflecting social costs are published between rounds so that each side revises in view of the whole. Remuneration follows effort and sacrifice, and jobs are balanced between rote and empowering tasks. Its standing difficulty is the time the rounds demand of every citizen.

### Q: Does the Soviet record test these models?

No, because the Soviet system embodied none of the designs on the modern shelf. Its firms answered to ministries rather than to worker electorates or coupon holders, its consumers had no councils, and its investment was decreed rather than negotiated. Citing its collapse against those designs is a category error. Yet the record is far from useless to the argument: it is the one large demonstration of what happens to information inside a command system, where success was taxed with stiffer norms, inputs were quietly stockpiled, and fulfilment was reported with an optimism the warehouses contradicted. The models must survive those behaviours; the record proves the behaviours, not the models' failure to survive them.

### Q: What would settle the planning question?

Settlement would require exhibits, not arguments. One would be a deliberative or market socialist design actually run, for a city or an industry and for long enough to be gamed, with its assortment, its shortages and its citizens' satisfaction measured beside a conventionally run counterpart. Another would be a proof that the information problems bite with equal force on a vaccination drive and on a fashion catalogue, which would collapse the sorting this article defends. A third would be a comprehensive system, somewhere, keeping pace with shifting consumer taste across a decade without queues becoming its rationing device. Until one of those appears, honest writing on the question ends with a verdict and its defeater, not with a full stop.

### Q: How does the domain answer grade the question whether planning works?

It treats the general question as malformed and marks the smaller questions hidden inside it. Asked wholesale, whether planning works invites a slogan; asked by territory, it invites evidence. In one territory the obstacle is that nobody can know the wants in advance; in another there is no such obstacle, because the requirement is set by physiology or by a political decision, and the obstacle is administrative competence instead. A candidate who performs that sorting on the page, territory by territory, with the governing obstacle named in each, has answered the only version of the question that evidence can grade.

### Q: What should a student separate between information that is technical and information that is preference-dependent?

Separate the facts that describe production from the facts that describe desire. A furnace's consumption of coke per tonne of iron belongs to the first kind: it was true before anyone decided anything, an engineer can verify it, and a lie about it surfaces when the furnace is lit. How much a household will sacrifice for a second bathroom, or for a flavour it has never tasted, belongs to the second kind: there is no fact of the matter sitting in the world beforehand, because the answer is manufactured by the offering and the choosing themselves. Systems fail characteristically when they file the second kind as if it were the first.

### Q: Why does the information a plan requires not exist before the process that generates it?

Because much of it is manufactured by the activity it is meant to guide, the way a path is manufactured by walking. What copper is worth to the economy is not inscribed anywhere before users bid against one another for it; the bidding, drawing on a thousand private circumstances, is what brings the valuation into being, and the resulting price is a summary of an event rather than a reading of a dial. Taste behaves the same way at the other end of the system, crystallising when a person meets the actual goods. A bureau that begins by collecting its information is therefore collecting, in part, for a world its own abolition of the process has already altered.

### Q: How does the constraints table order the models?

It ranks them by exposure rather than by elegance. Each design is profiled across the four rows, and its position is fixed by the row where its exposure is greatest. Leaving shop goods to prices shelters a design from the want problem almost entirely, which is why the ownership-only model sits at the sheltered end. Directing investment from a social fund drags a design into the reporting problem at maximum depth, because a forecast about an unbuilt plant cannot be checked the way a running plant's figures can. The ranking that results is therefore a ranking of wagers: which unsolved problem each author chose to live with.

### Q: What separates a model that elicits information from a model that assumes it?

Look at where the proposal's knowledge is born. In an eliciting design, the machinery itself gives birth to it as a by-product of running: buyers and sellers, by transacting, leave behind the costs and rankings the allocator needs, or citizens in a consultation are made to confront what their wishes would consume and revise them in public. In an assuming design, the knowledge simply arrives at the first page, as a table of capacities and a schedule of wants whose provenance is never interrogated. The distinction is a reader's shortcut because it converts a thick book into two questions, and a proposal that stumbles over either has told you where it will break.
