Parodies of the balcony scene form the longest-running comedy tradition attached to any single passage of Romeo and Juliet, and the tradition's verdict is available before its evidence: the spoofs do not erode the scene, they distribute it. A French print parody was mocking the lovers within a year of the play's first French staging in the early 1770s. A London travesty of 1859 rebuilt the tragedy as one act of slang and song and ended it with every corpse back on its feet. A Warner Bros. cartoon of 1959 let its Juliet leap from a castle window and declined to catch her. The Reduced Shakespeare Company turned the wooing into a sprint up a ladder, performed by a man in a wig, and ran the joke for nine years in the West End. Each of these works assumes a house that already holds the scene entire: the raised girl, the boy below, the call, the vow. None of them teaches that picture. All of them spend it, and spending it is what keeps it in circulation.

![A stage balcony with a climbing lover below and a figure leaning over the rail, the tableau every balcony scene parody inherits - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The argument of this article is that parody is the scene's second transmission system, parallel to the schoolroom and the serious stage and in some centuries busier than either. The first system transmits the text: two hundred lines of negotiation in an orchard, spoken mostly in ignorance of an audience of one hidden below. The second system transmits a picture and a call: a window misremembered as a balcony, a girl misheard as summoning the boy she cannot see, a parting stretched into a catchphrase. Parody belongs wholly to the second system. It quotes the picture with an accuracy the picture does not deserve, mangles the words with a freedom the words survive, and inverts the ending so reliably that the inversion has become a convention of its own. Mockery of this kind is not the opposite of preservation. It is preservation with its tie loosened, and the record assembled in the InsightCrunch parody survey, set out in full below, shows the loosened tie holding for two and a half centuries.

## What Parodies Actually Quote: The Tableau, Not the Text

A parody can only spend what its audience already owns, so the first question is what the balcony scene's owners actually hold. The honest answer is a still image and one sentence, and neither survives contact with the text unchanged. The passage that [the scene's full anatomy, line by line](/2023/07/21/balcony-scene-act-2-scene-2-analysis/) sets out is a long, wary negotiation: Juliet, alone at an upper window of the Capulet house, reasons aloud about names; Romeo, hidden in the orchard, debates whether to answer; the two then bargain, under repeated interruption, toward a marriage appointment for the following morning. The text gives Juliet a window and an upper playing area. It never gives her a balcony, and the word appears nowhere in the play. What the centuries added is furniture: a railed platform, an Italianate house front, a climb. [The Casa di Giulietta's invented balcony, assembled in Verona in the twentieth century](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/), is the same addition built in stone, and painting fixed the arrangement long before the builders did. Parody inherited the furnished version, because parody always inherits the version the public holds, and the public holds the picture.

### Does the play itself contain anything like a balcony?

No. The early texts place Juliet at an upper window and let Romeo's cry, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2), do the describing. No speech or stage direction names a balcony. The railed platform belongs to later staging, painting, tourism and spoof: every parody builds one, and no edition can find one.

The second holding is the call. "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33) circulates in the spoofs as a summons, a girl leaning over a rail and crying down into a garden for the boy she wants. In the text the line is the opposite of a summons. Juliet speaks it in the belief that she is alone, as the hinge of an argument about the arbitrariness of names: "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" (2.2.34) is an instruction addressed to an absent man, and the rose reasoning that follows, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44), is philosophy conducted in private. Romeo's contribution at that moment is a whispered aside, "Shall I hear more, or shall I speak at this?" (2.2.37), which is the sound of a man deciding whether eavesdropping may continue. The scene's engine is non-communication: one speaker who does not know she is heard, one hearer who must choose when to stop profiting from the error. Parody reverses the polarity. Its Juliet always knows, always calls, always performs for the boy and the house at once, because a soliloquy overheard is a subtle machine and a girl shouting from a rail is a poster.

Between the picture and the text, then, parody chooses the picture every time, and the choice is structural rather than lazy. A spoof has seconds to establish its ground. The window, the rail, the climber and the girl compose a single glance of information, legible in a cartoon frame, on a poster, across a music hall. The text's actual arrangement, a voice from an unlit upper window and a motionless listener in an orchard, composes nothing a glance can hold. There is a further asymmetry: the picture can be wrong in only one way, by furnishing, while the words can be wrong in thousands, and parody exploits both. It keeps the picture exact because the picture is its passport, and it garbles the words because garbling is its licence. The balcony in a spoof is therefore always more solid, more railed, more climbable than anything the serious stage builds, while the verse in a spoof is always thinner than anything the serious stage speaks. Fidelity and vandalism divide the labour between image and language, and the division is the tradition's founding rule.

One sound of the picture deserves its own note. Juliet's first utterance in the scene is not a speech but a sigh, "Ay me!" (2.2.25), overheard before she knows she is heard, and the sigh became the tableau's audio signature in the same way the rail became its visual one. The spoofs keep the sigh with the fidelity they keep the call. Their Juliets sigh before they speak, lean and sigh, sigh in wigs and in feathers, because the sigh is the picture's announcement that the serious feeling has begun, and the spoof needs the feeling announced before it can be deflated. Textually the sigh is a crack in the performance, a girl talking to herself; pictorially it is the overture. The distance between those two functions is the distance the whole tradition travels, from a soliloquy overheard to an emblem displayed, and the sigh's survival, unaltered, across every medium surveyed here is a small clean proof that parody preserves by the same act with which it mocks.

## The Furnished Balcony: The Picture the Parodists Inherited

It is worth pausing over the furniture, because the spoof's set is never built from the play. It is built from the picture trade. The parodist's balcony arrives with a stone balustrade at a regulation height, a pair of shutters or French doors behind the girl, an obliging moon, and something climbable, a vine, a trellis, a drainpipe, running up the wall beside her. Not one of these items is in the text. The text's contributions are a window, an orchard wall Romeo has already leapt before the scene begins ("By love, that first did prompt me to enquire; / He lent me counsel, and I lent him eyes", 2.2.79-80, is his retrospective account of a climb the play performs in a line and never shows), and a moon the lovers argue about. The furnishings accumulated across the staging and print centuries, as the companion survey of the lovers in painting records at length: Boydell's engravers and their successors standardised the house front, the raised Juliet, the reaching Romeo, until the arrangement could be recognised from a thumbnail. Parody received the standardised article and has been quoting the engraving, not the edition, ever since.

The consequences run deeper than set design. Because the picture is fixed, the spoof's variations are confined to behaviour: the girl can lean, faint, eat, telephone, drop things, mistake her caller; the boy can climb, fall, serenade, be arrested, be the wrong boy entirely. The frame forbidding change is what makes the changes legible. A cartoon frame of A Witch's Tangled Hare can spend its whole budget on a facial expression, because the castle window, the costume and the posture are pre-paid by the picture tradition. There is an economic way to state this. The parodist's production costs fall as the picture's fame rises: the more completely the audience owns the furnished balcony, the less of it any single spoof must build, and the more of its brief runtime can go to the inversion that is its actual product. Fame is a subsidy, and the balcony is the most heavily subsidised set in comedy.

The moon deserves its sentence. In the text the moon is an argument: Juliet forbids oaths by it, "O, swear not by the moon, th'inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111), and Romeo's counter-offer is struck down mid-sentence. In the picture the moon is décor, a silver disc hung behind the girl to certify romance, and in the spoofs it becomes a prop in the strict sense, something to be switched off, hidden behind cloud on cue, or blamed for the visibility of the climb. The migration of the moon from argument to lamp to gag is the whole history of the scene's transmission in one object: what the text thinks with, the picture hangs, and the parody plays with. A spoof that wanted to restore the argument would have to stop the show and gloss a pronoun's antecedent, which is why none does. The grammar selects for furniture and against thought, and the furnished balcony, beautiful, climbable and empty of negotiation, is the selection's monument.

The light makes the same journey, faster. Romeo's first perception of Juliet is not of a figure but of a radiance: "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3). The text introduces her as a light source before it introduces her as a person, and the serious stage has wrestled ever since with the practical problem of lighting a metaphor. The picture trade solved the problem by materialising her, a girl in white on a rail against a dark house, lit as if from within, and the spoofs inherited the solution with the problem forgotten. Their Juliets arrive pre-lit, glowing in the frame's centre, in cartoons drawn in flat colour where the glow is rendered as a literal radiance around a witch's head. The parody of a metaphor is impossible; nobody can burlesque a sunrise. What the spoofs burlesque is the stage's long attempt to build the metaphor out of timber, gauze and limelight, and the furnished balcony is that attempt's monument. When the cartoon lets its Juliet fall, it is the limelight solution that hits the ground.

## Why the Balcony Scene Invites Spoofing More Than Any Other Scene in the Play

No other passage of the tragedy offers a parodist the same concentration of gifts. The first gift is verticality. The scene is staged on two levels with the lovers separated by the full height of the house, desire drawn as a diagram: she above, untouchable, framed; he below, upturned, exposed. A diagram can be redrawn by any hand, and this one has the further virtue of a built-in physical problem, the climb, which comedy converts into ladders, drainpipes, ropes, falls and failed leaps. Serious productions must solve the height; spoofs must only exaggerate the solution. The second gift is the one-sided speech. Juliet's great utterances are soliloquy wearing the costume of address, and a speech that sounds like a call but is not one invites every literal-minded answer a comic can supply: the wrong man steps forward, the right man answers the wrong question, the girl repeats herself louder, as people do when they believe distance is the problem. The third gift is the name speech itself. An argument that a rose would smell as sweet under any other name is an engraved invitation to rename everything in sight, and parodists have accepted the invitation with noses, cheeses, feet and livestock for two centuries.

A further gift is built into the geometry, and it is the subtlest. Romeo spends the scene's first movement hidden, listening to a speech not meant for him, which makes him the audience's exact surrogate: a spectator inside the picture, hearing what the house hears, reacting as the house reacts, deciding, in his whispered aside, the very question every audience decides, whether to keep listening in silence or to speak. Comedy seizes on surrogates, because a surrogate can be made to voice the house's own irreverence from inside the sacred image. The spoof's Romeo is therefore always partly a heckler who has climbed into the painting: he comments, he doubts, he answers back, he steps aside at the leap. The travesties understood the arrangement instinctively, stationing their Romeos as stand-ins for the cheap seats, men from the audience's own world of slang and song who had somehow got into Verona and intended to enjoy the view. The scene's parody career begins, in that sense, inside the scene itself, in the figure of the hidden listener whose first impulse on hearing the most famous love speech in the language is to interrupt it.

### Why does every spoof begin halfway up a wall?

Because the climb is the scene's recognised unit of action, identifiable before a word is spoken. The text keeps Romeo earthbound in an orchard, negotiating across a height it never asks him to scale. Parody promotes the implied ascent into the main event: a man on a ladder is a joke in one image, desire, effort and risk at once.

The fourth gift is interruption. The play builds its own anti-climax into the wooing: a voice from inside the house, the Nurse calling "Madam" (2.2), hauls Juliet away mid-vow, and she returns, and is hauled away again, so that the scene's rhythm is advance and recall, advance and recall. Comedy lives on exactly that rhythm. A spoof needs no invention to end a swelling declaration; it inherits the Nurse's call as a ready-made deflation, the household reasserting itself against the sublime, and the device survives in parodies that discard everything else from the scene, because it is the scene's own admission that ecstasy has a curfew. The fifth gift is the ending everyone knows. An audience that can supply the tomb unasked gives the parodist a free swerve: end at the window, end at the kiss, end with the lovers alive and singing, and the laugh lands precisely where the suppressed ending presses against the invented one. The tomb scene, by contrast, offers the parodist almost nothing: two bodies, a vault, a monologue over corpses. Its picture is static without being iconic, and its speeches are arguments with the dead. The balcony scene is parodied more than any other passage because it is the play's most pictorial, most interruptible and most survivable moment, the one place where the tragedy behaves like a poster and can therefore be treated as one.

Two further gifts complete the inventory. The first is anthologisation. Long before any living parodist was born, the schoolroom and the quotation book had already excerpted the scene into a set of detachable splendours, the sun speech, the name speech, the parting couplet, and had taught generations to meet the wooing as a string of hits rather than a continuous negotiation. The parodist inherits an audience pre-trained in exactly the spoof's own method, which is quotation with the joins showing. When the Reduced Shakespeare Company's Juliet garbles the rose line, the laugh depends on hearers who met that line as an anthology piece, framed and labelled, in a classroom. The spoof and the syllabus are not opposites. They are the same excerpting machine running at different speeds, and the scene's parody career is unintelligible without the compliment the syllabus paid it first.

The second gift is tonal. The scene is the play's one sustained passage of happiness under pressure, wooing conducted at full stretch with the catastrophe still avoidable, and comedy prefers its victims undefeated. The lovers at the window are quicker, funnier and more verbally inventive than anywhere else in the play: Juliet's wit in the bargaining, Romeo's recovery of his native idiom after the feast, are comic writing by any measure, and the parodist who speeds the scene up is accelerating a liveliness already present rather than imposing one. Mercutio's mockery inside the play, the Nurse's deflations, the hero's own self-parody as a Petrarchan lover in the opening scenes, all rehearse the audience in laughing at this material. The external tradition of spoof is, in that sense, the play's internal comedy escaping the building. A tragedy that contains its own parodist in Mercutio can hardly complain when the profession takes the hint.

## Roméo et Paquette and the Print Parodies That Came First

The tradition's earliest documentable entry is a book, not a performance, and it is French. Jean-François Ducis brought his adapted Roméo et Juliette to the French stage in 1772, in the heavily rewritten manner that made foreign tragedy presentable to Parisian taste. Within a year, print answered with Roméo et Paquette, a parody of the lovers published in 1773, noticed in the January 1773 issue of the Mercure de France and issued in editions that both, with a straight face, claimed Verona as their place of printing; one was printed in Paris and the other in Dijon. Scholarship attributes the piece to a woman writing under the name Mlle Carrière-Doisin, about whom almost nothing else is recorded. The Folger Shakespeare Library, which acquired the work in 2019, describes its method as a deliberate mixing of low and high registers, and its ending as the burlesque signature already fully formed: the tragedy inverted, the lovers alive, and the whole company gathered in a garden to drink wine and celebrate the love the original kills.

Three features of this slight book matter for everything that follows. The first is speed. The parody did not wait for the play to become a monument; it arrived while the ink of the adaptation was barely dry, which corrects a lazy assumption that spoofing is what a later, irreverent age does to a classic. Parody here is contemporary with arrival. The lovers were mocked in France before they were naturalised there, and the mockery was part of the naturalisation, a way of cutting the foreign masterpiece down to a size the local stage could carry. The second feature is the happy ending. Roméo et Paquette does not merely joke on its way to the tomb; it refuses the tomb, and the refusal became the travesty tradition's deepest habit, repeated in London a century later when a resurrected cast closed a burlesque with a group song. The ending-swerve is not a failure of nerve. It is the parodist's thesis about the original: that an audience's love for the couple outruns its respect for the plot, and that the couple can therefore be returned, alive, to the garden. The third feature is register collision. High matter in low language, noble lovers in tavern company: the Bakhtinian account of parody as a hybrid of registers, which the scholar Nicoletta Caputo applies to the nineteenth-century burlesques, is already the operating system of a pamphlet from 1773.

Even the book's title page is in on the joke. Both editions of Roméo et Paquette claim Verona as their place of publication, and both lie, one being Parisian and the other from Dijon, so the parody begins before the first page is turned: the spoof is issued, fictitiously, from the lovers' own city, as though Verona itself had commissioned the deflation of its new monument. The false imprint is a small masterpiece of the form's economy. It costs nothing, it requires no staging, and it announces the work's whole method, which is to occupy the original's premises while changing the locks. Print parody could travel on such devices alone, and the pamphlet's survival in a great library's collection, catalogued and studied two and a half centuries later, shows the pamphlet outlasting nearly every serious French staging of its decade.

The pamphlet's target also deserves precision, because it establishes the tradition's reviewing function. Paquette followed Ducis, and what it travestied was in the first instance Ducis's own tidied, classicised staging, the adaptation through which Paris knew the lovers, rather than a text few French readers could consult. Print parody here is functioning as a review of the current legitimate production, written in the production's own materials. The pattern repeats through the whole survey. The Victorian burlesques keyed their jokes to the concurrent tragedies at the grand houses, updating as those productions changed; the cartoon answers the animated feature era's Shakespeare; the sketch answers the schoolroom's current manner. A balcony spoof is therefore evidence about two objects at once: the scene, and the scene's reigning custodians in the spoof's own decade. Read as reviews, the spoofs form a shadow history of the scene's serious performance, kept by its enemies, which is often the most complete kind.

Print kept the tradition warm in another way. Travesty scripts were published in acting editions, cheap, portable, licensed for performance anywhere, and the joke texts were revised for each revival so that their topical hits stayed topical, as the Folger's account of the travesty vogue emphasises. A published spoof is a spoof that can be restaged by strangers, in towns the original company never visited, with local names dropped into local gaps. The balcony scene's parody tradition is therefore a print tradition as much as a stage one, and its paperwork, the acting editions, the pamphlet parodies, the cartoon sheets, is the chief reason the record can be surveyed at all. Performance evaporates; print testifies. The survey in this article grades its entries by exactly that standard of survival.

## The Travesty Stage: Slang, Song and a Woman's Romeo

The English stage took the tradition industrial. The Folger's survey of Shakespeare travesties dates the vogue from John Poole's Hamlet Travestie, performed in London in 1810 and printed the following year, a piece that kept its original's speeches recognisably intact while stuffing them with jokes, topical hits and songs. A Romeo and Juliet Travestie followed on London stages among the imitators, and the form prospered, on the Folger's account, until about 1890: eight decades in which the lovers could expect, in any given season, to be mourned in blank verse at one address and rhymed in slang at another. The travesty's contract with its house was precise. It took a play the audience knew by heart, often from the legitimate production running concurrently, and re-delivered it at speed, in doggerel and demotic, with the verse's most famous passages bent into puns and the plot's most solemn turns set to the tune of whatever the music halls were humming. Shakespeare scholar Stanley Wells summarises the genre as taking a play for its point of departure and departing, cheerfully, a long way. The departure was the product. Nobody bought a ticket to learn the story.

### What did a travesty audience have to know before the first joke could land?

Everything. The travesty's humour is differential: it registers only against an original held in memory, line by line and pose by pose. A house ignorant of the legitimate production, its stars and its pieties could not measure slang against verse or breeches against hero. The form presupposed a saturated audience, and its jokes are evidence of saturation, not ignorance.

The fullest documented case is Andrew Halliday's Romeo and Juliet Travestie, or The Cup of Cold Poison, first performed at the Royal Strand Theatre in London on 3 November 1859 and published the same year in Lacy's Acting Edition, the trade's standard vehicle for carrying a spoof into every provincial and amateur hand in the country. The standard survey of Victorian burlesque records that the 1859 burlesque of the play carried twenty-three musical numbers, drawn from opera, the serenade from Don Pasquale among them, and from the popular song stock of the day, "Buffalo Gals" and "Nix my Dolly" included. Consider what that score list says about the balcony scene's place in the evening. The wooing of the world's most famous lovers is distributed across twenty-three borrowed tunes, none of them written for the purpose, each importing its own tavern and street-corner associations into the orchard. The lovers do not get music; they get everyone's music. That is the travesty's argument in its purest form: the sublime, re-scored by the crowd, becomes the crowd's property.

The acting edition deserves its own sentence in the record, because print was the burlesque's second stage and its longer-lived one. Halliday's text reached the public the same year as the premiere, in the forty-third volume of Lacy's Acting Edition, with a parallel issue in French's series, and the acting edition was a technology with a purpose: it put a priced, performable script into the hands of provincial companies, amateur societies and parlour theatricals, complete with the assumption that the buyers would mount the thing themselves. The spoof thereby escaped the building that commissioned it. A travesty reviewed in London in November could be re-performed, by amateurs who had never seen the Strand, in any town with a hall, and each re-performance re-ran the dependence: the amateur Juliet needed her neighbours to know the legitimate article for the jokes to register, so the burlesque's print career actively recruited audiences for the tragedy it mocked. The two systems shared not only a city and a repertory but a distribution network, and the network's paperwork, the cheap acting edition, survives in libraries as the tradition's most durable artefact. The pamphlet of 1773 and the acting edition of 1859 are the same instrument at two levels of industrial organisation, print carrying the spoof further than any stage could, and the survey's grades reflect the fact: the best-documented exhibits in the whole record are the ones that were printed.

The casting made the same argument in bodies. The burlesque historian William Davenport Adams, in A Book of Burlesque (1891), records the Strand cast: Marie Wilton, later one of the Victorian stage's great managers, as Juliet, and a Miss Saunders as Romeo. A woman played the lover, in the breeches convention that burlesque used to keep one foot in titillation and the other in critique: the breeches Romeo announced, before a line was spoken, that the evening's masculinity would be performed rather than presumed, and that the swooning hero of the legitimate stage was a costume anyone could rent. The surviving text of Halliday's piece, in the 1873 printing held by the Claremont Colleges library, shows the plot compressed into six scenes and one act, Paris cheerfully dumping Romeo into the crypt and Romeo bouncing out of it, and then the genre's fullest confession of purpose: Queen Mab, Mercutio's fairy, intervenes in person, the dead rise, Shakespeare himself is restored to life among them, and the tragedy closes in a group song. Eighty-six years after a French pamphlet ended the story in a garden with wine, a London burlesque was still ending it with a resurrection and a chorus. The swerve is not decoration. It is the form's founding amendment to the play, moved and carried at every performance: the house votes, nightly, for the lovers to live.

The form had enemies, and their testimony belongs in the survey, because opposition is the other half of the dependence Schoch describes. The Folger's account of the burlesque century preserves an 1833 critic calling a production of Othello, the Moor of Fleet Street "a species of sacrilege", and the word is diagnostic: sacrilege is a crime against a sacred object, and its use concedes that the object was sacred and that the burlesque had got its hands on it. The legitimate stage's defenders understood, earlier than its historians did, that the travesty was not competing with the tragedy for the same reverence. It was taxing the reverence, charging the sacred object a fee for its sanctity, payable in laughter. Wilton's own career supplies the coda. The Juliet of the Strand travesty went on, as Marie Bancroft, to co-manage the Prince of Wales's Theatre and become a byword for respectable, polished, modern stage management. The spoof's Juliet grew up to run the legitimate house. No parable of the two systems' interdependence could improve on the biography.

The form travelled. New York performance records list a Romeo and Juliet burlesque ballet playing thirteen times between 17 and 28 December 1867, the story delivered without words at all, as dance spectacle, in the Christmas season. A ballet burlesque is the reductio of the whole tradition and therefore its clearest evidence: strip the parody of slang, of song, of every verbal joke, and the residue that still reads as Romeo and Juliet is the picture, the raised girl, the reaching boy, the feud's pantomime. The words, it turns out, were always the dispensable layer. Manfred Draudt's observation, quoted in the Folger's account, that burlesques served as a link between the popular and the elevated theatres, understates the traffic's direction. The elevated theatre supplied the picture; the popular theatre supplied the distribution; and the balcony, furnished, lit and climbed in a thousand cheap houses, became more fixed in the public mind than any single legitimate staging could make it.

## The Serenade Re-scored: Song as the Spoof's Native Tongue

Underneath the whole tradition runs a musical assumption: that a man beneath a window at night is there to sing. The play gives Romeo no instrument and no song, nothing but speech overheard and speech exchanged, yet the folk version of the scene that Europe carried in its head was the serenade, the lover's formal music under the beloved's window, and the parodists, who worked from the folk version, scored the scene accordingly. The travesty scores are the documentary deposit of that assumption. When the standard survey of Victorian burlesque catalogues the 1859 burlesque's twenty-three numbers, the list reads like a deliberate re-classification of the scene by genre: the wooing is assigned a serenade borrowed from comic opera, Donizetti's Don Pasquale, an opera about an elderly bachelor's marriage scheme collapsing under its own intrigue; the street and the fairground contribute their own hits, "Buffalo Gals" and "Nix my Dolly"; and the tragedy is thereby re-housed, musically, in the two institutions that had always owned the window at night, the comic stage and the popular songbook.

The borrowing of the Don Pasquale serenade is criticism of a high order, whether or not its arranger intended it as such. In Donizetti's opera the serenade belongs to a plot of feigned marriage and managed deception, sung within a fiction the characters themselves are staging. To pour that music under Juliet's window is to assert, in the only language a burlesque orchestra commands, that the balcony scene is also a staged fiction within its play: two children performing a wooing out of the romance conventions they have absorbed, overheard by an audience that knows the script better than they do. The tragedy's admirers had always treated the scene as nature. The burlesque's score reclassifies it as repertoire. Every subsequent spoof that gives its Romeo a guitar, a ukulele or a lovesick croon is spending the 1859 insight, that the scene sounds most truthful when its music admits to being borrowed.

Song was also the travesty's fastest-moving part, and its speed explains the form's strange freshness across its eighty years. A spoken joke dates with its target; a song dates with its chart. The Folger's survey records that travesties were revised for revival so that the hits stayed current, and the score is where the revision bit hardest, since last season's melody in this season's burlesque is not archaeology but betrayal, an admission that the house has stopped listening to the street. The burlesque score is thus a dated document in a way the burlesque script is not: it preserves, season by season, the sound of the audience's own musical vernacular, the vernacular into which the wooing was being translated. The historian of the scene's reception can read the scores as a listening diary of the nineteenth-century crowd, and the crowd, on the evidence, insisted on hearing its own tunes under the window. The class war that Dentith hears in the travesty's slang had a soundtrack, and the soundtrack was the louder weapon.

The cartoon inherited the habit whole. Animation thinks in music the way burlesque did, cueing period, place and feeling from the orchestra pit, and a seven-minute short that must establish "Shakespeare" in seconds reaches for the same toolkit: period dress, elevated diction for a line or two, and music that certifies the old world before the gags knock it down. The serenade's long career, from the Strand's orchestra to the cartoon's score, is the transmission system's quietest channel. Audiences who could not quote a line of the wooing could hum its setting, and a tradition that can be hummed does not die.

## The Balcony in Cartoons: Bugs Bunny, an Uncaught Juliet and the Pun in the Title

The animated cartoon inherited the travesty's contract and tightened it. A theatrical short had perhaps seven minutes, no interval, an audience of children in front and adults behind, and a single obligation: the joke must land on sight. Shakespeare in animation, as the scholarship of the cartoon canon records, sorted itself into two kinds of borrowing. The first is the pun in the title, where the lovers' names are rented as a sign for romance itself: Popeye's Rodeo Romeo (1946), Pépé Le Pew's Scenti-mental Romeo (1951), Bugs Bunny's own Rabbit Romeo (1957), The Flintstones' "Dino and Juliet" (1964), in which the pun is applied to a pet dinosaur's courtship, and The Smurfs' "Romeo and Smurfette" (1981). In this tier nothing of the play is present but the names, and the names are enough, which is itself a finding: by the middle of the twentieth century "Romeo" in a title performed the whole work of announcement, promising desire, obstacle and pursuit in a single word. The tier has a subdivision worth marking. Several of its Romeos are pursuers in the comic mode, and the pursuit is the joke's engine: the Pépé Le Pew short casts its Romeo as a skunk whose Juliet is a cat fleeing his scent, so the balcony scene's geometry, one lover advancing in rapture and the other in retreat, is preserved with the rapture made one-sided. The animation scholarship that catalogues these shorts describes a standing rival-Romeo tradition, in which cartoon Romeos compete, scheme and fail, and it draws the moral the travesty century had already proved: the subversion and the sustenance are the same gesture. Every failed cartoon courtship re-advertised the successful one it was measured against, and children who had never heard of Verona learned that a Romeo is a creature who climbs toward a window and is refused, which is a distorted education in the play and an education all the same. The Flintstones entry marks the tier's outer limit and its quiet revelation. In "Dino and Juliet" (1964) the names are bestowed on a pet dinosaur and the neighbour's dog, a courtship of animals conducted across a fence, and the joke still works, which means the names have completed their migration from characters to grammatical function: they are no longer persons but the nominative case of thwarted love, applicable to any species, any scale, any fence. The animated feature of 2011, with its garden gnomes, is routinely treated as a novelty for applying the story to ornaments; on the cartoon record it is a late, expensive instance of a demotion the television tier had performed, cheaply and completely, half a century earlier. The names' durability is the point the tier system exists to prove. A dramatist's characters can be forgotten. A piece of grammar cannot, because grammar is re-spoken by every sentence that uses it, and the spoofs had converted two Veronese teenagers into a part of speech.

The second tier is the enacted spoof, where the cartoon stages the material, and its supreme document is Warner Bros.' A Witch's Tangled Hare (1959), directed by Abe Levitow from a story by Michael Maltese, with Mel Blanc and June Foray supplying the voices.

The cartoon's plot is a chase: Witch Hazel wants Bugs Bunny for her cauldron, and the pursuit runs through a castle whose mailbox reads "Macbeth", past a rhyming poet cut in the national playwright's image, who stands scribbling down everything the witch says. Mid-chase, the cartoon stops running and performs. Bugs, cornered in the castle tower, casts himself as Romeo and the witch as Juliet, and Hazel, delighted, begins Juliet's lines in earnest, in character, at the window, before the two of them slide into improvisation. Then comes the gag the whole short exists to deliver. Juliet leaps from the window into her Romeo's arms, as the tableau's ballet logic demands, and Bugs steps aside. She crashes to the ground. The spoof's thesis is contained in that step aside: the cartoon has measured the distance between the picture's promise, that love leaps and is caught, and the physics of the actual world, and it has charged the difference to the picture's account. The short then closes by abandoning the play entirely for its neighbour, as the poet is unmasked as a lovelorn Sam Crubish who stood his date up at the wrong address, and Bugs delivers the sign-off, "2-B or not 2-B, that is the question", a Hamlet joke, which makes the same point from the other side. In the cartoon economy the plays are one warehouse of famous lines, and the balcony is simply its best-lit shelf. The Crubish device repays a closer look, because it is the cartoon's one joke about the tradition itself rather than about any play. The poet who has stalked the action with his quill, apparently composing the works of Shakespeare out of a witch's conversation, is revealed as a nonentity whose whole claim to significance was a missed appointment: the authorship of the canon, the cartoon proposes, is a clerical accident, famous lines waiting for whoever happens to write them down. It is a seven-minute version of the anti-bardolatry the burlesque stage had practised, with the mockery aimed, as the adjudication below finds it always is, at the cult rather than the text. The cartoon even protects the lines while demoting the author: Bugs handles the Hamlet tag with the reverence of a master of ceremonies, and Hazel's Juliet is granted her moment of earnest quotation before the improvisation begins. The spoof's contract is honoured in the breach of everything except the words' fame.

The mailbox repays the same attention. Before the chase reaches the tower, the cartoon has already told its audience how to file what follows: the castle's mailbox reads "Macbeth", which is to say that in the cartoon's geography the plays are not works but addresses, neighbouring properties in a single famous street, and a chase may pass from one to another without changing district. The gag costs one painted word and buys the whole warehouse at once, the canon as a place a rabbit can be pursued through. It also quietly states the enacted spoof's terms of trade. A mailbox is where letters arrive for the resident; the cartoon's Shakespeare is a resident who receives the culture's mail, its allusions, its schoolchildren, its advertisers, whether he answers or not, and the spoofs are simply the loudest items in the delivery. The tower window, when the chase arrives at it minutes later, has already been framed by that one word as an address in the same street: the wooing is not an interruption of the cartoon's Shakespeare but its next delivery, and Hazel's earnest Juliet is the resident answering the door in character.

### Why does the cartoon Juliet jump, and why is she never caught?

Because the leap is the tableau's one moment of pure trust, and a cartoon's business is auditing trust. In the text nobody leaps; Juliet descends by arrangement and rope ladder, offstage. The picture tradition replaced the ladder with the leap, and the cartoon applies cartoon physics: the promise is tested, the arms withdrawn, the fall the verdict.

The title-pun tier deserves a second look, because it explains the enacted tier's confidence. A generation reared on Rodeo Romeo and Rabbit Romeo did not need the balcony explained to them when A Witch's Tangled Hare arrived; the names had been doing light romantic duty in the cartoon schedules for over a decade, and the enacted spoof could spend its seven minutes on variation rather than exposition. Animation scholarship on the Shakespeare cartoons makes the general point that the rival-Romeo tradition, the cartoon Romeos competing for their Juliets, both subverted the figure and sustained public interest in the plays across successive decades of shorts. Subversion and sustenance in one gesture is the travesty contract restated for the cinema, and the cartoon form sharpened one clause of it: brevity. The Strand burlesque had an act; the cartoon has minutes; the sitcom title, arriving later, has two words. Each compression strips another layer and finds the picture still legible underneath, which is the strongest evidence the tradition offers that the picture, not the play, is the unit being transmitted.

## Sketch Comedy's Verdict: Cod-Shakespeare, a Wig and a Ladder

Broadcast-era sketch comedy took the scene up with a different instrument. Where burlesque rewrote the play, the sketch rewrote the manner, and its founding document is British: "So That's the Way You Like It", the Shakespeare spoof in Beyond the Fringe, the revue by Alan Bennett, Peter Cook, Jonathan Miller and Dudley Moore that opened at the Edinburgh Festival on 22 August 1960 and carried the satire boom into London and New York. The sketch stages no plot from any play. Four performers in a muddle of modern dress and caricatured historical headgear deliver invented Shakespearean verse at full rhetorical throttle, Miller's king despatching his followers to battle in lines that pile clause on clause before collapsing into the exit line preserved in the revue's record: "I most royally shall now to bed / To sleep off all the nonsense I've just said." The target is not a story but a sound, the sound of schoolroom Shakespeare, grand, marmoreal and faintly unintelligible, and the sketch's discovery is that the sound alone, detached from every plot, is sufficient material. An audience that has been force-fed the manner will laugh at the manner faithfully reproduced with the sense removed. The revue was broadcast by the BBC in 1964 and its Shakespeare sketch survives in the national portrait record, the four performers photographed in the sketch's muddle of modern dress and mock-historical headgear, which gives the ephemeral thing a documentary afterlife few sketches earn. Its importance for the balcony is indirect and total. Beyond the Fringe taught two generations of revue, sketch and student companies that cod-Shakespeare was a complete comic instrument requiring no plot, no set and no knowledge beyond the schoolroom's, and every subsequent company that reached for a famous scene to spoof was spending capital that 1960 had raised. The balcony, as the most schooled scene in the canon, was the largest single beneficiary of the instrument's invention.

### How long does the Reduced Shakespeare Company give the lovers?

A slice of an evening built to hold everything. The Complete Works covers or mentions all of the plays in roughly an hour and a half, and within that economy the lovers receive one of the two longest treatments, a distinction shared with Hamlet. Compression has its own scale of honour, and the play ranks near its top.

That discovery governs the balcony's sketch career, because the balcony scene is the manner at its most concentrated: the most memorised wooing in the language, delivered in the most elevated register, across the most stagey piece of stage geometry. The balcony spoof in sketch form therefore rarely bothers with plot at all. It bothers with altitude, diction and duration, the three things a sketch can inflate in ninety seconds. The tradition's flagship is theatrical rather than televised. The Reduced Shakespeare Company's The Complete Works of William Shakespeare (Abridged), written and first performed by Adam Long, Daniel Singer and Jess Winfield, premiered at the Edinburgh Festival Fringe in 1987 and settled at London's Criterion Theatre from 1996 to 2005, a run of nine years and, on one production account, 3,744 performances. Its Romeo and Juliet is among the show's two longest pieces, and its central device is Victorian: Juliet is played by a grown man in a dress and wig, the breeches joke inverted and restored at once, since the all-male casting of the playwright's own stage and the cross-dressed Romeo of the Strand are both somewhere in the gag's ancestry. Reviewers of the touring productions record the balcony business done with ladders ranged against the stage, the climb promoted, exactly as the cartoon promoted the leap, into the scene's visible essence, and they record the rose speech mangled into the show's signature spoonerism, Juliet announcing that a nose by any other name would smell as sweet. The line is worth pausing on. It is a joke about the misquotation tradition itself: the parodist's Juliet does not merely garble the text, she garbles it along the groove two centuries of quotation had already cut, swapping one body word for another while the syntax stands untouched, a vandalism so conservative it functions as a memory aid. The show's afterlife confirms the function. The Complete Works has been seen in over twenty countries, and reviewers of its touring productions reach, unprompted, for the language of the classroom: an excellent night, one Southampton notice observed, for young adults studying the set texts, who will not forget the stories after this. The observation is Schoch's law operating inside the school market itself. A spoof that students use as a revision aid has crossed the last border between the two transmission systems, and the crossing was so complete that teachers of Shakespearean burlesque assign the burlesques themselves, a practice documented in the scholarship of Shakespeare teaching, where the travesty is set beside the tragedy as its most honest contemporary review. The parodist has become a set text about the set text.

The run's arithmetic belongs in the record as the institutional punchline. A production history of the Complete Works counts 3,744 performances across the show's nine years at the Criterion, a tenure in a single West End house that the overwhelming majority of straight Shakespeare productions, in any century, never approach. The spoof did not merely visit the legitimate theatre's territory; it took out a longer lease than the tragedies usually manage, night after night, in the same building, selling the canon back to the canon's own city at the canon's own prices. Draudt's ferry had become a scheduled service with a permanent berth. Whatever parody is, on the evidence of the Criterion years it is not a parasite at the feast. It is one of the feast's caterers, and on some streets, for some decades, the only one still open.

The sitcom tier of the tradition operates by the same economy at one further remove. When The Simpsons titled an episode "Rome-Old and Juli-Eh" (first broadcast 11 March 2007, written by Daniel Chun and directed by Nancy Kruse), the spoof consisted of nothing but the title and a plot about Grampa Simpson's romance with Selma Bouvier, a union opposed by family and shadowed by decay, played for the comedy of two exhausted people grabbing at late happiness. No balcony appears. No line is quoted. The names, inflected by two syllables of sitcom spelling, do all the work: they import the feud, the obstacle, the disproportion between feeling and circumstance, and they import, too, the audience's foreknowledge that this love story is unlikely to be granted a dignified ending. The episode drew an audience of 8.79 million on its first American broadcast, every one of whom was expected to decode the title without assistance. A joke that eighty million school lessons stand behind needs no footnote, and a title that needs no footnote is the measure of how completely the spoof tradition and the schoolroom tradition have merged into a single distribution network for the same two names.

## The Schoolroom's Revenge: The Spoof as Unofficial Tutor

Every tradition surveyed here converges on an institution neither the burlesque managers nor the cartoon studios built: the classroom. The balcony scene is, for a large share of its parodists' audiences, school property before it is theatre property, met at a desk, in an anthology, under instruction. The spoof's deepest dependence is therefore pedagogical. It does not merely require the audience to know the scene; it requires the audience to have been taught the scene, reverently, excerpt by excerpt, until the reverence itself became the most recognisable thing about it. Beyond the Fringe's manner-sketch is unintelligible except as an answer to lessons. The Reduced Shakespeare Company's reviewers, reaching for the language of revision and set texts, were recording the same dependence from the other side: the compressed spoof is legible to students precisely because the syllabus pre-installed its original, and useful to them because compression is a mnemonic.

The Folger's survey of the travesty centuries preserves a distinction that sorts this territory cleanly. The Library's scholars separate philosophical travesties, which use a play as an instrument for exploring ideas, from popular travesties, which traffic in a play's most famous touchstones for the broadest house. The balcony spoofs belong overwhelmingly to the popular line, and the reason is the schoolroom: the touchstones are what the syllabus and the anthology in fact distribute, and the window wooing is the canon's most distributed touchstone of all. A popular travesty of the tomb scene would fail the form's first test, instant recognisability, not because the tomb is obscure to readers but because the tomb was never excerpted into the common memory the way the wooing was. Parody's map of the play is drawn by the teachers. The spoof tradition is the syllabus's shadow, falling exactly where the lessons fell.

The shadow has begun, in the documented record, to be assigned as homework. A study of Shakespearean burlesque in the teaching journal This Rough Magic sets out the classroom use of the burlesques themselves, the travesties read beside the plays as instruments for teaching what the plays meant to the audiences who first consumed both. The reversal completes the circle the adjudication below describes. The parody that presupposed the lesson serves the lesson outright; the spoof that lived off the school's reverence pays the school a dividend in the only currency schools accept, comprehension that survives the examination. Schoch's dependence law, framed for the theatre, holds in the classroom with the polarity reversed: the original, as a teaching object, can no longer be fully understood without its burlesques either, because the burlesques are the record of what the original's fame felt like from inside the centuries that made it. The balcony scene's second transmission system ends by tutoring its first.

## The Thirty-Second Grammar of a Balcony Spoof

Lay the tradition's exhibits side by side and a fixed grammar emerges, a sequence of moves so stable across media and centuries that a competent spoof can be assembled from the moves alone, the way a sentence is assembled from parts of speech. The InsightCrunch parody survey records the principal exhibits and grades each by the tier of its documentation.

The grammar's first move is elevation: a raised playing space established at once, rail, window frame or ladder, because the spoof's contract requires the picture before any word can be risked. The second move is the call, delivered at full volume and always as a call, the soliloquy flattened into address. The third move is the answer from below, and here the tradition splits into its two great families: Romeo answers wrongly, mistaking the question, producing identification gags and name gags in industrial quantities, or Romeo answers with a body, falling off the ladder, missing the catch, arriving with the wrong equipment. The fourth move is the interruption, the voice from inside the house, inherited from the Nurse and reassigned to mothers, wardens, neighbours, policemen and the irate owners of the wall being climbed; the interruption is the spoof's metronome, the beat on which ecstasy is recalled to order. The fifth move is the swerve ending, the refusal of the tomb: a kiss, a fall, a chase, a song, a resurrection, anything that closes the circuit before the vault. Five moves, and the whole of the play's two hundred lines are nowhere required. A researcher assembling a dossier of these exhibits, entry by entry and grade by grade, works the way the compilers of [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) describe their own practice, keeping each document beside the note that grades it, so the survey can be audited rather than merely believed.

What the grammar omits is as fixed as what it keeps. The scene's middle, the long bargaining in which Juliet tests the vow, warns against swearing by the inconstant moon (2.2.109), extracts the marriage proposal and sets the hour for the messenger, has no move in the grammar at all. Parody keeps the frame of the wooing and discards its negotiation, because negotiation is talk about terms and spoof has no budget for terms. The consequence for the transmitted scene is plain. Generations who meet the balcony first in its spoofs meet a Juliet who calls, swoons and leaps, and never meet the Juliet who cross-examines, sets conditions and stage-manages the entire courtship from her window. The parody tradition's Juliet is the picture's Juliet, and the picture was never the play's.

Run the grammar once, slowly, and its economy shows. The window opens and the girl leans out: the audience's memory supplies Verona, the feud, the feast just finished and the deaths still to come, an entire tragedy imported in a single glance at no cost to the work in hand. The call sounds: recognition completes itself, and with recognition comes the spoof's licence, because a house that has identified the property stops auditing the workmanship and starts anticipating the jokes. The climb begins: anticipation converts into suspense of the purely physical kind, the one suspense that survives translation into every medium, including the still cartoon panel, where a figure frozen halfway up a drainpipe is a joke held in suspended animation. The inversion lands, in whichever of its licensed forms, and the laugh it earns is banked at once as the next set-up, the interruption, the grammar's fifth beat resetting the geometry the fourth disturbed. Each move is a withdrawal from an account the audience filled elsewhere, and the account is refilled, with interest, every time a schoolroom, a stage or a screen re-teaches the scene. The grammar is therefore not a description of individual works but of an economy, and like every economy it runs on confidence: the confidence that the window, the call and the climb will still be common property when the next spoof is built. On the whole of the record surveyed here, that confidence has never yet been misplaced.

## The Joke That Needs the Error: Parody and the Misheard Call

One component of the grammar deserves separate trial, because it is the point where parody stops merely inheriting the public's version and starts actively maintaining an error. Every balcony spoof stages a Juliet who calls down to a Romeo she can see. The text stages a Juliet who does not know Romeo exists within a mile of the orchard until he speaks and frightens her: "How camest thou hither, tell me, and wherefore?" is the cry of a girl discovering an intruder, not greeting a correspondent. [The misreading that turns "wherefore" from a question about reasons into a question about location](/2029/08/10/wherefore-art-thou-misreading/) has its own full anatomy elsewhere in this series, and its persistence is usually blamed on the classroom's failure to correct it. Parody's role in the persistence is more specific and more mechanical. A spoof cannot use the correct reading. A Juliet reasoning privately about names, unaware of her audience, offers the parodist nothing to answer, no cue for the gag, no geometry for the ladder. The joke requires an addressee, so the joke rebuilds the scene with the addressee installed, and every restaging re-teaches the rebuilt version to a fresh cohort of viewers who will carry it, as their picture of the scene, into every subsequent encounter with the text.

This is the one place where the tradition's defence, that mockery merely spends a fame it did not create, breaks down. On the wherefore point the spoofs are not spending; they are minting. The picture they circulate contradicts the text on a matter of plain fact, who is speaking to whom, and the contradiction is load-bearing: remove it and the standard spoof collapses into a girl talking to herself while a man watches from the dark, which is the play, and is not, in the grammar's terms, a spoof at all. The honest verdict on the misreading is therefore dual. Parody preserves the balcony scene's fame with a fidelity no serious institution matches, and it preserves, as part of the same package, a specific falsehood about the scene's most famous line, renewed at every performance. The error survives in cartoons and sketches not because teachers fail but because comedians succeed: the wrong version is the funny one, and funny is the stronger distribution network.

Why does no spoof correct the staging, when a correction would seem to offer a connoisseur's joke of its own? Because a corrected balcony scene is unstageable as comedy. Play the passage as written, a voice at an unlit window, a motionless listener below, a negotiation conducted across a dark garden, and for its first minutes the work is indistinguishable from a straight production; the house has nothing to laugh at until it works out that accuracy is the joke, by which time the sketch's thirty seconds are spent and its slot is over. The error, by contrast, is funny in the first frame. The gag economy exerts a selection pressure of the purest kind: variants of the scene that can be recognised instantly survive and multiply, while the true scene, which takes minutes to declare itself, is outcompeted in every medium where time is priced. Scholars print the correction in books, where time is cheap. Comedians transmit the error on stages and screens, where time is dear. The division of labour is not a conspiracy against accuracy; it is a market doing what markets do, and the misreading's ubiquity is the market's verdict on which version of the scene can pay its way in public. Even the knowing houses, the audiences of the compression shows who have the text by heart and laugh at the error as an error, pay for the rebuilt scene rather than the true one: their wink requires the rebuilding, since a wink at a correction nobody staged would be invisible. Knowledge, in this economy, does not rescue the text. It merely adds a second laugh on top of the error, and the error collects both.

## The Line Nobody Garbles: The Call as the Tradition's Fixed Point

For a tradition built on vandalism, the spoofs are strikingly protective of one element. The call itself, "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?", is delivered straight, or as straight as a wig and a ladder allow, in spoof after spoof, while everything around it is slang, song or pratfall. The Folger's account of the founding travesty makes the pattern's origin plain: Poole's method was to stick closely to the original's speeches while stuffing the joins with jokes, and the method never changed. Witch Hazel begins her Juliet in earnest, in character, with the lines done properly, and only then does the improvisation start. The Reduced Shakespeare Company's documented garble falls on the rose line, a sentence later in the scene, while the call that opens the wooing is the show's recognised cue, the sound that tells the house which sketch has begun. The tradition garbles promiscuously and spares the trademark.

The reason is commercial before it is reverent. The call is the spoof's ignition: it must be exact, because recognition is the product and an altered call would delay the laugh by the seconds a spoof cannot spend. But the protection has a side effect the commercial account does not capture. By keeping one sentence of the text inviolate across four centuries of handling, the spoof tradition has performed the office of a quotation dictionary with better distribution, fixing the canonical wording of the scene's most famous line in more memories than any edition has reached. The irony is layered. The sentence the spoofs protect is the sentence they misunderstand, since the call is staged as a summons by the same works that transmit its wording intact. Text preserved, sense inverted, in a single gesture: the tradition's whole double nature is audible in its one act of fidelity.

There is a corollary for the survey method. When a spoof is found garbling the call itself, treating the sentence as raw material rather than as the cue, it has usually stopped being a balcony spoof and become something else, a general Shakespeare burlesque in which no single scene is the property. The call's integrity is thus a diagnostic: it marks the border between the scene-specific tradition surveyed here and the broader Shakespop in which the balcony is one exhibit among thirty-seven plays. The border matters for the verdict. A tradition with a protected centre is not dissolving its object. It is curating it, with the curator's usual mixture of love, commerce and carelessness about everything outside the frame.

## The Happy Ending: The Tradition's One Serious Argument

Across two and a half centuries, through pamphlets, burlesques, ballets, cartoons and sitcom titles, the spoofs disagree about everything except the finish. The lovers must not die. Roméo et Paquette closes in a garden, with wine. The choice of a garden deserves its weight. The pamphlet does not merely spare the lovers; it relocates them to the one setting the play's own orchard promised and the plot withheld, an outdoor place of ease where the wooing's idiom of fruit and flowers can be cashed as refreshment rather than as metaphor. Every later swerve repeats the relocation in its own idiom: the resurrection staged as a party, the fall that substitutes embarrassment for injury, the gnomes left in possession of their garden. The tradition's happy endings are all set in the orchard the tragedy was too busy to enjoy, and the consistency of the setting is the criticism's signature. The spoofs are not refusing the play's world. They are insisting on the half of it the play itself kept deferring, the morning after the balcony, the life the wooing was for. Halliday's travesty sends Queen Mab onstage to reverse the body count in person. The cartoon substitutes a pratfall for a poisoning. The animated feature of 2011, filed by the Folger's survey among the popular travesties, keeps its gnomes alive and in love. Even the Reduced Shakespeare Company, which plays the deaths for speed, plays them as a routine, a bit of stage business to be applauded rather than a catastrophe to be absorbed. The consistency is too broad to be accident or cowardice, and it deserves to be read as what it is: the parody tradition's one sustained piece of criticism, aimed not at the lovers but at the plot that kills them.

### Whom is a balcony spoof actually mocking?

Almost never the lovers, and never for long. The standing targets are the machinery around them: the feud's etiquette, the staging tradition, the schoolroom's reverence, a plot running on an undelivered letter. The lovers are handled with residual care, being the asset the enterprise trades on. The mockery aims at the frame and stops at the centre.

The criticism embedded in the happy ending is precise. The tragedy asks its audience to accept that a sequence of accidents, a quarantined friar, a letter gone astray, a few minutes' mistiming, converts the play's most alive couple into its corpses, and that the conversion is somehow the meaning of the story. The spoof tradition votes against the conversion. Its resurrections are crude instruments, a fairy with a wand, a heroine who bounces, but the vote they register is a real one about where the play's value sits: in the wooing, not the waste. Read this way, the travesty's group song is a review in theatrical form, and a favourable one, since no company resurrects characters its house is indifferent to. The dead rise in spoof after spoof because the audience's attachment is the resource every parodist is mining, and the resurrection is the receipt that proves the attachment was there to be mined. [The still-image heirs of this tradition, the captioned frames and reaction pictures](/2029/10/12/romeo-and-juliet-in-memes/), operate under a different economy and belong to another article's survey; the performed and printed spoof, which must fill a stage or a page for a paying house, cannot afford indifference to its subjects, and its endings confess the fact.

## Mockery That Preserves: Hutcheon, Dentith and Schoch Judged

The tradition's students disagree about what kind of act a parody is, and the balcony record is a good courtroom for their disagreement, because it is long, dated and unusually well documented. Linda Hutcheon, in A Theory of Parody (1985), defines parody as "imitation characterized by ironic inversion, not always at the expense of the parodied text", and in another formulation as "repetition with critical distance". The definition is built to accommodate reverence: the parodist repeats because the original matters, and the irony marks a difference within a continuity the parodist has no wish to break. Simon Dentith, in Parody (2000), writes against exactly this softness. His definition makes contention definitional: parody is "any cultural practice which provides a relatively polemical allusive imitation of another cultural production or practice". The Japan Review's summary of the quarrel puts it flatly: Dentith chooses the word "polemical" in conscious opposition to Hutcheon, who considered it wrong to define parody by an attacking relation to its source. One theorist's parody is a dialogue; the other's is a duel.

Richard Schoch's Not Shakespeare: Bardolatry and Burlesque in the Nineteenth Century (2002) supplies the evidence both theories must answer. Schoch's burlesques are manifestly "not Shakespeare", yet they purport, in his account, to be the most Shakespearean things on the Victorian stage, and his explanation is a law of dependence: "burlesque can never displace Shakespeare: because it cannot be understood without Shakespeare." The burlesque house and the legitimate house were not rivals for the same evening so much as two departments of one institution, the spoof reviewing the production, extending its run in another register, and drilling its audience in the very text it travestied. As audiences' familiarity with the plays thinned, Schoch notes, their capacity for the burlesques thinned with it. The spoof's market is the original's readership, and the spoof knows it.

Two scholars of the burlesque itself testify in support, from inside the Folger's own survey of the travesties, and their evidence refines the verdict. Nicoletta Caputo reads the nineteenth-century burlesques through the Bakhtinian account of parody as a hybrid of registers, the elevated text and the demotic answer forced to occupy one utterance. The balcony travesties are the hybrid's perfect specimen, since the scene already contains its two registers in embryo, the lovers' high lyric strain and the Nurse's prose gravity, and the burlesque merely promotes the lower voice to co-author. Manfred Draudt, in the same survey, assigns the burlesques an institutional function: they served as an important link between the popular and the more elevated theatres, a ferry running between the two banks of the river. The ferry metaphor completes what Hutcheon and Dentith each half-describe. A ferry attacks neither bank; it lives by the distance between them, and it keeps both banks in business by making the crossing cheap. The travesty houses and the patent houses were, on Draudt's evidence, one system with a shuttle service, and the shuttle carried passengers in both directions, tragedy's audience to the joke and the joke's audience, in time, to the tragedy. Parody as transport, not warfare: the balcony evidence, with its songs drawn from the street and its Juliets promoted to manage the legitimate stage, fits the ferry better than the battlefield.

A fourth witness deserves cross-examination. Stanley Wells, surveying the burlesque heyday, describes the typical production as taking a play "as its point of departure" and creating from it a mainly comic entertainment bearing little relation to the original. The shrug in the formulation, departure with no return ticket, understates what his own examples show. The burlesque that bore no relation to its original would have been unintelligible and unsold; the relation was the entertainment, measured nightly against the audience's memory of the legitimate article. Wells is right about the distance travelled and wrong about the direction of the bond, and Schoch's dependence corrects him: the further the departure, the longer the tether had to be, and the tether was knowledge. Judged against the balcony evidence, each position wins its own century. Dentith's polemic describes the travesty era's surface accurately: the burlesques were combat in a class war of theatres, slang against blank verse, breeches against propriety, the cheap house jeering at the dear one's pieties, and an 1833 critic who called a burlesque Othello "a species of sacrilege" was reporting a real front line. But the polemic's target, examined closely, is almost never the play. It is the play's custodians: the tragedians' pomposity, the cult of the relic, the prices, the reverence. The text itself is the weapon both sides share. Hutcheon's account describes the later record, the cartoons and sketches, with equal accuracy and the opposite emphasis: the inversion is real, Juliet does fall, Romeo does climb a ladder in a fright wig's company, yet the expense falls on the tableau's furniture and the audience's own sentimentality, while the scene is re-broadcast, intact in outline, to millions who would never have opened the play. Where Hutcheon understates the case is in her gentleness about consequence: repetition with critical distance still edits, and on the wherefore question the distance collapsed and the repetition drilled an error. Schoch's law explains why the two verdicts coexist. A form that cannot be understood without its original cannot afford the original's death, so its attacks curve, necessarily, toward the frame and away from the centre. The balcony parodies are polemical about everything except the one thing they cannot survive losing.

## What the Spoofs Cannot Do: The Tradition's Own Confession

A survey of powers should end with the limits, because the limits are the tradition's most honest criticism of itself. The spoofs cannot do the bargaining. Across four centuries of exhibits there is no durable spoof of the scene's centre, the negotiation in which Juliet tests the vow, refuses the moon as a witness, extracts a proposal and sets the messenger's hour, for the sufficient reason that negotiation cannot be compressed into a glance: it is two intelligences changing each other's minds in real time, and a parody that reproduced it would have reproduced the scene. The grammar's silence about the middle is the spoof tradition's standing admission that the play's best writing is the part fame cannot excerpt. Teachers who complain that the parodies trivialise the scene have the geometry backwards. The parodies map, with precision, exactly the territory of the scene that fame had already trivialised, and they stop, as if at a surveyed border, where the writing begins.

The spoofs also cannot kill. The swerve ending, surveyed above as the tradition's one serious argument, doubles as its one absolute incapacity: no exhibit in the record plays the double suicide for its laugh and keeps the laugh, because death at the speed of a gag curdles the contract the whole form runs on. Even the compression company, which races the deaths as stage business, frames them as applause cues, corpses as choreography. And the spoofs cannot invent their rescuer from outside the play. When Halliday's travesty needs the dead undone, it sends for Queen Mab, Mercutio's fairy, the play's own resident fiction, the dream-figure of its most famous set speech. The rescue is drawn from inside the property, as if even the vandals observed a rule that help must come from the house. A fourth limit completes the confession. The spoofs cannot use the play's adults. The Friar, the Nurse in her serious function as go-between, the parents whose feud drives the machine, appear in the tradition only as interruptions and obstacles, voices and silhouettes, never as subjects, because the spoof's economy has no currency for motive: it can spend recognition but not explanation, and the adults are the play's explanation. A balcony grammar built from the feud's logic would have to stop and argue the feud, and argument is the one expense the thirty seconds cannot bear. The tradition's Verona is therefore a city populated exclusively by the young, the climbing and the interrupting, which is, not coincidentally, the Verona of the picture trade and the tourist poster as well. Parody, painting and tourism divide the play between them on identical lines, and the adults are left to the editions.

The pattern across the three limits is consistent, and it is the deepest evidence for the verdict that follows. The spoof tradition behaves, at every boundary, like a tenant rather than a demolition crew: it rearranges the furniture, paints the walls its own colours, and leaves the structure, the bargaining, the deaths, the fiction's own machinery, exactly where the lease requires them to stand.

## The Verdict on the Spoofing Tradition

The thesis has a test, and the record supplies it. If the spoofs were the scene's solvent, their attentions would wander as the scene faded, the tradition thinning as its object thinned, until some generation found the window unrecognisable and the jokes stopped landing. The observed pattern runs the other way at every point where it can be checked. The spoofs cluster thickest where the scene's fame is thickest: in the travesty decades, when the legitimate Romeo and Juliet filled the grand houses and the burlesque played across the street; in the cartoon decades, when animated Shakespeare reached its largest audiences; in the compression era, when the canon was re-sold to students as a single evening's entertainment. And the control case stands inside the same play. The tomb scene, equal in fame among readers and greater in consequence, is nearly absent from the spoof record, because it was never excerpted, pictured and taught into the reflexive recognition the gag economy spends. Parody does not follow merit, or even readership. It follows recognisability, and recognisability is the very thing it manufactures. The tradition is the fame's engine as well as its exhaust, and the two functions cannot be separated without stopping the machine.

There is a budgetary way to state the same finding. The scene's fame is not self-maintaining. It is kept up, expensively, by editions and examinations, by theatre companies and tourist boards, by the whole apparatus of legitimate custody, and the apparatus has always known that custody alone produces reverence, which is fame's least transmissible form. What the apparatus cannot manufacture is the fame's circulation among people who will never open an edition or sit an examination, and circulation is precisely the spoof tradition's product, delivered free of charge to the custodians by pamphleteers, burlesque managers, cartoon studios and sketch companies pursuing nothing but the laugh. The parodists are the fame's unpaid maintenance crew, and their wages are the custodians' discomfort. Every institution that deplored the travesties depended on the travesties' audiences to know why the deploring mattered; every syllabus that corrected the wherefore error relied on the error's carriers to have made the line worth correcting. Maintenance is never thanked in its own century. The survey's four centuries of evidence suggest it should be, in this one case at least, itemised.

The arrangement has one genuine vulnerability, and honesty requires it to be named. The spoof economy runs on a currency, instant recognition, that the spoofs themselves do not mint at full value: they amplify a familiarity the schools, the picture trade and the serious stage first issued. Schoch's law cuts in both directions. If the issuing institutions were to let the scene lapse, if the anthologies dropped the wooing and the stages let the play rest for a generation, the spoofs' capital would depreciate with a speed no other dramatic property would match, because no other property has so much of its wealth held in the derivative market. The balcony is, in that sense, the most leveraged asset in English drama: the largest fame, the thinnest textual stake, the greatest dependence on instruments its owner does not control. The record gives no sign of the lapse occurring. The syllabuses hold, the stages hold, the cartoons renew the lease on schedule, and the pamphlet of 1773, which bet on a two-season wonder, is still drawing interest on the longest-running joke in the Shakespeare business. But the leverage is the correct final measure of the tradition's meaning. Parody did not make the balcony immortal. It made the balcony famous in a way that requires the whole culture, custodians and vandals together, to keep paying for the upkeep, and then arranged for the vandals to enjoy the paying.

The balcony scene is the most parodied passage in the play because it is the play's most exportable asset: a picture that reads at a glance, a call that reads at a hearing, an interruption built into the masonry, and an ending the whole world can hold in reserve while the spoof declines to use it. Four centuries of comedians, from an anonymous French pamphleteer of 1773 to three Americans in tights at the Criterion, have run the same experiment with the same result. Strip the scene to slang and it survives. Strip it to dance and it survives. Strip it to a leap, a ladder, a pun in a title, and it survives. The spoofs are the stress test the serious theatre never had to pass, and their cumulative testimony is that the balcony, the furnished, misquoted, impossible balcony that the text never built, is the most durable piece of stage architecture in English drama.

The tradition's accounts, honestly kept, show one debit and one credit. The debit is the wherefore error, minted and reminted by every spoof that needs Juliet to call down to a visible Romeo: parody there is not a commentator on the culture's mistake but its manufacturer. The credit is everything else. The travesties drilled a mass audience in the text they travestied. The cartoons carried the picture into the one medium that reached everybody. The sketches made the manner itself a shared national joke, which is a form of literacy. And the happy endings, from the garden wine of 1773 to Queen Mab's resurrection at the Strand, constitute the longest-running favourable review the play has ever received, delivered nightly, in slang, by the opposition. The cliché reveres the balcony, as this series' thesis thread has it. Parody does something better than reverence. It keeps the balcony in working order, climbed, fallen from, sung under and laughed at, which is what working order means for a piece of theatre.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between a parody of the balcony scene and a serious performance of it?

A serious performance transmits the text; a parody transmits the picture and the call, and feels licensed to do everything else differently. The serious staging must solve the scene's actual problems, a girl reasoning aloud at a window and a hidden listener deciding when to speak, and it is judged by its fidelity to two hundred lines of negotiation. The spoof is judged by recognition speed. It keeps the raised girl, the climber, the famous sentence and the interruption, inverts the register, the casting or the ending, and discards the bargaining that fills most of the original. The two forms are therefore not rivals on one scale. They are separate distribution systems carrying different freight under the same title.

### Q: What is the earliest Romeo and Juliet parody that can be documented?

The earliest documentable parody is Roméo et Paquette, published in 1773, within a year of Jean-François Ducis's adapted Roméo et Juliette reaching the French stage in 1772. The piece was noticed in the January 1773 Mercure de France, appeared in editions falsely claiming Verona as their place of printing, and is attributed by scholarship to a woman writing as Mlle Carrière-Doisin. Its method was already the tradition's method entire: high matter cut with low register, and the tragedy inverted at the close, the lovers alive and the company drinking wine in a garden. Parody, in this record, did not wait for the play to harden into a classic. It attended the christening.

### Q: Who wrote the best-documented Victorian travesty of the play, and where was it first performed?

Andrew Halliday wrote it: Romeo and Juliet Travestie, or The Cup of Cold Poison, first performed at the Royal Strand Theatre in London on 3 November 1859 and published the same year in Lacy's Acting Edition. The acting edition matters as much as the premiere, since Lacy's cheap scripts carried the piece into provincial and amateur hands across the country, multiplying its performances far beyond the Strand's run. The burlesque historian William Davenport Adams records its casting in A Book of Burlesque (1891), Marie Wilton as Juliet and a Miss Saunders as Romeo, and the surviving text compresses the tragedy into one act and six scenes.

### Q: Which popular songs were folded into the 1859 burlesque of the play?

The standard survey of Victorian burlesque counts twenty-three musical numbers in the 1859 burlesque of the play, and its examples describe the method exactly. From opera came the serenade from Donizetti's Don Pasquale, a ready-made wooing tune with its own comic pedigree. From the popular stock came "Buffalo Gals" and "Nix my Dolly", street and music-hall property that every hearer already knew by heart. The lovers of Verona were thus made to conduct the most famous courtship in drama to a score assembled from the crowd's own singing, none of it composed for them. The borrowed score is the travesty's argument in miniature: the sublime, re-tuned, becomes common property.

### Q: Why did a woman play Romeo in the Strand travesty of 1859?

Because the breeches role was one of burlesque's standing instruments, and the Strand casting followed the convention exactly: William Davenport Adams records a Miss Saunders as Romeo opposite Marie Wilton's Juliet. A woman in the lover's dress announced the evening's terms before a line was spoken. It kept a promise of titillation, since the breeches displayed what skirts concealed, and it delivered a criticism at the same time, since the swooning hero of the legitimate stage was exposed as a costume and a set of gestures that any skilled performer could rent for the night. The device also had an accidental honesty. The playwright's own Juliet had been a boy in a dress; the burlesque merely redistributed the disguises.

### Q: Why do the travesties end with the lovers alive instead of dead?

Because resurrection is the form's verdict on the plot, delivered in the only language a burlesque finale speaks. Halliday's surviving text is the type specimen: Paris dumps Romeo into the crypt as a joke, Romeo bounces out, Queen Mab intervenes in person, the dead rise, Shakespeare himself is restored among them, and the tragedy closes in a group song. The French parody of 1773 had ended the same way, in a garden, with wine. Two centuries of parodists voted, with remarkable consistency, that the couple was the asset and the catastrophe was the imposition. A house does not resurrect characters it despises. The happy ending is the tradition's favourable review, signed nightly.

### Q: Which Bugs Bunny cartoon sends up the balcony scene, and what goes wrong in it?

A Witch's Tangled Hare, a Warner Bros. short of 1959 directed by Abe Levitow from Michael Maltese's story, with Mel Blanc and June Foray as the voices. Mid-chase, Bugs casts himself as Romeo beneath the castle window and Witch Hazel, delighted, begins Juliet's lines in earnest before the two slide into improvisation. What goes wrong is the catch. Juliet leaps from the window into her lover's arms, as the picture tradition and its ballets had taught audiences to expect, and Bugs steps aside and lets her crash. The gag is the tradition's physics audit: the tableau promises that love leaps and is caught, and the cartoon applies gravity and files the difference as comedy.

### Q: What does the "2-B or not 2-B" ending of that cartoon show about cartoon Shakespeare?

It shows that the cartoon economy treated the plays as a single warehouse of famous lines, from which any item could be drawn to pay off any scene. A Witch's Tangled Hare opens at a castle whose mailbox reads "Macbeth", sends its witch through the witches' chant, performs its balcony wooing, and then closes on a Hamlet pun, as the poet figure is unmasked as Sam Crubish, a nobody who stood his date up at the wrong apartment, and Bugs signs off with "2-B or not 2-B, that is the question." The balcony scene is simply the warehouse's best-lit shelf. The cartoon assumes a child audience able to recognise the stock across four plays, which is a large assumption and, in 1959, a safe one.

### Q: What do the cartoon title puns, from Rodeo Romeo to Rome-Old and Juli-Eh, actually parody?

They parody the names' function as a complete announcement of romance, not the play and not the scene. Animation scholarship lists the line: Popeye's Rodeo Romeo (1946), Pépé Le Pew's Scenti-mental Romeo (1951), Rabbit Romeo (1957), The Flintstones' "Dino and Juliet" (1964), applied to a pet dinosaur, and The Smurfs' "Romeo and Smurfette" (1981), with the sitcom's "Rome-Old and Juli-Eh" (2007) inheriting the device. In this tier the title is the whole spoof. Two names, bent by a syllable, promise desire, obstacle and pursuit, and the programme underneath can be about a sailor, a skunk, a dinosaur or a grandfather. The pun is a certificate of how far the names had travelled from the text that made them.

### Q: What happens in the Simpsons episode whose title spoofs the lovers' names?

"Rome-Old and Juli-Eh", first broadcast on 11 March 2007 and written by Daniel Chun, gives the names to Grampa Simpson and Selma Bouvier, whose late-life romance is opposed by the family and conducted under the shadow of exhaustion and decay. Nothing of the play is staged: no window, no quoted line, no feud beyond domestic disapproval. The title does the spoofing alone, importing the obstacle, the disproportion between feeling and circumstance, and the audience's foreknowledge that the story is unlikely to be granted dignity. The episode was watched by 8.79 million people on its first American broadcast, each expected to decode two bent names without a footnote. That expectation, and its safety, is the tradition's measure of itself.

### Q: How does the Reduced Shakespeare Company stage Juliet in its abridged version?

As a grown man in a dress and wig, in the central device of The Complete Works of William Shakespeare (Abridged), the show Adam Long, Daniel Singer and Jess Winfield premiered at the Edinburgh Festival Fringe in 1987 before its nine-year residence at London's Criterion Theatre from 1996. The cross-cast Juliet revives two ancestors at once: the boy heroines of the playwright's own stage and the breeches Romeos of the Victorian burlesque, with the genders of the joke reversed. Production reviews record the balcony business staged with ladders ranged against the set, the climb promoted into the scene's visible essence, and the wooing played at a speed that makes the lovers' haste, for once, a matter of running time rather than rhetoric.

### Q: Why do spoofs mangle the rose line into jokes about noses?

Because the line's syntax is a machine for substitution, and the mangling follows the groove the quotation tradition had already cut. "That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44) invites any noun into its frame, and spoofs accept with the nearest body part: reviewers of the Reduced Shakespeare Company's productions record its Juliet announcing that a nose by any other name would smell as sweet. The gag is conservative vandalism. The sentence's shape is preserved exactly, one word is swapped, and the swap only registers for hearers who carry the original wording with them. A mangled line of this kind is therefore also a memory aid, drilling the true text by the friction of the false one.

### Q: Which parts of the balcony scene do parodies almost never touch?

The bargaining. Spoofs keep the call, the climb, the swoon and the interruption, and discard the scene's long middle, in which Juliet tests the vow she has overheard, warns Romeo against swearing by the inconstant moon (2.2.109), refuses his rhetoric until it becomes a proposal, and fixes the hour and the messenger for the marriage. That middle is negotiation, talk about terms, conditions and evidence, and the spoof grammar has no move for it, because a joke cannot be built from a clause. The consequence is a transmitted Juliet who calls, swoons and leaps, and a lost Juliet who cross-examines, sets conditions and stage-manages the courtship from her window. Parody preserves the wooing's frame and evaporates its intelligence.

### Q: Why do parodies keep the Nurse's interruptions when they cut almost everything else?

Because the interruption is the scene's own comic machinery, inherited rather than invented, and it does the spoof's hardest work for free. In the text, the voice from inside the house hauls Juliet away mid-vow, and returns her, and hauls her again, so the wooing's rhythm is advance and recall, ecstasy under a curfew. A parodist who keeps that beat inherits a ready-made deflation for every swelling declaration, reassigned across the centuries to mothers, wardens, neighbours and the owners of the wall being climbed. The device survives when the verse, the plot and the ending have all been discarded, which identifies it as load-bearing: the spoof cannot generate its rhythm of inflation and puncture without borrowing the play's.

### Q: Why do balcony spoofs stop at the window instead of following the lovers to the tomb?

Because the spoof's contract is with the picture, and the tomb has no picture the culture owns in the same way. The vault offers two bodies, a torch and a monologue over corpses: static, unclimbable, impossible to interrupt, and resistant to the swerve, since a resurrection there requires the full machinery of a Queen Mab finale rather than a closing gag. The window, by contrast, is all affordance, height, call, climb and recall. Full-length travesties that do reach the tomb treat it as the resurrection's stage and nothing more. The balcony spoofs' refusal to travel the last hundred lines is therefore a finding about the play's economy of images: fame pooled at the window and never reached the vault.

### Q: How is a balcony-scene spoof different from a Romeo and Juliet meme?

A spoof is a performance or a printed work that fills time before a paying or gathered house; a meme is a still image and a caption, detached, copied and varied by strangers. The spoof must therefore sustain its joke across minutes, with actors, timing and an ending, and it answers to the grammar this article surveys: elevation, call, answer, interruption, swerve. The meme answers to a different economy, in which a single frame of the tableau does all the work and the caption supplies the whole inversion. The two forms share an ancestor in the picture tradition and a dependence on the audience's prior possession of the scene, but they are separate transmission systems, and the meme's survey belongs to the platform record rather than to the stage and page history kept here.

### Q: What does Linda Hutcheon argue that parody does to the work it imitates?

Hutcheon argues that parody repeats its source under the sign of difference, and that the repetition need not be an attack. Her definition in A Theory of Parody (1985) calls parody "imitation characterized by ironic inversion, not always at the expense of the parodied text", and, in a second formulation, "repetition with critical distance". On her account the parodist is engaged in a dialogue with a valued original, authorised by the very tradition being handled, and the irony marks distance within continuity rather than rupture. The balcony record largely bears her out for the broadcast era: the cartoons and sketches invert the furniture, the casting and the physics, while re-broadcasting the scene's outline to audiences the serious stage could not reach.

### Q: Why does Richard Schoch say burlesque can never displace Shakespeare?

Because the burlesque is unintelligible without its original, and a form that depends on the audience's memory of a text cannot afford that text's disappearance. In Not Shakespeare (2002), his study of the nineteenth-century burlesques, Richard Schoch states the law directly: "burlesque can never displace Shakespeare: because it cannot be understood without Shakespeare." His evidence is the market's own behaviour. As audiences grew less familiar with the plays, their capacity to appreciate burlesques of them thinned in step, so the spoof's fortunes rose and fell with the original's readership. The Strand travesty and the legitimate production were departments of one institution, and the jeering department was the one drilling the customers in the text.

### Q: How does the InsightCrunch parody survey grade each spoof it records?

By the tier of its documentation. The survey's nine rows run from the French print parody of 1773 to the animated feature of 2011, setting down each entry's date and medium, what the work keeps from the scene, and what it inverts, and the grade attached to an entry names the kind of record behind it: a performance entry in a theatrical database, a surviving acting edition, a named review of a named production, a broadcast episode record, or a classification in a scholarly survey. The grading is the survey's discipline. A spoof that cannot be tied to a date, a venue, an outlet or a named maker at that standard is excluded from the table, however often comedy lore repeats it.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch parody survey to argue that spoofs preserve the scene they mock?

By following the survey's two columns of evidence in sequence. The "what it keeps" column shows every exhibit, across print, stage, cartoon and sketch, retaining the same inventory: the raised girl, the climbing boy, the call, the interruption. The "what it inverts" column shows the variation confined to register, casting, physics and ending, the layers a work can change without breaking recognition. An essay can then deploy the survey's dated span, 1773 to 2011, as the argument's spine: a tradition that must keep its object identical in outline for two and a half centuries in order to keep varying it is functioning as a transmission system, and the inversions are the toll it charges for carriage, not a campaign against the cargo.
