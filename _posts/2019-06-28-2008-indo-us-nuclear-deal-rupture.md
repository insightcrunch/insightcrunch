The 2008 Indo-US nuclear deal is usually told as a diplomatic achievement that Indian party politics almost destroyed, yet the sequence of July 2008 reads more exactly in reverse: a domestic coalition bargain reached the limit written into it in May 2004, and foreign policy supplied the occasion on which that limit became visible. The Left Front withdrew its outside support from the United Progressive Alliance on 9 July 2008, the government of Manmohan Singh survived the confidence vote of 22 July 2008 by 275 votes to 256, and the agreement with the United States proceeded through the International Atomic Energy Agency and the Nuclear Suppliers Group to signature on 10 October 2008. Every element of that sequence was contained, in embryo, in the National Common Minimum Programme of May 2004, whose foreign policy section promised principles and procedures while its domestic chapters promised programmes, targets and institutions. The deal fell into the gap between those two kinds of promise.

## The 2008 Indo-US Nuclear Deal in the Standard Telling

The standard telling begins on 18 July 2005, when Prime Minister Manmohan Singh and President George W. Bush issued their joint statement in Washington and committed their two governments to civil nuclear cooperation. India undertook to separate its civil and military nuclear facilities, to place the civil facilities under safeguards administered by the International Atomic Energy Agency, and to maintain its voluntary moratorium on nuclear explosive testing. The United States undertook to adjust its laws and policies, and to work with its partners in the Nuclear Suppliers Group, so that full civil nuclear cooperation with India became possible despite India's position outside the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. The joint statement of 18 July 2005 was a framework of reciprocal intentions, and the three years that followed converted those intentions into instruments: a separation plan announced in March 2006 during President Bush's visit to New Delhi, the Henry J. Hyde United States-India Peaceful Atomic Energy Cooperation Act signed on 18 December 2006, the text of the bilateral agreement for peaceful nuclear cooperation concluded in July 2007, the India-specific safeguards agreement approved by the IAEA Board of Governors on 1 August 2008, and the Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver granted at Vienna on 6 September 2008.

Read as diplomatic history, the sequence has a clean logic of ascending difficulty. Each instrument removed one legal obstacle that the previous instrument had exposed. The Hyde Act of 18 December 2006 amended the effect of the United States Atomic Energy Act of 1954 for the Indian case, because that statute barred nuclear cooperation with a state that had detonated a nuclear device after 1978 and refused full-scope safeguards. The bilateral agreement, named the 123 Agreement after the section of the Atomic Energy Act that governed such agreements, supplied the treaty-level text that the Hyde Act had authorised the President to negotiate. The safeguards agreement with the IAEA supplied the verification machinery that the separation plan required, since a state separating civil from military facilities needs an external inspector for the civil side of the line. The Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver of 6 September 2008 removed the multilateral obstacle, because the Group's guidelines, tightened in 1992, required full-scope safeguards as a condition of nuclear trade with any state outside the treaty. The final American approval legislation, signed by President Bush on 8 October 2008, and the signature of the 123 Agreement by External Affairs Minister Pranab Mukherjee and Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice on 10 October 2008, closed the sequence.

The standard telling then treats the Indian domestic crisis of July 2008 as an interruption inside this diplomatic sequence: a supporting coalition partner objected, withdrew, forced a confidence vote, lost the arithmetic, and departed. In that telling, the crisis has causes that are biographical and ideological. Manmohan Singh appears as a prime minister unusually committed to one agreement, willing to stake his government on it in a manner that surprised commentators who had read him as a cautious administrator. Prakash Karat, general secretary of the Communist Party of India (Marxist) from 2005, appears as an ideologue who misjudged his leverage and led the Left Front into a withdrawal that cost the Left its influence and, in 2009, many of its seats. The Bharatiya Janata Party appears in a supporting role of partisan convenience, voting against an opening to Washington that its own government of 1998 to 2004 had pursued, on the stated ground that the negotiated text compromised India's strategic autonomy. Each of these characterisations contains evidence, and each is attributed in the sections that follow wherever it does argumentative work. The defect of the standard telling lies elsewhere: it explains the crisis by the personalities at its surface and leaves unexplained the crisis's most striking feature, its predictability. Nobody who had watched the arrangement since 2004 was entitled to be surprised in July 2008, and the documentary record shows that the participants were not surprised. The Left had stated its objection in writing, repeatedly, from August 2007 onward. The Congress leadership had counted the votes it would need if the Left departed, and had identified the Samajwadi Party as the replacement bloc, well before the departure. The confidence vote of 22 July 2008 was close, theatrical and consequential, and its outcome had been the working assumption of both camps for at least a year.

### Was the Left's withdrawal in July 2008 a surprise?

The withdrawal surprised nobody inside the arrangement it ended. The Left parties had recorded their objections from August 2007, negotiated through a joint committee for ten months, and named the approach to the IAEA as the step that would end their support. The government took that step knowing the stated consequence.

The inversion this article defends follows from that predictability. A crisis that every participant forecasts, schedules and prepares for is not an accident that befalls a policy; the crisis is the policy's domestic form. The nuclear deal's international instruments were negotiated in Washington, Vienna and New Delhi, and each of them was real, technical and hard to obtain. The deal's meaning for Indian politics, however, was settled by a document negotiated four years earlier in New Delhi alone: the National Common Minimum Programme, adopted on 27 May 2004 as the founding charter of the United Progressive Alliance. That document bound the coalition's domestic conduct in dense detail and bound its foreign policy hardly at all. The deal was negotiated inside the unbound space. The rupture of July 2008 was the sound of a coalition discovering, four years late and in public, exactly where its founding text had declined to bind it. The sections that follow reconstruct the gap from the text of 2004, trace the Left's structural position inside the arrangement, follow the deal's instruments one by one as each pressed harder against the gap, read the confidence vote of 22 July 2008 as arithmetic rather than drama, and then adjudicate the disagreement between the diplomatic reading associated with C. Raja Mohan and Sumit Ganguly and the domestic reading defended here, with Pratap Bhanu Mehta's essays on the post-2008 order as the bridge between them.

## The Common Minimum Programme of May 2004: Eighteen Pages of Domestic Specificity

The National Common Minimum Programme was released on 27 May 2004, in the interval between the election verdict that had humbled the National Democratic Alliance and the swearing in of Manmohan Singh's government. The document performed a precise constitutional function inside the coalition that the verdict had produced. The Indian National Congress had won 145 Lok Sabha seats, short of a majority, and the United Progressive Alliance it led required the outside support of the Left Front's 61 members to govern. Outside support, as the coalition era had already taught every participant, is a contract that must be written down if it is to survive contact with events. [The outside-support coalition model](/2019/06/06/deve-gowda-gujral-interregnums-india/), under which parties sustain a government in the House while refusing office in it, had carried the United Front governments of 1996 to 1998 and had collapsed with them; the CMP was drafted by politicians determined that the 2004 version of the model would rest on text rather than on goodwill. The result, as [the 2004 UPA verdict's CMP architecture](/2019/06/22/2004-upa-shock-verdict-explained/) records, was a programme that read less like a manifesto than like a schedule of works.

The domestic chapters of the CMP bound the government programme by programme. On employment, the document committed the UPA to enact a National Employment Guarantee Act providing a legal guarantee of at least one hundred days of employment every year to at least one member of every rural household, a commitment discharged in the National Rural Employment Guarantee Act of 2005. On agriculture, it promised a doubling of the flow of rural credit within three years, a debt-relief package for farmers in distress, and protection for farmers against import shocks. On education and health, it committed the government to raise public spending to at least six percent of gross domestic product for education and to two or three percent for health, with a cess on all central taxes to finance elementary education. On the public sector, it stated that profit-making public sector enterprises would not be privatised, that the Navaratna companies would retain their public character, and that no disinvestment would proceed in a manner that transferred a strategic asset to private monopoly. On labour, it promised that the government would not support any proposal for hire and fire in the name of flexibility, and that existing labour protections would be enforced. On women, it committed the alliance to legislate reservation of one third of seats in Parliament and the state assemblies. Even the administrative chapters carried figures and mechanisms: a National Advisory Council, a revived communal harmony machinery, a national campaign for water harvesting. A reader of the CMP in May 2004 could test the government against the document at almost any point of domestic policy and reach a verdict of kept or broken.

That density matters to the argument of this article because density is what a coalition contract is for. Specificity converts disagreement into a scheduling problem: each partner can see the concessions it has won, price the concessions it has made, and monitor compliance at low cost. The Left Front's negotiators in May 2004, led on the CPI(M) side by Harkishan Singh Surjeet and Sitaram Yechury, obtained a text that constrained the Congress's economic instincts across privatisation, labour law, foreign direct investment in insurance and banking, and fiscal policy. The Congress obtained a text that authorised it to govern. Both sides understood the document as the price list of the arrangement. What neither side did, and what the next four years exposed, was extend the same pricing discipline to the conduct of foreign policy, an omission that cannot be explained by haste, since the negotiators had time to specify the interest rate regime for rural credit cooperatives. The omission was structural: in May 2004, foreign policy did not present itself to the negotiators as coalition business. The Congress had conducted India's external relations for most of the republic's history and regarded the field as an executive preserve. The Left, whose foreign policy doctrine was among the most developed parts of its programme, accepted general language because general language, in May 2004, appeared to cost nothing: the inherited external posture of non-alignment and opposition to unilateral war, fresh from the Iraq invasion of 2003 on which the Left and the Congress had stood together against the Atal Bihari Vajpayee government's ambivalence, seemed a settled consensus rather than a negotiable position.

### Did the Common Minimum Programme mention the nuclear deal?

The programme contained no commitment on civil nuclear cooperation with the United States, and no sentence from which a reader in May 2004 could have predicted one. Its external chapter spoke of an independent foreign policy, multi-polarity and opposition to unilateral military action. The agreement was built entirely outside the programme's text.

## The Foreign Policy Paragraphs: Principles Without Instruments

The foreign policy section of the CMP rewards close reading precisely because of its brevity and its grammar. Where the domestic chapters use the grammar of commitment, naming statutes, percentages, ceilings and deadlines, the external chapter uses the grammar of disposition. The UPA, the document declared, would pursue an independent foreign policy keeping in mind the multi-polarity of the world. It would oppose all attempts at unilateralism in world affairs. It would work for the democratisation of the United Nations and for the revival of multilateral processes. It would give priority to improving relations with neighbours, to a negotiated settlement of outstanding issues with Pakistan, and to strengthening traditional ties with Russia, China and the countries of West Asia, alongside deeper economic engagement with the United States and the European Union. Commitments of this kind are not empty, and in 2004 they carried a real signal: after the Vajpayee government's strategic courtship of Washington, a UPA government would reassert distance, multilateral form and the vocabulary of non-alignment. The signal, however, was a direction of travel rather than a map of permissible destinations, and directions of travel can be ridden toward destinations the drafters did not picture. A prime minister could describe a strategic partnership with the United States as fully compatible with an independent foreign policy and multi-polarity, and Manmohan Singh did describe it in exactly those terms, in Parliament and outside it, from 2005 onward. The text supplied no test by which the Left could convict him of breach, and the Left's negotiators, who had supplied tests for bank privatisation and labour flexibility, had supplied none for the reorientation of India's external alignments.

The contrast with an earlier Indian tradition is instructive, because [the foreign-as-domestic framework](/2019/03/04/nehru-foreign-policy-domestic-politics/) that governed the Nehruvian decades treated external posture as an extension of a domestic settlement and bound the two together by doctrine: non-alignment abroad mirrored planned autonomy at home, and a critic could test the one against the other. The CMP severed that linkage by leaving doctrine at the level of adjectives. It is equally instructive to contrast the CMP with the document practice of the coalition it displaced. The National Democratic Alliance's National Agenda for Governance of 1998, and the common programme the Bharatiya Janata Party's partners extracted in 1999, contained their own silences, and [the 1998 NDA's strategic-paradox precedent](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/), in which a coalition programme froze the contentious items of the lead party's agenda, showed that Indian coalitions knew how to fence a subject when they intended to fence it: Ayodhya, Article 370 and the uniform civil code were named, and named in order to be suspended. The CMP named nothing in foreign policy in order to suspend nothing. Where the NDA agenda froze by enumeration, the CMP left open by generality, and the difference between a frozen subject and an open one is the difference between a crisis postponed by text and a crisis scheduled by silence.

A further feature of the 2004 text deserves emphasis, because it converts the article's claim from an observation about drafting into a claim about knowledge. The Left's leadership was not composed of innocents in coalition drafting. The CPI(M) had negotiated the terms of its support for the United Front in 1996 and for the Congress-led arrangement in 2004 with its eyes on the Congress's record, and its internal documents of the period, including the political resolutions of the CPI(M)'s eighteenth congress at Delhi in April 2005, treated American strategic designs on India as a standing object of vigilance. The party congress of April 2005, meeting after the first Singh-Bush exchanges and before the joint statement of 18 July 2005, recorded the party's opposition to a strategic alliance with the United States in terms that left no ambiguity. The awareness existed; the textual binding did not. The claim defended here is therefore not that the Left was deceived. The claim is that the Left, like the Congress, treated the foreign policy chapter as a declaration of shared outlook rather than as a contract, and thereby accepted, knowingly in substance if not in consequence, that the first occasion on which outlook and executive action diverged would have to be settled by power rather than by text. July 2008 was that settlement.

## The Left Front's Structural Position: Sixty-One Seats and a Doctrine

The arithmetic of the Fourteenth Lok Sabha defined the Left's leverage and its limits. The four Left parties together held 61 seats after the election of 2004: the CPI(M) 43, the Communist Party of India 10, the Revolutionary Socialist Party 3, the All India Forward Bloc 3, with two further members aligned to the bloc. The Congress, at 145, and its pre-poll allies together commanded a bloc in the region of 218, and the addition of the Left's 61 carried the arrangement past the majority mark of 272 with a margin too thin for comfort and too thick for daily blackmail. The Left chose outside support: it would sustain the government against the Bharatiya Janata Party, participate in a UPA-Left coordination committee, and hold no ministerial office. The choice preserved the Left's freedom to oppose the government on policy while accepting responsibility for the government's survival, and it institutionalised a dual position that the nuclear deal would eventually render untenable: the Left was simultaneously the government's majority-maker and the opposition's most organised ideological pole on questions of external alignment.

The coordination machinery gave the dual position a working form. The UPA-Left coordination committee, chaired by Pranab Mukherjee, met through the government's first years on economic legislation, disinvestment proposals and welfare measures, and the Left's interventions within it were frequently effective: the disinvestment of a portion of Bharat Heavy Electricals Limited was withdrawn in 2005 after Left objection, the privatisation of the Delhi and Mumbai airports proceeded on restructured terms, the pension legislation was stalled, and banking and insurance liberalisation bills were held in the committee's antechamber for the life of the Parliament. The machinery worked because the subjects it handled were inside the CMP's perimeter, where the text supplied both sides with a shared standard. Foreign policy entered the committee's room by a side door, as one agenda item among many, and it behaved differently there from the start. When the Left objected to the government's vote against Iran at the International Atomic Energy Agency in September 2005, a vote cast under American pressure in the judgement of the Left's leadership, the objection had no CMP clause to stand on, and the government absorbed the protest without changing course. The Iran vote of September 2005 was the gap's first operational use: a foreign policy act of the first importance to the Left's doctrine, executed by the executive, protested by the supporting partner, and absorbed without institutional consequence. Every later stage of the nuclear negotiation repeated that pattern at higher stakes.

The Left's doctrine, which the government had every opportunity to know, made the collision a matter of time. The CPI(M)'s programme treated imperialism as a structural category of world politics and the United States as its centre, and the party's political resolutions through the 1990s and 2000s opposed India's strategic convergence with Washington across defence cooperation, missile defence consultations and joint military exercises. The Left's objection to the nuclear agreement, when it matured, was therefore not tactical. Prakash Karat and Sitaram Yechury argued in print, in the party journal People's Democracy and in the national press, that the agreement's cost was not measured in safeguarded reactors but in the subordination of India's foreign policy to American global strategy, of which the Iran vote was for them the demonstration piece. Supporters of the agreement, including C. Raja Mohan in his Indian Express columns of the period, answered that the Left's doctrine mistook a changing world for a permanent one and that the agreement enlarged rather than contracted India's room for manoeuvre. That disagreement, the deepest the coalition contained, was adjudicated by neither text nor committee between 2004 and 2007, because the CMP had not assigned it a forum. [The coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/), under which every coalition bargain in the period from 1989 to 2014 was reopened whenever an unpriced commitment fell due, describes the mechanism exactly: a coalition survives by pricing its disagreements as they arise, and a disagreement that cannot be priced inside the text is priced by exit.

The Left's internal awareness of the position deserves the documentary emphasis the brief assigns it, because the strongest version of the opposing case holds that the Left blundered into the rupture through Karat's rigidity. The record of the Left's internal proceedings, preserved in the CPI(M) Politburo's published statements and the central committee's deliberations of 2006 and 2007, shows a leadership tracking the negotiation stage by stage and debating the moment of rupture as a question of timing. The party's politburo statements of August 2007 warned that the operationalisation of the agreement would compel a reconsideration of support. The central committee in September 2007 authorised continued negotiation with the government while holding the withdrawal sanction in reserve. The Left's coordination with its three partner parties was maintained through the Left Front's own committee, which met repeatedly on the question. A leadership that debates timing for two years has not been ambushed; it has been waiting for the contingency its founding text failed to govern, and its eventual act of 9 July 2008 executed a decision whose substance had been settled long before its date was chosen.

## Three Years of Instruments: The Deal Acquires a Legal Body, 2005 to 2007

Between the joint statement of 18 July 2005 and the conclusion of the 123 Agreement's text in July 2007, the agreement acquired each of the components of a legal body, and each component deepened the domestic difficulty in a distinct way. The separation plan, announced on 2 March 2006 during President Bush's visit to India, committed India to designate fourteen of its twenty-two thermal power reactors as civil and to place them under IAEA safeguards in phases, with the fast breeder programme and the military facilities outside the safeguarded perimeter. The plan was a technical document with a political property: it required India to draw, in writing, the line between the civil and military atoms that Indian policy since 1974 had refused to draw, and the drawing of that line under an agreement with the United States was itself, for the Left, a strategic act independent of the line's placement. The Hyde Act, signed on 18 December 2006, presented the difficulty in statutory form. An American statute, debated in a foreign legislature, set conditions for an agreement to which India was to be party, and its text was available to every Indian parliamentarian for quotation. The 123 Agreement, whose text was finalised in July 2007 after negotiations led on the Indian side by Foreign Secretary Shivshankar Menon and on the American side by Under Secretary of State Nicholas Burns, presented the difficulty in diplomatic form: a negotiated text whose silences and formulations each side read to its own advantage, so that the same document could be defended in New Delhi as preserving every Indian freedom and attacked in the same city as a charter of American supervision.

### When did the Left first place its objections to the deal on record?

The objections entered the written record in August 2007, when the four Left parties submitted their note opposing operationalisation of the agreement. Politburo statements had followed each stage of the negotiation, and the joint committee meetings of 2007 and 2008 accumulated a file of objections the government acknowledged at every stage.

The government's public defence, led by Manmohan Singh's statement in the Lok Sabha of 13 August 2007, rested on the agreement's energy arithmetic and on three assurances: that India's strategic programme stood outside the agreement's reach, that the right to test was unrenounced in law whatever its practical consequences, and that fuel supply assurances and the right to build strategic reserves protected the civil programme against interruption. The Prime Minister's statement of 13 August 2007 is the indispensable primary text of the Indian case, and its emphases confirm the article's inversion from the government's own side. The statement addressed Parliament, not a foreign audience; its argumentative targets were Indian critics; its assurances were drafted for the coalition's internal consumption. The diplomatic negotiation had produced its text in July 2007, and the remaining negotiation, the one that would consume the government for a year, was with its own supporting partner in New Delhi. [The 1991 reform's institutional consummation](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/) had taught the same lesson in economic policy a generation earlier, in the government Manmohan Singh had served as finance minister: a reform announced to the world is completed, or not completed, in the domestic arena where its costs are assigned. The nuclear agreement followed the reform pattern exactly, with the difference that the arena's rules had been written in 2004 and had left the decisive question unpriced.

## What the Left Objected To: The Hyde Act Read in New Delhi

The Left's critique, set out in its note of August 2007 and elaborated in the joint committee through the following year, fastened on the Hyde Act because the Act, unlike the 123 text, could not be glossed. Its provisions required the American President to report to Congress on India's foreign policy conduct, including India's cooperation with international efforts regarding Iran, as a condition attached to the waiver authority under which cooperation proceeded. Its statement of policy commended objectives, among them securing India's support for American efforts to dissuade and restrain Iran's nuclear programme, that the Indian government had not accepted as objectives of its own. It preserved the operation of American law under which cooperation would terminate if India detonated a nuclear device, whatever the 123 Agreement's consultations clauses might promise about the process preceding termination. It barred transfer of enrichment and reprocessing technology outside the agreement's consent arrangements and made India's access to such technology a matter of American legislative grace. The Left's rendering of these provisions, at its strongest, ran as follows: the agreement's Indian text promised permanence, and the agreement's American statute promised conditionality, and a bargain whose two halves contradict each other will be performed according to the half held by the stronger party's legislature. That rendering was contested by the government, whose spokesmen answered that the 123 Agreement was the operative international instrument, that the Hyde Act bound the American executive rather than India, and that fuel supply assurances, the commitment to support an India-specific fuel reserve, and the promise to convene friendly states in the event of disruption answered the termination objection in substance. Sumit Ganguly's essays on the agreement's strategic dimensions supported the government's reading of the bargain's value, arguing that the geopolitical gain of ending India's nuclear isolation outweighed textual risks that diplomacy could manage. The dispute was genuine, technical and irresolvable by quotation, since each side quoted a different half of the bargain's double text.

Two features of the dispute matter for the argument here. The first is that the dispute's irresolvability was structural, not a failure of drafting skill on either side. A bilateral agreement between a state whose legislature conditions cooperation and a state whose executive negotiates it will always present two texts to a domestic critic, and the critic's choice between them is a political act dressed as a legal one. The second feature is the venue the dispute lacked. In a coalition bound by a foreign policy chapter with tests and thresholds, the Hyde Act's provisions would have been measured against the coalition's own text, and the measurement would have produced a verdict the partners had agreed in advance to accept. The CMP supplied no such measure, and so the dispute migrated to the only venue left: the confidence of the Lok Sabha. The Left-UPA committee on the agreement, constituted in September 2007 under Pranab Mukherjee's chairmanship after the Left's August note, was an attempt to build the missing venue after the fact. The committee met nine times between September 2007 and June 2008, received the government's clarifications, recorded the Left's objections, and concluded without a joint finding, an outcome its members on both sides described without surprise. A committee created to negotiate a text that the founding charter should have contained can clarify positions; it cannot manufacture the authority that only the charter could have conferred.

The table's diagnostic value lies in the final column. At no stage did the founding text of the coalition supply either partner with a standard against which the other's conduct could be judged and found wanting. The Congress could not convict the Left of breaching a programme that contained no foreign policy tests; the Left could not convict the Congress of breaching a programme that contained no nuclear clause. Each side therefore argued from doctrine and interest, the two currencies a coalition text exists to retire, and the argument's settlement required the one procedure that needs no text at all, a division of the House.

## July 2008: The Countdown Reaches the Floor of the House

The final sequence ran on rails laid down over the preceding year. In June 2008 the UPA-Left committee ended without a joint finding, and the government indicated that it would proceed to the International Atomic Energy Agency for approval of the safeguards agreement, the step the Left had named as its trigger. Manmohan Singh, travelling to the G8 summit at Toyako in Japan, stated in the first week of July that his government would approach the IAEA very soon, a formulation the Left's leadership read, correctly, as the decision they had forecast. On 8 July 2008 the four Left parties announced the withdrawal of their support, and on 9 July 2008 their leaders delivered the letter of withdrawal to President Pratibha Patil. The government, reduced to a minority, sought a vote of confidence, and the Lok Sabha debated the motion on 21 and 22 July 2008. Nothing in this sequence improvised anything. The withdrawal executed the sanction the Left had described in August 2007; the confidence motion executed the remedy the Congress had prepared since the same month; the debate rehearsed arguments that the joint committee had already recorded across nine meetings.

The replacement arithmetic had been assembled before it was needed. The Samajwadi Party, holder of 39 Lok Sabha seats from Uttar Pradesh, had moved toward the Congress through the first half of 2008 as its relations with the Bahujan Samaj Party government of Mayawati in Lucknow hardened into open warfare. Its general secretary Amar Singh conducted the negotiation with the Congress leadership, and Mulayam Singh Yadav's endorsement carried the party's bloc, with honourable and dishonourable exceptions that the division lists preserve, into the government's lobby. The party's price and motive were discussed with unusual frankness at the time: the Samajwadi Party sought a counterweight to Mayawati's ascendancy in Uttar Pradesh, protection against the central investigative attention its leaders complained of, and a national relevance that opposition to both the BJP and the BSP could not supply. Kalyan Singh's Bharatiya Janata Party dissidents and fragments of smaller parties added their fragments to the government's count, while the Telugu Desam Party and other regional formations divided along lines of state calculation that had nothing to do with uranium. The transfer demonstrated the article's thesis in its purest form: the government's survival was purchased with domestic political currency, in a domestic political market, over a question that the standard telling files under foreign policy.

### What did the government gain arithmetically from the Samajwadi Party's support?

The party's bloc replaced most of the seats the withdrawal had removed, converting a minority government into a defensible one before the debate opened. Its 39 members, minus a handful of rebels, supplied the foundation on which cross-voting and abstentions built the final margin of 275 to 256.

The division of 22 July 2008 returned 275 votes for the government and 256 against, a margin of nineteen in a House where the majority mark stood at 272. The closeness of the figure conceals the stranger arithmetic beneath it, which James Chiriyankandath's study of the vote, presented to the British Association for South Asian Studies in November 2008, reconstructed from the division lists: had every member voted strictly on party instruction, the government would have lost by 271 to 268. The margin of survival was manufactured by cross-voting and abstention: fourteen members of opposition parties voted with the government, eight further opposition members absented themselves or abstained against their parties' instructions, and the government's own losses, six Samajwadi rebels and one Congress dissident, were smaller than its gains. Ten members abstained in total, among them Mamata Banerjee of the Trinamool Congress, whose party had resolved to support neither camp. The debate's most replayed episode belonged to the same arithmetic of inducement alleged and denied: three Bharatiya Janata Party members displayed bundles of currency on the floor of the House, one crore rupees by their account, which they alleged had been offered to purchase their abstention, an allegation the government denied, a parliamentary committee under V. Kishore Chandra Deo later examined without a conclusive public verdict, and which the opposition's campaign carried into the election of 2009 as the cash-for-votes scandal. The allegation is recorded here as an allegation, at the strength its makers gave it, because its historical function does not depend on its forensic resolution: a confidence vote understood by every participant as a market in votes had become, in the public telling, a literal market, and the government's victory carried the stain of the suspicion into its second term.

The session that produced the division had been summoned for the purpose, a special sitting of the Lok Sabha on 21 and 22 July 2008 in advance of the monsoon session's regular business, and the fortnight's newspaper record conveys the settlement's market character in detail no official document preserves: members flown to Delhi from hospital beds in their constituencies, members produced from judicial custody under court permission, party managers maintaining hourly counts in the lobbies, and whips whose authority the division lists showed to have been negotiable in at least twenty cases. The Left's leaders, surveying the same market, drew from it the conclusion their statements of the following days recorded: that a government sustained by such means had confirmed the charge that the agreement's defence required the corruption of the very parliamentary process in whose name the CMP had been written. The government's managers answered, in briefings and in later memoirs, that every governing coalition in the republic's history had counted heads by the means the heads required, and that the Left's purity had been purchased with other parties' flexibility. Both defences are recorded here at the strength their makers gave them, and both, notably, argue about the market and not about the text of 2004, which neither side could quote for its case.

The Left's conduct in the division completes the picture of a rupture executed by the book. The four parties voted against the government they had sustained for four years, in company with the Bharatiya Janata Party whose defeat had been the arrangement's founding purpose, a conjunction the Congress's speakers, led by Rahul Gandhi in a speech that praised the Prime Minister's conviction and ranged across energy poverty, treated as the Left's self-condemnation. Prakash Karat's defence of the conjunction, given in the party's statements of the period, held that opposition to the agreement united parties that agreed on nothing else and that the Left's responsibility ran to the policy rather than to the government's survival. The defence was coherent on its own terms and it marked the arrangement's end more exactly than the vote did: the CMP had been written to keep the BJP from office, and a settlement of the nuclear question that placed the Left and the BJP in the same lobby against the government demonstrated that the unpriced question had consumed the priced ones. Manmohan Singh's reply to the debate, disrupted by the currency episode, was tabled in written form, and his statement outside Parliament after the division, that Parliament had spoken in an unambiguous manner, claimed for the outcome a clarity that the division lists, with their cross-currents and abstentions, only partly supply. The government had survived. The coalition that had won the election of 2004 had not, and the difference between those two propositions is the subject this article exists to separate.

## The Government's Side of the Gap: Executive Freedom as a Congress Inheritance

The silence of the CMP's foreign policy chapter served the Congress as faithfully as it exposed the Left, and any account that treats the gap as an accident must explain why the party that controlled the drafting left its own freedom intact. The explanation lies in the Congress's institutional memory. For the four decades in which the party governed India almost without interruption, foreign policy had been the least parliamentary of the state's activities: conducted from the Prime Minister's Office and the Ministry of External Affairs, debated in the Lok Sabha at intervals, and never subjected to coalition discipline because there had been no coalition. [Indira Gandhi as personality and institution](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) had carried the fusion of executive and external policy to its height, and the Congress of 2004, for all its diminished arithmetic, inherited the reflex that external relations were the executive's estate, to be reported to partners rather than negotiated with them. A CMP chapter that tested foreign policy acts against agreed thresholds would have been, from the Congress negotiators' side of the table in May 2004, an innovation without precedent and without a sponsor. The Left's negotiators, whose doctrine made them the natural sponsors of such tests, accepted declaratory language, and the executive's estate survived the coalition's founding intact.

Manmohan Singh's conduct of the negotiation expressed that inheritance in personal form. His commitment to the agreement was documented and deep: the joint statement bore his signature, his statement of 13 August 2007 staked his government's credibility on the text, and his refusal through the committee year to accept any formulation that reopened the 123 Agreement's terms was the fixed point around which the committee's failure organised itself. Commentators hostile to the Prime Minister read the refusal as subservience to Washington; commentators sympathetic to him, and his own later account in his memoir-era interviews and in Sanjaya Baru's portrait of his prime ministership published in 2014 as The Accidental Prime Minister, read it as the conviction of an economist who had watched India's energy insecurity and technological isolation compound for three decades and had been offered, once, the instrument that ended both. The attributed readings agree on the fact that matters here: the Prime Minister treated the agreement as an executive commitment of the state, in a category above coalition management, and the category itself was the Congress inheritance operating through him. When the committee deadlocked in June 2008, the government's decision to proceed to the IAEA rather than to seek a renegotiated text was announced by the Prime Minister's actions on the G8 journey, not debated to a conclusion inside the coalition's machinery, because in the executive's estate the machinery's role ended at consultation.

Sonia Gandhi's position, as Congress president and chairperson of the UPA, supplied the arrangement's other fixed point. Her public interventions on the agreement were few and were timed to the coalition's crises rather than to the negotiation's stages: her address to the Congress Parliamentary Party and her statements of October 2007, in which she described the opposition to the agreement in terms her critics read as a challenge to the Left and her defenders read as a defence of the government's development programme, marked the moment the Congress leadership accepted that the question would be settled by strength. The dual structure of the UPA, in which the prime ministership and the party presidency were held by different persons, is often analysed as a weakness of the government of 2004 to 2014; in the nuclear crisis it functioned as a division of labour, the Prime Minister holding the agreement and the party president holding the arithmetic. The July 2008 settlement required both holdings at once, and the confidence vote's management, conducted by the party's parliamentary managers with Pranab Mukherjee at their head, was a party operation executed for an executive commitment. Foreign policy supplied the question, and the oldest machinery of Indian domestic politics, the counting and holding of a parliamentary majority, supplied the answer.

## The Opposition's Two Fronts: The BJP Against Its Own Opening

The division of 22 July 2008 placed the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Left Front in the same lobby, and the conjunction deserves its own examination because it disciplines any account that reads the crisis as a simple contest between the deal's supporters and its opponents. The BJP's government of 1998 to 2004 had conducted the opening to Washington that the agreement completed: the Jaswant Singh and Strobe Talbott dialogue of 1998 to 2000, the Next Steps in Strategic Partnership of January 2004, and the defence framework negotiations that preceded the 2005 joint statement all belonged to the Vajpayee government's record, a record the party's spokesmen cited with pride whenever the Congress claimed the agreement as its own achievement. The BJP's opposition in 2007 and 2008 therefore required a ground that distinguished the opening it had made from the agreement it opposed, and the ground its leadership chose was textual: the 123 Agreement, read against the Hyde Act, compromised India's right to test, accepted a fuel regime without binding supply guarantees, and subordinated the strategic programme that the Pokhran tests of May 1998, conducted by a BJP government, had established. L. K. Advani's speeches in the confidence debate and Yashwant Sinha's interventions in the committee year pressed the testing and fuel arguments at their strongest, and the party's position was coherent as far as it went. Its limit was equally plain, and the Congress's speakers in the debate exploited the limit without mercy: a party that had sought the same destination under its own government was voting to bring down a government for arriving there, and the vote's consequence, had it succeeded, would have been the agreement's death at the hands of the party that had drafted its first chapters.

The Left's speakers treated the conjunction with the BJP as an embarrassment to be explained, and their explanation, that the division united all who opposed the agreement for whatever reason and that the Left answered for its own reason alone, was formally complete. The conjunction's significance for this article's argument lies elsewhere. The CMP's founding purpose, stated in its preamble and in every Congress and Left statement of May 2004, was the exclusion of the BJP from power as a communal danger to the republic. The nuclear question, unpriced in the founding text, grew within four years into the one question on which the Left would vote to install the possibility of the BJP's return rather than sustain the government the CMP had created. A priced question could not have done that work: had the founding text bound foreign policy, the Left's objection would have been adjudicated inside the arrangement in 2007, and the arrangement's founding purpose would not have been wagered on a Vienna safeguards text in 2008. The wager, and its acceptance by men who had written the founding purpose themselves, is the measure of how completely an unpriced commitment can consume a coalition's priced ones.

## Adjudicating the Two Readings: Diplomacy First or Coalition First

The disagreement the brief assigns for adjudication can be stated in the strongest available versions of its two sides. The diplomatic reading, whose most influential Indian statement is C. Raja Mohan's Crossing the Rubicon: The Shaping of India's New Foreign Policy, published in 2003 before the agreement existed and all the more powerful for it, holds that India's foreign policy underwent a generational reorientation at the end of the Cold War, from an ideology-ordered posture toward a pragmatic engagement with the distribution of power, and that the nuclear agreement was the reorientation's decisive institutional act: the settlement that ended India's nuclear isolation, regularised its status as a de facto nuclear weapons state outside the Non-Proliferation Treaty, and anchored a strategic partnership with the United States as the organising fact of India's external relations. On this reading, the domestic crisis of 2008 was friction on the surface of a transformation whose drivers were structural and international, and the Left's resistance was the resistance of a doctrine, formed for the Cold War world, to the world that had replaced it. Sumit Ganguly's essays on the agreement's strategic dimensions extend the reading: the agreement's value lay in the geopolitical signal it sent to Beijing, in the technology and fuel access it unlocked, and in the precedent it set for India's treatment as an exception to regimes built without reference to India's case. The diplomatic reading explains the agreement's content better than any rival: no domestic analysis accounts for the separation plan's technicalities, the safeguards text, or the waiver's negotiation in Vienna.

The domestic reading defended in this article does not contest that explanation of content; it contests the diplomatic reading's explanation of the crisis. The crisis of July 2008 was not produced by the Cold War's end, by India's rise, or by the agreement's technical clauses. The crisis was produced by a coalition contract that had priced disinvestment, labour law and rural credit, and had left foreign policy unpriced, operating through partners whose doctrines on the unpriced subject were known, published and opposed. The test of the two readings is predictive. A reader equipped only with the diplomatic reading, standing in May 2004 with the CMP in hand, could have forecast a strategic partnership with the United States as a direction of Indian policy; that reader could not have forecast a confidence vote in July 2008, because the diplomatic reading contains no variable that generates one. A reader equipped with the domestic reading, standing in the same place with the same document, could have forecast both the partnership's possibility, since the text forbade nothing, and the form of its crisis, since the text governed nothing: the first foreign policy act that engaged the Left's doctrine at the level of survival would be settled by exit and division. The events of 2005 to 2008 are the working out of that forecast, down to the detail that the trigger was an approach to an international agency, an act of pure executive foreign policy with no domestic legislative form for the coalition to bargain over. Where a disagreement turns on which reading explains the event the article is about, and the event is a rupture rather than a negotiation, the domestic reading carries the adjudication.

Pratap Bhanu Mehta's Economic and Political Weekly essays on the politics of the UPA's second term supply the adjudication's corroborating witness, because Mehta wrote from neither camp's brief. His analyses of the post-2008 order treated the confidence vote as a hinge in the republic's political morality and in the Congress's governing method: a government that had survived by assembling a replacement majority in a market atmosphere, he argued, had learned a lesson about the substitutability of partners that would govern its conduct after 2009, when the Left's disciplining presence was gone and the Congress, at 206 seats, mistook a larger mandate for a freer hand. The observation connects the rupture to its consequences with a precision neither the diplomatic nor the purely arithmetical account achieves. The CMP gap did not merely schedule a crisis in 2008; it taught the surviving partner that coalition discipline was a commodity, and the second UPA government's troubles, from the Commonwealth Games and 2G spectrum scandals of 2010 and 2011 to the legislative paralysis of its final years, unfolded in a House where the Congress no longer faced a partner whose objections arrived as doctrine and whose exit had been priced by nobody, least of all by itself.

The election of 2009 then marked both readings against the electorate, and the result is worth recording because each camp had invited the test. The diplomatic reading's partisans, in the government's campaign and in the strategic press, presented the completed agreement as an achievement the voters would reward, and the Congress's rise from 145 to 206 seats allowed the claim. The domestic reading makes the narrower and stronger point about the same numbers: the voters were not asked about uranium, safeguards or the Hyde Act, none of which appeared as a discriminating question in the campaign's recorded debates, and the verdict distributed its rewards along the coalition lines the rupture had drawn, enlarging the partner that had survived the settlement and shrinking the partner that had executed it. An electorate ratifying a foreign policy would have had to know the policy's terms; an electorate settling a coalition account needed only to know the partners, and the partners were the one thing the campaign made unmistakable.

### Did the confidence vote change the terms of the nuclear deal itself?

The division changed no comma of the agreement. The safeguards text approved on 1 August 2008, the waiver of 6 September 2008 and the signature of 10 October 2008 all proceeded on drafts settled before the vote. What changed was the coalition that owned the agreement, not its terms.

## The Strongest Counter: The Substance Was Real

The counterargument that this article is required to answer at full strength holds that the inversion, however elegant, underweights the agreement's foreign policy substance, and the counterargument is correct about the substance. The agreement ended a thirty-year regime of nuclear denial that had constrained India's power generation, its access to uranium, and its standing in every technology control regime. It produced, after September 2008, a queue of supplier agreements, with France on 30 September 2008, Russia, Canada, Kazakhstan and others in the following years, and it re-founded the India-United States relationship on a strategic rather than a transactional basis, with consequences for defence trade, intelligence cooperation and the diplomacy of the Indo-Pacific that no domestic reading generates. The energy case that animated the government's public defence was not a pretext: India's uranium scarcity had driven its safeguarded reactors to low capacity factors in 2007 and 2008, the Department of Atomic Energy's projections tied the twelfth plan's targets to imported fuel, and the agreement's defenders inside the atomic energy establishment, including Anil Kakodkar, its chairman through the negotiation, argued the energy case in technical forums with a consistency that a purely coalitional account cannot explain away.

The answer the inverted reading gives is a distinction between the vehicle and the cause. The substance was real, and the substance was the vehicle: it was because the agreement mattered internationally that it engaged the Left's doctrine existentially, and it was because the CMP had priced no foreign policy question that the engagement could only be settled by exit. A trivial agreement would have passed through the gap without trace, as dozens of executive acts did between 2004 and 2008; the Iran vote of September 2005 shows the gap absorbing a lesser charge. The deal's very weight is what converted a textual silence into a governmental crisis, and the conversion mechanism, not the weight, is the article's subject. The complication therefore refines the claim rather than defeating it: the 2008 nuclear deal was a foreign policy achievement of the first rank, and the rupture that carried it through the Indian Parliament was a domestic political event from first to last, scheduled by a founding text, executed by a replacement majority, and paid for by the coalition that had won the election of 2004. Both propositions stand, and the standard telling's error is to let the first proposition's grandeur hide the second proposition's mechanism. Students assembling the documentary record for examination purposes will find the instruments easier to file than the mechanism, a bias that a careful reading plan can correct; journalists who covered the July 2008 fortnight and later reconstructed its back rooms have described comparable problems of sources and access, of the kind addressed by the guidance at [VaultBook on protecting journalistic sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), which belongs in the same file as the division lists and the committee's unpublished minutes.

## After the Rupture: UPA-II Without a Restraint, 2009 to 2014

The election of April and May 2009 returned the verdict that converted the rupture from an episode into a structure. The Congress rose to 206 seats, the United Progressive Alliance to 262, and the Left Front fell to 24 seats, with the CPI(M) reduced to 16, its lowest tally since the party's formation in 1964, a collapse concentrated in West Bengal where the Trinamool Congress harvested the electorate the Left had held for three decades. The Samajwadi Party, the rupture's beneficiary, took 23 seats and found its support unnecessary to the government's survival, a demotion its leadership registered with bitterness as the second UPA government distributed its favours elsewhere. The government formed in May 2009 governed, accordingly, in the condition the CMP gap had prepared: without a doctrinal partner on its flank, sustained by regional parties whose demands were priced in portfolios, projects and forbearance rather than in programme. The restraint the Left had exercised, forcing the withdrawal of the BHEL disinvestment in 2005 and stalling the pension and banking bills across a Parliament, had no successor institution. The National Advisory Council under Sonia Gandhi supplied programme language; no partner supplied discipline.

The consequences ran in both policy directions, as the brief's framework predicts. In foreign policy, the strategic alignment the deal had inaugurated proceeded without a parliamentary counterweight: defence framework agreements, the logistics and communications negotiations with Washington, and India's voting conduct in multilateral forums ceased to be coalition questions at all, confirming that the 2008 settlement had transferred foreign policy permanently to the executive's estate. In domestic policy, the absence of a priced programme told in the government's method: the second UPA term legislated by ordinance and improvisation, met its gravest crises, the Commonwealth Games contracts of 2010, the 2G spectrum allocations examined by the Supreme Court in its judgment of February 2012, the coal block allocations that the Comptroller and Auditor General's report of 2012 placed at the centre of public argument, without a coalition text against which its conduct could be measured and found wanting by its own partners. The anti-corruption mobilisation of 2011, led by Anna Hazare and the India Against Corruption campaign, filled the accountability space that a priced coalition programme might have occupied, and the Congress's defeat in the election of 2014, reduced to 44 seats, completed the arc that the rupture had opened: the partner that had survived July 2008 by purchasing a majority discovered, across six years, the price of governing without a text. The Left, outside, discovered the reciprocal price. Its withdrawal had been executed on doctrine, at the moment its founding text had scheduled, and its reward was irrelevance to the decade that followed, in which the questions it had priced in 2004, welfare, labour and public ownership, were contested by parties that had never signed its programme.

The Left's own trajectory after the rupture supplies the argument's last piece of evidence, because a partner that had been cheated or ambushed might have expected the electorate's sympathy, and no sympathy arrived. In the West Bengal assembly election of May 2011, the Left Front lost the state it had governed continuously for thirty-four years, defeated by the Trinamool Congress in an election where the Singur and Nandigram land conflicts supplied the immediate causes and the Delhi rupture supplied the national frame: a Left that had surrendered its leverage at the centre in 2008 met its state electorate three years later without either office or influence to plead. The CPI(M)'s internal reviews of the period debated the withdrawal's timing and its campaign presentation, and the debate itself confirms this article's reading from inside the party that paid the price: the question the reviewers argued over was never whether the doctrine required exit, but whether the exit had been prepared, priced and explained as the founding text's silence made necessary. A grievance can be litigated; a mechanism can only be acknowledged, and the Left's post-2008 literature, for all its bitterness against the Congress, acknowledges the mechanism every time it explains that the party had stated its terms in advance and kept them.

The claim can therefore be stated in its final form. The 2008 nuclear deal was a domestic-political event that took foreign-policy form; the Left always knew the CMP gap existed; and the confidence vote of 22 July 2008, 275 to 256, was not the near-death of a diplomatic achievement but the scheduled settlement of a coalition account that had been left open since 27 May 2004. The instruments signed at Vienna and Washington endure in India's external relations. The lesson of the rupture endures in a different archive, in the text of every coalition programme drafted in India since, whose authors write their foreign policy chapters, or conspicuously fail to write them, in the shadow of the gap that consumed the arrangement of 2004.

## The Committee Year: September 2007 to June 2008

The ten months of the UPA-Left committee constitute the documentary heart of the article's case, because the committee's record shows two partners negotiating in full knowledge of the ending. The committee was constituted in September 2007, after the Left's note of August 2007 and the Prime Minister's Lok Sabha statement of 13 August 2007 had fixed the public positions, with terms of reference that directed it to examine the implications of the Hyde Act for the 123 Agreement, for India's foreign policy independence, and for the strategic programme. Pranab Mukherjee chaired it, a choice that itself testified to the question's domestic character: the government's negotiator with its partner was not the foreign secretary who had negotiated with Washington but the Congress's senior-most coalition manager, the minister whose portfolio of skills ran to the holding of majorities. The committee's membership joined the government's principals, among them A. K. Antony and P. Chidambaram, to the Left's, among them Prakash Karat, Sitaram Yechury, A. B. Bardhan of the Communist Party of India and Debabrata Biswas of the Forward Bloc. Nine meetings, held between September 2007 and June 2008, worked through the Hyde Act clause by clause, received the government's assurances on fuel supply, testing and the agreement's termination provisions, and recorded the Left's dissatisfaction with each assurance in turn.

The committee's rhythm repeated a pattern at every meeting. The government offered an interpretation: the Hyde Act bound the American President, the 123 Agreement bound the two states, and where the two diverged the international instrument governed. The Left answered with the statute: a future American administration, armed with the Hyde Act's reporting requirements and termination law, would perform the bargain in its American half, and India's remedy would be a consultation clause against a superpower's legislature. Neither side's argument improved with repetition, because neither side's argument was the real subject. The real subject, visible in the committee's scheduling, was time. The government required the committee to sit while the safeguards text was negotiated with the IAEA secretariat, a process the government treated as technical and therefore exempt from the committee's freeze on operationalisation. The Left required the committee to sit while its parties prepared their organisations for a rupture and an election, and while the Congress's soundings of the Samajwadi Party matured into an understanding. The committee was, in the precise sense, a waiting room in which both partners sat out the interval between a decision each had taken and the moment each had chosen to announce it. Its final meeting of June 2008 produced the interval's only honest document, an agreement to record disagreement, after which the government's journey to the IAEA and the Left's journey to the President's secretariat proceeded as scheduled.

Two episodes of the committee year illuminate the mechanism from inside. In October 2007, after Sonia Gandhi's public remarks defending the agreement and the development programme it served, the Left's leaders suspended the committee's meetings and the government's spokesmen briefly conceded, in unattributed briefings that filled the Delhi press for a fortnight, that the agreement might be deferred in the interest of the government's survival. The episode demonstrated the gap's arithmetic in miniature: the agreement could be slowed by pressure, because the CMP had not priced it, and it could be revived by decision, because the CMP had not forbidden it, and the choice between slowing and reviving was a pure function of the government's reading of its majority. In March and April 2008, the Left escalated its public campaign, with Karat's articles in People's Democracy naming the IAEA approach as the act of rupture and the party's state units instructed to prepare the argument for the electorate. The Congress's managers responded in kind, not with argument but with enumeration, as the Samajwadi Party's statements grew warmer through the spring. By June 2008 the committee's failure was a formality both sides required for the record, the documentary proof that negotiation had been exhausted before exit, which each partner's subsequent history of the crisis duly cites. The proof is genuine and the exhaustion was real; what the record shows is that the exhaustion had been reached, in substance, the previous August.

## Vienna as the Trigger's Geography: Safeguards, the Waiver and the Sequence of Consent

The deal's final instruments were executed in Vienna and Washington in the autumn of 2008, and their geography completes the inversion's logic. The safeguards agreement, approved by the IAEA Board of Governors on 1 August 2008, was an India-specific text of a kind the Agency had not previously adopted for a state with an unsafeguarded military programme: it applied safeguards to the facilities India designated as civil under the separation plan, in phases keyed to India's notifications, and it recorded, in its preamble and in the understandings surrounding its adoption, India's position that the agreement's operation presumed the continuity of fuel supply to the safeguarded facilities. The Left's critique of the text, delivered from outside the arrangement it had sustained, noted that an agreement premised on assurances was being administered by an agency whose board had approved it under the shadow of the same American sponsorship the Left had opposed at home. The critique changed nothing in Vienna, where the audience was composed of governments, and therein lies the point: the trigger the Left had named, the approach to the IAEA, was chosen by the calendar of an international negotiation, and the domestic crisis broke on a date set by the Board of Governors' schedule rather than by the Lok Sabha's. A domestic event in foreign policy form could hardly ask for a cleaner emblem.

The Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver of 6 September 2008 carried the emblem further. The Group, whose 45 participating governments acted by consensus, had maintained since 1992 a rule requiring full-scope safeguards as the condition of nuclear supply to any state, and the rule's amendment for a single named state required the consent of governments with no stake in Indian coalition politics: Austria, Ireland and New Zealand, whose non-proliferation convictions produced reservations that American and Indian diplomacy spent the Vienna session of 4 to 6 September 2008 dissolving; China, whose delegation's posture through the session was read in Delhi, in attributed commentary by strategic analysts including Brahma Chellaney, as obstruction delayed rather than consent withheld. The waiver's text, adopted on 6 September 2008, permitted supply to India under the safeguards agreement without conditions on testing or enrichment transfer of the kind the Hyde Act's critics had predicted the Group would impose, a result the government celebrated as the clean exemption and the Left's remaining spokesmen discounted as a reprieve purchased by dependence. The American approval statute followed on 8 October 2008, the President's signature converting the United States-India Nuclear Cooperation Approval and Nonproliferation Enhancement Act into law, and Pranab Mukherjee and Condoleezza Rice signed the 123 Agreement on 10 October 2008. The sequence from the confidence vote to the signature consumed eleven weeks, during which the Indian state performed, in three capitals, the acts its domestic crisis had been fought over. The acts were foreign. The fight had been at home, and it had been finished before the acts began.

## How the Participants Explained Themselves

The literature the crisis generated divides along the line this article has drawn, and the division is itself evidence. The negotiators wrote as diplomats. Shivshankar Menon, who as foreign secretary from 2006 to 2009 conducted the 123 negotiation's final stages, treated the agreement in his memoir Choices: Inside the Making of India's Foreign Policy, published in 2016, as a problem of statecraft: the management of American domestic law, the protection of the strategic programme, the sequencing of Vienna and Washington. Ashley J. Tellis, who advised the American side and wrote the agreement's most cited strategic briefs for the Carnegie Endowment, argued in essays of 2006 and after that the agreement's worth lay in its transformation of India's international position, a judgement that takes the domestic crisis as weather. K. Subrahmanyam, the strategic analyst whose advocacy for the agreement in the national press supplied the government with its most authoritative public voice, framed the question throughout as one of India's arrival in the councils of the powerful. In each of these accounts the Indian Parliament appears as an arena to be managed, and the management's success is registered in the outcome of 22 July 2008 rather than examined as the event itself.

The politicians wrote as coalition actors. Sanjaya Baru's account of the Singh prime ministership, published in 2014, located the agreement at the centre of its subject's authority and read the confidence vote as the moment the Prime Minister asserted a personal ownership of policy against his party's instincts, an interpretation that credits the rupture with a function inside the Congress's own dual structure. The Left's documentary trail, the CPI(M)'s political resolutions, its politburo statements collected across 2007 and 2008, and Karat's retrospective defences in the party press, presents the withdrawal as the execution of a stated position at a stated trigger, and complains, with documentary justice, that a party which had announced its terms for two years was reported as having sprung a surprise. The Samajwadi Party's principals explained their transfer in the vocabulary of Uttar Pradesh: Mayawati's government, the harassment of their cadres, the defence of their electorate's interests against a hostile state administration. No participant's account is innocent, and each is attributed here to its holder rather than adopted. Taken together, however, the accounts perform an unintended service: the diplomats explain the instruments and cannot explain the crisis, the politicians explain the crisis and treat the instruments as given, and the literature's division of labour reproduces, in print, the structure of the event it describes. A reader who wishes to understand July 2008 must read the second shelf. A reader who wishes to understand October 2008 must read the first. The standard telling's error is to shelve the crisis under the signature.

## The Counterfactual Test: A Priced Foreign Policy Chapter

Counterfactual argument earns its place in political history only when the alternative it imagines was available to the actors at the time, and the alternative imagined here passes that test. The drafters of May 2004 knew how to bind a subject: they bound disinvestment, labour flexibility and privatisation in clauses with tests. A foreign policy chapter of the same construction was available to them in at least three forms, any one of which would have changed the crisis's form and probably its outcome. A clause committing the alliance to no strategic or military partnership with any single power would have forced the joint statement of July 2005 into the coalition's machinery at its birth, when the government's dependence on the Left was at its maximum and the agreement's sunk costs at their minimum. A clause requiring the concurrence of the coordination committee for international agreements affecting India's strategic posture would have converted the committee of 2007 from an improvised venue into a chartered one, whose findings both sides had agreed in advance to treat as the arrangement's verdict. A clause suspending judgement, on the NDA's 1998 pattern, naming civil nuclear cooperation with the United States among the subjects on which the partners agreed to differ and defer, would have postponed the question to a Parliament in which the arithmetic might have settled it without a government's survival in the balance. Each form was within the drafting competence the document itself displays. None was adopted, because neither partner, in May 2004, wished to pay the price of binding a field each expected to command: the Congress expected to command it as the executive's estate, and the Left expected to command it through the moral authority of a doctrine it believed the Congress shared. The expectations were the gap, and the gap was a choice.

The test's value is that it disciplines the article's central word, predictable. The rupture was predictable not because history repeats or because coalitions fail, but because a specified mechanism, visible in a specified document, generated it: an unpriced commitment of the first magnitude, falling due inside an arrangement whose partners held opposed doctrines on its subject, in a system that offers exit as the only remedy for an unpriced breach. Remove any element and the crisis changes its nature. A priced commitment produces adjudication; a shared doctrine produces acquiescence; a remedy short of exit produces bargaining. The India of 2004 supplied none of the three correctives, and the India of 2008 collected the result, in a division lobby, by a margin of nineteen votes, on a question of uranium and safeguards that the founding text of the governing coalition had never mentioned. The mechanism outlived the event. Every Indian coalition programme drafted after 2004 has been written by men and women who watched the CMP gap operate, and the care with which the programmes of 2009 and 2014 handled, or avoided, the subjects on which their partners' doctrines diverged is the rupture's quietest and most durable monument.

## The Energy Arithmetic That Forced the Question

The domestic reading defended here requires an account of why the executive pressed the question at all, since a government does not ordinarily wager its survival on a negotiable item. The answer its principals gave, in Parliament and in print, was arithmetic of a physical kind. India's installed nuclear capacity in 2008 stood near four thousand megawatts, a fraction of the targets the Department of Atomic Energy had projected for the decade, and the safeguarded reactors at Tarapur and Rajasthan were operating at reduced capacity factors for want of uranium, whose import the Nuclear Suppliers Group's rules barred and whose domestic production, from the mines of Jharkhand and Andhra Pradesh, could not close the gap. Anil Kakodkar, chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission from 2000 to 2009, stated the Commission's case in technical addresses across the negotiation years: the three-stage programme conceived by Homi Bhabha required time the electricity economy did not have, imported reactors and fuel were the bridge, and the bridge required the waiver. The Prime Minister's statement of 13 August 2007 translated that case into the language of development: energy security as the precondition of the growth rates on which the CMP's welfare commitments themselves depended, an argument that welded the agreement to the domestic programme the Left had priced, and the weld deserves notice. Manmohan Singh's deepest public argument for the deal was that the CMP's promises required the deal's energy, so that fidelity to the programme's domestic chapters compelled freedom in its foreign one. The Left's answer, that a programme cannot be saved by a method its charter omitted and its partner abhorred, was the same argument read from the other end. The energy arithmetic was real, the reactors' fuel shortage was measurable, and neither fact could adjudicate the question between the partners, because the question was never whether India needed uranium. The question was who, inside the arrangement of 2004, had been authorised to decide what India would pay for it in alignment, and the CMP's silence returned the only answer it contained: nobody had been authorised, and so the House decided, 275 to 256.

## The Debate and Its Casualties: 21 and 22 July 2008

The debate that preceded the division deserves record as more than a prelude, because its speeches stated the positions the division then priced, and its casualties extended beyond the government's survival. Rahul Gandhi, speaking for the Congress on 22 July 2008, built his intervention around the figure of Kalavati, a farmer's widow from Vidarbha whom he had visited, and argued that energy poverty, the darkness of villages without power, was the ground on which the agreement stood or fell: a speech that translated the atomic question into the vocabulary of the CMP's own welfare chapters and addressed, over the heads of the House, the electorate of the following spring. L. K. Advani, leading the Bharatiya Janata Party's case, charged the government with mortgaging India's strategic autonomy and with converting a confidence motion into a market, the two charges the opposition's campaign would carry into 2009. The Left's speakers, among them Rupchand Pal and Basudeb Acharia of the CPI(M), restated the Hyde Act case in its committee form, a valedictory performance for an argument that had been rehearsed for a year and was heard, by a House whose division was already counted, as a statement for the record rather than an attempt at persuasion.

The rupture's most concentrated casualty sat in the Speaker's chair. Somnath Chatterjee, a CPI(M) member of four decades' standing who held the Speakership, had refused his party's direction to resign the chair and vote with the opposition, holding that the Speaker's office stood above the division his party demanded he join. The CPI(M) Politburo expelled him from the party on 23 July 2008, the day after the vote he had presided over, an act the party defended as discipline and its critics, across parties, read as the doctrine consuming its most distinguished parliamentarian. Chatterjee completed his term as Speaker, unaffiliated, and the episode entered the record of the rupture as its purest emblem: an arrangement whose founding text could not price a foreign policy question ended by pricing, instead, the men who had tried to stand between the question and its settlement. The confidence the House expressed on 22 July 2008 was in a government; the confidence the season destroyed was in the proposition that the coalition's text, and the offices beneath it, could contain the forces the text had left ungoverned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 2008 Indo-US nuclear deal?

The deal was the set of instruments that opened civil nuclear cooperation between India and the United States and ended India's exclusion from international nuclear commerce. Its components were the joint statement of 18 July 2005 by Manmohan Singh and George W. Bush, India's separation plan of March 2006 dividing civil from military facilities, the Hyde Act of December 2006 in the United States Congress, the bilateral 123 Agreement concluded in 2007 and signed on 10 October 2008, the India-specific safeguards agreement approved by the IAEA on 1 August 2008, and the Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver of 6 September 2008. In return for placing its designated civil facilities under IAEA safeguards, India gained access to nuclear fuel, reactors and technology from supplier states despite standing outside the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, while its military programme remained outside the safeguards perimeter.

### Q: Why did the Left withdraw support over the nuclear deal?

The Left Front withdrew because the agreement collided with the doctrine on which the four Left parties had sustained the UPA since 2004. The CPI(M) and its partners held that the deal subordinated India's foreign policy to American strategy, pointing to the Hyde Act's conditions, its reporting requirements on India's conduct toward Iran, and American termination law in the event of an Indian test. The parties had recorded these objections from August 2007, negotiated through the UPA-Left committee until June 2008, and named the government's approach to the IAEA as the step that would end their support. When the government proceeded toward the IAEA in July 2008, the Left executed the stated sanction: the withdrawal was announced on 8 July and the letter delivered to President Pratibha Patil on 9 July 2008. The Common Minimum Programme of 2004 had bound the coalition's domestic policy in detail and its foreign policy hardly at all, so no agreed text could adjudicate the dispute, and exit was the only remedy the arrangement contained.

### Q: What is the Hyde Act?

The Hyde Act is the Henry J. Hyde United States-India Peaceful Atomic Energy Cooperation Act of 2006, signed by President George W. Bush on 18 December 2006. The Act amended the application of the United States Atomic Energy Act of 1954 to the Indian case, authorising the President to waive provisions that barred nuclear cooperation with a state that had tested a nuclear device and refused full-scope safeguards. It attached conditions to that waiver, including presidential determinations and reports to Congress on India's separation plan, safeguards progress and foreign policy cooperation, with statements of policy that commended American objectives regarding Iran. In India the Act became the centre of the political dispute, because the Left argued that its conditions and its preservation of American termination law contradicted the permanence the Indian government claimed for the bilateral agreement, while the government answered that the Act bound the American executive and not India.

### Q: What was the 22 July 2008 confidence vote?

The confidence vote of 22 July 2008 was the division by which the Lok Sabha decided whether the government of Manmohan Singh, reduced to a minority by the Left Front's withdrawal of support on 9 July 2008, would continue in office. The motion was debated on 21 and 22 July, in a sitting marked by the display of currency notes on the floor by three Bharatiya Janata Party members who alleged an attempt to purchase abstentions. The government won by 275 votes to 256, a margin of nineteen against a majority mark of 272, sustained by the Samajwadi Party's transfer of support and by cross-voting and abstentions that James Chiriyankandath's reconstruction of the division lists showed to have been decisive: on strict party lines the government would have lost 271 to 268. Survival preserved the government and authorised, in political fact, the completion of the nuclear agreement's remaining steps at Vienna and Washington.

### Q: How did Mulayam Singh Yadav save the UPA government?

Mulayam Singh Yadav, leader of the Samajwadi Party, carried his party's bloc of 39 Lok Sabha members into the government's support in July 2008, replacing most of the 61 seats the Left's withdrawal had removed. The transfer was negotiated through the party's general secretary Amar Singh and rested on Uttar Pradesh calculations rather than on endorsement of the agreement: the Samajwadi Party was locked in conflict with Mayawati's Bahujan Samaj Party government in Lucknow and sought a counterweight at the centre, protection for its cadres and leaders, and national relevance. A handful of Samajwadi members rebelled and voted with the opposition, but the bloc's transfer, assembled before the Left's letter reached the President, converted a minority government into a defensible one and supplied the foundation on which cross-voting and abstentions built the final margin of 275 to 256.

### Q: Did the nuclear deal succeed?

The deal succeeded in its stated international objectives and divided judgement in its domestic ones. Internationally, the instruments were all completed: the safeguards agreement approved on 1 August 2008, the Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver granted on 6 September 2008, the American approval statute signed on 8 October 2008 and the 123 Agreement signed on 10 October 2008, after which France, Russia, Canada, Kazakhstan and other suppliers concluded cooperation agreements with India, ending the nuclear isolation that had lasted since 1974. The pace of reactor construction and fuel import that followed fell short of the projections the government had cited, and critics of the agreement, including the Left parties, treated the shortfall as vindication. Strategically, the alignment with the United States deepened across defence and diplomacy. Domestically, the deal's success was purchased with the coalition that had commissioned it, and its political costs, measured in the rupture of 2008 and the governing method of the second UPA term, remain part of any complete verdict.

### Q: What is the 123 Agreement?

The 123 Agreement is the bilateral agreement for peaceful nuclear cooperation between India and the United States, named after Section 123 of the United States Atomic Energy Act of 1954, which governs such agreements. Its text was concluded in July 2007 after negotiations led by Foreign Secretary Shivshankar Menon and Under Secretary of State Nicholas Burns, and it was signed on 10 October 2008 by External Affairs Minister Pranab Mukherjee and Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice, after the American Congress approved it and President Bush signed the approval legislation on 8 October 2008. The agreement provided the framework for trade in nuclear material, equipment and technology, recorded American assurances on fuel supply and support for an Indian strategic fuel reserve, addressed consent rights for reprocessing under agreed arrangements, and set out consultation procedures before either side terminated cooperation. In India its silences, on testing and on the consequences of termination, were as contested as its text.

### Q: What did the Common Minimum Programme say about foreign policy?

The foreign policy section of the National Common Minimum Programme, adopted on 27 May 2004, was brief and procedural beside the document's detailed domestic chapters. It committed the United Progressive Alliance to an independent foreign policy mindful of a multi-polar world, to opposition to unilateralism in world affairs, to the democratisation of the United Nations and the revival of multilateral processes, and to improved relations with neighbours, including a negotiated settlement of issues with Pakistan, alongside stronger ties with Russia, China and West Asia and deeper economic engagement with the United States and Europe. It contained no commitment on civil nuclear cooperation, no test by which a strategic partnership could be judged, and no subject suspended by enumeration. That generality is the document's decisive feature for the events of 2008: the programme priced the coalition's domestic conduct clause by clause and left its foreign policy to the executive's discretion, so the nuclear agreement grew inside an ungoverned space until the space closed in July 2008.

### Q: What was India's separation plan of March 2006?

The separation plan was the commitment, announced on 2 March 2006 during President George W. Bush's visit to India, by which India divided its nuclear facilities into civil and military categories as the foundation of the cooperation framework promised in the joint statement of July 2005. India undertook to designate fourteen of its twenty-two thermal power reactors as civil and to place them under safeguards administered by the International Atomic Energy Agency in a phased programme extending to 2014, while the fast breeder programme, the military facilities and a future right to classify facilities remained outside the safeguarded perimeter. The plan required India to draw in writing a line that its policy since the 1974 test had refused to draw, and the drawing of that line under an American agreement was itself a central Left objection, independent of where individual facilities were placed.

### Q: Which Hyde Act provisions did the Left single out as unacceptable?

The Left's note of August 2007 and its committee interventions concentrated on four features of the Hyde Act. The first was the requirement that the American President report to Congress on India's foreign policy conduct, including cooperation on Iran, which the Left read as an annual certification of Indian behaviour. The second was the Act's statement of policy commending objectives, among them Indian support for efforts to restrain Iran's nuclear programme, that India had not adopted as its own. The third was the preservation of American law terminating cooperation if India detonated a nuclear device, which the Left set against the Indian government's assurances on the freedom to test. The fourth was the restriction on transfers of enrichment and reprocessing technology. The government answered that the Act bound the American executive, that the 123 Agreement was the operative instrument between the two states, and that fuel assurances answered the substance of the objections.

### Q: What was the UPA-Left committee chaired by Pranab Mukherjee?

The committee was the joint body constituted in September 2007, after the Left's note of August 2007 opposed the agreement's operationalisation, to examine the Hyde Act's implications for the 123 Agreement, for India's foreign policy independence and for the strategic programme. Pranab Mukherjee, the Congress's senior coalition manager, chaired it, and its members joined ministers including A. K. Antony and P. Chidambaram to Left leaders including Prakash Karat, Sitaram Yechury, A. B. Bardhan and Debabrata Biswas. It met nine times between September 2007 and June 2008, received the government's clarifications on fuel supply, testing and termination, and recorded the Left's dissatisfaction with each. It ended without a joint finding. In the argument of this article the committee is the improvised substitute for the foreign policy chapter the Common Minimum Programme had omitted in 2004: a venue built after the fact, able to clarify positions and unable to bind them.

### Q: How many Lok Sabha seats did the Left Front hold in 2004?

After the general election of 2004 the four Left parties together held 61 seats in the Lok Sabha: the Communist Party of India (Marxist) 43, the Communist Party of India 10, the Revolutionary Socialist Party 3 and the All India Forward Bloc 3, with two further members aligned with the bloc. The tally made the Left the largest organised support available to the United Progressive Alliance, whose Congress core held 145 seats and whose pre-poll allies brought the alliance to the region of 218. The Left's 61 carried the arrangement past the majority mark of 272. That weight explains both the Left's influence across the government's first four years, exercised through the coordination committee on economic legislation, and the severity of the crisis when the seats were withdrawn on 9 July 2008: no single transfer could replace them, and the Samajwadi Party's 39 supplied only the largest part of the substitute majority.

### Q: What did the IAEA safeguards agreement of August 2008 do?

The safeguards agreement, approved by the Board of Governors of the International Atomic Energy Agency on 1 August 2008, created the verification machinery for the civil side of India's separation plan. It was an India-specific agreement under which the facilities India designated as civil would be offered to the Agency for safeguards in phases, with inspections applied to those facilities and their material, while the military programme remained outside its scope. The text was adopted with understandings recorded around the continuity of fuel supply to safeguarded facilities, a point the Indian government emphasised because a safeguarded reactor starved of fuel would have made the agreement an instrument of the isolation it was meant to end. The agreement's approval was the step the Left had named as its trigger for withdrawal, and the government's decision to seek it in July 2008, before the Board's August meeting, converted the coalition's four-year disagreement into the confidence vote of 22 July 2008.

### Q: What was the Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver of September 2008?

The waiver was the decision of the Nuclear Suppliers Group, taken by consensus at Vienna on 6 September 2008 after a session running from 4 September, to exempt India from the Group's rule, in force since 1992, that required full-scope safeguards as a condition of nuclear trade with any state. The exemption allowed the Group's 45 participating governments to supply nuclear material, equipment and technology to India's safeguarded civil facilities notwithstanding India's position outside the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. Reservations voiced by Austria, Ireland and New Zealand, and the posture adopted by China through the session, were resolved in the final text, which the Indian government described as a clean exemption because it attached no conditions on testing or technology transfer of the kind critics had forecast. The waiver completed the multilateral side of the bargain and cleared the way for the American approval statute of October 2008 and for supplier agreements with France, Russia and other states.

### Q: Why did the Bharatiya Janata Party vote against the agreement?

The Bharatiya Janata Party voted against the government in July 2008 on grounds its leaders stated as textual and strategic. L. K. Advani, Yashwant Sinha and the party's other speakers argued that the 123 Agreement, read with the Hyde Act, compromised India's freedom to conduct a nuclear test, failed to secure binding fuel supply guarantees, and subordinated a strategic programme that the party's own government had advanced with the Pokhran tests of May 1998. The position required the party to oppose the completion of an opening to Washington that its government of 1998 to 2004 had begun through the Jaswant Singh and Strobe Talbott dialogue and the Next Steps in Strategic Partnership of January 2004, a contradiction the Congress exploited throughout the confidence debate. The party's vote placed it in the same division lobby as the Left Front, uniting the agreement's opponents across every other line of Indian politics.

### Q: What were the cash-for-votes allegations of July 2008?

The cash-for-votes allegations arose on the floor of the Lok Sabha during the confidence debate of 22 July 2008, when three Bharatiya Janata Party members, Ashok Argal, Faggan Singh Kulaste and Mahavir Bhagora, displayed bundles of currency notes amounting, by their account, to one crore rupees, which they alleged had been delivered to them as an advance on a larger sum offered for their abstention in the division. The government denied the allegation and the Samajwadi Party's Amar Singh, named in the opposition's account as the channel of the offer, denied it as well. A committee of the Lok Sabha chaired by V. Kishore Chandra Deo examined the episode and reported without a conclusive public verdict on the money's source. The allegation's historical weight does not depend on its forensic resolution: it fixed the confidence vote in public memory as a transaction, and the opposition carried the scandal into the general election of 2009.

### Q: What did Manmohan Singh stake on the nuclear deal?

Manmohan Singh staked the survival of his government and the authority of his prime ministership. His signature stood on the joint statement of 18 July 2005; his statement to the Lok Sabha of 13 August 2007 committed the government to the concluded text and refused the renegotiation the Left demanded; through the committee year he declined every formulation that would have reopened the 123 Agreement, and in July 2008 he chose to proceed to the IAEA and face a confidence vote rather than defer the agreement to preserve his majority. Sanjaya Baru's account of his prime ministership, published in 2014, reads the episode as the assertion of personal ownership over policy within the UPA's dual structure, and the Prime Minister's reported willingness to resign rather than retreat was recorded by contemporaries across the political press. The division of 22 July 2008 vindicated the stake arithmetically, and the agreement's completion in October 2008 vindicated it diplomatically, at the price of the coalition that had made him prime minister.

### Q: How did the Left Front fare in the 2009 general election?

The general election of April and May 2009 reduced the Left Front from 61 Lok Sabha seats to 24. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) fell from 43 seats to 16, the lowest tally in its history since the party's formation in 1964, and the collapse was concentrated in West Bengal, where the Trinamool Congress under Mamata Banerjee captured the electorate the Left had held through three decades of state government, and in Kerala, where the Congress-led front recovered ground. The Congress rose in the same election from 145 seats to 206, and the United Progressive Alliance approached a majority without outside support. The verdict is read in this article as the electorate's settlement of the rupture: the partner that had exited on doctrine in July 2008 paid for the exit at the polls, while the partner that had purchased a replacement majority was rewarded with a larger one, a result that shaped both parties' conduct across the following decade.

### Q: How did the rupture change the second UPA government?

The second UPA government, formed in May 2009 with the Congress at 206 seats and the alliance near a majority, governed without the Left's outside support and therefore without the restraint that support had imposed. No partner after 2009 held a doctrine against which the government's economic legislation had to be tested in a coordination committee; the disinvestment stalls, pension delays and banking bill freezes of the first term had no equivalent, and the supporting parties' demands were priced in portfolios and projects rather than in programme. In foreign policy the strategic alignment with the United States proceeded without a parliamentary counterweight. Pratap Bhanu Mehta's essays on the period argued that the government had learned from July 2008 that partners were substitutable, and that the lesson, applied across a term marked by the Commonwealth Games contracts, the 2G spectrum scandal and the coal allocation controversy, dissolved the discipline that the Common Minimum Programme era, for all its crises, had maintained.

### Q: Why does the predictability of the rupture matter to its interpretation?

Predictability is the test that separates the two readings of the event. If the rupture of July 2008 was an accident of personality, the story belongs to biography: a rigid Left general secretary, a prime minister unusually stubborn on one agreement, a fortnight of theatrical arithmetic. If the rupture was predictable, and the record shows each participant forecasting it in writing from August 2007 onward, the explanation must be structural, located in the founding text of May 2004 that priced the coalition's domestic conduct and left its foreign policy unpriced. The structural reading explains features the biographical reading cannot: the committee year spent rehearsing a known ending, the replacement majority assembled before it was needed, the choice of the IAEA's calendar as the crisis's clock, and the settlement's form as a division of the House. On the evidence of the documents, the rupture was the scheduled consequence of a bargain with a gap in it, and the gap, not the uranium, is the event's cause.
