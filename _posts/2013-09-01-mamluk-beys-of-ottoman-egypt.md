In January 1517 an Ottoman army broke the last Mamluk field force outside Cairo, and a few weeks later the final Mamluk sultan, Tuman Bay, was hanged at the Bab Zuwayla gate in front of the city he had failed to hold. Every textbook treats that execution as an ending. The Mamluk Sultanate had ruled Egypt and Syria for two and a half centuries; a gunpowder empire arrived, the cavalry state lost, and Egypt became an Ottoman province. Two hundred and fifty years later, a European traveler arriving in Cairo would have found the Ottoman governor confined to the Citadel with a ceremonial escort and no army worth the name, while the real decisions about taxation, appointments, the grain supply, the pilgrimage caravan, and war and peace were taken by a council of Mamluk commanders who bought boys in the Caucasus, trained them as cavalry, and fought each other for the right to speak for Egypt. The elite that lost in 1517 was running the country again.

![The Mamluk beys of Ottoman Egypt and how they took back power - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-82.webp)

That is the fact this article exists to explain, and it is the fact most accounts of [Ottoman Egypt](/2013/05/01/ottoman-egypt-complete-guide/) skate over, because it is genuinely awkward. A defeat is supposed to remove a ruling class. This one did not. What it removed was a dynasty and a title. The men who held the sultanate died or fled; the system that produced them, the household that bought and trained slave cavalry and converted military service into landed income, was left standing, because the conquerors found it useful and because dismantling it would have cost more than it was worth. Within a century that system had regrown inside the Ottoman framework. Within two it had hollowed the framework out.

The argument set out here is what can be called the defeated-not-destroyed thesis. The Ottoman conquest of 1517 beat the Mamluk army, killed the Mamluk sultanate, and left the Mamluk method of making soldiers and paying them entirely intact. Because the method survived, the elite regrew, and because it regrew inside an administration designed to be run cheaply from a distance, it captured that administration from the inside rather than overthrowing it. The beys never declared a Mamluk restoration, never repudiated the sultan in Istanbul except in one brief and failed episode, and never needed to. They simply took every function that mattered and left the Ottomans the paperwork.

## What a bey was, and why the word matters

The word bey is Turkish, and in the Ottoman provincial system it named the holder of a sanjak, an administrative district, hence the standard term sanjak bey. In most provinces of the empire that is exactly what it meant: a district governor with a military command, appointed by Istanbul, rotated out after a few years, paid from a revenue assignment. Egypt did not work that way, and the difference is the whole story.

When the Ottoman administration for Egypt was settled in the 1520s, it created a college of roughly twenty four beys rather than a set of territorial districts on the Anatolian model. The number was never rigid, and it fluctuated with deaths, purges, and political bargains, but the conventional figure of twenty four is what the chronicles and the registers use. These men were not sent from Istanbul to govern pieces of Egypt. They sat in the capital, they held functional offices rather than territories, and from the beginning a substantial share of them were Mamluks or the clients of Mamluks. The office was Ottoman. The men filling it were the survivors and successors of the regime the Ottomans had just destroyed.

That is why the phrase Mamluk bey is not a contradiction. It names precisely the fusion that happened: an Ottoman rank filled by a man produced by the Mamluk system of purchase, conversion, military training, and manumission into the household of his master. The rank gave him standing in the imperial order. The household gave him armed men, money, and a faction. Over time the second mattered far more than the first, and a bey with a large household could ignore a pasha who had the higher title and no troops of his own.

### Why did the Ottomans not destroy the Mamluks in 1517?

Because destroying them would have left Egypt without cavalry, without tax collectors, and without anyone who knew how the Nile revenue worked. Selim I executed the sultan and the irreconcilables, then recruited the rest. Mamluk commanders who submitted kept their men, their estates, and their function, and the conquest settlement was built around their cooperation.

The clearest evidence is the man Selim left in charge. Khayr Bey had been the Mamluk governor of Aleppo, and his defection at the battle of Marj Dabiq in 1516 helped destroy the army he was supposed to be fighting for. Selim made him the first Ottoman governor of Egypt, and he held the post until his death in 1522. The conquering sultan's chosen instrument for holding Egypt was a Mamluk amir. Everything that followed flowed from that decision, which was a sound one at the time and a costly one over the long run.

## The conquest settlement and the hole it left

The Ottoman arrangement for Egypt was not improvised. After a serious revolt by the governor Ahmad Pasha in 1524, the grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha came to Egypt in person and oversaw the issue of a provincial law code, the Qanunname of Egypt, in 1525. It is one of the most detailed provincial constitutions the empire produced, and it defined the shape of Egyptian government for the next three centuries. It established the pasha as the sultan's representative, a consultative council known as the divan, a set of garrison regiments, and the college of beys. The full mechanics of that structure belong to the article on [how Egypt was governed under the Ottomans](/2013/07/01/egypt-under-ottoman-rule/); what matters here is the gap the code left open.

The code specified who held which title. It did not specify who would hold the guns, the money, or the loyalty of the men in the street. The pasha was given authority but no independent army; his escort was small and his troops were the same garrison regiments that were recruited locally and paid from Egyptian revenue. He was rotated frequently, often after only two or three years, precisely so he could not build a local base. That rotation is the classic imperial safeguard against a governor turning into a rival, and in Egypt it worked exactly as designed and then backfired. A man who knows he will be gone in thirty months does not invest in reforming the countryside. He invests in extracting what he can and keeping the province quiet enough that Istanbul does not recall him early.

Meanwhile the tax base was farmed out. The Ottoman state in Egypt did not collect the land tax directly through a salaried bureaucracy. It sold the right to collect, an arrangement called iltizam, to men who paid a fixed sum forward and kept whatever surplus they could extract. The holder of such a contract was a multazim. In principle a multazim was a revenue contractor. In practice he became a rural lord with armed retainers, and the men best placed to bid for and defend such contracts were the men with armed retainers already, which is to say the beys.

Put those three features together and the outcome is close to inevitable. A governor with authority and no army; a permanent local military class with no authority and plenty of men; and a revenue system that rewarded whoever could enforce collection. The empire had built a structure that would transfer real power to the beys unless it was actively policed, and policing it would have required stationing an imperial army in Egypt, which is the expense the whole arrangement was designed to avoid.

## The household as the real unit of Egyptian politics

The single most useful correction a reader can carry away from this subject is that the beys were not individuals in the way a list of names suggests. The unit of politics in Ottoman Egypt was the household, called the bayt, and a bey was the head of one.

A household was a compound and an organization. At its center stood a senior commander. Around him were the mamluks he had bought as boys, usually from the Caucasus, converted, trained in horsemanship and arms, and then manumitted into his service as adult soldiers. Alongside them were his freed clients from earlier cohorts, now grown men with their own smaller followings, his womenfolk and their kin networks, his stewards, his secretaries, his financial agents, and the merchants and artisans who depended on his patronage and protection. A large household might contain several hundred fighting men and reach into the guilds, the tax farms, the grain trade, and the religious establishment.

The men within it were bound by a specific kind of tie. A mamluk owed loyalty to the master who had bought and freed him, and shared a horizontal bond with the others bought in the same cohort, called khushdash. These were not blood relations, and that was the point. The system produced solidarity by manufacture rather than by birth, which meant it could be rebuilt from scratch in any generation provided the money and the slave route held.

This is why a defeat that killed the sultanate did not kill the elite. A dynasty dies when its men die. A household system does not, because it does not depend on descent. Every generation the seniors bought a fresh cohort, trained it, freed it, and promoted the ablest into command, and every generation the factions reshuffled as households split, merged, and absorbed each other. What the Ottomans destroyed in 1517 was the top of one pyramid. The machine that built pyramids was still running in the barracks and the slave markets. For the origins of that machine and how it first produced rulers in Egypt, the ground is covered in the article on [who the Mamluks actually were](/2012/08/01/who-were-the-mamluks/).

## From bought boy to commander: the eighteenth-century career

The path from slave market to the council of beys was long, competitive, and surprisingly formal, and tracing it explains why the system kept producing capable and ruthless men.

A boy was bought young, typically in his early teens, and typically from the Caucasus. Georgian and Circassian origins dominate the eighteenth-century record, and the chroniclers routinely note a commander's ethnic origin as a matter of ordinary fact. He entered the household of his purchaser, was converted to Islam, and was given a new Turkish or Persian name, which is why the names in the sources are so uniform and so unhelpful for tracing family. He was taught to ride, to use the lance, the sabre, and the firearm, and to read enough for a commander's needs. The training was serious and prolonged, because a household's survival depended on the quality of its cavalry.

On completing it he was manumitted. This matters more than it sounds. He was not kept as a slave soldier. He became a free man and a client, and the tie that bound him to his master was now voluntary loyalty reinforced by interest, which is a far stronger bond than compulsion. He drew a stipend, was given a horse and arms, and began to serve.

From there he advanced through the household's own grades and then into the Ottoman offices his master could secure for him. A capable client might be made a kashif, a provincial officer responsible for a district's revenue and order, which was both a test and a source of income. Success there brought a recommendation for a beylicate. The formal appointment came through the divan and carried the sultan's authority, but everyone understood that the name had been supplied by whichever household had the weight at that moment to supply it.

A new bey then began building a household of his own, buying his own mamluks, placing his own clients, competing with his own former khushdash. When his master died he might inherit the household outright, or the household might split among several heirs, or it might be absorbed by a rival. That churn is the mechanism behind the bewildering factional history of the period, and it is also the reason the elite never calcified into a hereditary aristocracy. Sons of beys existed, were called awlad al-nas, and were often wealthy and respectable, but they were largely excluded from the top military commands. Power went to the imported and the trained, not to the born.

## Faqariyya and Qasimiyya: the century of factions

By the seventeenth century the households had sorted themselves into two great rival blocs, the Faqariyya and the Qasimiyya, and Egyptian politics for roughly a hundred years is the story of their competition.

The origins of both are obscure and the chronicles supply legendary explanations that historians treat with caution, including a tale of two brothers and a set of color symbols, white for one bloc and red for the other, that reads more like folklore than record. What is solid is the function. Each bloc was a coalition of households, each with clients in the garrison regiments, each with a share of the tax farms, and each with allies among the bedouin tribal confederations of Upper and Lower Egypt, whose cavalry could tip a campaign. A faction was not a party with a program. It was a patronage network that offered protection and a share of revenue in exchange for men when men were needed.

The competition was violent and periodically catastrophic. Street fighting in Cairo, assassinations, purges of a defeated faction's officers, and outright expulsions from the country punctuate the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. In 1711 a prolonged conflict in Cairo, remembered as a great sedition, shut the city down for months. The instability was real, and it is the basis of the standard judgment that this was a period of chaos and misgovernment.

That judgment needs qualifying rather than rejecting. Factional war was destructive, and its costs fell on the countryside and the urban poor who were taxed to fund it. But the factional system also performed a function no one designed: it kept any single household from monopolizing Egypt, which suited Istanbul precisely, and it gave the pasha his only real instrument of control, the ability to tilt toward whichever bloc was weaker. A governor who could not command could still arbitrate, and for a century the arbitration worked well enough to keep the tribute flowing.

What broke that balance was the rise of a household strong enough to end the two-bloc system, and it did not come from where the older histories assumed.

## The Qazdughli ascent and the historiographical correction

Here the standard picture of a straight Mamluk continuity requires a genuine revision, and it is the most important scholarly development in this field.

The older account, inherited from nineteenth-century writers and repeated in general histories, treats the eighteenth-century beys as the direct institutional descendants of the medieval Mamluk sultanate, an unbroken slave-soldier aristocracy that simply carried on under a nominal Ottoman flag. The research of Jane Hathaway on the politics of households, and the parallel work of Daniel Crecelius, Michael Winter, and P. M. Holt on the province's social and political structure, complicates that story considerably.

The dominant household of the later eighteenth century, the Qazdughliyya, did not descend from the medieval Mamluk amirs at all. It grew out of the Ottoman garrison regiments, specifically from the Janissary corps in Egypt, and traced itself to an officer of Anatolian origin. Its early leaders held regimental ranks such as kahya rather than beylicates, and they built power through the regiments' control of urban protection payments, customs, and coffee revenues before they moved into the beylicate and the tax farms. Ibrahim Kahya and Ridwan Kahya, the two men who dominated Egypt in the 1740s and early 1750s, were regimental officers, not sanjak beys, and they ruled through the regiments.

What the Qazdughlis did adopt, wholesale, was the Mamluk method. They bought Caucasian boys, trained them, freed them, and promoted them exactly as the medieval households had done, and by the 1760s the household that ran Egypt was a mamluk household in every operational sense even though its institutional pedigree ran back to an Ottoman regiment rather than to the sultanate of Baybars or Qaytbay.

The honest verdict is therefore narrower and more interesting than the popular version. The continuity from the medieval sultanate to the eighteenth-century beys is a continuity of method, not of institution or lineage. The Ottoman conquest did not preserve the old Mamluk corporation intact; it destroyed the corporation and left the recruitment technique lying around, and Ottoman institutions in Egypt then picked that technique up and reproduced the same kind of elite from new materials. That is a stranger and more damning finding than simple survival, because it means the imperial structures themselves became the incubator.

## The offices that carried real weight

A reader looking at the formal chart of Ottoman Egypt sees a pasha at the top and concludes he governed. A reader looking at which offices the beys fought over sees the actual distribution of power, because men do not kill each other for empty titles.

The most important was shaykh al-balad, which translates roughly as chief of the country. It was not an Ottoman office at all in origin, appears in no imperial law code as a constitutional position, and carried no salary from Istanbul. It was a recognition, granted in practice by the weight of the other households, that one man was the leading figure in Egypt. Whoever held it distributed the beylicates, brokered the tax farms, settled disputes between households, and dealt with the pasha as something close to an equal. The rise of this informal office is the clearest single indicator of where power had migrated, because the province had generated a chief executive the empire had never authorized.

Next in weight came amir al-hajj, commander of the pilgrimage caravan. It sounds ceremonial and was anything but. The caravan to Mecca was an enormous annual operation carrying pilgrims, treasure, grain for the holy cities, and the ceremonial palanquin that symbolized Egyptian stewardship of the route. Commanding it meant controlling a large armed force, a vast budget, and the negotiations with the bedouin tribes along the road, and it conferred immense religious prestige. A bey who delivered the caravan safely acquired legitimacy that no appointment could buy.

Third came defterdar, the treasurer, who supervised the province's accounts and the remittance owed to Istanbul. Control of the books meant control of what Istanbul was told, and a defterdar allied to the leading household could make the annual settlement look correct while the surplus stayed in Egypt.

There was also the qa'immaqam, the acting governor who ran the province between the departure of one pasha and the arrival of the next. The beys learned to engineer those gaps, and a leading household could arrange for a pasha to be deposed by the divan and then supply the caretaker who replaced him. By the middle of the eighteenth century the deposition of a governor by the Egyptian grandees had become a recognized procedure rather than a scandal.

## The slave route that supplied the households

A system that refused to recruit from the local population had to import its soldiers continuously, and the logistics of that import are one of the least discussed and most important features of the period.

The supply came overwhelmingly from the Caucasus and the lands north of the Black Sea, with Georgians and Circassians dominating the eighteenth-century intake. Children were acquired through a mixture of raiding, tribute arrangements among Caucasian lords, and sale by impoverished families, moved to Black Sea ports, and shipped through Ottoman waters to Anatolia and then onward to Egypt. The trade was legal, long established, and conducted by specialist merchants who dealt in this particular commodity and understood what a household wanted: boys of the right age, sound, and from the recognized origins.

Price mattered enormously, because a trained cavalryman represented years of investment on top of the purchase price, and a household's fighting strength was therefore a direct function of its cash flow. This is the link that makes the tax farms and the slave market two halves of one machine. Revenue bought boys, boys became cavalry after a decade, cavalry defended and extended the revenue. A household that lost its tax farms could not replace its losses and would be absorbed within a generation.

The route's vulnerability is worth noting because it eventually mattered. A supply line running from the Caucasus through the Black Sea depended on Ottoman control of those waters and on conditions in the Caucasus itself, and Russian expansion in the region over the eighteenth century steadily complicated both. The elite's very method of reproduction was hostage to a frontier a thousand miles from Egypt over which it had no influence whatever, which is a vulnerability no hereditary aristocracy would have carried.

## The rhythm of a bey's year

Abstractions about households and tax farms become concrete when set against the calendar, and the annual cycle of Egyptian government under the beys followed the river, the harvest, and the pilgrimage with very little reference to Istanbul.

The year's foundation was the flood. From roughly August the Nile rose, the basins filled, and the level reached at the Cairo gauge determined what the country would produce and therefore what could be collected. The measurement was a public event with religious and fiscal significance at once, and the canal-cutting ceremony that opened the flow into Cairo's waterway was presided over by the leading grandees, who attached themselves to the one annual ritual that mattered to everyone in Egypt.

Collection followed the harvest through the winter and spring. The multazim's agents assessed and extracted, disputes went to the religious courts or to the household's own rough arbitration, and the accounting worked its way upward toward the treasury and the annual settlement with the imperial remittance. This was also the season when shortfalls became visible and when the irregular levies were imposed to cover them.

The pilgrimage caravan dominated the calendar's other half. Assembling it meant provisioning thousands of people and animals, arranging the treasure and grain destined for the holy cities, negotiating passage and protection with the tribes along the desert road, and mustering the escort. The commander departed with an enormous entourage and returned months later, and his absence was a standing invitation to his rivals in Cairo, which is why the office was both a prize and a risk.

Campaigning season in Upper Egypt filled the remaining months in most years. A household went south to collect arrears, to punish a tribal confederation that had raided the roads, to install a compliant local authority, or to hunt a defeated rival who had gone there to rebuild. These expeditions were the ordinary business of government rather than emergencies, and they consumed a substantial share of the cavalry's time.

Set out in sequence, the year describes a government that was doing real administrative work with real competence in the things it cared about, while doing nothing whatever about the things a state is supposed to do: no reserve against famine, no investment in the irrigation network beyond the immediate, no standing infrastructure of justice or defense outside the households themselves.

## The commanders worth knowing by name

A collective profile still needs its individuals, and a handful of figures carry the arc of the period well enough that fixing them in sequence makes the rest legible.

Ridwan Bey al-Faqari dominated the middle of the seventeenth century as commander of the pilgrimage caravan for an extraordinary run of years, and he is the clearest early example of how the caravan command could be converted into national standing. He is also notable for the genealogy commissioned in his circle that attempted to trace Circassian mamluk descent back to a prestigious ancient lineage, a piece of self-legitimation that tells us the elite was conscious of its foreignness and working on the problem.

Ibrahim Kahya al-Qazdughli and Ridwan Kahya al-Julfi ran Egypt jointly in the 1740s and early 1750s, and their partnership is the template Murad and Ibrahim would later reproduce. Both were regimental officers rather than sanjak beys, which is the detail that anchors the argument about where the eighteenth-century elite actually came from. Ibrahim Kahya's household became the nursery of everything that followed: Ali Bey al-Kabir was his mamluk, and so were several of the men who fought Ali Bey for the succession.

Ali Bey al-Kabir is the pivot, treated at length above. Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab, his mamluk, his son-in-law, his best commander, and his destroyer, ruled Egypt from 1773 until his death on campaign in 1775, and his brief ascendancy is a useful corrective to any reading of the period as simple factional chaos: he restored the remittance, secured imperial recognition, and governed with competence, which suggests the system could produce administrators as well as warlords when the incentives pointed that way.

Murad Bey and Ibrahim Bey, both Abu al-Dhahab's mamluks, held the country from the later 1770s until 1798 with the interruption of Hasan Pasha's expedition. Murad was the soldier, impulsive and interested in weapons and ships; Ibrahim was the negotiator who handled the divan, the merchants, and the sultan's representatives. Between them they represent the two halves of what the household system could do, and neither half was sufficient when the French arrived.

Around and beneath these names stood dozens of others whom the chronicles record with the same careful attention to whose mamluk a man had been, which household he had married into, and which faction he joined when his master died. That relentless attention to clientage in the sources is itself the evidence for the argument of this article: contemporaries did not describe their politics in terms of offices and districts, they described it in terms of who belonged to whom, because that was the structure that actually determined outcomes.

## Iltizam: the money that made the households

Armed men need paying, and the answer to where the beys got their money is the tax farm. Understanding iltizam explains both how the elite grew and why the countryside suffered.

The Ottoman state assessed Egyptian agricultural land and then auctioned the right to collect its taxes. A bidder paid an advance to the treasury plus an annual sum, and in return held the collection rights over a village or a cluster of villages. Anything he could extract above his obligation was his. The contract also came with a portion of land held directly by the contractor and worked as his own, which gave him a resident economic base as well as a collecting right.

Two features turned this from a fiscal device into a transfer of sovereignty. The first was duration. Contracts that began as short-term leases drifted toward life tenure and then toward de facto heritability, with the state confirming transfers in exchange for fees. The second was enforcement. The treasury had no field force to make a village pay, so the contractor supplied his own, which meant the men best able to hold tax farms were the men with cavalry, and the men with cavalry used tax-farm income to buy more cavalry. That loop is the engine of the whole period.

The consequences for the fiscal relationship with Istanbul were precisely what one would expect. The annual remittance to the imperial treasury, the irsaliyye, was a fixed obligation rather than a share of actual yield, so as the beys extended their grip on the tax farms they were not competing with Istanbul for a percentage; they were pocketing the growth. Istanbul received a payment that looked stable in the ledgers while the share of Egyptian revenue reaching it shrank in real terms. A province can be drained and remitting on schedule at the same time, and Egypt was.

The revenue picture also interacted with the province's commercial life, above all the coffee trade through the Red Sea, whose rise and later erosion is traced in the article on [trade and decline in Ottoman Egypt](/2013/08/01/trade-decline-ottoman-egypt/). The households taxed that commerce through customs farms and protection arrangements as well as through the land, and the regiments that controlled the customs posts were among the first to accumulate the capital that later bought mamluks.

### How did tax farming make the beys rich?

Iltizam let a contractor keep everything he collected above a fixed payment to the treasury, and enforcement was his own responsibility. Households with cavalry could therefore bid for the best farms, extract hard, and reinvest the surplus in more cavalry, which won them more farms. Wealth and armed force compounded together.

## The pasha reduced to a figurehead

By the second half of the eighteenth century the Ottoman governor of Egypt was a resident dignitary rather than a ruler, and the mechanics of that reduction are worth setting out precisely, because they were institutional rather than dramatic.

He had no army. The regiments in Egypt were recruited and paid locally and had long since been penetrated by the households, so their officers were clients of the beys rather than servants of the governor. His personal escort was a few hundred men at most, adequate for ceremony and useless for coercion.

He had no independent revenue. His allowances came out of Egyptian collections administered by Egyptians, which meant the men he theoretically supervised controlled his cash flow.

He had no time. Frequent rotation meant he arrived knowing nobody, spent a year learning who mattered, and was recalled before he could build anything, while the men across the table had been in Cairo for thirty years and expected to die there.

And he had a council that could depose him. The divan, which the Qanunname created as a consultative body, became the instrument through which the grandees declared a governor unacceptable, confined him to the Citadel, and installed a caretaker while Istanbul was informed. Because the imperial center preferred a quiet Egypt that paid to a contested Egypt that did not, the deposition was usually ratified after the fact.

What he retained was not nothing. He held the sultan's warrant, and appointments made without his seal were irregular. He remained the channel through which Istanbul's demands arrived and through which Egyptian requests were forwarded. He could recognize one faction over another and thereby add legitimacy to a claimant who already had the men. In a province where every household needed its beylicates confirmed, the man who signed the confirmations was not irrelevant. He simply could not act against the consensus of those who had the cavalry.

### What did an Ottoman governor of Egypt actually control?

His seal, his channel to Istanbul, and his power to recognize one faction rather than another. He could legitimize an outcome the households had already decided, and he could complain to the capital. He could not collect a tax, remove a bey, or field a force against one.

## The third force: the Egyptian regiments

A picture with only pashas and beys in it is incomplete, and the missing element is the garrison establishment, the seven corps known as the ocaks, of which the Janissaries and the Azaban were the most significant.

These were meant to be an imperial garrison, a standing force loyal to the sultan and distinct from the local elite. Within a century they had become something else entirely. Recruitment shifted to local men, including Egyptians and Syrians, and service became hereditary and part-time; a Janissary might be a shopkeeper or a merchant who drew a stipend and turned out when his corps did. The corps took over lucrative urban functions, above all protection payments from merchants and artisans and a share of the customs, and their senior officers became wealthy men in their own right.

The regiments were therefore a genuine third power for much of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, and the rise of the Qazdughliyya out of the Janissary corps is the proof of how much weight they carried. But their trajectory was ultimately absorption rather than rivalry. As the great households grew, they placed their own clients in regimental commands until the corps became instruments of household politics rather than counterweights to it. By the time of Ali Bey al-Kabir the regiments were largely an auxiliary of whichever household ran Cairo, valuable for their urban revenues and their numbers and negligible as an independent political actor.

The table below sets out how the balance moved across the three centuries, which is the clearest way to see that this was a slow institutional drift rather than a single coup.

## Ali Bey al-Kabir: the rise

The single most famous Mamluk bey of the Ottoman centuries was a Georgian, bought as a boy, who by his fifties had expelled the sultan's governor, stopped the tribute, conquered the Hijaz, taken Damascus, and opened negotiations with a European fleet. His career is the test case for how far bey power could actually go, and its ending is the test case for where the limits lay.

Ali Bey was of Georgian origin and entered Egypt as a purchased mamluk of Ibrahim Kahya al-Qazdughli, the regimental officer who dominated the province in the 1740s. He rose through the household during the confused and violent competition that followed Ibrahim Kahya's death in 1754, and the details of that period are murky enough that the chroniclers disagree about the sequence. What is clear is that he emerged by 1760 as shaykh al-balad, was driven out, returned, and by 1768 had destroyed or subordinated every rival household in Egypt.

The consolidation was brutal and methodical. He eliminated rival beys, exiled others, and filled the vacated beylicates with his own mamluks and clients, which meant that for the first time in generations the college of beys was effectively one household's roster rather than a balance of competing blocs. The two-bloc system that had structured Egyptian politics since the seventeenth century was simply gone, replaced by a single pyramid with Ali Bey at the top.

The epithet al-Kabir, the great or the elder, is how the chronicles distinguish him, and it reflects the scale of what he attempted rather than a consensus that he succeeded.

## The bid for independence

In 1768 Ali Bey took the decisive step of expelling the Ottoman governor from Egypt and ceasing to forward the annual remittance to Istanbul. The empire was in no position to respond. The Russo-Turkish war that broke out in 1768 consumed the imperial army and, after the destruction of the Ottoman fleet at Cesme in 1770, the imperial navy as well. Egypt's overlord was fighting for survival in the Black Sea and the Danube, and had nothing to send.

Ali Bey used the window aggressively. He asserted authority over the Red Sea ports and the customs, and in 1770 he sent an expedition under his leading mamluk, Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab, into the Hijaz, where it installed a favorable sharif at Mecca. Control of the holy cities' supply and their patronage was the most prestigious asset in the Islamic world, and claiming it was a statement about who now spoke for Sunni Egypt.

In 1771 he went further, sending Abu al-Dhahab north into Syria in alliance with Zahir al-Umar, the effectively autonomous ruler of Acre and Galilee. The campaign was a military success. Damascus fell, and for a moment an Egyptian-based power held the Syrian capital, which had not happened since the Mamluk sultanate. Ali Bey also entered into communication with the Russian fleet operating in the eastern Mediterranean, which was the closest any Egyptian ruler had come to treating with a European great power as a sovereign since 1517.

The question of how far he formally declared independence is genuinely debated and should not be flattened. Reports that he had coins struck in his own name and had the Friday sermon pronounced in his name circulate in the sources and in later accounts, and these are the classic markers of sovereignty in Islamic practice. Historians who have examined the numismatic and documentary evidence treat the claim with caution and note that the evidence is thinner and more ambiguous than the confident assertions in popular retellings. What is not in doubt is the substance: he expelled the governor, withheld the tribute, made war and alliances on his own account, and behaved as a sovereign whatever he called himself.

### Did Ali Bey al-Kabir make Egypt independent?

For roughly five years he made it independent in practice, expelling the governor, halting the tribute, and conquering the Hijaz and Damascus. Whether he declared sovereignty formally is debated, and the experiment collapsed in 1773 when his own commander turned against him, so the independence never outlived the man.

## The betrayal and the fall

The end came from inside the household, which is the most revealing fact about the whole episode.

In 1771, with Damascus taken, Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab abruptly withdrew his army from Syria and returned to Egypt. The sources offer several explanations and they are not mutually exclusive: overtures from Istanbul, which had every reason to detach him; religious unease at making war on the sultan and caliph in alliance with a Christian power and a rebel in Acre; and the ordinary ambition of a second man who could see that his master's position was dangerous and his own opportunity had arrived.

Abu al-Dhahab was not a stranger to Ali Bey. He was his own mamluk, bought, trained, freed, and promoted by him, married into his household, and given the most important command in his service. The bond that the whole system rested on, the loyalty of the freed client to the master who made him, failed at the top of the largest household in Egypt.

The sequence that followed was short. Abu al-Dhahab gathered support in Upper Egypt, marched on Cairo, and forced Ali Bey to flee in 1772. Ali Bey took refuge with Zahir al-Umar at Acre, assembled a force with Russian naval support, and returned in 1773 to retake Egypt. He was defeated at Salihiyya on the eastern approaches, wounded, and captured, and he died within days in May 1773. Abu al-Dhahab took control of Egypt, resumed the remittance to Istanbul, received the sultan's recognition, and was confirmed in authority. He died in 1775 during a campaign in Palestine.

The most economical reading is that Istanbul did not defeat Ali Bey. The household system defeated him, by producing a successor with the same training, the same followers, and the same appetite, at a moment when the sultan could offer that successor legitimacy at no cost.

## What the Ali Bey episode actually proves

The temptation is to treat Ali Bey as a forerunner of modern Egyptian independence, a nationalist before nationalism. That reading does not survive scrutiny and should be set aside without condescension toward the people who hold it.

He commanded a Georgian-led military caste that ruled a population it did not come from, did not intermarry with at the top, and taxed hard. His project was the aggrandizement of a household, pursued by the classic methods of a Mamluk amir: eliminate rivals, seize the revenue, take the holy cities, take Syria. That is the program of the fifteenth-century sultanate, attempted with eighteenth-century resources. Nothing in it appeals to Egyptians as a people or imagines a state resting on them.

What the episode does prove is the thesis of this article, more sharply than any other evidence available. When the empire was distracted, the residual sovereignty in Egypt lay with the beys and nowhere else. There was no Ottoman administrative machine that could function without them, no imperial garrison that could hold the province against them, and no Egyptian institution that filled the gap when the governor was thrown out. The pasha left and nothing broke, because for practical purposes he had not been governing for decades.

It also proves the limit. Ali Bey could take Egypt and could not keep it, because the very mechanism that produced men capable of seizing power produced them faster than any one man could absorb them. A system that manufactures ambitious cavalry commanders every generation is a system that cannot stabilize into a dynasty. The beys could always rule Egypt. They could never institutionalize the rule, which is why their two and a half centuries of dominance left behind no state, no succession law, and no administrative apparatus that survived them.

## The duumvirate of Murad Bey and Ibrahim Bey

The last phase of bey rule was a partnership, and it is the arrangement a French army would meet in 1798.

After Abu al-Dhahab's death in 1775 his household fractured and then reconstituted around two of his mamluks, Murad Bey and Ibrahim Bey. Both were Caucasian in origin, both were products of the same training, and instead of destroying each other they arrived at a division of labor that held, with interruptions, for over two decades. Ibrahim Bey took the office of shaykh al-balad and the civil and fiscal side, dealing with the divan, the merchants, the religious establishment, and the remittance negotiations with Istanbul. Murad Bey took the amir al-hajj role and the military side, commanding the cavalry, running the campaigns in Upper Egypt, and handling the bedouin.

The partnership was not harmonious and it broke down more than once into open fighting, with one or the other driven into Upper Egypt for a season before a settlement restored the arrangement. What kept bringing it back was arithmetic. Neither household could finish the other, and a war between them opened the door to third parties and to imperial intervention. Two men who cannot win share.

The regime they ran was extractive and increasingly desperate for cash. They pressed the tax farms hard, levied forced contributions on merchants both Egyptian and European, seized the property of the wealthy on various pretexts, and debased what they could. The chronicler al-Jabarti, who lived through it and had no sympathy for the French who followed, is scathing about the arbitrary exactions of the final bey regime, and his account carries particular weight because he was a member of the Cairo religious establishment writing about rulers he knew personally.

## Hasan Pasha's expedition and what it revealed

In 1786 Istanbul made its one serious attempt to break the beys by force, and the outcome is a precise measurement of how far the province had drifted.

The empire dispatched the admiral Hasan Pasha, one of the ablest Ottoman commanders of the century, with a fleet and an army. He landed, occupied Cairo, drove Murad and Ibrahim into Upper Egypt, installed compliant beys, appointed a new governor, and began reordering the tax farms. On the imperial ledger the operation succeeded.

It then unwound. Hasan Pasha was recalled when war with Russia resumed, the expeditionary force went with him, and the beys walked back down the Nile and resumed their positions. The compliant beys were removed, the tax farms returned to their previous holders, and within a few years the arrangement looked exactly as it had before the expedition arrived.

The lesson is precise and it is the one the empire could not act on. Ottoman power could reconquer Egypt whenever it was willing to send an army and keep it there. It could not govern Egypt without one, because there was no imperial administrative layer beneath the governor that could function on its own. Remove the expeditionary force and the only people capable of collecting a tax or fielding a horseman were the same households you had just expelled. The empire faced a permanent choice between occupation and accommodation, and occupation cost more than Egypt was worth while the Russian front was open.

## What bey rule cost the Egyptian countryside

An article that explains how an elite regained power owes the reader a clear account of what that power did to the people underneath it, and here the evidence supports a harsh verdict without requiring exaggeration.

The tax farm was the point of contact between the household and the village, and its incentives were destructive. A contractor whose surplus was whatever he could extract above a fixed payment had every reason to collect to the limit of endurance and little reason to invest. The one investment that mattered in Egypt was irrigation, and irrigation is the classic case of a good that benefits a whole basin while costing one contractor, which means a fragmented system of private collectors underprovides it as a matter of structure rather than of malice. Canals silted, dikes went unrepaired in places, and the basin system that depended on coordinated maintenance degraded where no authority could compel the coordination.

On top of the regular assessment came irregular exactions, the levies raised when a household needed cash for a campaign or a bribe or a purchase of mamluks. These were unpredictable, which is worse for a cultivator than a high but known burden, because it destroys the calculation on which planting and saving depend. Peasants responded as peasants everywhere do under arbitrary extraction: they concealed production, fled villages, and in periods of severe pressure abandoned land altogether, which shrank the tax base and drove the next round of exactions higher.

Famine and epidemic compounded it. Egypt in these centuries suffered recurrent plague and repeated low Nile floods, and a government that held no reserves and whose elite competed for revenue rather than managing it had no capacity to buffer a bad year. Grain was hoarded and speculated on by the powerful during shortages, a pattern al-Jabarti records with disgust.

Two qualifications keep this honest. The first is that the decline was uneven; commercial agriculture and the coffee and textile trades generated real wealth in this period, and Cairo remained one of the largest and richest cities in the world, so a picture of uniform misery is wrong. The second is that measuring the damage is genuinely hard, because the records that survive are the records the elite kept about what it collected, not surveys of what cultivators retained, and the historian is reading extraction data and inferring welfare from it.

## Cairo under the households

The same regime that pressed the villages hard also made Cairo a city of patronage, and holding both facts together is necessary to understand why the period looks so different depending on where the historian stands.

A great household needed a visible seat, and the eighteenth century filled Cairo with the palaces, courtyard houses, fountains, schools, and mosque complexes that the beys and their womenfolk endowed. The architectural record of the period is substantial, and the surviving domestic architecture in particular, with its reception halls, screened galleries, and interior courtyards, is the physical trace of household politics: buildings designed to receive clients, house retainers, and display the standing of the man at the center. This is a different building program from the great Mamluk funerary complexes of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries that dominate the medieval skyline, smaller in ambition and more domestic in character, and the earlier tradition it descends from is treated in the article on [Mamluk architecture and Cairo's golden age](/2013/01/01/mamluk-architecture-of-cairo/).

Patronage extended well beyond stone. Households endowed religious foundations, the waqf, which carried the double advantage of genuine piety and of sheltering property from confiscation, since endowed assets were harder for a rival or a governor to seize. They supported scholars at al-Azhar, funded charitable distributions, and maintained the water supply infrastructure that Cairo's neighborhoods depended on. The religious establishment in turn became a political actor of real weight, because the ulama were the one group with standing among the population that the beys lacked, and a household that quarrelled with the shaykhs of al-Azhar could find the city's crowds turned against it.

The commercial economy ran alongside all this. Cairo's merchant community, including the great coffee and spice traders of the Red Sea routes and the Syrian and North African merchant colonies, was wealthy, organized in guilds, and thoroughly entangled with the households through protection payments, partnerships, and loans. The relationship was mutually parasitic rather than simply predatory: merchants needed a patron with armed men to protect their caravans and their warehouses, and households needed merchant capital to fund the purchase of mamluks and the payment of retainers. When a bey fell, his merchant creditors fell with him, which gave the commercial class a direct stake in factional outcomes.

## The beys and the bedouin

The households could not hold Egypt without the tribes, and this relationship is the most consistently underrated element of the period.

The bedouin confederations of the Delta margins, the eastern desert routes, and above all Upper Egypt were substantial military powers with their own cavalry and their own territorial control. They dominated the trade and pilgrimage roads, they could raise or ruin a harvest through raiding, and in Upper Egypt particular confederations exercised something close to autonomous authority over long stretches of the valley.

The beys dealt with them through a standing combination of subsidy, alliance, marriage in some cases, and periodic punitive expedition. A faction that secured a powerful tribal ally acquired thousands of horsemen at short notice, and the pattern of a defeated bey retreating to Upper Egypt to rebuild with tribal support recurs so often in the chronicles that it functions almost as a rule of the system. Murad Bey's repeated campaigns in the south were not colonial adventures but maintenance of a relationship that his regime's survival rested on.

This dependence cut both ways. It meant the beys could always regenerate a field force after a defeat, which is part of why Hasan Pasha's expedition could not finish them. It also meant Egyptian military power was a coalition rather than an army, assembled by negotiation before every campaign, and coalitions of that kind do not maneuver, do not hold formations under fire, and do not sustain a long engagement against disciplined infantry.

## The military weakness behind the political strength

The beys dominated Egypt with a military instrument that had stopped being competitive with European and reformed Ottoman armies a long time before anyone in Cairo had to find out.

Their arm was heavy cavalry, and by the standards of cavalry it was formidable. The training was long and serious, the horses were excellent, the swordsmanship was of a very high order, and observers who saw these men fight, including hostile ones, record real respect for their courage and individual skill. Each was equipped at his own expense or his master's, carried firearms as well as blades, and was accompanied by armed servants.

What they had not built was everything that decides a modern battle. There was no disciplined infantry that could hold a line under fire, because infantry service carried no prestige and the regiments that might have supplied it had degenerated into stipend-drawing urban corporations. There was no field artillery train of any consequence and no trained gun crews, because artillery required a state to fund it and an engineering establishment to maintain it, and Egypt had neither. There was no staff, no standardized drill, no logistics corps, and no unified command, because the force was an assembly of households each of which fought under its own commander and could leave.

The reason for the gap is structural rather than cultural, and it is worth stating plainly because the alternative explanations are usually condescending. Disciplined infantry and artillery are expensive collective goods that only a centralized fiscal state can sustain. A political system built on competing households cannot produce one, because any household that converted its revenue into drilled infantry and cannons would be spending on a force that could not defend its own tax farms against a rival's cavalry in the meantime. The military backwardness was a direct product of the political arrangement that made the beys powerful, which is why it could not be fixed from inside.

### Why could the beys not fight a modern army?

Because their system produced superb individual cavalry and nothing else. Disciplined infantry, artillery, engineers, and staff are collective goods that require a centralized treasury, and a province run by competing households had no such treasury. Each household spent on horsemen because horsemen won the contests that actually mattered to it.

## A foreign elite: what the beys were to the people they ruled

One of the hardest things to convey about this system is how completely separated the rulers were from the ruled, and how deliberately that separation was maintained.

A bey was almost always born outside Egypt, usually in the Caucasus, and had been brought to the country as a boy through a slave route that ran through Ottoman Black Sea ports. His first language was not Arabic. The internal language of the households was a form of Turkish, and command, correspondence within the elite, and much of the ceremonial operated in it, while Arabic was the language of the country, the courts, the scholars, and the market. A bey learned enough Arabic to govern and often a great deal more, but the linguistic line between the military caste and the population was real and visible.

The line was reproduced rather than dissolved. Sons of beys, the awlad al-nas, were Arabic-speaking, locally born, and generally excluded from the senior military commands, which were reserved for newly imported and trained mamluks. This is the single most counterintuitive feature of the whole system to a reader used to hereditary aristocracies, and it was not an accident. An elite that recruits by purchase rather than by birth renews its manpower from outside every generation and never assimilates, and the households preferred it that way because an imported client with no local kin had nowhere else to go and nothing to inherit except through his master.

What did the population make of them? The evidence, filtered as it is through the urban religious classes who left the chronicles, suggests a settled and unsentimental view: these were the people who held the horses and took the taxes, some of them were better than others, their quarrels were a public nuisance, and their patronage was worth having. There is little sign of the population regarding them as illegitimate simply because they were foreign, in a world where rule by an imported military caste had been the norm in Egypt since the thirteenth century. There is also no sign whatever of the population regarding them as their own. When a French army destroyed the bey regime in 1798, the chronicles record shock and dislocation, not grief.

## Why Istanbul tolerated it for so long

An empire that let a province slip this far might look simply incapable, and that reading is too easy. The accommodation was in large part a choice, and the logic behind it held up for a remarkably long time.

Egypt's value to the empire was measurable and specific. It supplied the annual cash remittance, the grain that provisioned the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, the organization and security of the pilgrimage caravan, and a strategically vital position controlling the Red Sea approach. All four of those continued to be delivered through almost the entire period of bey dominance. The beys wanted the revenue system functioning because they lived off it, wanted the caravan secure because commanding it was the most prestigious office available, and wanted the sultan's recognition because it settled their internal disputes. A household that captured Cairo needed Istanbul to confirm its beylicates, which meant the empire retained a genuine lever even when it had no soldiers in the country.

Against that, the cost of reconquest was high and the alternative uses of the army were pressing. The empire spent the eighteenth century in repeated and expensive wars with Russia and Austria on frontiers where defeat meant losing territory permanently. Egypt was not going anywhere, its tribute still arrived, and the one serious expedition mounted in 1786 demonstrated that control could be restored at will and could not be maintained cheaply. An imperial council weighing a permanent garrison in Cairo against reinforcements for the Danube made a defensible choice.

There was also a doctrinal comfort that mattered more than it might appear. The beys never denied the sultan's sovereignty except in Ali Bey's brief rupture. The Friday sermon named the sultan, the coinage carried his name, appointments arrived in his form, and the fiction of imperial government was maintained scrupulously by the very men who had emptied it. An empire whose legitimacy rested on being the protector of the Muslim community could tolerate a province that acknowledged it and paid, even while ignoring its instructions, far more comfortably than it could tolerate an open secession. Ali Bey found this out precisely: the moment he crossed from de facto autonomy to declared rupture, Istanbul acquired both the motive and the means to detach his own commander from him.

## The women of the households and the transmission of wealth

A system that excluded sons from command still had to transmit property between generations, and the mechanism ran substantially through women, which is why the great households' wives and widows appear in the record far more prominently than a purely military account would suggest.

Marriages between households were a standard instrument of alliance, and a commander frequently married the widow of his predecessor or patron. This was not merely symbolic. A widow held property in her own right under Islamic law, including her dower, her inheritance share, and whatever endowments and tax-farm interests had been settled on her, and she often held the household's accumulated moveable wealth. Marrying her brought that property, and with it the household's clients and credibility, into the new commander's hands. Abu al-Dhahab's marriage into Ali Bey's household followed exactly this logic.

Women of the elite were also substantial endowers in their own right, founding and funding mosques, schools, fountains, and charitable distributions, and the endowment deeds that record these foundations are among the most valuable documents historians have for the period, because they list properties, revenues, staff, and conditions in precise detail. The waqf served the same protective function for a woman's property as for a man's, shielding assets from confiscation during the violent reversals that followed every factional defeat.

None of this made the system anything other than a military patriarchy in which the decisive resource, armed men, was held exclusively by males. But it does mean that the household, rather than the individual bey, is again the right unit of analysis: property, alliance, and continuity ran through a structure that included women as holders and transmitters of wealth, even while command ran through purchased and manumitted male clients.

## How we know: the sources for the bey century

The evidence for this period is far richer than for most of Egyptian history, and knowing what each body of it can and cannot tell us is the difference between repeating a story and understanding one.

The indispensable narrative source is the chronicle of Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti, a Cairo scholar of the religious establishment who wrote a year-by-year account of the later eighteenth century and the upheavals that followed, with obituaries of the notable dead that amount to a biographical dictionary of the elite. His value is unusual: he knew many of the men he describes, he understood the households from the inside of Cairo society, and he wrote with a moral seriousness that makes his judgments legible even when one disagrees with them. His limits are equally clear. He is an urban scholar writing about urban politics, so the countryside appears mainly as a source of grain and grievance. He is hostile to the arbitrary exactions of the final bey regime and hostile to the French who displaced it, and his verdicts should be read as the verdicts of a particular class.

The archival record is the counterweight to the chronicle. The Ottoman imperial archives hold the appointment orders, the correspondence between the capital and the province, the treasury registers, and the remittance accounts, and they show the empire's version of what was happening, which is frequently a version in which nothing unusual is happening at all. Egyptian court registers, the records of the religious courts in Cairo, are even more valuable for social and economic history, because they record sales, endowments, marriage settlements, inheritance disputes, partnerships, and loans involving the households and the merchants who financed them. Endowment deeds, as noted above, are precise property inventories. Work by scholars such as Stanford Shaw on the financial and administrative organization of the province, and by Andre Raymond on Cairo's artisans and merchants, is built on exactly these materials.

European accounts form the third body: consular correspondence, merchant records, and travel writing. They are useful for commercial data, for events they witnessed, and for descriptions of things insiders never bothered to describe because everyone knew them. They are unreliable on internal politics, which their authors usually misunderstood, and they carry heavy assumptions about oriental despotism that shape what they report.

The reading that emerges from combining these is the one this article has followed. The imperial paperwork describes a governed province; the chronicle describes a country run by households; the court registers show property and money flowing through those households; and the European material confirms the practical arrangements a foreign merchant had to deal with. Where the sources conflict, the documents recording what was bought, endowed, and inherited generally beat both the official orders and the narrative, because a sale deed records what actually changed hands.

## 1798: the regime meets a modern army

When a French expeditionary force landed near Alexandria in the summer of 1798, the bey regime had roughly three weeks left. The details of that campaign belong to the article on [Napoleon's invasion of Egypt](/2013/10/01/napoleon-invasion-of-egypt/), and the decisive engagement outside Cairo is treated in the article on [the battle of the pyramids](/2013/11/01/battle-of-the-pyramids/). What belongs here is why the collapse was so fast.

The force Murad Bey led against the French was the system's maximum effort: several thousand mamluk cavalry, the best that money, training, and horseflesh could produce, supported by infantry levies and bedouin auxiliaries of variable value. The force it met was a modern army of the late eighteenth century, with drilled infantry that could form square, field artillery served by trained crews, engineers, and a command structure that coordinated all of it.

The outcome was not close and it was not, as the romantic accounts imply, a matter of courage against technology. The cavalry charged with tremendous force and no effect, because a square of disciplined infantry with bayonets and supporting guns cannot be broken by horsemen without artillery preparation, and the beys had none. The mamluk formations had no way to combine arms, no reserve doctrine, and no means of disengaging and re-forming. Murad withdrew up the Nile with what he could save; Ibrahim went east toward Syria.

The speed of the collapse is the retrospective verdict on the whole arrangement. A political system that had defeated every rival inside Egypt for two hundred and fifty years lost its country in a single afternoon to an army from a state that could fund infantry and artillery. Everything that had made the beys unbeatable at home, the self-funding household, the private cavalry, the absence of a central treasury capable of taxing them, was exactly what made them helpless abroad.

## The scramble after the French

The French occupation did not restore Ottoman authority and it did not end the beys, and the decade of confusion that followed is the actual ending of the story.

The French were forced out in 1801 by a combined British and Ottoman effort, and the province was left with four armed parties and no settled authority: the surviving bey households, still holding cavalry and claiming their tax farms; the Ottoman forces that had come to expel the French and now expected to govern; an Albanian contingent within those forces that had its own commanders and its own interests; and the Cairo population and religious establishment, which had discovered during the occupation that it could bargain, protest, and depose.

Istanbul tried to settle the matter by force and treachery, including an attempt to trap and massacre bey leaders that failed to finish the job, and the beys tried to reassemble their old dominance and found they no longer commanded either the revenue or the streets. The ulama of Cairo, having learned their own weight, intervened decisively in 1805 by demanding the removal of one governor and the installation of the Albanian commander who had built the best relationships in the city.

That commander was Muhammad Ali, and the state he built out of the wreckage, which is the subject of the article on [Muhammad Ali and the birth of modern Egypt](/2014/03/01/muhammad-ali-and-modern-egypt/), was constructed in direct opposition to everything the household system had been. He centralized the revenue, abolished the tax farms, conscripted peasants into drilled infantry, and built an artillery establishment, which is to say he built precisely the fiscal-military state the beys had structurally been unable to build.

## 1811: the end at the Citadel

The Mamluk beys were finished by a man who understood them because he had spent six years operating inside their world, and he finished them by exploiting the one thing they could never protect against, which was an invitation.

Between 1805 and 1811 Muhammad Ali systematically stripped the households of what had made them powerful. He broke the great tax farms and pulled revenue collection toward the center, which cut the cash that bought mamluks and paid retainers. He played the remaining bey factions against one another, drove some into Upper Egypt, and detached others with appointments and grants. He built a force loyal to himself rather than to a household.

In March 1811, with a campaign against the Wahhabi movement in Arabia announced, he summoned the leading beys and their retinues to a ceremony at the Cairo Citadel. They came, in numbers and in their finest, because attending such a ceremony was what a bey did and because refusing would have been a declaration of hostility they were not yet strong enough to make. The gates were closed on the procession in the narrow passage leading down from the Citadel and the assembled men were shot. Hundreds died in the passage and the killings continued across the country in the weeks that followed as the households' remaining members were hunted down.

It was treacherous, it was effective, and it ended a form of political organization that had governed Egypt for five and a half centuries. Individual mamluks survived, some fleeing south into Nubia, and the word continued to describe household retainers for a while afterward. What ended was the system: the purchase of boys as a route to command, the household as the unit of politics, and the self-funding cavalry elite as the holder of Egyptian sovereignty.

## Why the beys could not adapt

Given two hundred and fifty years, immense wealth, and repeated demonstrations that their military model was obsolete, why did the beys never build what Muhammad Ali built within a decade of destroying them?

The answer is not that they were ignorant. They had seen Ottoman artillery in 1517 and again in 1786, they traded with Europeans continuously, and the French comprehensively demonstrated the point in 1798. Murad Bey in particular showed real interest in firearms and even in naval matters. The obstacle was structural.

Building a fiscal-military state requires first taking the revenue away from the men who already hold it, and in Egypt those men were the beys themselves. A reforming bey would have had to break the tax farms that funded his own household, disarm his own khushdash, and convert his cavalry into a salaried standing army answerable to a treasury rather than to him personally. Every step in that sequence would have weakened him against his rivals long before it strengthened him against foreigners, and his rivals would not have waited politely. The household system punished exactly the behavior that would have saved it.

Muhammad Ali could do it because he arrived from outside the system with a force that was not a household, and because he destroyed the competing claimants first and reorganized the revenue afterward. The order of operations was the whole trick, and it was available only to someone who did not owe his position to the arrangement he was dismantling.

Ali Bey al-Kabir is the closest the system came to producing its own reformer, and his career shows the ceiling. He unified the elite under one household, seized the revenue, and projected power into Syria and Arabia, which is further than anyone had gone since the sultanate. He still fought with the same cavalry, still funded it through the same tax farms, and still depended on the loyalty of manumitted clients, and it was a manumitted client who destroyed him. He did not fail for lack of ambition. He failed because he was trying to build a state out of the one material that cannot hold one.

## What disappeared and what survived

The destruction of the beys removed a ruling class, and it is worth being precise about what went with them and what did not.

What went was the household as a political institution, the military slave route from the Caucasus into Egyptian command, the tax farm as the basic unit of rural administration, and the idea that armed private establishments were a normal component of government. Muhammad Ali's monopoly system, his conscript army, and his centralized land administration replaced all four within twenty years, and none of them returned.

What survived was more than the ledger suggests. The physical fabric of Cairo, the houses, mosques, sabil fountains, and schools built by household patronage, is one of the great surviving urban ensembles of the early modern Islamic world. The endowments the households founded continued to fund institutions long after their founders were gone, which is exactly what endowments are designed to do. The religious establishment that had learned during the French occupation and the 1805 crisis that it could speak for the city remained a political factor into the modern period.

The administrative map survived too, in a quiet way. The provincial divisions, the fiscal categories, and much of the accounting vocabulary that Muhammad Ali's officials used were inherited rather than invented, because a new ruler in a hurry uses the categories the existing clerks already understand. The state that replaced the beys was built on top of the paperwork the beys had left lying around.

## Judging the beys: the case against

The prosecution is strong and it should be stated at full strength before any qualification.

They were an extractive foreign caste that ruled a population it did not belong to, did not assimilate into, and did not represent, and it funded itself by taxing that population as hard as the population could bear. Their internal competition repeatedly turned Cairo into a battlefield and Upper Egypt into a campaigning ground, with the costs falling on people who had no stake in the outcome. They presided over a fiscal system whose incentives destroyed the irrigation infrastructure Egyptian agriculture depended on, and they held no reserves and offered no relief when the Nile failed or plague arrived. They produced no institutions, no law of succession, no schools of administration, and no army capable of defending the country, and when a foreign force finally arrived they lost the whole province in an afternoon. Al-Jabarti, who is the closest thing this period has to an informed contemporary judge and who despised the French, still wrote about the final bey regime's exactions with open contempt.

## Judging the beys: the case for

The defense is narrower but not empty, and dismissing it produces a worse history rather than a more moral one.

They kept Egypt's commercial economy functioning, and Cairo through this period remained among the largest and wealthiest cities in the world, with a coffee trade, a textile industry, and a merchant class of real substance. They secured the pilgrimage route and provisioned the holy cities for two centuries, which was a genuine administrative achievement requiring the coordination of supply, finance, and desert diplomacy on an enormous scale. They endowed the buildings and foundations that constitute a major part of Cairo's surviving heritage. And they governed, in the minimal sense that taxes were collected, courts sat, caravans moved, and the Nile flood was distributed, through a period when central imperial authority in the province had effectively ceased to exist.

There is also a comparative point that cuts against the usual framing. Provincial elites capturing local power from a weakening center was the general pattern of the eighteenth-century Ottoman world, visible in the ayan of Anatolia and the Balkans, in Baghdad under its own mamluk governors, and in the semi-autonomous rulers of Syria and Tunis. Egypt was an extreme case, not an aberration, and explaining it as a peculiarly Egyptian failure misses that the empire was undergoing a general decentralization from which some provinces emerged with functioning local states. Whether that decentralization amounts to decline at all is the central question of the debate examined in the article asking [whether Ottoman rule caused Egypt's decline](/2014/02/01/did-ottoman-rule-cause-decline/), which is where that argument properly belongs.

## The verdict

The defeated-not-destroyed thesis holds, with one significant correction that makes it more disturbing rather than less.

The Ottoman conquest of 1517 was a complete military victory and a partial political one. It killed the sultanate, executed the last sultan, and reduced a great independent power to a province, and on the level of dynasties and titles it was decisive. The full account of that campaign and what it settled is given in the article on [how the Ottomans took Egypt in 1517](/2013/06/01/ottoman-conquest-of-egypt-1517/). What it did not do, because doing it would have been expensive and because the conquerors saw no reason to, was dismantle the machinery by which Egypt produced its military elite. The slave route stayed open, the household stayed legal, the tax farm stayed the basis of rural finance, and the province was administered by a governor with a seal and no soldiers.

Given those conditions, the regrowth of a Mamluk-style elite was not a surprise, an anomaly, or a failure of Ottoman vigilance. It was the predictable output of the system the Ottomans installed. Within a century the households were the decisive force in the province; within two they were the government in everything but name; and the one time the empire tested the arrangement with an army, in 1786, it found it could reconquer Egypt and could not hold it without paying for a permanent occupation.

The correction is the one the recent historiography supplies. This was not the old Mamluk corporation surviving underground and re-emerging. The dominant household of the final century grew out of an Ottoman regiment and adopted the mamluk recruitment method because it worked, which means the imperial institutions themselves became the vehicle for reproducing the elite the empire had conquered. The continuity was of technique, not of blood or corporation. An empire that beats an enemy and then keeps the enemy's method of making soldiers, because it is cheap and effective, should expect to meet that enemy again in a new uniform.

The beys' ceiling is the other half of the verdict, and Ali Bey al-Kabir marks it exactly. A household system can take a country and can never make a state out of it, because the thing that makes a household strong, self-funding armed force loyal to a person, is precisely the thing a state must abolish to exist. That is why the beys ruled Egypt for two and a half centuries and left no government behind, and why the man who destroyed them in 1811 had to destroy them first and build afterward. They were not defeated by Istanbul, by Napoleon, or by history. They were defeated by the arithmetic of their own arrangement, and everyone who studies them is really studying the limits of rule by armed households.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did the Mamluk beys hold power under the Ottomans?

They held power by controlling the three things that mattered and leaving the titles to the governor. They controlled armed force, because each great household maintained hundreds of trained cavalry paid from its own revenue while the pasha had only a ceremonial escort. They controlled money, because the land tax was farmed out to contractors and the men best able to bid for and enforce those contracts were the men with horsemen. And they controlled the offices that carried real weight, above all the informal leadership called shaykh al-balad, the command of the pilgrimage caravan, and the treasurership. The Ottoman governor retained the sultan's seal and the channel to Istanbul, so appointments still passed through him, but he could not collect a tax, remove a commander, or put a force in the field, and by the later eighteenth century the council in Cairo could depose him and have the deposition ratified afterward.

### Q: How did the beys keep power after 1517?

The conquest destroyed the Mamluk sultanate but not the method that produced Mamluks. Selim I executed the last sultan and the irreconcilable commanders, then recruited the rest, and he installed a Mamluk defector, Khayr Bey, as the first Ottoman governor. Mamluk officers who submitted kept their men, their estates, and their administrative functions, because a province of this size could not be run without people who understood the Nile revenue and could put cavalry on a road. The households that produced those men, buying boys, training them, freeing them into service, were left untouched as a legal and social institution. Since the system reproduced by purchase rather than by descent, it did not need a surviving dynasty to continue. Each generation bought a new cohort, and within roughly a century the households had regrown into the decisive force in the province.

### Q: Who was Ali Bey al-Kabir?

Ali Bey al-Kabir was a Georgian bought as a boy into the household of Ibrahim Kahya al-Qazdughli, who rose through the violent competition following his master's death in 1754 to become the dominant figure in Egypt. He took the leadership of the country by 1760, was driven out, returned, and by 1768 had destroyed or subordinated every rival household, replacing the old two-faction balance with a single pyramid under himself. He then expelled the Ottoman governor, halted the annual remittance to Istanbul, sent an expedition that installed a favorable authority in the Hijaz, and in 1771 took Damascus through his commander Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab. His epithet means the great or the elder. He was defeated in 1773 after his own commander turned against him, and died of wounds within days of his capture.

### Q: Did the Mamluk beys rebel against Istanbul?

Open rebellion was rare and the normal pattern was something more effective: scrupulous formal obedience combined with complete practical independence. The households kept the sultan's name in the Friday sermon and on the coinage, forwarded the annual remittance, and had their appointments confirmed in the proper imperial form, while ignoring instructions they disliked and deposing governors they found inconvenient. That arrangement suited both sides, because the empire needed the tribute and the pilgrimage caravan more than it needed obedience, and the households needed imperial recognition to settle their own succession disputes. The one genuine rupture came under Ali Bey al-Kabir between 1768 and 1773, when the governor was expelled and the tribute stopped. It ended badly and was not repeated, which taught the elite that formal submission was the cheaper route to the same substantive freedom.

### Q: How powerful were the Mamluk beys?

By the second half of the eighteenth century they were the government of Egypt in everything except name. They set taxation through their control of the tax farms, made appointments including the beylicates themselves, commanded the only significant armed force in the country, ran the pilgrimage caravan, negotiated with foreign merchants and consuls, and made war on their own account in Upper Egypt, the Hijaz, and Syria. Their limits were equally real. They could not defend the country against a modern army, as 1798 demonstrated in a single afternoon, and they could not protect themselves from each other, which meant no household held supremacy for long. Their power was also strictly domestic in character: overwhelming inside Egypt against rivals who fought the same way, and close to useless against disciplined infantry and artillery.

### Q: How did the beys divide power among themselves?

They divided it by household and by office, and the division was renegotiated constantly by force. Roughly two dozen beylicates existed at any time, and each was held by the head of a household who commanded his own cavalry and controlled his own tax farms. The functional offices were the prizes: the informal leadership of the country, the command of the pilgrimage caravan, the treasurership, and the acting governorship between pashas. For about a century the households sorted themselves into two great rival blocs, and after those collapsed the pattern shifted toward one dominant household distributing beylicates to its own clients. The final arrangement was a partnership, with Ibrahim Bey taking the civil and fiscal side and Murad Bey the military. Settlements were frequently broken by fighting and then reassembled, because no household could quite finish another.

### Q: What happened to the Mamluk beys?

They were destroyed in stages between 1798 and 1811. A French expeditionary force shattered their army outside Cairo in 1798 and drove the leading commanders into Upper Egypt and Syria. The French withdrawal in 1801 left four armed parties contending for the province, and the households found they no longer controlled either the revenue or the streets. Muhammad Ali, who took power in 1805, then dismantled them methodically, breaking the great tax farms that funded the purchase of cavalry, setting the surviving factions against one another, and building a force loyal to himself. In March 1811 he summoned the leading commanders to a ceremony at the Cairo Citadel and had them shot in the passage below, with the killings continuing across the country afterward. Individuals survived and some fled south, but the household system as a form of government ended there.

### Q: How did the beys tax Egypt?

Through tax farming, known as iltizam. The state auctioned the right to collect the land tax over a village or group of villages, and the holder paid an advance plus a fixed annual sum and kept everything he could extract above it. He also held a portion of land directly as his own. Two features turned this into a transfer of power. Contracts drifted from short leases toward life tenure and then toward effective inheritance, and enforcement was the contractor's own responsibility, which meant the men with cavalry won the best farms and used the proceeds to buy more cavalry. On top of the regular assessment came irregular levies imposed whenever a household needed cash for a campaign or a purchase of new recruits. The imperial remittance was a fixed obligation rather than a share of yield, so the growth in extraction stayed in Egypt.

### Q: What did the title bey actually mean?

Bey is a Turkish term that in the Ottoman provincial system named the holder of a sanjak, an administrative district, which is why the standard designation is sanjak bey. In most provinces that meant a district governor appointed from the capital, given a revenue assignment, and rotated after a few years. Egypt was organized differently. The settlement of the 1520s created a college of roughly two dozen beys who sat in Cairo and held functional offices rather than territories, and a large share of them were drawn from the local military households rather than sent from the capital. The rank was therefore Ottoman while the men filling it were products of the Mamluk system of purchase and training. Over time the household behind the man mattered far more than the rank, and a commander with hundreds of cavalry outweighed any number of formal superiors.

### Q: Who was the shaykh al-balad?

The shaykh al-balad, meaning roughly chief of the country, was the leading figure among the Egyptian grandees, and it is the most revealing office of the period because it did not officially exist. It appears in no imperial law code, carried no salary from Istanbul, and was conferred by nothing more formal than the recognition of the other households that one man now carried the most weight. Whoever held it distributed the beylicates, brokered the tax farms, arbitrated between households, and dealt with the Ottoman governor as something close to an equal. The rise of this position is the clearest single indicator of where sovereignty had migrated, since a province had generated a chief executive that the empire never authorized and could not abolish. Ali Bey al-Kabir, Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab, and Ibrahim Bey all held it.

### Q: What was a Mamluk household?

The household, called the bayt, was the basic unit of politics in Ottoman Egypt, and understanding it is the key to the whole period. At its center stood a senior commander. Around him were the men he had bought as boys, converted, trained in horsemanship and arms, and then freed into his service as adult soldiers, along with his earlier freed clients who now had followings of their own, his wives and their kin networks, his stewards and financial agents, and the merchants and artisans who depended on his protection. A large one might hold several hundred fighting men and reach into the guilds, the tax farms, the grain trade, and the religious establishment. The bonds were manufactured rather than inherited: loyalty to the master who bought and freed you, and solidarity with the others bought in the same cohort.

### Q: Which factions fought for control of the province?

From the seventeenth century the households sorted into two great rival blocs, the Faqariyya and the Qasimiyya, and their competition structured Egyptian politics for roughly a hundred years. Each was a coalition of households with clients in the garrison regiments, a share of the tax farms, and allies among the bedouin confederations whose cavalry could decide a campaign. The origins of both are obscure, and the colorful explanations in the chronicles involving two brothers and rival color symbols read more like folklore than record. The contests were violent, with street fighting in Cairo, assassinations, and purges of defeated officers, and a prolonged conflict in 1711 shut the capital down for months. The two-bloc system was eventually ended not by either bloc winning but by the rise of a household out of the Janissary corps, the Qazdughliyya, which absorbed or destroyed the rest.

### Q: Why could the pasha not control the beys?

Because he had authority and nothing to enforce it with. He commanded no independent army, since the garrison regiments were recruited and paid locally and their officers had become clients of the households. He had no independent revenue, since his allowances came from Egyptian collections run by the men he theoretically supervised. He had no time, because governors were rotated after two or three years specifically to stop them building a local base, so he faced men who had been in Cairo for decades. And the consultative council created by the provincial law code had become the instrument through which the grandees declared him unacceptable, confined him to the Citadel, and installed a caretaker. Istanbul preferred a quiet province that paid to a contested one that did not, so it generally ratified the removal after the fact.

### Q: Who betrayed Ali Bey al-Kabir?

Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab, who was his own mamluk. Ali Bey had bought him, trained him, freed him, married him into his household, and given him the most important command in his service, and in 1771, with Damascus taken and the Syrian campaign going well, Abu al-Dhahab abruptly withdrew the army and returned to Egypt. The sources offer several explanations that are not mutually exclusive: overtures from Istanbul, which had every reason to detach him; religious unease at making war on the sultan in alliance with a Christian naval power and a rebel in Acre; and ordinary ambition. He gathered support in Upper Egypt, marched on Cairo, and forced his master to flee in 1772. When Ali Bey returned with a force from Acre in 1773 he was defeated, wounded, and captured, and he died within days.

### Q: How did Murad Bey and Ibrahim Bey share command?

They divided the work rather than the country. Both were mamluks of Abu al-Dhahab, and after his death in 1775 they arrived at an arrangement that held, with interruptions, for over two decades. Ibrahim Bey took the informal leadership of the country and the civil and fiscal side, dealing with the council, the merchants, the religious establishment, and the negotiations with Istanbul over the remittance. Murad Bey took the pilgrimage command and the military side, running the cavalry, campaigning in Upper Egypt, and managing relations with the bedouin confederations. The partnership broke down into open fighting more than once, with one or the other driven south for a season before a settlement restored it. What kept bringing it back was arithmetic: neither could finish the other, and a war between them invited outside intervention.

### Q: Were the beys good for ordinary villagers?

On the whole, no, and the mechanism is clear rather than a matter of character. The tax farm gave a contractor everything he could extract above a fixed payment and made enforcement his own responsibility, which rewarded hard collection and discouraged investment. The investment that mattered most in Egypt was irrigation, and a basin system requiring coordinated maintenance is exactly what fragmented private collection underprovides, so canals silted and dikes went unrepaired where no authority could compel cooperation. On top of the regular assessment came unpredictable levies raised whenever a household needed cash, which is worse for a cultivator than a high but known burden. Cultivators responded by concealing production, fleeing villages, and abandoning land. Two honest qualifications: commercial agriculture and the coffee and textile trades generated real wealth, and the surviving records measure extraction rather than what cultivators retained.

### Q: Did the beys still buy slave soldiers in the eighteenth century?

Continuously, and the trade was the elite's lifeline rather than a residual custom. Boys were acquired in the Caucasus and the lands north of the Black Sea, with Georgians and Circassians dominating the eighteenth-century intake, moved through Black Sea ports, and shipped to Egypt by specialist merchants. A household's fighting strength was a direct function of its cash flow, since each recruit represented a purchase price plus roughly a decade of training before he was a useful cavalryman. That is the link binding the tax farms to the slave market: revenue bought boys, boys became cavalry, cavalry defended the revenue. A household that lost its tax farms could not replace losses and would be absorbed within a generation. The route was also a strategic vulnerability, since it depended on conditions in a region a thousand miles away over which the elite had no influence.

### Q: Were the beys Egyptian by birth?

Almost never, and the separation was maintained deliberately rather than merely inherited. A commander was typically born in the Caucasus and brought to the country as a boy, so his first language was not Arabic, and the internal language of the households was a form of Turkish while Arabic was the language of the country, the courts, and the market. The line was reproduced in each generation because sons of commanders, called awlad al-nas, were locally born and Arabic-speaking and were generally excluded from senior military command, which was reserved for newly imported and trained recruits. An elite that renews itself by purchase never assimilates, and the households preferred it that way: an imported client with no local kin had nowhere else to go and nothing to inherit except through his master.

### Q: How large were the armies the beys could field?

Estimating numbers here requires care, because the surviving figures come from chroniclers and European observers rather than from muster rolls, and both had reasons to round generously. What the evidence supports is an order of magnitude rather than a precise count: the trained cavalry of the great households together numbered in the low thousands, with a single dominant household maintaining several hundred, and the total swelled considerably on campaign through armed servants, regimental levies, and bedouin auxiliaries recruited for the occasion. The quality was concentrated entirely in the cavalry core. There was no disciplined infantry, no field artillery of consequence, no trained gun crews, and no unified command, because the force was an assembly of households each fighting under its own commander and free to depart.

### Q: Why did Istanbul tolerate the bey households for so long?

Because the arrangement delivered what the empire actually needed and reconquest cost more than it was worth. Egypt supplied the annual cash remittance, the grain that provisioned Mecca and Medina, the organization and security of the pilgrimage caravan, and a position commanding the Red Sea approach, and all four continued to arrive through almost the whole period. The households wanted the revenue system working because they lived off it and wanted imperial recognition because it settled their own succession disputes, which left the empire a genuine lever even without soldiers in the country. Meanwhile the imperial army was consumed by repeated wars with Russia and Austria on frontiers where defeat meant permanent losses. The one serious expedition, in 1786, proved the point exactly: the province could be reconquered at will and could not be held without a permanent occupation.
