The Preventive Detention Act 1950 was on the statute book within a month of the Constitution it appeared to contradict. The Constitution had come into force on 26 January 1950, carrying a chapter of fundamental rights that promised personal liberty, freedom of movement, and protection against arrest without stated grounds. Before February was out, the Provisional Parliament had enacted a law under which a person could be imprisoned on the satisfaction of the executive, without charge, without trial, and without any offence having been committed. The statute was Act IV of 1950, dated 25 February 1950. It was defended by its mover, Home Minister Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as a temporary necessity. It was enacted with a life of one year. It was then renewed, and renewed again, until it had governed preventive detention at the Centre for close to twenty years, and the pattern it set, a temporary justification attached to a power that outlives every expiry date written for it, became the standard shape of Indian detention law.

![The Preventive Detention Act 1950, showing Sardar Patel piloting the first detention law of the Republic through the Provisional Parliament in February 1950 - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-04.webp)

The passage of this Act is usually told as a riddle or as an accusation. The riddle asks how the same generation that wrote the fundamental rights could, within weeks, write a detention law. The accusation answers that the founders never meant the rights seriously. Both versions are too small for the record. The men and women who enacted the Preventive Detention Act 1950 had spent the previous September debating preventive detention inside the Constituent Assembly, and they had written detention into the Constitution itself, in Article 22, with safeguards they believed adequate and limits they believed real. They enacted the statute in a month when the state faced an armed communist insurgency in Telangana, a refugee crisis of enormous scale, communal violence that had not burned itself out, food scarcity, and provincial detention laws whose validity the new Constitution had thrown into doubt. None of that settles whether they were right. It does settle that they knew what they were doing, said why they were doing it, and left a full record of their reasoning, their opponents' reasoning, and the compromises both sides struck. That record, rather than the short and unremarkable text of the Act, is the subject here.

The system within which this single statute operated, the constitutional architecture running from Article 22 down through the Union and Concurrent List entries to the central and state detention laws, is mapped in [the complete guide to preventive detention law in India](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/), and this article does not retrace that architecture. Its ground is narrower and, for a legislative history, more important: how this bill became this Act, what the passage record shows about the intentions and the blind spots of the people who passed it, what the Supreme Court did to the Act within three months of its enactment, and how a legislature that had voted for one year of detention power kept voting, across two decades and three general elections, to keep the power alive.

## The world the bill was written for

A legislative history that begins with the bill misses most of the story, because the Preventive Detention Act 1950 was not an invention of February 1950. It was the last in a line of detention powers that ran back through the colonial state, and its drafters were working inside a tradition of executive detention that many of them had personally experienced from the receiving end. The Bengal Regulation III of 1818 had empowered the colonial government to detain persons for reasons of state without recourse to the ordinary courts. The Rowlatt legislation of 1919, enacted against Indian opposition so united that it helped ignite the first all-India phase of the national movement, authorised detention and trial by special procedure for revolutionary activity. The Defence of India Act 1939 and the rules made under it gave the wartime executive a sweeping power to detain on reasonable suspicion, and that machinery had been used against the Congress leadership itself during the Quit India movement. When members of the Provisional Parliament rose to attack Patel's bill as a colonial instrument, they were not reaching for a metaphor. They were identifying the bill's ancestry, which was a matter of documentary fact.

The provincial layer mattered as much as the imperial one. After independence, the provinces, soon to become states under the Constitution, had enacted or revived public safety and maintenance of order legislation to deal with insurgency, communal disorder, and food scarcity, and detention orders were being passed under those state laws in considerable numbers. The Constitution complicated that position at a stroke. From 26 January 1950, detention had to answer to Part III of the Constitution, and the provincial Acts, drafted for a pre-constitutional world, were exposed to challenge under Articles 19, 21, and 22. The Hindustan Times report of the bill's passage, preserved in the Collected Works of Sardar Patel, recorded one of the government's practical motives precisely: the bill was considered necessary because doubts had been thrown on the validity of some of the existing Public Safety Acts in the states on account of High Court judgments. A central Act, drafted to fit the safeguards in Article 22, would give the Centre and the states a detention power whose constitutional footing had been thought through rather than inherited by accident.

The pressures were not abstractions, and a fair history has to state them at the strength the government felt them. In Telangana, the communist-led armed struggle that had begun under the Nizam continued against the Indian state after the police action of 1948, with attacks on communications, on landlords, and on the apparatus of administration. Communist organisations in other regions were committed, in this period, to a strategy of disruption that their own statements described in insurrectionary terms. The Partition settlement was two and a half years old; the refugee movement across both new borders continued; communal killings remained a live possibility in several regions; and the assassination of Mahatma Gandhi in January 1948, by a conspirator from within the majority community, was fresh evidence that political violence did not come from one direction only. Food scarcity made the maintenance of supplies and services a security question rather than a merely administrative one, and hoarding and black marketing were treated by governments of the period as crimes against the community's survival. Patel, commending the bill, drew the line where the government wanted it drawn, and the Hindustan Times report preserved his words: the fight, he said, was not with communism or with those who believed in the theory of communism, but with those whose avowed object was to create disruption and dislocation, to tamper with communications, to suborn the loyalty of the people, and to make it impossible for normal government to function.

Against that, the opposition case in February 1950 also deserves to be stated at its strength, because time has proved parts of it out. The critics argued that detention without trial was the defining instrument of the colonial state, and that a republic founded by its victims should be slower to reach for it than a government two years from its own imprisonment under similar laws had been. They argued that the ordinary criminal law, used with energy, was sufficient for acts of violence and sabotage, and that a preventive power was wanted precisely because it relieved the executive of the burden of proof that the criminal law imposed. They argued that a power justified by emergency acquires a constituency, officials who plan around it, governments that budget for the order it produces, and that the temporary character written into a bill is the least reliable sentence in it. And they argued that the people against whom the power would mostly be used would not be saboteurs caught in the act but political organisers, trade unionists, and critics, because those are the people a preventive power can reach and a prosecution cannot. Each of these arguments was made in substance during the passage debates and at the renewals that followed. Each can be tested against the two decades that followed, and the test does not run in one direction.

### How long could a person be held under the 1950 Act?

A detention under the Act was capped at twelve months in all, and detention beyond three months required the opinion of an Advisory Board, composed of persons who were, had been, or were qualified to be judges of a High Court, that sufficient cause existed for the detention to continue.

The twelve-month ceiling and the three-month reference to the Advisory Board were the two load-bearing limits in the Act's design, and both came straight from the constitutional text the Assembly had written the previous year. Article 22 provided that no law could authorise detention beyond three months unless an Advisory Board reported sufficient cause before that period expired, and it empowered Parliament to prescribe the maximum period for detention in specified classes of cases. The Act exercised that power to fix the outer limit at twelve months. Whether those limits were strong protection or thin ceremony divided opinion from the start, and the division is worth understanding, because it recurs in every later detention statute. The government's position was that the Board converted executive satisfaction into reviewed satisfaction, and that a fixed outer limit converted an open-ended power into a bounded one. The critics' position was that a Board which heard the government's grounds without the detenu having the ordinary rights of a party, and which operated on material the detenu could not fully test, was review in form more than in substance. The record of the next twenty years contains evidence for both readings, and the honest conclusion is that the Board system prevented some detentions from running their full course while leaving the structure of executive judgment untouched.

## The dress rehearsal: detention politics from 1947 to 1950

Between independence and the Constitution, the Indian state had already governed by detention for two and a half years, and the experience formed the assumptions everyone carried into the debates of 1949 and 1950. The instruments were provincial: public safety and maintenance of order Acts inherited from the late colonial state and adapted by the new provincial governments, under which detention orders required no more constitutional justification than the old order had demanded. The populations detained in those years map the young state's anxieties in sequence. After the assassination of Mahatma Gandhi in January 1948, the government banned the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and detained large numbers of its members and sympathisers under the provincial powers, a use of detention against the Hindu right that preceded the better remembered use against the communist left and that complicates, from the very first season, any account in which preventive detention in India was born as an instrument against a single ideology. The ban was lifted in 1949 after negotiations and the adoption by the organisation of a written constitution, and the detentions wound down, but the precedent stood: a government of the national movement had imprisoned without trial, at scale, within six months of independence, and had done so against a movement whose members it regarded as a danger to the state's founding settlement rather than as criminals awaiting proof.

The communist detentions ran on a longer fuse. Through 1948 and 1949, as the Communist Party under its insurrectionary leadership called strikes, built armed squads in Telangana, and treated the new state as an enemy to be disrupted, provincial governments detained its organisers in waves, Gopalan among them from December 1947. By the time the Constituent Assembly debated Article 22 in September 1949, preventive detention was not a hypothesis to be imagined but a going concern to be regulated, with detenus in the jails of several provinces, habeas corpus petitions moving through the High Courts, and ministers in Delhi and the provincial capitals who had signed the orders or defended them. This chronology matters to the moral bookkeeping. The Assembly that wrote detention into the Constitution was not choosing between a India with preventive detention and an India without it. It was choosing whether an existing, operating, provincially scattered practice would be brought inside a constitutional frame, with grounds, representations, boards, and ceilings, or left to continue under inherited statutes of doubtful fit with the rights chapter. The choice it made can still be criticised, and was, but it should be criticised as what it was, a decision to constitutionalise a practice, taken by men who knew the practice from both sides of the prison gate.

The provincial experience also taught the administrators their craft, and the 1950 Act is legible as its codification. The grounds of Section 3 catalogue the occasions on which provincial governments had actually detained: security of the state and public order for the insurgent and the communal organiser, defence and foreign relations for the frontier and the spy, essential supplies for the hoarder and the saboteur of food movement. The satisfaction standard carried over the form by which district officers had acted. The Advisory Board, which appears in the Constitution and the Act as a safeguard, had antecedents in the review arrangements of the wartime and provincial practice as well, a review of the executive by the executive's nominees being a device the colonial state had also found convenient. None of this makes the Act a mere continuation, because the constitutional frame was new and, as Gopalan showed within three months, had teeth. But a legislative history should resist the temptation, flattering to both sides, to treat February 1950 as a creation. It was a consolidation, and consolidations are how emergency powers become ordinary law while everyone is looking at the emergency.

## The argument the Assembly had already had

The Preventive Detention Act 1950 cannot be understood apart from the debates of September 1949, when the Constituent Assembly took up the draft article that became Article 22. The Assembly was not writing on a blank slate and was not ambushed by the subject. Preventive detention was discussed as what it was, a power to imprison without trial, by an Assembly many of whose members had been imprisoned without trial by the colonial government. The discussion produced the distinctive structure that the Constitution still carries: clauses of Article 22 that give an arrested person the right to be told the grounds of arrest, to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner, and to be produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours, followed by a clause that withdraws those protections from a person detained under a preventive detention law, and then by clauses that substitute a different and thinner set of protections for that person. The detenu was to be told the grounds of detention as soon as might be, was to have the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order, and could not be held beyond three months unless an Advisory Board found sufficient cause. The authority could withhold facts whose disclosure it considered against the public interest, a provision whose practical importance the next two decades would demonstrate.

The Assembly heard the case against including any such power at full strength. Members including Thakur Dass Bhargava and Mahavir Tyagi spoke against writing detention without trial into a constitution of free India, and the unease was not confined to a fringe. The defenders of the draft article, among them K. M. Munshi, rested their case on the safeguards and on the proposition that a constitution which made no provision for preventive detention would not abolish the practice but merely drive governments to meet disorder with less regulated instruments. B. R. Ambedkar, piloting the draft, presented the Assembly with the safeguards as the settlement on offer and did not disguise that the subject was one on which the Drafting Committee's product was a compromise between the liberty the Assembly proclaimed and the order it feared losing. The Assembly adopted the article. That adoption matters for everything that follows, because it means the Act of February 1950 was not a legislature evading a constitution that forbade detention. It was a legislature using a power the constitution-makers had deliberately and reluctantly conferred only months before, and the argument between the government and its critics was an argument about whether the conferral should be exercised, on what scale, and for how long, rather than about whether it existed.

Two features of that September settlement shaped the Act's drafting five months later. The first is that Article 22 speaks in terms of what a detention law must contain rather than granting a free-standing power, so the Act had to reproduce the constitutional machinery: grounds, representation, Advisory Board, maximum period. The second is the exclusion in Article 22 itself of the ordinary arrest protections from preventive detention, which meant the Act's drafters did not have to argue for the exclusion. The Constitution had performed it. Critics who describe the Act as a betrayal of the Constitution have to explain away the fact that the Constitution's own text builds the detention regime and its exclusions; critics who describe the Act as constitutionally compelled have to explain away the fact that the same text set the power inside safeguards and left its exercise entirely to political choice. The record supports neither slogan. It supports a harder proposition: the founders created a lawful channel for detention without trial, fenced it, and trusted future legislatures to walk through the gate rarely. The history of the Act is the history of how that trust was used.

### Which earlier laws formed the lineage the 1950 Act inherited?

The Act stood at the end of a line that ran from the Bengal Regulation III of 1818 through the Rowlatt legislation of 1919 and the wartime Defence of India framework, and alongside it ran the provincial public safety Acts under which most detentions before 1950 had in practice been ordered.

That lineage explains two things about the 1950 text. It explains its grounds of detention, which track the categories the colonial and provincial statutes had used: the defence of India, the security of India, the security of the state, the maintenance of public order, and the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. And it explains the readiness with which the machinery was assembled, since the administrative practice of detention orders, grounds statements, and review boards had been built over decades and did not have to be invented. What the Republic added to the inheritance was not the power but the fences: a written constitutional standard against which the power could be measured, a Supreme Court that could strike the statute down in whole or in part, and a legislature that had to return periodically to renew the power because the Act carried its own expiry. Those fences were the founders' answer to the lineage. The question the next twenty years put to them was whether fences that depend on the detained person's ability to invoke them, and on the legislature's willingness to let a law die, would hold against a power that every government found useful.

## Why February, and not a season later

The timing of the bill is the fact from which the whole debate grows, and it has a precise explanation that slogans obscure. The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950. From that morning, every detention in India had to be justified under a fundamental rights chapter that had not existed the day before, and every provincial security Act had to survive scrutiny under Articles 19, 21, and 22 in courts that were already being moved. The government's legal position in February 1950 was therefore not that it desired a new power in the abstract. It was that detentions were in progress, under instruments of doubtful validity, against movements the government regarded as active dangers, and that each week of delay multiplied the habeas corpus petitions and the risk that courts would dismantle the provincial detention regime piecemeal, on grounds of form, leaving nothing in its place. The Hindustan Times report of the passage preserves exactly this reasoning in its account of the bill's necessity: doubts thrown on the validity of state Public Safety Acts by High Court judgments. A central Act, moved immediately, converted a collapsing patchwork into a single instrument whose conformity with Article 22 had been considered in advance, and whose first great test, when it came in Gopalan, the government could fight on ground of its own choosing. The speed that critics attacked as indecent haste was, in the government's own calculus, the point of the exercise.

The political timing pressed in the same direction. The communist movement's insurrectionary phase, associated with the strategy adopted by the Communist Party under B. T. Ranadive in 1948, had produced armed struggle in Telangana, sabotage and strikes elsewhere, and a governmental reading of the movement as directed at the state's communications and food system as much as at its authority. In Telangana the struggle continued after Hyderabad's integration, in a countryside where the party's squads exercised a parallel authority in village after village. Refugee resettlement in Bengal and Punjab consumed administrative capacity and generated its own disorders. Communal violence remained a standing risk, with the memory of 1947 and 1948 unspent. And the state's food position made the movement of grain a security subject: a government that could not guarantee supplies could not guarantee anything, which is why the maintenance of supplies and services stands in Section 3 beside the security of India without any sense, in the drafting, that the two subjects differ in kind. February 1950, in short, was not a quiet month in which a government chose to reach for an extreme power at leisure. It was a month near the peak of the founding state's insecurity, and the bill's date is a fact about that insecurity as much as about the government's philosophy.

The alternatives not taken deserve the same precision, because the opposition's case in the debate was partly that alternatives existed. The government could have relied on the ordinary criminal law, prosecuting sabotage, conspiracy, and unlawful assembly as crimes; its answer, given in substance by Patel and by Setalvad, was that prosecution reaches acts already committed or provable conspiracies, while the danger it faced was organised preparation whose interruption could not wait for a completed offence and could not be evidenced without exposing sources. The government could have legislated province by province, repairing the state Acts as the courts struck them; its answer was uniformity and speed, and the fact that defence and the security of India were central subjects on any view. The government could also have proceeded for a shorter period or with a select committee's scrutiny; on this the record gives the critics the better of the argument, since the one-year duration was itself the government's concession to scrutiny, a promise that the House would judge the working of the Act within twelve months, and the five-hour debate was the scrutiny the principle received while the machinery received almost none. What the passage record predicts, and the renewal record confirms, is that a legislature which substitutes a future judgment for a present one will usually find, when the future arrives, that the judgment has been pre-empted by the working of the thing itself. The House that reassembled to judge the Act in its renewal debates was judging detentions already made, administrations already organised around the power, and a ministerial case already written. That is not a corruption of the founders' design. It is the design, operating exactly as its critics in February 1950 said it would.

## Five hours in February: the passage of the bill

The bill that became Act IV of 1950 was moved in the Provisional Parliament by Sardar Patel in February 1950 and was law within the month, dated 25 February 1950, after a debate that the Hindustan Times described as heated and that ran for five hours, with question hour dispensed with so that the business could be completed. The speed deserves emphasis because it determined the kind of scrutiny the bill received. There was no select committee stage in which clause-by-clause evidence could be taken and amendments drafted at leisure. The Provisional Parliament was the Constituent Assembly sitting in its legislative capacity, a body that had not faced a general election and would not do so until the elections of 1951 and 1952, and the common charge that the Act was passed by an elected Parliament in the modern sense is therefore wrong in a way that cuts both ways. The body that passed it was unelected, which weakens any claim that the detention power carried a fresh popular mandate. It was also the body that had just written the Constitution, which destroys the comfortable theory that the Act was the work of politicians ignorant of the rights they were limiting. The same hands wrote both documents, and they wrote the second within weeks of finishing the first.

Patel's defence of the bill, as the contemporary report records it, had three elements. The first was necessity grounded in named conditions: insurgency, sabotage of communications, communal disorder, and the doubtful footing of the provincial laws after the Constitution. The second was discrimination of target: the government's fight, he insisted, was not with belief but with disruption, a distinction he drew expressly against the charge that the bill was an anti-communist measure aimed at opinion. The third was temporariness: the Act would expire on 1 April 1951, and when it expired the detentions under it would end as well, a point the Attorney General, M. C. Setalvad, reinforced by telling the House that the bill accorded with both the spirit and the letter of the Constitution. Patel is reported in accounts of the debate to have described the measure as one he had weighed through sleepless nights, and the Hindustan Times characterised the bill, in its mover's own framing, as the minimum evil necessary to safeguard democratic institutions. A historian should handle the sleepless nights as what they are, a reported parliamentary utterance rather than a verified private fact, but the minimum evil framing is documented and significant. The government did not present detention as a good. It presented it as the least bad instrument available to a state whose ordinary instruments were failing, and it priced the concession in public: one year, then Parliament would judge again.

The opposition's reply in that debate set the terms on which the Act would be argued about for twenty years. The critics fastened on the ancestry, asking why a Parliament of nationalists was re-enacting the logic of the Rowlatt period against Indians rather than against a foreign ruler. They fastened on the burden of proof, arguing that a government which cannot persuade a court should not be able to imprison by satisfying itself. They fastened on the breadth of the grounds, pointing out that the maintenance of supplies and services could reach strikers and organisers far removed from sabotage. And they predicted the renewal: that a House asked to extend the Act in 1951 would be told the emergency had not passed, and in 1954 would be told the same, until the expiry date became a ritual rather than a limit. The government carried the bill. The gap between the bill as introduced and the Act as passed was, by the standards of legislative history, remarkably small, because a bill moved and passed within days, without a committee stage, does not accumulate the layers of amendment that slower legislation acquires. The significant gap in this statute's story is not between the bill and the Act. It is between the Act as enacted, one year of power with a borrowed colonial shape and constitutional fences, and the Act as it came to live, its most aggressive clause struck out by the Supreme Court within three months and its one year stretched by renewal into two decades.

It is worth pausing on the legislature that did this, because it did not disperse when the detention bill passed. The same Provisional Parliament went on to enact the First Amendment in 1951, and the reader who wants the measure of that body should read [how the First Amendment was passed by the same Provisional Parliament](/2010/01/15/first-amendment-act-1951-explained/) alongside this passage record, since the two statutes together show a founding legislature simultaneously expanding the state's power over speech, property, and liberty and insisting, in both cases, that the expansion was a reluctant answer to disorder rather than a programme. Judging that insistence is the work of the sections that follow.

## The bill as introduced, and the gap that opened later

The discipline of legislative history is to compare texts across time: the bill as introduced, the Act as passed, and the Act as it stood when its life ended. For most statutes in this series the first comparison does the analytical work, because bills are drafted, committed, reported, amended on the floor, and enacted in altered form, and the alterations are the fossil record of the argument. The Preventive Detention Act 1950 is the instructive exception. A bill moved in February, debated for five hours with question hour dispensed with, and enacted within the month, without a select committee stage, reaches the statute book carrying its introduced shape almost intact. The haste was itself a policy choice, defended on urgency, and its consequence for scrutiny should be stated plainly: the Provisional Parliament debated the principle of preventive detention with passion and knowledge, and examined the machinery of preventive detention hardly at all. The clause-by-clause work that a committee stage performs, testing the width of grounds, the standard of satisfaction, the composition and procedure of the reviewing body, the treatment of the detenu's representation, was done, insofar as it was done in 1950, by the Supreme Court in Gopalan rather than by the legislature that passed the bill.

The second comparison, between the Act of February 1950 and the Act as it stood in December 1969, is where this statute's real legislative history happened. The differences are few in number and large in meaning. The original Section 14 is gone, removed after the Court destroyed it, and with it went the executive's attempt to keep the grounds and the representation out of the courtroom. The one-year duration in the first section is gone, replaced by a terminal date in 1969 that the first section reached only after a chain of substitutions, each moving the end three years further away. The extent provision was recast in the middle 1950s to state the Act's application to Jammu and Kashmir in the qualified form the constitutional settlement with that state required, detention for reasons connected with defence, foreign affairs, or the security of India reaching there while the general scheme did not. And the Act accumulated the procedural accretions of two decades, execution of orders, treatment of absconding persons, the maximum period machinery, that a working detention statute gathers as administrators and courts find its gaps. A reader who compares only the bill and the Act will conclude that nothing happened in February 1950 beyond enactment. A reader who compares the Act at birth and at death sees the truth: the statute's meaning was made afterwards, by a court that cut it, by executives that used it for purposes its mover had not named, and by legislatures that kept it alive by formula.

There is a federal dimension to the passage that the pillar article owns as architecture but that belongs here as politics. Preventive detention in the constitutional scheme sat astride the Union and Concurrent Lists: the Centre's competence ran to detention for reasons connected with defence, foreign affairs, and the security of India, while detention for the security of a state, public order, and essential supplies was a field in which states could also legislate. A central Act in this field was therefore not the only possible instrument, and the government of February 1950 knew that provincial Acts already covered much of the ground. The central Act did three things the provincial patchwork could not. It gave the Central Government its own detention power for the subjects at the heart of national security. It offered state governments a centrally drafted, constitutionally fitted template under which their own officers could act, which mattered at the moment when High Court judgments had thrown the older provincial texts into doubt. And it placed the political responsibility for the detention regime, in the Republic's first season, on the founding government at the Centre, where the argument about it would be conducted at the highest level and reported in full. The choice of a central Act was thus a choice about where the controversy would live, and the controversy obliged by living in Parliament, at every renewal, for twenty years.

## What the Act actually empowered

The text of the Preventive Detention Act 1950 was short, and its shortness is part of its history. Section 3, the operative provision, empowered the Central Government and the State Governments to direct the detention of a person where the government was satisfied that detention was necessary to prevent that person from acting in a manner prejudicial to the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, or the security of India, or to the security of the state or the maintenance of public order, or to the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. A further limb of the same provision addressed foreigners, whom the government could detain with a view to regulating their continued presence in India or arranging their expulsion. Every element of the drafting carried weight. The trigger was satisfaction, the subjective state of the detaining government, rather than proof of any act. The object was prevention, directed at what the person might do rather than at anything the person had done. And the grounds ranged from the security of India down to the continuity of essential supplies, a span wide enough to cover the saboteur, the communal organiser, the black marketeer, and the strike leader within one section.

The Act also armed the executive below the level of government itself. Detention orders could be made by officers to whom the power was delegated under the Act's scheme, subject to reporting and approval requirements that kept the order tethered, in form, to governmental responsibility. The grounds and the machinery around the order were meant to do the work that a charge sheet does in a prosecution, and the comparison exposes the design. In a prosecution the state must specify an offence, produce evidence, and survive cross-examination. Under the Act the state had to specify grounds, place them before the detenu and, on reference, before an Advisory Board, and survive a review that tested the grounds for relevance rather than for proof. That difference was not a defect in drafting. It was the entire point of a preventive statute, stated by its defenders as the reason preventive powers exist: the state claimed to be acting on information about future danger that could not be converted, in time or at all, into courtroom evidence.

### What did Section 7 require the detaining authority to hand over?

Section 7 required the authority making a detention order to communicate the grounds of the order to the person detained as soon as might be, and to give that person the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate government.

The duty in Section 7 reproduced Article 22's own words, and its practical content was contested from the first detentions. The grounds communicated had to be enough to let the detenu make an effective representation, since a representation against unstated reasons is no safeguard at all, yet the authority retained the constitutional permission to withhold facts whose disclosure it judged against the public interest. The distinction between grounds, which had to be communicated, and the facts and materials behind them, which could be withheld, became the seam along which most detention litigation ran. Detenus argued that skeletal grounds made the representation right illusory. Governments argued that the sources and particulars behind the grounds were precisely what prevention required them to protect. The courts of the period largely accepted the distinction, testing whether the grounds stated bore a rational connection to the statutory purposes rather than whether the underlying material justified the detention. A reader who grasps that distinction grasps the operating system of the 1950 Act: the safeguard policed the face of the order, the executive kept the file.

The Advisory Board machinery converted the three-month constitutional line into an administrative event. Where the government wished a detention to continue beyond three months, the case had to go before a Board constituted under the Act, drawn from persons who were, had been, or were qualified to be appointed as judges of a High Court, and the Board's report on whether sufficient cause existed governed whether the detention could lawfully continue. The government retained the power to revoke an order at any time, and detentions were in practice ended by revocation as well as by expiry. The Board system was the founders' principal institutional answer to the charge of unreviewed executive imprisonment, and its weakness was structural rather than accidental: it reviewed the government's satisfaction on the government's material, in proceedings that were not a trial and were not meant to be one, and its opinion bound the continuation of detention without converting the process into an adjudication of innocence or guilt.

Then there was Section 14, the provision around which the Act's legal history turned. Section 14 barred the disclosure, in any court, of the grounds of detention communicated to the detenu and of any representation the detenu had made against the order, and it restricted what could be placed before a court examining a detention. The government's logic for the provision was the logic of prevention carried to its conclusion: if the grounds could be produced and contested in court, the sources behind them would be exposed, informants would be endangered, and the preventive power would collapse into the prosecutorial power it existed to supplement. The constitutional difficulty was equally direct. Article 22 guaranteed the detenu the earliest opportunity of making a representation, and a provision that prevented a court from seeing either the grounds or the representation made judicial enforcement of that guarantee close to impossible at exactly the point where it mattered. Within weeks of the first detentions, that difficulty was before the Supreme Court in the case of a communist leader who had already spent years in custody, and the Court's answer removed Section 14 while leaving the rest of the Act standing, an outcome that satisfied neither the detenu nor the theory of either side.

## The first test: A. K. Gopalan and the judgment of 19 May 1950

A. K. Gopalan was among the most prominent communist leaders in the country, and he had been in detention since December 1947, held first under the provincial security legislation of Madras and then, after the Constitution and the new central Act, under the Preventive Detention Act 1950. From custody he moved the Supreme Court under Article 32 for a writ of habeas corpus, attacking the Act itself rather than merely the order against him. The challenge was framed across the fundamental rights chapter: the Act, he argued, contravened Articles 13, 19, 21, and 22, and his detention under it was therefore illegal. It was the first great constitutional case of the Republic, argued before a bench of six judges, Harilal Kania, Chief Justice, with Fazl Ali, Patanjali Sastri, Mehr Chand Mahajan, B. K. Mukherjea, and Sudhi Ranjan Das JJ, and decided on 19 May 1950, less than three months after the Act it examined. The speed is itself a historical fact worth registering: the detention regime was tested at its foundations almost before it had begun to operate, by the class of litigant, the detained political organiser, whom its critics had predicted would fill its cells.

The majority, speaking through opinions associated with Kania CJ and Patanjali Sastri, Mukherjea, and Das JJ, with Fazl Ali and Mahajan JJ dissenting on the principal question, upheld the Act, with one exception. The reasoning had three connected parts, and each part governed Indian constitutional law for a generation. First, the majority treated the fundamental rights as occupying distinct and largely self-contained fields. Article 19, with its freedoms of speech, assembly, association, and movement, protected the free citizen; a law of preventive detention operated on a person through the procedure Article 21 and Article 22 permitted, and did not have to survive Article 19's requirement of reasonableness. The rights, on this reading, were compartments, and a detention law was to be tested in the compartment built for it, which was Article 22. Second, the majority read the phrase "procedure established by law" in Article 21 as meaning a procedure established by enacted law, and declined to import the American doctrine of due process, under which courts examine the fairness and reasonableness of the procedure itself and, in its stronger form, of the law's substance. The framers, the majority reasoned, had known of due process and had chosen different words, and the choice had to be respected: if a competent legislature enacted the procedure, Article 21 was satisfied. Third, on preventive detention specifically, the majority treated Article 22 as the Constitution's own code for the subject, so that a law conforming to Article 22's requirements, its grounds, representation, Advisory Board, and maximum period, was not to be dismantled by arguments drawn from neighbouring articles.

### How did the silo doctrine in Gopalan treat Articles 19, 21 and 22?

The majority held that the three articles governed separate fields: a preventive detention law fell to be tested under Articles 21 and 22, the provisions written for deprivation of liberty and for detention, and not under Article 19, whose freedoms and reasonableness test applied to the citizen at liberty.

The compartment reading, later known through the metaphor of silos or water-tight compartments, was the load-bearing element of the judgment, because it decided which questions a court could ask about a detention law. If Article 19 applied, the state would have to justify detention as a reasonable restriction on the freedom of movement, and courts would weigh the proportionality of imprisonment without trial against the danger apprehended. If Article 19 did not apply, the court's inquiry narrowed to the design of Article 22: were the grounds communicated, was a representation permitted, did a Board stand behind any detention beyond three months, did the Act respect its own ceiling. Gopalan chose the narrower inquiry, and for twenty years the consequence held. Preventive detention law developed as a jurisprudence of compliance with Article 22's forms, grounds judged for relevance and mala fides rather than for sufficiency, while the larger question of whether imprisonment without trial could be a reasonable restriction on a free citizen was placed outside the courtroom's reach.

The exception was Section 14, and on Section 14 the Court was unanimous. All six judges held that the section, insofar as it prohibited a detained person from disclosing to a court the grounds on which the detention order had been made or the representation made against it, contravened Article 22's guarantee of the earliest opportunity of representation and was to that extent void. The reasoning exposes the difference between the majority's deference and its limit. The majority would not test the Act against Article 19 or against an unwritten standard of fairness, but it would hold the Act to the text of the article under which Parliament claimed the power, and Section 14 failed that test on its face, because a right to represent is emptied if the court enforcing it cannot be shown what was represented or on what grounds. The section was held severable: its invalidity did not infect the rest of the Act, the Act minus Section 14 stood, and Gopalan's detention was not illegal. The petition failed. The communist leader remained in detention, under a statute whose most protective-looking wound had been inflicted by the Court itself.

Fazl Ali and Mahajan JJ went further, and their disagreement marks the road the law would eventually take. In their view Section 12 of the Act, which authorised detention beyond the ordinary pattern in classes of cases prescribed under the Constitution's enabling clauses, was also invalid, and since it struck at the provision under which Parliament derived its competence, the detention could not stand. Fazl Ali's wider reasoning, in which on the Article 19 question he stood alone, rejected the compartments altogether. Preventive detention, he reasoned, directly abridged the freedom of movement guaranteed by Article 19, and a law abridging it had to answer for its reasonableness under that article; the fundamental rights were to be read together, as parts of one charter addressed to one citizen, and a procedure that deprived a person of liberty had to be judged against the charter as a whole. Mahajan J shared the conclusion on Section 12 without travelling the whole of Fazl Ali's road on Article 19. Any flat statement of the Gopalan split therefore misleads: the principal holding, that the Act apart from Section 14 was valid, carried four of the six judges against two; the destruction of Section 14 was the work of the full Court; and the proposition that Article 19 had no application to a detention law commanded five voices, Fazl Ali J dissenting alone. The judgment was a set of overlapping majorities, and its later history unpacked them one by one.

### What separated the majority from Fazl Ali in Gopalan?

The majority read the fundamental rights as separate codes, so a detention law needed only to satisfy Articles 21 and 22. Fazl Ali read the rights as one charter, so a law imprisoning a citizen without trial also had to answer Article 19 and justify itself as a reasonable restriction on freedom.

The displacement of Gopalan, when it came, came in the two stages the brief for this article identifies, and both stages belong to this history because both were arguments with the silo. In R. C. Cooper v Union of India in 1970, the bank nationalisation case, a bench of eleven judges rejected the premise that a law's validity was exhausted by assigning it to a single article's compartment, holding that the effect of state action on the citizen's rights, rather than the form of the action, determined which rights were engaged. In Maneka Gandhi v Union of India in 1978, the Court carried the point into Article 21 itself, holding that a procedure depriving a person of personal liberty had to be just, fair, and reasonable, and reading Articles 14, 19, and 21 as a connected whole. The doctrinal detail of those cases belongs to the articles that own them. What belongs here is the sequence: the silo doctrine was the 1950 Act's constitutional shelter, it held for the twenty years of the Act's life and a little beyond, and its dismantling after the Act had lapsed is the largest reason the judgment of May 1950 reads, to a lawyer trained later, like a dispatch from a different constitutional country. The statute died in 1969. Its shelter died in the decade after.

One further consequence of Gopalan is easy to miss and belongs to the passage history rather than to doctrine. The Court had struck down a section of the government's flagship security statute within three months of enactment, and the government's response was instructive in its restraint and its calculation. Parliament removed the original Section 14 from the Act by an amending Act later in 1950, regularising the statute book to match the judgment, and no attempt was made to restore the disclosure bar in another form. The episode cuts against two lazy readings at once. It cuts against the reading that the early Supreme Court was a rubber stamp, since the Court voided a provision the executive valued, unanimously, in the Republic's first season. And it cuts against the reading that judicial review tamed the detention regime, since the power the executive valued most, detention on satisfaction under Section 3, survived intact and was exercised for twenty years under the shelter the same judgment built.

## The surviving Act, and the detenu's road through it

The Act that emerged from Gopalan was not the Act that had entered the Court, and the difference structured the litigation of the next twenty years. With Article 19 placed outside the detention field and Article 21 satisfied by enacted procedure, the detenu's practical remedies ran through Article 22's own guarantees, and the case law of the 1950s is a record of detenus testing each guarantee in turn. The richest seam was the communication of grounds. In the Atma Ram case of 1951, the Supreme Court considered detention grounds under the Act and held that the grounds communicated had to be such as to enable the detenu to make an effective representation; grounds so vague that the detenu could not answer them failed the constitutional standard, and a detention resting on them could not stand. The principle mattered beyond its case. It meant that the executive's satisfaction, though subjective in form, had to leave a paper trail specific enough to be answered, and that courts would police the trail even while refusing to weigh the judgment recorded in it. Detention orders fell in the years that followed for vagueness, for grounds irrelevant to the statutory purposes, for delay in considering representations, and for the detaining authority's failure to apply its own mind, a body of doctrine that no reader of the bare Act would predict and that constitutes the judiciary's real, continuing amendment of the statute.

This is the sense in which the passage record and the judicial record of the 1950 Act must be read together. Parliament had written a short Act with wide grounds and thin procedure, trusting the Advisory Board and the expiry date. The Court, forbidden by its own Gopalan premises from asking whether the Act was reasonable, spent two decades asking whether the Act had been followed, and in the asking built a procedural common law of detention: grounds must be relevant and sufficiently particular, representations must be considered by the appropriate government itself and without unexplained delay, the Board reference must be made in time, the satisfaction must be that of the authority the Act named. None of this appears in the text of February 1950. All of it was law by the time the Act lapsed. A legislative history that treats the statute as finished at enactment would miss the process by which its meaning was actually settled, in habeas corpus petitions argued district by district and term by term, most of them brought by the class of detenus, political organisers, criminal suspects held without charge, and men caught in communal and scarcity enforcement, whose names never reached the law reports' headlines.

The detenu's road, reconstructed from that case law, ran as follows in prose. An order was made on the satisfaction of the government or of an officer empowered under the Act, and the person was arrested and held, in the Act's own vocabulary detained, without being produced before a magistrate on a charge. The grounds were to be communicated as soon as might be, and the detenu could represent against the order to the appropriate government, which was bound to consider the representation. Where detention was to run beyond three months, the file went to the Advisory Board, before which the detenu could appear in the manner the Act and the Board's procedure allowed, and the Board reported whether sufficient cause existed. If the Board found no sufficient cause, the government was bound to revoke the order and release the detenu. If it found cause, the detention could continue within the twelve-month ceiling, subject always to the government's power to revoke and to the courts' power, on habeas corpus, to test the order's formal and constitutional validity. The general position described here is drawn from the Act and its case law as legal information; it does not substitute for advice on an individual case, where the forum, the limitation discipline, and the state of the record are decisive and unforgiving.

Set out as a road, the system looks more protective than it felt from inside a cell, and the gap between the map and the experience is the honest place to end this section. The grounds a detenu received were often a paragraph of conclusions. The facts behind them were withholdable. The representation went to the government that had ordered the detention. The Board sat on the government's material. The courts would intervene for defects of form and relevance, not for the detenu's innocence. Every element of that experience had been visible in February and September of the founding debates, had been named by the Act's opponents in almost these terms, and had been accepted by its defenders as the price of a preventive rather than a punitive system. The 1950 settlement was not a failure of foresight. It was a foresight that priced other people's liberty and found the price acceptable, and a history that obscures that clarity, in either direction, has not understood the record it claims to teach.

## The Act at work, and the constituency it built

A legislative history that stops at passage mistakes the statute for its first chapter. The Preventive Detention Act 1950 lived for twenty years, and its working life explains why legislatures that had voted for one year of it found reasons, Parliament after Parliament, to vote for more. The Act's first detentions fell where the government had said they would fall: on communist organisers and cadres in the regions of insurgency and industrial militancy, of whom Gopalan was the emblem rather than the exception. The communist movement of the early 1950s was the government's stated target, and after the Communist Party abandoned the insurrectionary line and contested the first general elections, the population of detenus changed character. Through the middle and later 1950s, and into the 1960s, the grounds that did the daily work were increasingly the public order and essential supplies limbs of Section 3: communal organisers in districts prone to riot, habitual offenders and gang figures whom the police could not convict, hoarders and black marketeers during scarcity, smugglers on the coasts and borders, and participants in agitations that governments judged likely to turn violent. State governments, which made the great majority of detention orders under the Act, built the power into ordinary district administration. A district magistrate's satisfaction became a standing instrument of local order, used at festival seasons, during strikes, and ahead of visits and elections, in a rhythm that had little to do with the national emergencies of the 1950 debates.

This migration of use is the single most instructive feature of the Act's life, because it vindicates and embarrasses both sides of the founding argument in sequence. It vindicates the government's defenders in one respect: the feared mass political imprisonment of the opposition as a class did not become the Act's ordinary mode, and the first decades of the Republic were not governed from prison. It embarrasses them in another: a power sold to Parliament as the minimum evil for a named emergency became, without any single decision that can be pointed to, a routine supplement to policing, aimed at categories of person, the hoarder, the goonda, the communal hothead, whose detention no emergency explained. And it vindicates the critics' deepest prediction, which was never mainly about scale. The prediction was about habituation: that the executive would organise its expectations around the power, that each renewal debate would be conducted in the shadow of detentions already being made under the Act, and that a Parliament asked to end the power would in fact be asked to disarm serving administrations midstream, which legislatures are reluctant to do. The renewal record is that prediction working itself out in votes.

The scholarly verdict of the period tracked the same shift. David Bayley, the American scholar who studied the Act's first fourteen years most closely, concluded by the middle 1960s that the Preventive Detention Act could no longer be called a temporary expedient and had to be acknowledged as a permanent part of India's democratic experiment, whatever its title page said. Granville Austin, the historian of the Constitution, treated the detention regime as one of the founding settlement's standing compromises with its own libertarian text. These were not opposition pamphleteers; they were the record's careful readers, and their shared point was descriptive rather than denunciatory: by the time a decade had passed, the question before Parliament at each renewal was no longer whether India should have preventive detention, but whether any government would accept the political cost of being the one that let it lapse. That reframing, from principle to cost, is the ratchet at work, and the extension timeline shows its teeth.

## The states as the Act's second legislature

Most accounts of the 1950 Act are written from the Centre outward, because the Centre drafted it and the Supreme Court judged it, but the Act's working life was lived in the states, and its history is incomplete until that life is given its weight. The constitutional design invited it. Public order and essential supplies were the daily business of state governments, the Concurrent List entry under which much of the Act operated was shared ground, and Section 3 placed the power in the hands of State Governments alongside the Centre. In practice the states also kept their own security legislation in being for much of the period, so that a detenu in Madras or Bengal or Bombay might be held under the central Act, under a state Act of similar ancestry, or successively under both, and the choice of instrument was an administrative decision taken at the level of the district. The central Act's contribution to this landscape was standardisation: one text, fitted to Article 22, construed by one Supreme Court, replacing a patchwork whose variations had begun to embarrass governments in the High Courts.

Delegation carried the power further down. Under the Act's scheme, state governments could empower senior district officers, district magistrates and commissioners of police in the great cities, to make detention orders themselves, with the order then requiring the state government's approval within a short statutory period if it was to continue. The safeguard logic was the logic of the whole Act in miniature: speed at the point of arrest, responsibility at the point of review. Its practical consequence was to make preventive detention a standing resource of district administration rather than an exceptional act of government, and the district level is where the Act's character was formed. A district magistrate facing a riot season, a grain riot, or a strike wave held in Section 3 a power that required no warrant, no charge, and no evidence fit for a court, only satisfaction, grounds in writing, and survival of the Advisory Board's review at the third month. That officers used the power in those terms is not an accusation against them; it is a description of what it means to hand such a power to the level of government that answers for order street by street. The founders in Delhi designed a national instrument for national dangers. The districts inherited it, and the history of the Act is largely the history of that inheritance, which is why the renewal debates in Parliament, conducted in the language of national security, so often proceeded in ignorance of the use that was actually deciding the Act's reputation in the country.

## Who was detained, and the politics the detentions made

The detentions of the Act's first seasons fell into a pattern that the parliamentary argument had sketched in advance, and tracing who was actually held is the surest way to see which sketch was accurate. The government had drawn its target narrowly in words: not belief, but disruption. The Act's grounds, however, were not drawn narrowly at all, and a preventive system takes its population from its grounds rather than from its speeches. The insurgent cadres of Telangana and the organisers of the communist movement elsewhere were the first and most publicised detenus, held alongside men detained under the surviving state legislation, and among them were figures of national standing, Gopalan at their head, whose continued imprisonment gave the opposition a martyr with a name, a constituency, and eventually a Supreme Court citation. Around that political core, the Act and its state companions held the population that preventive systems always hold: men against whom the police had suspicion without proof, organisers of strikes and peasant movements in districts where agrarian conflict ran hot, participants in communal disturbances detained in advance of festivals and processions, and traders accused of hoarding and black marketing in years when food scarcity made the grain trade a branch of public order. Published figures for the Act's use were fought over at the renewals, with the opposition reading the totals as proof of a police state in embryo and the government reading the same totals as proof of burdens carried, and the figures themselves shifted with the counting, since detentions under state Acts, detentions under the central Act, and detentions converted into prosecutions were counted differently by different speakers. The pattern beneath the numbers is what a careful history can assert: the political detenu of the founding debates became, across the decade, one figure among several in a population increasingly composed of the administratively troublesome.

The politics the detentions made were as consequential as the detentions themselves. Every prominent detention created a habeas corpus petition, and every petition created a public occasion on which the government's grounds, or its refusal to state them, were argued in open court and reported. The communist movement, driven from insurrection toward electoral competition in the early 1950s, made the release of detenus and the repeal of the detention laws a standing plank, and carried the argument into the first Lok Sabha, where Gopalan and his colleagues renewed it from the opposition benches with the authority of men describing a system they had inhabited. The government, for its part, learned to price detention politically: orders against figures of standing attracted litigation and debate, while the Act's quieter use at district level attracted neither, and the distribution of the Act's use across the decade reflects that pricing as faithfully as it reflects any theory of security. A legislative history should record this without cynicism and without innocence. The Act was passed against named dangers. It was used, increasingly, where its use was cheap. The distance between those two sentences is the distance the renewal debates existed to argue about, and mostly did not.

## What the Act did not contain

The absences in a statute are part of its legislative history, and the 1950 Act's absences were choices, several of them visible in the founding debates. The Act contained no compensation for the person detained and released without charge, however long the detention and however thin the grounds proved on examination; the detenu's remedy was release, and the state's error, where error was shown, cost the state nothing. It contained no duty to lay the record of its own use before Parliament, no annual accounting of orders made, grounds invoked, Boards' reports, and revocations, so that the legislature which had to judge the Act at each renewal judged it on figures supplied by the executive in answers and debate rather than on a statutory return. It fixed no tighter standard for the communication of grounds than the Constitution's own phrase, as soon as may be, leaving the Atma Ram doctrine to do, years later and case by case, the specifying work that a careful drafting stage might have done in a week. It provided the detenu no right to legal representation before the Advisory Board of the kind a party enjoys in court, treating the Board's proceeding as an administrative review rather than an adversarial hearing, a classification the government defended as inherent in prevention and the critics attacked as the system's tell. And it contained no mechanism by which the detenu could test the material behind the grounds, the withheld facts being withheld by design. Each absence was consistent with the theory of prevention the government had defended in February 1950: a system for acting on unprovable danger cannot carry the furniture of a trial without becoming one. Each absence was also, as the critics had warned, a standing invitation to carelessness, since a power whose errors are costless and whose record is unreported will be used at the level of its convenience rather than at the level of its justification. The Act's twenty years supplied instances in both directions, and the fair conclusion is not that the absences were oversights but that they were the price schedule of the founding choice, printed in the statute for anyone who read it whole.

## The temporary-statute ratchet: the extension timeline

The findable artifact of this article is the extension timeline of the 1950 Act, because the whole argument of the statute's life is visible in it at a glance: a one-year grant, renewed first in short steps and then in a settled three-year rhythm, each renewal defended in nearly identical language, until the rhythm stopped, not with a repeal debate won by the critics, but with a Parliament that simply did not renew.

| Stage | Instrument and sponsor | What changed | The circumstances Parliament was shown |
|---|---|---|---|
| February 1950 | The Preventive Detention Act, 1950 (Act IV of 1950), moved by Sardar Patel | Detention power created with a life of one year, expiring 1 April 1951 | Insurgency in Telangana, post-Partition disorder, provincial detention laws in constitutional doubt |
| Later in 1950 | Amending Act following the Gopalan judgment | The original Section 14, struck down by the Supreme Court, was omitted from the Act | The Court had held the disclosure bar void under Article 22, unanimously, on 19 May 1950 |
| 1951 to 1952 | Successive short continuances, then a two-year extension in 1952 | The one-year horizon abandoned; substantive amendments adjusted the Act in its first years | The first general elections approached and were held while the Act operated |
| 1954 | Extension defended by Home Minister K. N. Katju | Three-year continuance to 31 December 1957; the three-year rhythm set | The objects statement argued the Act had proved an effective instrument for law and order |
| 1957 | Extension defended by Home Minister G. B. Pant | Three-year continuance to 31 December 1960 | The objects statement said experience showed the powers should continue for a further three years |
| 1960 | Continuance Act | Expiry moved to 31 December 1963 | Renewal had become a settled item of parliamentary business |
| 1963 | The Preventive Detention (Continuance) Bill, 1963 | Expiry moved to 31 December 1966 | The bill's own annexure restated the expiring date and extended it by the standard three years |
| 1966 | The Preventive Detention (Continuance) Bill, 1966, defended by Vidya Charan Shukla | Final three-year extension, by Act 48 of 1966, to 31 December 1969 | The objects statement repeated the formula of 1954: an effective instrument, essential to continue |
| End of 1969 | No continuance enacted | The Act ceased to have effect on 31 December 1969, as its own first section provided | The lapse left no central preventive detention Act until MISA in 1971 |

Read as a narrative rather than as a table, the timeline tells a story about language. The statements of objects and reasons that accompanied the continuance bills of 1954, 1957, and 1966, which survive in the parliamentary bill papers, repeat a formula so stable it can be quoted across the decades: the Act was due to expire on the coming 31 December; experience in the working of the Act had shown it to be an effective instrument in the maintenance of law and order; it was accordingly considered essential that the powers be continued for a further period of three years. Katju's statement for the 1954 extension added one revealing sentence, proposing three years rather than one partly so that the Act could be re-examined after the general election of 1956 and 1957, as though the electoral calendar, and not the emergency, had become the natural unit of the Act's life. Pant's statement of December 1957 and Shukla's of November 1966 are exercises in administrative continuity: no new emergency is named, no sunset reasoning is offered, and the case rests on the Act's usefulness to those working it. A power that must be justified afresh each time will produce fresh justifications. A power justified by formula has already become permanent in everything except the statute book's punctuation.

The counting of the extensions illustrates why this article has treated the sequence rather than a single number as the fact. Secondary accounts commonly say the Act was extended seven times, and the count is defensible if what is being counted is the series of continuance measures that carried the Act from its first expiry to its last. But the count changes with the counting rule: the earliest renewals of 1951 and 1952, the substantive amendments interleaved with them, and the amending Act that removed Section 14 can each be included or excluded, and accounts that include every parliamentary return to the Act reach a higher figure, while the scholarly count of re-approval occasions between February 1950 and December 1963 alone stands at eight. What no counting rule alters is the structure: between 1950 and 1966, no Parliament allowed the Act to die, every government of the period sought its continuance, and the opposition to it, though vocal at every renewal, never assembled the votes to end it. The number is a detail. The unanimity of governments is the fact.

For the researcher assembling this record, the sources are scattered in an instructive way: the bare text and its amendment footnotes in the statute book, the statements of objects in the bill papers of the Lok Sabha, the debate reports, the Hindustan Times account of the first passage, and the headnotes of the Gopalan report. Keeping such a file private and searchable is not a clerical preference but a professional one for anyone working on detention history, and the working method described in [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources and story notes](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) shows how a researcher holding interview material beside documentary extracts can keep the whole file on a single device under the researcher's own control.

## The renewal debates as a genre

By the time of the great renewal debates of the middle 1950s, the argument had settled into a genre with fixed parts, and the genre itself is evidence. The Home Minister of the day, K. N. Katju in 1954 and G. B. Pant in 1957, opened with the record of the Act's working and an appeal to administrative necessity, speaking the language of prevention itself: disorder was to be nipped before it ripened, and the executive should not be required to wait, evidence in hand, while communications were cut and crowds gathered. The opposition replied with the philosophy of the founding argument, refreshed by a decade of the Act's use. A contemporary compilation of the detention debates preserves the opposition's pitch in terms that had changed little since 1950: that the government's impatience with legality was itself the danger, that no French statesman would dream of keeping a state of siege power on the ordinary statute book, that the individual human being, for whom all social institutions exist, was being subordinated to an abstraction called the nation, and that governments reach for detention on slight provocation because it is easier than the leisurely process of law. The Home Minister's answer, preserved in the same record, was that every kind of trouble was to be nipped early, which is the preventive principle stated without embarrassment. Neither side was performing. Both were describing, accurately, the system each preferred, one in which the executive is trusted to act on suspicion within fences, and one in which it is forbidden to act until suspicion has become proof.

The genre had a third voice, neither ministerial nor oppositional in the party sense, which the passage of time made more prominent: the voice of members who had themselves been detained, or whose parties' workers had been. A. K. Gopalan entered the Lok Sabha at the first general elections and became one of the House's leading opposition figures, so that the man whose name the Act's founding judgment carries spent the renewal decades arguing against the Act's continuance from the floor of the House that kept renewing it. The biographical fact concentrates the history. The Republic's detention debate was never an argument between the state and an abstraction called the detenu. It was an argument among parliamentarians who knew one another, some of whom had been imprisoned by the regime whose laws they were renewing, conducted under rules that gave the imprisoned of one decade the floor in the next. That the renewals nonetheless continued, session after session, measures the strength of the administrative case as governments of the period felt it. That the argument nonetheless continued, in the same terms, for twenty years, measures the refusal of the losing side to let the question be treated as settled. Both measurements belong to the record, and a series committed to stating each side at its strongest should end the point there, with the genre intact: necessity, ancestry, habituation, and the detenu's empty chair, debated on schedule every three years like a clause in the Act itself, which in effect it was.

## The lapse, and what the lapse did not end

The Act died the way it had lived, by the operation of its own first section. When no continuance was enacted in 1969, the Preventive Detention Act 1950 ceased to have effect on 31 December 1969, saving things done under it before that date. The manner of the ending repays attention, because it was not the ending the critics of 1950 had imagined fighting for. No repeal motion carried the day against a resisting government. Instead, the political weather of the late 1960s, a Congress split, a Parliament in which the government's majority could not be assumed, and two decades of accumulated argument about the Act, made another three-year continuance a bill no ministry chose to spend its strength on. The ratchet, which had worked by making renewal the path of least resistance, finally met conditions in which renewal was the expensive option. The lesson generalises, and it is one of the two or three durable lessons this article exists to teach: sunset clauses do not restrain a power while renewal is cheap, and they do restrain it, sometimes decisively, when the politics of a single session turn. The expiry date written into Patel's Act in February 1950 did nothing for nineteen years and then, in the twentieth, did everything.

And then the qualification, without which the lapse becomes a fairy tale. The end of the central Act did not end preventive detention in India. State public safety and maintenance of order legislation continued to authorise detentions in several states, and the interval at the Centre lasted only until 1971, when [the Maintenance of Internal Security Act took the 1950 Act's place in the central armoury](/2010/07/01/misa-1971-emergency-detention/) under a government facing war, refugee influx, and insurgency, and went on to a career beside which the 1950 Act reads as a study in restraint. The line of succession continued afterwards to [the National Security Act 1980, the operative central detention statute of the later settlement](/2010/08/01/national-security-act-1980-explained/), which dropped the pretence of a short life altogether. The namable claim of this article, the temporary-statute ratchet, is precisely this pattern stated generally: preventive detention entered Indian law as an emergency measure with an expiry date, and every successor statute repeated the pattern of temporary justification and indefinite renewal, so that the expiry of any one Act has never reduced the total quantum of detention power available to the Indian state. The 1950 Act is the pattern's first instance and its clearest, because its paperwork, the one-year promise, the formulaic renewals, the quiet lapse, is complete and can be read whole.

## The archive in which this history lives

A legislative history is only as good as its archive, and this statute's archive is unusually complete, which is one reason it can bear the weight this series places on it. The parliamentary layer survives in the bill papers and debate reports: the text of the bill and of each continuance bill, with the statements of objects and reasons in which Katju, Pant, and Shukla made the renewal case in their nearly interchangeable language, and the extracts of the Act annexed to those bills, in which the moving terminal date can be watched travelling from 1954 to 1957 to 1960 to 1963 to 1966 and finally to 1969 in the first section's own words. The judicial layer survives in the Supreme Court Reports, whose headnote to Gopalan preserves the overlapping majorities with a precision that later summaries flatten, and in the run of habeas corpus decisions through which the courts built the procedural law of the Act. The political layer survives in contemporary reportage, of which the Hindustan Times account of the February passage, reprinted in the Collected Works of Sardar Patel, is the single most useful document, since it preserves in a few paragraphs the government's motive, the Attorney General's assurance, the mover's framing, and the temperature of the debate. And the scholarly layer, Bayley's study of the Act's policy across its first fourteen years and Austin's account of the constitutional settlement, supplies the detachment that participants could not: Bayley's conclusion that the temporary expedient had become a permanent part of the democratic experiment was published while the Act still had years to run, and it remains the sentence against which every renewal debate after 1963 has to be read.

Reading that archive in the order of its creation produces the discipline this article has tried to practise. The documents of February 1950 show men who believed they were conceding the minimum. The documents of the renewal years show the concession becoming a formula. The documents of 1969 show the formula failing, for reasons no document of 1950 foresaw. A history assembled from any one layer misleads: from the first, it becomes a story of reluctant necessity; from the second, of bureaucratic momentum; from the third, of liberty's quiet victory. The whole archive gives a less comfortable and more useful story, in which a power honestly justified was honestly opposed, survived both its justification and its opposition by becoming ordinary, and ended not with its refutation but with its abandonment. That is the biography of most emergency legislation in most democracies, and the reason the Preventive Detention Act 1950, a short statute from a single month of a founding year, rewards the length at which this series treats it.

## The gotcha and the record

No statute in this series attracts more sloganised history than this one, and the slogans come, as the brief for this article warned, from every direction. The first slogan holds that Nehru and Patel created the detention regime that later governments abused, and that the Emergency of 1975 was the 1950 Act's true child. The record gives this claim a kernel and then refuses it the rest. The kernel is institutional: the founders did build the channel, Article 22, the satisfaction standard, the Advisory Board pattern, and later governments did pour worse conduct through channels the founders built. The refusal is factual: the 1950 Act was not the Emergency's instrument, having lapsed five years before it; the Emergency worked through MISA and the Defence of India framework, amended for the purpose; and the distance between Patel's Act, with its one-year face, its twelve-month ceiling, its functioning Advisory Boards, and a Court that struck its worst clause within three months, and the detention regime of 1975 and 1976 is not a nuance but the whole difference between a fenced power and an unfenced one. Conflating them flatters the later regime and libels the earlier argument.

The counter-slogan holds that the 1950 Act was a narrow, reluctant, well-fenced measure, nothing like what followed, and therefore beyond criticism. The record refuses this one too, on the evidence of the renewal decades. A measure renewed by formula for twenty years, used by district administrations as routine equipment, and defended in Parliament by appeal to its usefulness rather than to any emergency is not well described as reluctant, whatever its mover's sincerity in February 1950. The counter-claim also has to absorb the critics' strongest point, which the passage record preserves: that the breadth of Section 3, reaching from the security of India to the maintenance of supplies, was chosen knowingly, and that the choice guaranteed the migration of use from insurgents to ordinary disorder. Both slogans fail because both treat the Act as a single moment. It was twenty years of moments, and they do not point the same way.

The third slogan is the deepest, the claim that the Constituent Assembly's acceptance of Article 22 was the original sin from which Indian detention law descends. As history, this at least aims at the right target, because the constitutional settlement of September 1949 is where the real decision was taken, and the debates show the Assembly taking it with open eyes and stated reluctance. As analysis, it still oversimplifies, because it cannot explain the variation the settlement permitted: the same Article 22 coexisted with a Supreme Court that voided Section 14, with legislatures that let the central Act lapse in 1969, and with the far harsher regimes built later under the same constitutional text by different political choices. A provision that permits both restraint and abuse is the occasion for the outcomes, not their cause, and the causes lie in the politics of each decade, which is why this series insists on legislative history rather than constitutional demonology. Whether any framer opposed the settlement is a question the record answers plainly: yes. Members spoke against detention in the Assembly debates, members voted against the bill's philosophy in the passage and renewal debates, and the dissenting tradition inside Parliament runs unbroken from 1949 through every continuance to 1966. The opposition was never a secret and never won. Both halves of that sentence belong in any honest account.

Nehru's position, on which the first slogan depends, also deserves the record rather than the legend. The Act was a measure of Nehru's government, piloted by his Home Minister, and Nehru neither disowned it nor pretended it was other than his government's considered act. His recorded utterances across the period combine acceptance of the necessity his government asserted with a distaste for detention without trial that he expressed in terms his critics quoted back at him, and the combination is not hypocrisy but something more ordinary and more instructive: a Prime Minister who found the instrument repellent in principle and indispensable in office, and whose government renewed it accordingly. Patel's case requires no such reconstruction, because his February defence survives in contemporary report: a named emergency, a discriminated target, a one-year limit. The founders were not hypocrites acting in bad faith, and they were not innocents either. They were experienced administrators who had weighed liberty against order in a burning season, chosen order with limits, and trusted the limits. The limits were real. So was the choosing.

## Testing the three objections against twenty years

The passage debate left three objections on the record, and two decades of the Act's life constitute the evidence on which each can now be judged. The first objection was ancestry: that a detention law on the colonial model would reproduce colonial habits, an executive governing Indians by suspicion in the manner Indians had been governed. The evidence supports the objection in form and complicates it in substance. In form, the Act's grounds, machinery, and administrative rhythm descended directly from the instruments of the Raj, and district practice under it would have been recognisable to the officers of 1939. In substance, the descent was interrupted by exactly the two innovations the Republic added: a Supreme Court that voided the Act's most colonial clause within three months and policed its procedures thereafter, and a legislature that had to renew the power in public, against opponents who included its own former detenus. Colonial detention answered to no electorate and no fundamental rights chapter. The objection therefore lands as a warning that was half heeded: the instrument was inherited, the accountability was new, and the Act's history is the contest between the two.

The second objection was the burden of proof: that preventive detention exists to imprison where the state cannot persuade a court, and that its true function would be revealed by its use against the unconvictable rather than the dangerous. The evidence here runs hardest against the Act's defenders in the long term. The early detentions fitted the government's account, organisers of an insurrectionary movement whose activities were matters of record. The later population, habitual offenders, suspected hoarders, communal hotheads held in advance of trouble, fitted the critics' account, men held because prosecution was inconvenient, slow, or doomed. Between the two populations there was no moment of decision, and that is the critics' deepest vindication: the migration required no amendment, no debate, and no one's signature, because Section 3 had been drafted widely enough to hold both populations from the first day. A government that drafts for the saboteur and bequeaths the text to the district has, in any honest accounting, drafted for the district.

The third objection was habituation: that the temporary would become permanent by renewal. On the mechanism, the evidence is a complete vindication, set out in the extension timeline: seven continuances by the common count, formulaic justifications, no government's hand ever forced. On the conclusion, the evidence contains the objection's one great surprise, which is 1969. Permanence turned out to be a political condition rather than a legal destiny, and when the parliamentary arithmetic changed, the sunset clause that had slept for nineteen years operated exactly as written. The critics of 1950 were right about the ratchet and wrong about its irreversibility, and their being wrong is as instructive as their being right, because it locates the real safeguard where the founders, at their best, had located it too: not in the text of the power, nor in the promise of its mover, but in a legislature willing, on a particular day, to let a law die. The Act's final service to the Republic's understanding of itself may be that demonstration, purchased at the price of twenty years.

## What the passage record predicted

Template and hindsight agree on one test for a legislative history: what did the passage record, read carefully at the time, predict about how the Act would work? Read in February 1950, the record predicted the Act's career with uncomfortable completeness. The one-year expiry predicted the renewals, because a government that had defined the threat as disruption rather than as a datable event had given itself no criterion by which the threat could ever be declared over. The breadth of Section 3 predicted the migration of use, because a power that reaches essential supplies will be used for essential supplies long after the insurgency fades. The exclusion of Article 19 by constitutional design predicted the narrowness of judicial review, and Gopalan, three months later, confirmed the prediction almost to the letter. The Advisory Board machinery predicted its own ambiguous record, real review of the face of orders, no review of the merits of satisfaction. And the government's own framing, the minimum evil, predicted the politics of every renewal, because a measure conceded to be an evil must be re-conceded at every extension, and the formulaic objects statements of 1954, 1957, and 1966 are what that ritual looks like when the concession has become a habit. The one thing the record did not predict was the ending: that the power would lapse not because its critics persuaded a majority but because a fractured Parliament made renewal the costly course. Legislative historians should keep that unpredicted ending in view. It is the standing reminder that sunset clauses are political instruments, not mechanical ones, and that their force depends on the price of renewal in a particular session.

The verdict on the Act itself follows from its whole record rather than from its first month. As a piece of drafting, it was a competent transfer of a colonial instrument into a constitutional frame, and its fences, the representation right enforced in Gopalan, the Board reference, the twelve-month ceiling, the expiry date, were genuine features that later statutes have not always bothered to reproduce. As a piece of statecraft, it solved the problem of February 1950 and created the problem of the following twenty years, which is the standard biography of emergency legislation everywhere and was predicted, on the record, by the people who voted against it. As a piece of history, its importance is that it was first: the first working out, in statute, judgment, and renewal debate, of the Republic's permanent negotiation between the liberty it had proclaimed and the order it intended to keep. Every Indian detention statute since has been a renegotiation of the settlement struck in five hours in February 1950, by a legislature that had written the fundamental rights the previous winter and saw no contradiction, only a hierarchy of urgencies, in what it did next. Understanding that legislature's reasoning, at its strongest, and its opponents' reasoning, at theirs, is the difference between knowing this history and merely quoting it.

## The first year, and the precedent it set

The Act's first year compressed its whole biography into twelve months, and it is worth following that year as a unit. The statute began operating in the last days of February 1950, against the insurgency and disorder its mover had named. Within weeks the habeas corpus petitions began, and by 19 May the Supreme Court had delivered the Gopalan judgment, voiding Section 14 and sheltering the rest. Later in 1950, Parliament passed the amending Act that removed the dead section from the book, a small legislative act with a large meaning: the first Parliament of the Republic had accepted, without a fight it could easily have picked, that a court could cut a section out of its security legislation and that the correct response was to tidy the statute rather than to defy the judgment or pack the bench. Before the first year expired, the government was preparing the Act's continuance, converting the emergency grant into a going concern and setting the question that every later Parliament would inherit: not whether detention should exist, but on what terms its existence should be prolonged this time. Patel himself died in December 1950, within the Act's first year, and the statute he had piloted as a one-year necessity passed into the hands of successors who had not made his speeches and were not bound by his framing, a transfer that the renewal debates make visible, since the ministers who defended the Act in 1954 and 1957 spoke of administrative effectiveness where Patel had spoken of a minimum evil reluctantly accepted. The precedent the first year set was therefore double. Institutionally, it established that detention legislation would be litigated, pruned, amended, and renewed inside the constitutional forms, which is not nothing; the century's worse detention regimes are marked by the abandonment of exactly those forms. Substantively, it established that the forms would accommodate the power indefinitely, provided each accommodation arrived in the correct procedural dress. Both halves of the precedent were available to every later government, and the history of Indian detention law since 1950 can be written as the story of which half each government chose to use.

The first year also fixed the cast of the long argument. The government benches held the men who had written the Constitution and now administered the state it created. The opposition held the heirs of the movement those men had led, together with socialists and communists who measured the new state against its own professions. The courts held a Constitution whose detention clauses they had not written but had immediately to construe. And outside the institutions stood the detenus themselves, whose petitions, representations, and eventual releases supplied the human material from which doctrine was made. Every renewal debate, every habeas corpus argument, and every scholarly treatment of the Act across the following twenty years drew its personnel and its positions from the alignments of that first year, which is why a legislative history of this statute can be organised, without distortion, as the biography of a single year's decisions working themselves out over two decades.

## The counterfactual: India in 1950 without the Act

Legislative history earns its conclusions partly by testing the road not taken, and the road not taken in February 1950 is unusually easy to reconstruct, because its materials were all in place. Without a central Act, the detentions of the founding years would have continued under the provincial public safety statutes, challenged province by province under the new Constitution, with High Courts striking clauses and governments amending in haste, and the law of preventive detention would have developed as a patchwork of provincial texts and provincial judgments rather than as one national statute construed by one Supreme Court. The insurgency would still have been fought, since no serious reading of 1950 supports the belief that the Indian state would have left Telangana to the squads for want of a detention law; the fighting would have been done with the military, the police, and the ordinary criminal law used at its stretch, as much of it was in any case. What would have been missing is the thing the Act actually supplied: a single, Article 22-shaped instrument, a founding judgment defining the constitutional space, and a parliamentary ritual of renewal in which the detention power was debated, on the record, by the whole political nation every few years. The counterfactual therefore cuts against both mythologies at once. Against the government's mythology, it shows that the Act was not the thin line between order and collapse, since the state had other instruments and used them. Against the critics' mythology, it shows that refusing the Act would not have produced a detention-free republic, only a less visible, less uniform, and less debated detention practice, conducted under statutes nobody had fitted to the Constitution and nobody had to renew. The choice of February 1950 was not between detention and liberty. It was between detention in the open, under a text, with a clock, and detention in the provinces, under inherited texts, without one. The founders chose the open version. The twenty years that followed are the evidence of what that choice was worth, and the evidence is genuinely mixed, which is the most that honest history can ever say for a founding compromise.

One further element of the counterfactual deserves record, because it disciplines the temptation to judge 1950 by the standards of quieter decades. The men who enacted the Act had themselves been governed, within living memory, by a state that detained them without any constitution at all, and several of them had organised a national movement from inside that experience. Their willingness to build a detention power into their own republic was not the product of unfamiliarity with its abuses. It was the product of a judgment, formed in the movement and hardened in office, that a state which refuses itself every preventive instrument will lose control of events to men who refuse themselves nothing, and that the remedy for abusive power is not the absence of power but its fencing, its publication, and its periodic return to a legislature for judgment. The subsequent history shows the fences bending and the judgment ritual decaying into formula, and a reader is entitled to conclude that the founders misjudged their successors. A reader is not entitled to conclude that the founders had not thought about the question, because the debates of 1949 and 1950 are precisely the record of their thinking, objection by objection, safeguard by safeguard, in a detail that many later legislatures, renewing the power by rote, did not trouble to match.

## The errors this history attracts, corrected

Because the Act is a fixture of political argument, it accumulates confident errors, and a reference article earns its keep by removing them. The first is dating: the Act is sometimes assigned to 1949, as though the founding legislature had armed itself before the Constitution existed. It did not. The detention debates of 1949 were the Constituent Assembly's debates on Article 22; the statute is of February 1950, Act IV of 1950, and the distinction is not pedantry, because the whole controversy turns on the sequence of a rights charter followed within weeks by a detention law. Compress the sequence and the story's actual difficulty, that the same body did both in that order with its eyes open, disappears. The second error is the elected Parliament: the Act was passed by the Provisional Parliament, the Constituent Assembly in its legislative capacity, before the first general elections, and accounts that give the detention power a democratic mandate from 1950 are anachronistic, just as accounts that treat the enacting body as ignorant of the Constitution are false to its identity. The third error concerns Gopalan, which is often summarised as the case that upheld the Act. It upheld most of the Act, struck down Section 14 unanimously, released nobody, and built the doctrinal shelter under which the Act then lived for twenty years; a summary that omits any one of those four elements is not a summary but a slogan, and the four pull in different directions, which is why the case resists the uses to which it is put. The fourth error is the confusion with MISA. The 1950 Act had lapsed at the end of 1969; MISA was a statute of 1971, harsher in design and catastrophic in its Emergency career, and attributing MISA's record to the 1950 Act, or the 1950 Act's relative restraint to MISA, corrupts the history of both and dissolves the very distinctions, expiry dates, ceilings, functioning courts, on which any serious judgment of either statute depends.

A fifth error is subtler and common in otherwise careful writing: the description of the Act as having been struck down, repealed, or abolished. It was none of those things. It was amended, pruned by a judgment, renewed seven times by the common count, and then allowed to cease under its own terms on a date its first section had carried, in one substituted form or another, since its enactment. The difference between repeal and lapse is the difference between a legislature deciding against a law and a legislature declining to decide for it, and the politics of the two are distinct enough that the whole interpretation of 1969 turns on keeping them apart. The government of that year did not lose a vote on preventive detention. It never called one. The Act died of an unheld division, and its death certificate, the first section's terminal date of 31 December 1969, had been printed, in successive editions, by the very Parliaments that kept postponing it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why was the Preventive Detention Act passed weeks after the Constitution?

The Act answered an immediate legal and political emergency. From 26 January 1950 every detention in India had to survive the new fundamental rights chapter, and High Court judgments had thrown the validity of the provincial public safety Acts, under which most detentions were being made, into doubt. At the same time the government faced an armed communist insurgency in Telangana, post-Partition disorder, communal violence, and food scarcity. A single central Act, drafted to fit Article 22, gave the Centre and the states a detention power with considered constitutional footing, and the government chose speed over deliberation, enacting it within a month of the Constitution.

### Q: Who piloted the Preventive Detention Bill in 1950?

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, the Deputy Prime Minister and Home Minister, moved and piloted the bill in the Provisional Parliament in February 1950. Patel presented the measure as a reluctant necessity, argued that the government's fight was with disruption rather than with belief, and rested heavily on the bill's temporary character. The Attorney General, M. C. Setalvad, supported the bill in the House by advising that it accorded with the spirit and the letter of the Constitution. The bill was passed after a heated debate of five hours and became Act IV of 1950, dated 25 February 1950.

### Q: What did the Preventive Detention Act 1950 authorise?

Section 3 authorised the Central Government and the State Governments to order the detention of a person whose activities they were satisfied needed to be prevented as prejudicial to the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, or the security of India, or to the security of the state, the maintenance of public order, or the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. Foreigners could be detained with a view to regulating their presence or arranging expulsion. Detention required no charge or trial, was capped at twelve months, and depended for continuation beyond three months on an Advisory Board's report.

### Q: Which section of the Preventive Detention Act did the Supreme Court strike down?

Section 14. In A. K. Gopalan v State of Madras, decided on 19 May 1950, all six judges of the Supreme Court held that Section 14, insofar as it prohibited a detained person from disclosing to a court the grounds of the detention order or the representation made against it, contravened Article 22's guarantee of the earliest opportunity of making a representation, and was void to that extent. The Court held the section severable, so the rest of the Act survived and the petitioner's detention was not illegal. The majority otherwise upheld the Act against challenge under Articles 13, 19, and 21.

### Q: How many times was the Preventive Detention Act extended?

The common count is seven extensions, and it describes the continuance measures that carried the Act from its first expiry on 1 April 1951 to its final expiry on 31 December 1969. The count varies with the counting rule, because the early renewals of 1951 and 1952, the substantive amendments interleaved with them, and the amending Act that removed Section 14 can each be included or excluded, and a scholarly count of the occasions on which Parliament re-approved the Act between 1950 and 1963 alone reaches eight. The settled pattern, whichever count is used, is a one-year grant renewed in short steps and then in three-year continuances in 1954, 1957, 1960, 1963, and 1966.

### Q: When did the Preventive Detention Act finally lapse?

The Act ceased to have effect on 31 December 1969, the terminal date written into its own first section by the final extension of 1966, saving things done under it before that date. It was not repealed and no repeal motion defeated it. When the time came for another continuance bill, no government enacted one, and the Act died by the expiry mechanism it had carried since February 1950. The lapse left India without a central preventive detention Act until the Maintenance of Internal Security Act of 1971, although state detention legislation continued in operation in several states during the interval.

### Q: What did A.K. Gopalan argue in his challenge?

Gopalan, a communist leader detained since December 1947 and held under the 1950 Act, petitioned the Supreme Court under Article 32 for habeas corpus, arguing that the Act contravened Articles 13, 19, 21, and 22 and that his detention was therefore illegal. In substance he argued that preventive detention abridged the freedoms guaranteed by Article 19, including freedom of movement, and had to justify itself as a reasonable restriction; that a procedure depriving him of personal liberty under Article 21 had to meet a standard of fairness; and that the Act failed the safeguards of Article 22 itself, on which the Court agreed with him in respect of Section 14 alone.

### Q: Was preventive detention debated in the Constituent Assembly?

Yes, directly and at length. When the Assembly considered the draft article that became Article 22 in September 1949, members debated preventive detention as imprisonment without trial and divided over whether a free India's Constitution should authorise it at all. Members including Thakur Dass Bhargava and Mahavir Tyagi spoke against the power, while defenders such as K. M. Munshi rested on the safeguards of grounds, representation, Advisory Board review, and a maximum period. The Assembly adopted the article with those safeguards, which is why the Act of February 1950 exercised a power the Constitution had deliberately conferred rather than evading a prohibition the Constitution had imposed.

### Q: How long did the Provisional Parliament take to pass the Preventive Detention Bill?

Days rather than months. The bill was moved by Sardar Patel in February 1950, debated for five hours in a sitting for which question hour was dispensed with so the business could be completed, and enacted within the month, the Act bearing the date 25 February 1950. There was no select committee stage at which clauses could be examined in detail on evidence. The speed was defended as urgency, given the doubtful validity of the provincial detention laws after the Constitution, and attacked as indecent haste for a measure authorising imprisonment without trial. Both descriptions of the same timetable have remained in use ever since.

### Q: Was the Preventive Detention Act 1950 passed by an elected Parliament?

No. It was passed by the Provisional Parliament, which was the Constituent Assembly sitting in its legislative capacity, a body chosen indirectly before independence whose members had not faced a general election under the new Constitution. The first elected Lok Sabha met only after the general elections of 1951 and 1952. The point cuts in two directions: the detention power did not carry a fresh popular mandate in February 1950, as is sometimes implied, but the body that enacted it was also the body that had drafted the Constitution and its fundamental rights chapter in the preceding years, so the Act cannot be explained as the work of legislators ignorant of the rights it limited.

### Q: What was the original expiry date written into the Preventive Detention Act 1950?

The Act as enacted was to cease to have effect on 1 April 1951, giving it a life of a little over one year from its commencement in February 1950. The short duration was a deliberate part of the government's case for the bill: the Attorney General told the House that when the Act expired the detentions under it would also end, and the sunset obliged the government to return to Parliament and justify the power afresh within a single year. That obligation was honoured in form at each renewal and defeated in substance by them, since the expiry date was repeatedly moved until it stood at 31 December 1969.

### Q: What did Sardar Patel say in defence of the Preventive Detention Bill?

As the contemporary Hindustan Times report of the debate records, Patel commended the bill as a necessity forced by insurgency, sabotage, and disorder, and the newspaper characterised the measure, in its mover's framing, as the minimum evil necessary to safeguard democratic institutions. His central distinction was between belief and conduct: the government's fight, he said, was not with communism or with those who believed in the theory of communism, but with those whose avowed object was to create disruption and dislocation, tamper with communications, suborn the loyalty of the people, and make normal government impossible. He is also reported to have told the House that he had spent sleepless nights weighing the measure.

### Q: What happened to Section 14 after the Supreme Court struck it down?

Parliament removed the original Section 14 from the Act by an amending Act later in 1950, regularising the statute book to conform to the Gopalan judgment. The government did not attempt to restore the disclosure bar in another form, and it did not defy or circumvent the Court's holding. The episode is part of the Act's significance: within the first year of the Republic, the Supreme Court had voided a provision of the government's principal security statute, unanimously, and the executive's response was compliance and a tidying amendment. The detention power itself, resting on Section 3, was untouched by the judgment and was exercised for the remaining life of the Act.

### Q: What changed when the Preventive Detention Act was extended in 1954?

The 1954 extension, defended by Home Minister K. N. Katju, set the pattern for all later renewals. Instead of another short continuance, the Act was extended for three years, to 31 December 1957, and the three-year rhythm then held for the rest of its life. Katju's statement of objects argued that the Act had proved an effective instrument in the maintenance of law and order and proposed the longer period partly so that Parliament could re-examine the Act after the general election of 1956 and 1957. The same amending period also restated the Act's application to Jammu and Kashmir in qualified form. Emergency reasoning had given way to administrative reasoning.

### Q: Who defended the Preventive Detention Act's extensions in Parliament?

Successive Home Ministers of different governments carried the continuance bills, which is itself the central fact about the extensions. K. N. Katju defended the three-year extension of 1954, arguing that the Act had proved an effective instrument for law and order. G. B. Pant presented the extension of 1957 in nearly identical terms, saying experience showed the powers should continue for a further three years. Vidya Charan Shukla presented the final continuance in 1966, repeating the formula of effectiveness and essential continuance. The sponsors changed with the cabinets; the argument did not, and no government of the Act's twenty years declined to seek its renewal.

### Q: Why did Parliament let the Preventive Detention Act lapse in 1969?

No single repeal decision ended the Act. By 1969, the political conditions that had made renewal routine had changed: Parliament was fractured, the ruling party had split, and a further three-year continuance was a bill a weakened government had reason not to spend its strength on, against an opposition that had argued the case against the Act for twenty years. Because the Act carried its own expiry date, inaction was sufficient: with no continuance enacted, it ceased to have effect on 31 December 1969. State detention laws continued, and the Centre returned to preventive detention with the Maintenance of Internal Security Act in 1971.

### Q: Was there a central preventive detention law in force in 1970?

No. Between the lapse of the Preventive Detention Act 1950 on 31 December 1969 and the enactment of the Maintenance of Internal Security Act in 1971, the Centre had no preventive detention statute of its own in force. Detention without trial did not disappear from India during the interval, because several states operated their own public safety and preventive detention legislation under the Concurrent List competence, and the ordinary criminal law continued to apply everywhere. The interval matters because it disproves two opposite claims: that the 1950 Act's lapse ended Indian preventive detention, and that a central detention law has stood on the statute book without interruption since 1950.

### Q: How did opponents in Parliament argue against the Preventive Detention Bill?

Opposition speakers argued from ancestry, from the burden of proof, and from the future. Detention without trial, they said, was the colonial state's characteristic instrument, descending from the Bengal Regulation of 1818 and the Rowlatt legislation, and a republic founded by its victims should not re-enact its logic. A government unable to persuade a court, they argued, should not be able to imprison by satisfying itself, and the Act's breadth, reaching from the security of India to the maintenance of essential supplies, would carry its use far beyond saboteurs to organisers and critics. Finally, they predicted that a temporary power would be renewed by formula until the expiry date ceased to be a limit, which the next twenty years substantially bore out.

### Q: Did Jawaharlal Nehru support the Preventive Detention Act 1950?

The Act was a measure of Nehru's government, piloted by his Home Minister, and Nehru neither disowned it nor sought to distance himself from it; in that institutional sense he supported it and his governments repeatedly renewed it. His recorded positions across the period combined that acceptance with a distaste for detention without trial that he expressed openly and that his critics quoted back at him. The combination should not be flattened in either direction. Nehru was not the bill's mover and its defence in the House was Patel's, but the Prime Minister under whom it was enacted, and under whom it was first renewed, owned the policy as his government's considered act.

### Q: What role did the Attorney General play in the passage of the Preventive Detention Bill?

M. C. Setalvad, the first Attorney General of India, addressed the Provisional Parliament during the passage debate in support of the bill. As the contemporary report records, he told the House that the bill was in accordance with both the spirit and the letter of the Constitution, and he reinforced the government's reliance on the sunset by observing that when the Act expired on 1 April 1951 the detentions under it would also come to an end. His intervention mattered because the bill's vulnerability was constitutional rather than political: the government's majority was assured, but the argument that a detention law could sit inside the new fundamental rights chapter needed the authority of the Republic's chief law officer, and Setalvad supplied it.
