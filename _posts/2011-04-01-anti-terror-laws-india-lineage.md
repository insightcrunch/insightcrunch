The statute book offers three names, and the three names suggest three separate stories. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act of 1967, usually shortened to the UAPA, stands as permanent legislation without a built-in date of expiry. The Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, usually shortened to TADA, entered as temporary legislation in 1985, was renewed in successive forms, and lapsed in 1995 after Parliament declined further renewal. The Prevention of Terrorism Act of 2002, usually shortened to POTA, also entered as temporary legislation, and Parliament repealed the legislation in 2004. Read through the operative provisions, the sequence reads differently from the surface story of creation, experiment, and rejection. One statute survived every political turn. Two statutes were built to die, and did die. The puzzle sits in the distance between those facts: the names disappeared, while much of the legal machinery assembled under the names remained available to the state through another door.

That distance matters because anti-terror law is not ordinary criminal law with a louder title. The field governs the definition of a terrorist act, the proscription of an organisation, the forfeiture of property, the admissibility of statements, the grant or refusal of bail, the length of custody before charge, the protection of witnesses, and the forum of trial. A change in any one provision alters the balance between investigation and liberty. The definition decides which conduct leaves the ordinary law of offence and enters a regime of aggravated punishment. The proscription power decides whether membership, support, or fundraising can become criminal conduct in itself. When a statute lapses or stands repealed, the first question is therefore not whether a title has vanished. The first question is whether those operative powers vanished with the title, moved to another statute, or stayed exactly where earlier legislation had placed them.

Commentary on the field often treats each statute as a free-standing event, and that habit hides the continuity the record contains. TADA receives treatment as a Punjab story, then as a cautionary tale closed by lapse. POTA receives treatment as a response to an attack on Parliament, then as an election promise, then as a repeal presented as a restoration of ordinary law. The UAPA receives treatment as a separate and older law, as though the legislation occupied a different shelf from the two temporary statutes. Each treatment captures part of the record and misses the mechanism that connects the parts. The mechanism is transfer. A provision tested under a temporary statute could be redrafted, softened in wording, hardened in effect, or reproduced almost intact inside the permanent statute. Event history ends at the repeal debate or the lapse date. Provision history continues past both.

The method followed here is plain and strict. The unit of study is the provision, not the title, not the speech, and not the public controversy that surrounded a bill. A definition counts when the words defining a terrorist act change hands or change scope. A bail restriction counts when the threshold for release changes, regardless of the statute carrying the threshold. The same standard applies to organisational bans, schedules, forfeiture, interception, witness anonymity, and confessions made to police officers. Where later wording copies earlier wording, the lineage is direct. Where wording differs but the legal consequence matches, the lineage is functional, and the difference in drafting deserves attention rather than concealment. Where a power disappears without replacement, the disappearance registers as a genuine break, because a method built to find continuity has value only when the method records absence, narrowing, and loss as well.

Four questions govern each change of regime, for the lapse of TADA, the repeal of POTA, and every major amendment of the UAPA. The first question asks what the change moved: which definitions, powers, procedures, penalties, or institutional arrangements passed from a dying statute into a surviving statute. The second question asks what the change retired: which powers were allowed to end, which safeguards were dropped, and which practices lost legal authority without a successor provision. The third question asks what the change left exactly where the change found the material, because a great deal has never moved at all: the ordinary police organisation, the executive habit of proscription, and the judicial burden of testing executive action after the event. The fourth question asks who decided, since a transfer may be the work of Parliament in amendment, of a government drafting under pressure after an attack, of a court reading a provision down or striking a provision out, or of administrative practice settling a question left open.

Applied across the whole period, the four questions expose a pattern that title history conceals. TADA lapsed in 1995, and the lapse was real: the special courts lost the foundation for new work, and harsh provisions on confessions and bail ceased to govern fresh cases. Yet the UAPA remained in force, and TADA had already normalised the idea that terrorism required a distinct code apart from the ordinary penal and procedure codes. POTA repeated the pattern. The repeal of 2004 removed the statute, and the same parliamentary moment amended the UAPA to carry chapters on terrorist acts, terrorist organisations, and forfeiture. The repeal and the transfer belonged to a single legislative settlement. The UAPA sits at the centre of the lineage. Enacted in 1967 amid concern over associations challenging the territorial integrity of India, the statute began with a narrower concern than terrorism in the later sense. The amendments of 2004 absorbed much of the architecture that POTA had built, and later amendments extended proscription to individuals and widened the reach of the definition of a terrorist act. Powers placed in permanent legislation no longer need renewal debates at fixed intervals, and those powers acquire the protection of inertia.

The claim that organises the whole inquiry can be stated plainly. Repeal in this field has never reduced the stock of anti-terror power held by the Indian state. Lapse has not reduced the stock. Repeal combined with simultaneous amendment has not reduced the stock. On the record assembled here, each ending of a statute has left the state with the same range of powers, or a wider range. That is the ratchet claim, the pattern this article names the ratchet without a reset: the wheel turns in one direction, the pawl holds the gain, and the next turn begins from the position reached rather than from the position abandoned. The article will test that claim against the record, provision by provision and regime by regime, including the places where the claim fails.

## The Law That Was Already There: The UAPA Before Terrorism

The lineage of Indian anti-terror law does not begin with a statute directed at terrorism. It begins with a constitutional amendment directed at the vocabulary of permissible restriction, and with a statute that followed four years afterwards directed at the unlawful association. The distinction matters for everything that came afterwards, because the law that survived into the era of special anti-terror legislation was not designed for the terrorist, for the terrorist act, or for the trial of offences of mass violence. It was designed for a different problem, the problem of organised advocacy that challenged the territorial and constitutional unity of the Indian Union, and its machinery bore the marks of that origin in every section. The recovery starts in 1963, in the aftermath of war on the northern border and in the presence of a political demand for a separate Dravidian state voiced within electoral politics.

The Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act, 1963 inserted a single phrase into several distinct places in the constitutional text, and the insertion was deliberate in each place. The phrase was the sovereignty and integrity of India. Article 19 protected the freedom of speech and expression, the freedom to assemble peacefully and without arms, and the freedom to form associations or unions, subject to reasonable restrictions tied to specified grounds. Restrictions on assembly were tied to public order, and restrictions on association were tied to public order or morality. Neither ground comfortably reached the central case that concerned the state in the early 1960s, an association whose very object was the secession of a part of the territory of India from the Union, pursued by propaganda, by organisation, and by electoral mobilisation rather than by disorder in the street. The amendment closed that gap. It amended clause (2) of Article 19 so that reasonable restrictions on speech could be imposed in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, clause (3) so that restrictions on assembly could rest on the same ground, and clause (4) so that restrictions on the freedom to form associations or unions could likewise be imposed in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India or public order. It made the integrity of the country a free-standing constitutional reason for limiting three distinct freedoms, and it placed association, the collective and durable form of political action, squarely within the reach of that reason.

The same phrase was carried into the forms of oath and affirmation in the Third Schedule. Candidates for Parliament and for the state legislatures, ministers of the Union and of the states, judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts, and the Comptroller and Auditor General were required, in the revised forms, to swear or affirm that they would uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India. The pairing was not accidental. The restriction clauses addressed the citizen and the association from the outside, by defining the ground on which legislatures could act against them. The oath forms addressed the office holder from the inside, by making fidelity to sovereignty and integrity a formal condition of holding constitutional office.

The amendment gave effect to recommendations of the Committee on National Integration and Regionalisation, constituted in the setting of the National Integration Council, which had been set up to address casteism, communalism, regionalism, and linguistic separatism as threats to national unity. The background was concrete. The war with China in 1962 had shaken confidence in the security of the frontier. In the south, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam had carried the demand for a separate Dravida Nadu into mainstream politics, and although the party abandoned the secessionist demand in the climate that followed the war, the episode had shown that a demand for the dismemberment of the Union could be advanced through speech, assembly, association, and election. The recommendation translated a political resolution about national integration into a permanent feature of constitutional drafting, available to every later legislature that wished to invoke it.

The design idea embedded in the amendment supplied the premise of the statute of 1967. The idea was that integrity could be made a ground for restricting association itself, and not only a ground for punishing particular acts done in pursuit of disintegration. A legal order could punish a conspiracy to wage war, or sabotage, or a speech that incited violence, without touching the association that connected the persons involved. The Sixteenth Amendment pointed in a different direction. By permitting reasonable restrictions on the freedom to form associations in the interests of sovereignty and integrity, it authorised legislation whose object was the association as a continuing body, its membership, its funds, its publications, and its capacity to act collectively over time. The association could be reached before any member had committed a substantive offence against the state, because the ground attached to the tendency and object of the combination rather than to a completed act of violence. That move, from act to association and from incident to organisation, is the conceptual foundation of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act.

The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967, Act 37 of 1967, received the assent of the President on 30 December 1967. Its long title described an Act to provide for the more effective prevention of certain unlawful activities of individuals and associations, and for matters connected with that purpose. The definitions gave content to unlawful activity, and the centre of gravity lay in action, whether by act or by speech or through any other medium, intended or supporting a claim to bring about the cession of a part of the territory of India or the secession of a part of the territory from the Union, or inciting any individual or group to bring about such cession or secession, or disclaiming, questioning, disputing, or disturbing the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India. An unlawful association was an association that had as its object any unlawful activity, or that encouraged or aided persons to undertake unlawful activity, or whose members undertook such activity. The debt to the Sixteenth Amendment was plain. The statute took the constitutional phrase, converted it into a description of prohibited advocacy and organisation, and attached consequences to the status of the association that embodied that advocacy. Secession, cession, and the questioning of territorial integrity were the mischief. Terrorism, as violent action directed at the state or at the public to compel a government or to strike terror in a section of the people, had no place in the scheme, because the scheme had been drafted for a mischief primarily political and organisational in character.

The machinery followed from that choice of subject. If the Central Government was of opinion that an association was or had become an unlawful association, it could declare the association to be unlawful by notification in the Official Gazette, stating the grounds on which the declaration rested. The declaration was an executive act of great consequence, since it converted membership and support into the raw material of criminal liability, but the Act did not leave it to rest on executive opinion alone. A notification was not to take durable effect unless confirmed by a Tribunal constituted under the Act, to which the Central Government was required to refer the notification within the period prescribed. The Tribunal was to consist of one person appointed by the Central Government, and that person was required to be a Judge of a High Court, a requirement that placed a sitting member of the superior judiciary at the centre of the confirmation process. The Tribunal held powers resembling those of a civil court for inquiry, and it was required to decide, after giving the association an opportunity to be represented and heard, whether there was sufficient cause for the declaration. If the Tribunal confirmed the declaration, the ban took effect for a period measured in years from the date of the notification, and at expiry the association could be banned again only through a fresh declaration and a fresh reference, so that the prohibition was renewable but never permanent on a single executive act. If the Tribunal found insufficient cause, the government was required to cancel the notification.

The offences were built around the declaration, and their logic was the logic of membership and support. Once an association had been declared unlawful, taking part in its meetings, becoming or remaining a member, contributing or soliciting contributions for its purposes, and otherwise assisting its operations attracted punishment. Separate provisions dealt with handling the funds of an unlawful association, with contravention of orders relating to notified places, and with activity that kept a banned body alive under the same name or a different one. The penalties were serious but not extraordinary by the standards of later special legislation, and the scheme contemplated trial through the ordinary criminal courts under the ordinary law of procedure and evidence. Provisions also reached the individual who committed an unlawful activity without a banned association, but even there the emphasis fell on advocacy and action directed against sovereignty and integrity, not on a catalogue of violent acts graded by terroristic intent or effect. The subject of the Act was the unlawful association, and the terrorist had not yet entered the statute book through this door.

What the Act did not contain is as instructive as what it contained, and the absences were structural. It contained no definition of a terrorist act, no chapter devoted to terrorism, no schedule of terrorist organisations distinct from unlawful associations, and no offence of raising funds for a terrorist act as a separate and specially punished category. It contained no special courts of the later kind, constituted for the speedy trial of a class of grave offences under a modified procedure, and no provision displacing the ordinary courts in favour of a designated forum with special powers in the parent Act. It contained no reversed bail regime, under which a court would be barred from releasing an accused unless the public prosecutor had been heard and the court was satisfied that there were reasonable grounds for believing that the accusation was not prima facie true, a formula that later became one of the most contested features of Indian anti-terror law. It contained no statutory presumptions under which recovery of arms or explosives or fingerprints at the scene could be treated as presumptive proof of guilt in defined circumstances, no provision making a confession to a senior police officer admissible, no extension of police custody beyond ordinary limits as a special feature, and no sanction requirement for prosecution for terrorism under a special chapter. Their absence was not an oversight. It was the consequence of a statute written to ban associations and punish membership, for which ordinary courts, the ordinary burden of proof, ordinary bail, and ordinary evidence were considered sufficient.

The early amendments confirmed that understanding, because they adjusted the machinery rather than changed the subject. Amendments in 1969, in 1972, and in 1986 altered provisions of detail, touching the reach and application of the Act, the definitions and ancillary arrangements on which a declaration depended, the procedure surrounding the Tribunal and the orders that could follow a declaration, and penalties and incidental provisions that gave the scheme practical effect. The precise content of each amending Act matters less for the lineage than the direction all three took in common. None converted the Act into a code against terrorism. None inserted a definition of a terrorist act, created a special court, reversed bail, or introduced a presumption against the accused. None treated the bomb, the assassination, or the armed seizure of a public place as the organising case around which the statute should be rebuilt. The amendments maintained an instrument regarded as serviceable for its original purpose, the suppression of associations that challenged the sovereignty and integrity of India, in a period when separatist politics remained a live concern in more than one region. That continuity across nearly two decades, through changes of government and through very different regional crises, underlined how firmly the Act remained identified in legislative practice with unlawful association rather than with terrorism as a distinct category of crime.

That problem arrived in the 1980s in a form the Act of 1967 had never been built to answer. Insurgency in Punjab, violence associated with demands for a separate Sikh state, assassinations, attacks on passenger transport and on places of public resort, and the growing use of sophisticated weapons and explosives presented Parliament with a problem defined by acts of terror rather than by the objects of an association. The response, in 1985 in the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, was a new statute, temporary in stated duration, equipped with special courts, modified procedure, admissible confessions to senior police officers, stringent bail, and presumptions that had no counterpart in the Act of 1967. Parliament reached for a new statute instead of this one, and the choice was intelligible. The Act of 1967 carried a constitutional history tied to national integration, a Tribunal mechanism tied to confirmation of bans, and an offence structure tied to membership and support, each fitted to suppression of unlawful associations far better than to trial of terrorist offences. The consequence was lasting. Indian anti-terror law developed for two decades as special and temporary statutes standing beside a permanent Act that continued its older work in the background, and only after repeal of the last special statute did Parliament return to the Act of 1967 and transform it, by amendment, into the principal anti-terror law of the country. The shape of the lineage, its long separation between the law of unlawful associations and the law of terrorism and its eventual convergence in a single amended statute, was determined in large part by that decision at the outset of the terrorism era, to leave the survivor to its first life and to legislate for terror elsewhere.

## TADA: The Template Written in Punjab

The Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act arrived as a statute written for a specific emergency, and the emergency was centred in Punjab. Through the early nineteen eighties Punjab experienced a sustained campaign of political violence that included assassinations of officials and public figures, attacks on police personnel, killings of civilians, bombings, and armed confrontations that ordinary criminal law struggled to contain. The violence drew upon separatist demand, communal provocation, criminal enterprise, and retaliation. Investigation faced witnesses who feared reprisal, accused persons who received support across districts and across borders, and trials that moved slowly through burdened courts. That setting shaped both the timing and the design of the legislation that followed.

The assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi on 31 October 1984 deepened the sense of crisis in which Parliament acted. The killing, carried out by members of the security detail assigned to the Prime Minister, demonstrated that the threat had penetrated the centre of the state itself. The communal violence that followed in Delhi and in other cities added a second layer of disorder to the first. On one side stood a claim of necessity grounded in the scale and organisation of terrorist violence. On the other side stood an awareness that exceptional powers, once written in general language, reach persons and places far removed from the circumstances that produced them. The statute attempted to hold those considerations together through a limited duration and procedures that departed deliberately from ordinary law.

Parliament enacted the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act 1985 as Act 31 of 1985, and the Act came into force on 24 May 1985 with a life of two years written into the statute itself. The sunset provision mattered because it presented the departure from ordinary procedure as temporary and as subject to a fresh legislative decision before continuation. The Act applied in the first instance to Punjab and to Chandigarh, the union territory that served as capital to Punjab and to Haryana and that stood at the administrative heart of the affected region. Application to other parts of India followed afterwards through extension, as governments notified its operation in additional areas. A statute drafted in the language of a Punjab emergency thus became available across the country.

When the original two years ran out in 1987, the statute did not end with the expiry date emphasised in 1985. The government kept the regime alive by ordinance and then secured re-enactment as the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act of 1987, which continued the essential framework with amendments. Renewal followed in 1989, again in 1991, and again in 1993, each renewal extending the life of a law that rested upon the logic of temporary necessity. A limit that requires periodic approval still compels a government to defend continuation in Parliament, and the debates on each extension recorded disagreement over use, over conviction outcomes, and over the treatment of arrested persons. TADA supplied a template in two senses. It supplied operative provisions that later anti-terror legislation borrowed and adapted, and it supplied a legislative habit in which a short lived statute provided the bridge to a longer regime.

The new machinery began with designated courts. TADA offences were assigned to courts designated for that purpose, with judges appointed to try cases under the Act and with procedures intended to secure speed, concentration, and protection. A designated court could take cognisance without the committal proceedings that marked the ordinary route in serious cases, and the trial was to proceed on a day to day basis with priority over other matters. The design reflected a diagnosis that delay served the objectives of terrorist organisation by keeping witnesses exposed and by keeping accused persons in custody without adjudication. Concentration also produced expertise, since a limited number of courts dealt repeatedly with admissibility, presumption, and bail under the same statute.

Section 15 contained the most contested innovation in the Act. Under ordinary law, a confession made to a police officer was inadmissible, and a confession made in police custody faced strict exclusion unless made in the immediate presence of a magistrate. The rule rested upon a long institutional distrust of statements obtained by investigators who controlled the conditions of detention. Section 15 reversed that position for TADA offences by making a confession recorded by a police officer of the rank of Superintendent of Police or above admissible at trial, subject to recording conditions in the statute and in the rules framed under it. The officer was required to explain that the person was not bound to confess and that a confession could be used in evidence against that person. The recording was to be in writing or on a mechanical device from which the statement could be reproduced. Admissibility did not compel acceptance, since the trial court retained the task of deciding whether a confession was voluntary and truthful, but admissibility placed before the court a form of evidence that ordinary law excluded altogether.

The confession provision interacted with extended pre-charge detention. Ordinary law required production before a magistrate within a short period after arrest and placed limits upon custody during investigation before the accused acquired a right to bail upon default in filing the charge sheet. TADA stretched that period substantially, and the designated court could authorise detention during investigation extending up to one year where the statutory conditions were satisfied and where the public prosecutor reported on the progress of the investigation and on the reasons for further detention. The extension addressed investigations involving multiple accused persons, recoveries in different districts, forensic examination, and the tracing of finance and supply networks. The same extension magnified every other feature of the regime. A confession obtained during long custody acquired greater practical weight, and a restrictive bail provision acquired greater severity because the alternative to bail was custody measured in months.

Section 20(8) governed bail and reversed the ordinary approach in terms that defined the experience of arrest under the Act. An accused person was not to be released on bail unless the public prosecutor had been given an opportunity to oppose the application and unless the court was satisfied that there were reasonable grounds for believing that the accused was not guilty of the offence and was not likely to commit an offence while on bail. The language required the court, at the stage of investigation or early trial, to reach a provisional satisfaction concerning innocence before liberty could follow. The result was long incarceration before trial for large numbers of arrested persons, including persons against whom the prosecution later failed or against whom the court eventually recorded acquittal. The bail clause became central to assessments of TADA as punishment through process, resting less upon sentences after conviction than upon custody endured before judgment.

Section 21 introduced presumptions that altered the distribution of proof in defined situations. Where arms or explosives or other specified articles were recovered from the possession of the accused, and where there was reason to believe that such articles had been used in the commission of a similar offence, the designated court was to presume that the accused had committed the offence under trial unless the contrary was proved. A parallel presumption attached to fingerprints. Where a fingerprint of the accused was found at the site of the offence or on anything, including arms and vehicles, used in connection with the offence, the court was to draw the prescribed presumption concerning participation. Presumptions have a recognised place in Indian evidence law, particularly in statutes dealing with corruption, narcotics, and economic offences, and supporters placed Section 21 within that tradition of response to crimes committed in secrecy. The difference lay in the gravity of the offences and in the severity of the punishments that followed.

Protection of proceedings and of witnesses formed the remaining part of the machinery. TADA permitted trials to be held in camera where the designated court considered open proceedings undesirable for reasons that included the safety of witnesses and the maintenance of public order, and the court could restrict publication of proceedings. The Act also empowered the court to keep the identity and address of a witness secret, including directions that the name and address should not be disclosed in orders or in material accessible to the accused or to the public. Proceedings could be adapted so that a witness was not confronted in a manner that revealed identity. The provisions answered a documented feature of terrorist crime in Punjab, where witnesses faced intimidation and in some instances lethal attack, and where families shared the exposure created by testimony. The same provisions strained the ordinary understanding of a fair trial as a public contest in which the accused can test testimony through open cross examination.

The constitutional test came before a Constitution Bench of five judges of the Supreme Court in Kartar Singh v State of Punjab, decided in 1994. The challenge attacked the Act on grounds that included legislative competence, violation of the guarantees of equality and of life and personal liberty, the admissibility of police confessions, the presumptions in Section 21, the bail restriction in Section 20(8), and the procedures of designated courts. The Court upheld the statute in substance. Parliament was held competent to enact legislation directed against terrorist activity that threatened the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India and that created public disorder beyond the capacity of ordinary state policing alone. The classification underlying special procedure was accepted as resting upon the distinctive character of terrorist offences, and the departures from ordinary procedure were sustained where the statute supplied guidance and where courts retained powers of scrutiny.

The confession provision survived, and the treatment of Section 15 illustrates the method of the judgment. The Court upheld admissibility of a confession recorded by a senior police officer, accepting the legislative judgment that rank and statutory conditions supplied sufficient protection against abuse. At the same time the Court laid down safeguards that narrowed the distance between a police recorded confession under TADA and a confession recorded under judicial supervision in ordinary law. The confession was to be recorded in a free atmosphere, free from threat, inducement, or pressure of any kind, and the exercise was not to become an extension of interrogation conducted under conditions of fear. The recording was to be in the language of the maker, or in a language that the maker understood, so that the document represented the statement of the maker rather than a version shaped by the recorder. The officer was to warn the maker that confession was not obligatory and that the statement could be used in evidence, and time for reflection formed part of the expected protection.

Two further safeguards attached to Section 15 introduced an external check after recording. The person from whom a confession had been recorded was to be produced before the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or the Chief Judicial Magistrate, together with the original statement. The magistrate was to ascertain the circumstances in which the confession had been made and to record any statement concerning the treatment of the maker. Where the person produced complained of torture or of ill treatment, medical examination was to follow, so that an allegation of coercion acquired a contemporaneous record rather than depending entirely upon assertion made months afterwards at trial. Kartar Singh therefore left Section 15 standing while supplying trial courts with concrete questions concerning warning, language, atmosphere, production, and medical record.

The collapse of TADA arose less from a single judicial decision than from the accumulated record of use across a decade. Extension beyond Punjab placed the Act in states that experienced violence of different kinds, including communal conflict, organised crime, smuggling, insurgency in the north east and in Jammu and Kashmir, and local patterns of armed offence that bore little resemblance to the Punjab campaign that had supplied the original justification. Police forces invoked TADA in situations that ranged from grave conspiracy to relatively minor public order and property offences, and the breadth of the definitions of terrorist act and disruptive activity allowed argument at the margins of almost every serious crime. Arrest brought consequences that did not depend upon ultimate conviction. Custody could extend for a long investigation, bail required satisfaction of innocence under Section 20(8), and trial before a designated court carried the stigma of a terrorist label in employment, in family standing, and in community relations. The disparity between arrests and convictions became the most damaging feature of the public record, because the coercive parts of the statute operated at full strength while the adjudicative parts produced guilt in only a small fraction of cases.

The figures remain contested, and responsible discussion requires attribution rather than assertion. Human Rights Watch recorded that over 76,000 people were arrested while TADA was in force and that the recorded conviction rate was under one per cent. A statute defended as an instrument against organised terrorism produced a caseload in which vast numbers of arrested persons were discharged, acquitted, released after screening, or held for long periods without a conviction. Particular controversy attached to use in Gujarat and in other states with little terrorism of the Punjab kind, where arrests were recorded in connection with communal incidents, smuggling allegations, and offences that critics described as ordinary crimes carrying an extraordinary label.

Screening by review committees supplied an internal recognition that the initial decision to invoke TADA required correction in a substantial number of cases. Governments constituted committees at the central and state levels to examine cases registered under the Act, to consider whether the material justified continuance under a terrorist statute, and to recommend withdrawal or transfer to ordinary law where the ingredients of a TADA offence were absent. The screenings thinned the caseload. Cases were dropped, charges were reduced, and prosecutions were sent back to ordinary courts for trial under the Indian Penal Code and other general laws. The committees assisted individual accused persons who obtained release or a less restrictive forum, and the pattern of recommendations also documented the scale of over inclusion at the stage of registration. Screening could mitigate custody and stigma in particular cases, but screening could not restore time spent in detention, nor could screening alter the fact that arrest had already triggered the restrictive bail regime.

By 1995 the political conditions that had sustained repeated renewal had weakened beyond repair. The government of the day faced opposition in Parliament from parties objecting on civil liberties grounds, from parties objecting to particular patterns of use in particular states, and from parties unwilling to accept responsibility for another extension of a law associated with mass arrest and minimal conviction. The insurgency in Punjab had also receded from the peak that had framed the legislation of 1985, and the claim that a nationwide terrorist statute remained indispensable lost force as the original theatre grew calmer even while serious violence continued in other regions. In 1995 the government of the day declined to renew the Act and TADA was allowed to lapse upon the expiry of the term granted by the final renewal.

The legal effect of a lapse of that kind is precise and limited. A temporary statute that expires ceases to authorise new action under its powers. Police and prosecutors cannot register fresh cases under a law that has ceased to exist, cannot seek fresh extensions of custody under its detention provisions, cannot invoke its bail restrictions in new proceedings, and cannot ask a designated court to apply its presumptions to offences prosecuted after expiry. The machinery of designated courts, of police recorded confessions under Section 15, of presumptions under Section 21, and of witness protection directions depends upon the statute for authority, and the authority ends with the statute. Savings principles in Indian law preserve a different category of matter. Liability incurred, investigation completed, and proceedings instituted during the period of operation are not erased by expiry, and a trial for an offence committed during the life of TADA could proceed to judgment and to appeal under the law that governed the offence when committed. The lapse therefore closed the future while leaving the past to be finished in the courts. Pending prosecutions for acts committed while the statute lived continue; the powers do not.

## The Gap Years and the State Laboratory

Between the lapse of the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act in 1995 and the arrival of the Prevention of Terrorism Act in 2001, India had no central anti-terror statute in operation. The gap is easily misunderstood, because the absence of a central statute did not mean an absence of law, of policing, or of prosecution under the ordinary codes. What was missing was a distinct central code for terrorism as a category, with its own definitions, its own custody and bail rules, its own presumptions, its own special courts, and its own machinery for proscribing organisations. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act as it then stood could declare an association unlawful and could punish membership of such an association, but it defined no terrorist act. The lineage traced in this article concerns prosecution for offences defined by statute, not detention without trial, and a separate [preventive detention law guide](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/) covers the distinct constitutional and statutory world in which the State restrains a person in order to prevent anticipated conduct rather than to punish past conduct through a court.

Two attempts to refill the central space failed, and the manner of their failure explains much about the politics of the gap years. The first was the Criminal Law Amendment Bill of 1995, prepared as TADA approached its end. Its design was revealing. Instead of proposing another temporary anti-terror statute with a short life and a renewal debate attached to it, the Bill would have written features drawn from TADA into the ordinary codes permanently. The Bill was left unpassed. Opposition rested on the record of TADA itself, on the scale of arrests measured against the small number of convictions, on the use of the statute in situations far removed from the insurgencies invoked at its birth, and on a wider reluctance to make permanent a set of departures from ordinary criminal procedure that had been defended for a decade precisely on the ground that the departures were temporary. The second attempt came around the work of the Law Commission at the close of the decade and produced a successor anti-terror Bill prepared around 2000. That draft again sought a central statute addressed to terrorism, with special procedures and special offences, and it again failed to become law. The political cost of a central anti-terror statute remained high enough to block enactment, while the administrative appetite for special powers did not disappear. The result was a period in which innovation moved downwards, from Parliament to the state legislatures, and outwards, from terrorism as such to organised crime as the organising category.

The states experimented instead, and the state laboratory produced the statutes that shaped much of the vocabulary of the next central law. The Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act 1999 was the first and the most influential. Maharashtra framed its statute around organised crime and the syndicate, around continuing unlawful activity, and around the difficulty of prosecuting structured criminal enterprises through ordinary evidentiary and bail rules alone. The Act brought stringent bail conditions, extended periods for investigation, admissibility of intercepted communications under stated conditions, and a provision for confessions recorded by a senior police officer to be considered by the court under safeguards. The Maharashtra Act was extended to the National Capital Territory of Delhi in 2002, a sign that the model was travelling beyond the state that invented it.

Karnataka followed with its organised crime Act of 2000, the Karnataka Control of Organised Crimes Act, which adopted a comparable architecture of definitions, special courts, extended investigation, and stricter bail. Andhra Pradesh had its own organised crime law, the Andhra Pradesh Control of Organised Crime Act, which ran for a period and expired in 2004, an outcome that itself illustrated the uneven life of state experiments, some settling into permanence, some lapsing when a legislature declined to sustain them. Chhattisgarh took a different route with the Chhattisgarh Special Public Security Act of 2005, directed at public security and unlawful organisations in a state confronting Maoist violence, and framed in broader language that drew sustained criticism for the breadth of its definitions and the reach of its offences concerning support and association. Gujarat passed the Gujarat Control of Terrorism and Organised Crime Bill in its Assembly in 2003, joining terrorism and organised crime in a single title, but the Bill waited years for presidential assent, which arrived only in 2019. The long wait in Gujarat was not an administrative accident. The Uttar Pradesh Gangsters and Anti-Social Activities (Prevention) Act, older than the gap years in origin, policed organised offending from the state side through its own definitions of gang activity and its own special procedures, and it showed that states had long used special statutes for collective offending even when no central anti-terror law existed.

The assent gate under Article 254(2) of the Constitution is the constitutional reason several state Bills stalled, and no account of the state laboratory is complete without it. Criminal law and criminal procedure sit in the Concurrent List, where Parliament and a state legislature may both legislate. Under Article 254(1), where a state law in that field conflicts with a central law, the central law prevails and the state law is void to the extent of the repugnancy. Article 254(2) provides the exception. Where a state law in the concurrent field that conflicts with an earlier central law has been reserved for the consideration of the President and has received assent, that state law prevails in that state, subject to the power of Parliament to legislate again on the matter and to override it. A state statute that relaxed or displaced central provisions on confessions, bail, interception, or procedure therefore often needed that assent to survive a repugnancy challenge. Assent became a gate through which the centre could delay, condition, or deny a state experiment without defeating it in the state Assembly. Gujarat supplied the clearest example of delay. Other state proposals in the same field met return, revision, or prolonged silence. The constitutional structure thus produced a paradox of the gap years. The centre would not enact a statute of its own between 1995 and 2001, yet the centre retained, through repugnancy and assent, a veto over the speed and survival of state statutes that attempted to occupy adjoining ground. The laboratory metaphor therefore requires qualification. The states could draft, enact, and administer experiments in special criminal law, and Maharashtra in particular supplied a working model that prosecutors and legislators elsewhere studied closely. The states could not, however, guarantee the survival of an experiment that displaced central procedure, because the assent process placed the final word in several important cases outside the state capital altogether.

## POTA: Ordinance, Joint Sitting, and a Three-Year Clock

The Prevention of Terrorism Act arrived through emergency in form as well as in substance. The Prevention of Terrorism Ordinance had already been promulgated on 24 October 2001, weeks before the event that fixed the statute in public memory. The ordinance route mattered. An ordinance under Article 123 is a temporary exercise of legislative power by the executive when Parliament is not in session, justified by a claim of immediate necessity and destined to lapse unless Parliament approves it within a stated period after reassembly.

The attack on Parliament on 13 December 2001 altered the conditions under which the Bill replacing the ordinance would be debated. Armed attackers entered the Parliament House complex in New Delhi and were killed by security personnel, with loss of life among those defending the complex, at a time when Parliament was in session and the political leadership of the country was present in or near the building. The Bill nevertheless failed in the Rajya Sabha, where the governing coalition lacked a majority. The government then resorted to Article 108 and a joint sitting of both Houses, at which the larger numerical strength of the Lok Sabha could prevail. The Bill was passed at a joint sitting of Parliament on 26 March 2002, only the third joint sitting in the history of the Republic. The Prevention of Terrorism Act 2002, Act 15 of 2002, was deemed in force from 24 October 2001, the date of the ordinance, so that acts and proceedings under the ordinance were carried into the Act without a break. The Act carried a built-in life of three years from its commencement. That sunset clause placed POTA in the lineage of TADA rather than in the lineage of permanent codes. A three year clock promised a future Parliament a decision point, promised critics a date at which the experiment would end unless renewed, and reminded administrators from the first day that custody, trial, and appeal under the Act would race against the statute itself.

Its machinery invites direct comparison against TADA, because POTA was drafted by men and women who had the TADA record, the Kartar Singh judgment, and a decade of criticism open on the desk. Section 32 carried the confession provision forward with the Kartar Singh safeguards written into the text. Under TADA, the admissibility of a confession made to a senior police officer had rested on the statute as interpreted and disciplined by the Supreme Court in Kartar Singh v State of Punjab, where a majority upheld the provision while attaching conditions concerning warning, voluntariness, recording, and production before a magistrate. POTA wrote much of that discipline into the section itself. A confession to a police officer not lower in rank than a Superintendent of Police could be admissible if the person was informed of the right to silence and of the consequence that the statement could be used in evidence, if the statement was recorded in a prescribed manner, if the atmosphere of voluntariness was secured, and if the person was produced before a Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or a Chief Judicial Magistrate within forty eight hours, with medical examination where a complaint of torture was made. The drafting choice was a claim about learning. Parliament presented the provision not as a bare repetition of TADA but as a codification of the safeguards that constitutional adjudication had required.

Custody, investigation, and bail under POTA were less severe in some particulars than under TADA and remained far removed from the ordinary code. The period for completing investigation and filing a charge sheet ran to 180 days, with the court able to extend the ordinary period on a report showing progress and specific reasons for detention beyond the initial period, against the one year that TADA had permitted at its height. Police custody could run to 30 days, double the ordinary limit, within the larger period of remand. Bail was barred during the first year of custody in the sense that the Act imposed a stringent condition that a court could not release an accused on bail during that first year unless the public prosecutor had been heard and the court was satisfied on the material that there were grounds for believing that the accused was not guilty and was not likely to commit an offence while on bail, and beyond that first year the restrictive standard continued to govern release in a form that reversed the ordinary presumption in favour of liberty pending trial. Presumptions operated on recoveries and intercepted material. Where arms, explosives, or other specified material were recovered from the possession of the accused, or where fingerprints or other defined evidence connected the accused to such material or to a place of use, the special court could presume guilt of the relevant offence unless the contrary was proved. Interception of communications was brought within a statutory framework of authorisation and review, and intercepted material was given evidentiary standing that the ordinary law had withheld or admitted only through other routes. A scheduled list of terrorist organisations replaced open ended liability in one important respect. Instead of leaving the identity of a terrorist organisation entirely to executive notification in each instance or to proof at large, the Act placed named organisations in a Schedule and provided a mechanism for addition and removal, with offences of membership, support, invitation of support, and arrangement of meetings tied to that listed status. Review committees and special courts returned from the TADA design. An executive review committee screened the application of the Act, and special courts, staffed by serving judges with the consent of the Chief Justice of the relevant High Court, conducted trials with day to day procedure as the stated norm and with appeals lying directly to the High Court.

In PUCL v Union of India (2003), the Supreme Court upheld the Act while reading a mens rea requirement into the membership and support offences. The Peoples Union for Civil Liberties challenged central provisions as violations of equality, of freedom of speech and association, and of the guarantee of life and personal liberty. The Court sustained the legislative competence of Parliament, sustained the core definitions and procedures against the challenges brought against them, and treated several contested expressions as requiring a mental element before criminal liability could attach. Membership of a terrorist organisation, inviting support for such an organisation, and kindred acts of association were not to be read as offences of mere passive status divorced from intention to further the activities of the organisation. That reading mattered in practice and in doctrine. In practice, it gave trial courts and appellate courts a textual and constitutional basis for acquitting or discharging persons whose connection with a listed organisation was nominal, historical, or unaccompanied by an intention to further its terrorist activities.

The time pressure point deserves separate statement, because POTA was passed under the pressure of an attack weeks old, and the circumstances of passage matter to how a statute gets read afterwards. The ordinance had already set the text before the attack occurred, the session was shadowed by war talk and mobilisation on the border, and the joint sitting device resolved disagreement between the Houses by arithmetic rather than by a negotiated text that both Houses had separately accepted. None of that made the Act invalid, and the Supreme Court did not treat haste as a ground of invalidity. Haste nevertheless left marks that later readers could not ignore. Broad expressions entered the text without the prolonged testing that committee scrutiny in calmer conditions might have supplied, which increased the work that courts later had to perform through narrowing construction. Assurances given in debate about restrained use acquired an importance beyond their legal status, because the text alone had not been the product of a settled consensus. The three year sunset, offered as a discipline, also shaped reading, since a court asked to construe a temporary emergency statute in 2003 knew that Parliament had reserved to itself an early opportunity to let the experiment die. When repeal came in 2004, the circumstances of passage returned as part of the case against renewal, as evidence that a law made in the shadow of an attack had been used in the ordinary politics of the states in ways its sponsors had promised to prevent. Statutes carry their birth circumstances into their interpretation, not as a rule of law, but as context that judges, legislators, and historians use to decide how much trust to place in broad words.

## 2004: Repeal as Transplant

Repeal was an election commitment in 2004, and the sequence by which repeal was accomplished shows a legislature determined to end POTA without leaving a vacuum in the law of terrorist offences. The general election of that year returned a coalition whose Common Minimum Programme promised repeal of POTA, a promise made against a record that included highly publicised prosecutions, allegations of use against political opponents and against members of minority communities, and the findings and anxieties that had accumulated around arrests in cases that later collapsed or ended in acquittal. The repeal ordinance came on 21 September 2004, using the same executive instrument through which POTA had first entered the law three years earlier, and the Prevention of Terrorism (Repeal) Act followed in December 2004, giving parliamentary form to the ending.

The repeal carried its own savings, and the savings were more considered than a simple erasure. Every pending POTA case was sent by the repeal scheme to the review committee constituted under Section 60 of POTA for a prima facie screening within a year. The committee was to examine whether there was a prima facie case for proceeding against an accused under POTA, and cases in which no such prima facie case was found were to end in discharge or withdrawal according to the stage the proceedings had reached, while cases that survived screening could continue under the repealed Act as if repeal had not intervened for that purpose. The design acknowledged a practical and a constitutional difficulty. A practical difficulty was that hundreds of persons stood charged, many in custody, under a statute that the new Parliament regarded as discredited, and an unexamined continuation of every case would have contradicted the premise of repeal. A constitutional difficulty was that abrupt termination of all proceedings by repeal, without a screening mechanism, would have invited challenge and would have freed without distinction persons accused of grave acts of violence supported by strong evidence. Screening within a year was the compromise. It imposed a deadline on the State, placed the burden of justification on continuation rather than on the accused at that stage, and converted repeal from a slogan into an administrative process with files, committees, and dates. The record of that screening, with large numbers of cases closed after review, became part of the later argument about how widely POTA had been applied beyond the cases that could survive even a prima facie test.

In the same season Parliament passed the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act 2004, Act 29 of 2004, and in that Act the transplant was performed. The amendment did not revive POTA by name, did not re-enact its special courts as a separate system in the same form, and did not restore its confession or presumption clauses. What it did was to rebuild the standing statute of 1967 so that the standing statute could perform much of the work that a special anti-terror Act had performed. It inserted Chapter IV, Sections 15 to 23, into the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, under a heading concerning punishment for terrorist activities. Section 15 defined the terrorist act, in language centred on an act done with intent to threaten the unity, integrity, security, or sovereignty of India or to strike terror in the people or in a section of the people, by means of bombs, dynamite, firearms, lethal weapons, poisonous or noxious gases, chemicals, or other substances, where the act was likely to cause death, injury, damage to property, disruption of supplies or services essential to life, or damage to monetary stability through smuggling or circulation of counterfeit currency, together with connected acts of detention and threat directed at compelling a government or an international organisation. Sections that followed prescribed punishments, including death or imprisonment for life where the terrorist act resulted in death, and graded terms for conspiracy, organisation of camps, recruitment, harbouring, and possession of proceeds or of unauthorised arms in notified areas. The definitional core of POTA, refined and rearranged, had crossed into the permanent law.

The amendment also created the machinery for declaring terrorist organisations and placed the list in a Schedule to the Act. An organisation could be specified as a terrorist organisation where it was involved in terrorism under the statutory test, with provision for application, notification, and removal, and with offences attached to membership of a terrorist organisation, to raising funds for such an organisation, to organising camps, and to recruitment. The amendment carried over proceeds and forfeiture provisions, directed at the property and funds of terrorism, with powers of attachment, seizure, and forfeiture under stated procedures and with offences concerning the holding and use of proceeds of terrorism. Membership offences crossed as well, in a form that attached punishment to membership of a gang or organisation declared terrorist, and to the acts of support and fund raising that surround formal membership in practice. Taken together, Chapter IV, the Schedule, the proceeds provisions, and the membership offences gave the amended UAPA the shape of a general anti-terror code housed inside a statute that had begun life in 1967 as a law against unlawful associations and secessionist activity. The house was old. Much of the furniture was new, and much of the new furniture had been made for POTA.

Then the honest counter reading must be stated plainly, because an account of transplant that lists only what crossed will mislead about the character of repeal. Two of the hardest devices of POTA did not cross. The confession provision died with POTA and has no descendant in the UAPA text. No section of the amended Act makes a confession to a police officer admissible in a trial for a terrorist act, and a confession in a UAPA prosecution therefore falls under the ordinary law of evidence, under which a confession made to a police officer cannot be proved against the maker and a confession made in police custody requires the presence of a magistrate to be admissible, subject to the narrow and contested discovery exception in the Indian Evidence Act. The broad presumptions did not cross either. The amended Act of 2004 contained no general clause directing a court to presume guilt from recovery of arms or explosives or from fingerprints at a scene, of the kind that Section 53 of POTA had supplied. The consequence was a defined interval in the lineage. The UAPA between 2004 and 2008 ran without a confession clause and without a general presumption clause, even though it contained a terrorist act definition, a schedule of organisations, proceeds provisions, and membership offences modelled on the statute that had just been repealed. Prosecutors in that interval worked with extended investigation periods and other procedural features that later amendments would adjust further, but on confession and presumption the law had returned to the ordinary baseline. That return was deliberate, was understood at the time as the moral centre of repeal, and was defended as the removal of the two devices most closely associated in public argument with coerced statements and with a reversal of the burden that ordinary criminal law treats as fundamental.

What repeal actually did, therefore, was to retire the name and the two most condemned devices while transplanting the definitions, the listing machinery, and the offence structure into the standing statute. The name mattered. POTA had become a political symbol, attached to particular prosecutions and to a particular government, and a permanent law bearing that name could not have commanded the acquiescence that an amendment to a 1967 Act could gather from parties that had voted against POTA itself. The devices mattered as well, and their removal was a real change in the law of evidence for terrorist trials, not a cosmetic one. Yet the continuity mattered most for the long lineage. After December 2004 India again had no separate anti-terror Act with a sunset clause, and after December 2004 India was not without a central law defining and punishing the terrorist act. The centre of gravity had moved from a temporary special statute, renewed and repealed in cycles since 1985, to a permanent chapter inside a standing Act that required no renewal and attracted no automatic parliamentary reconsideration at the end of a fixed term. Every later amendment, in 2008 and afterwards, would build on that settlement by adding to the UAPA rather than by proposing a new named anti-terror Act. Repeal as transplant is the accurate description of 2004, because the year ended a statute and preserved a system, and because the argument that followed for the next generation was no longer whether India should have a special law of terrorism, but how far the permanent law that had quietly absorbed that special law should be allowed to depart from the ordinary criminal process that surrounded it.

## Direct Answers: The Structural Questions

### What is an anti-terror law, structurally?

An anti-terror law is a statute that departs from ordinary criminal procedure in defined ways, through special definitions of terrorist acts, executive listing of organisations and persons, longer pre-charge custody, a harder bail test, presumptions against the accused, admissibility rules unlike the ordinary law of evidence, and designated special forums for trial.

### Why is the UAPA the constant in the lineage?

The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act is the constant because it alone carries no sunset clause, it alone survived every repeal and lapse, and it holds both the association machinery built from 1967 and, since 2004, the terrorism chapters transferred from the Prevention of Terrorism Act.

### What did POTA's repeal actually change?

The repeal changed the name on the statute book, the administering politics surrounding anti-terror enforcement, and two devices, the police confession clause and the broad presumptions, while the core definitions, the listing machinery, the custody periods and the bail restrictions moved into the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act.

### Where does the Indian anti-terror system live?

The system resides in three layers: the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act supplies the central definitions, listing, custody and bail regime, the National Investigation Agency Act supplies the investigating agency and its scheduled offences, and state organised crime Acts supply parallel state machinery, while ordinary codes supply the base offences of murder, explosives and conspiracy.

### What never returned after TADA and POTA died?

Two devices never returned after the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act and the Prevention of Terrorism Act died: the clause that made confessions to senior police officers admissible in evidence, and the broad statutory presumptions that treated recovery, fingerprints or association as proof of guilt without ordinary evidentiary foundations.

The transplant method begins with a refusal to treat a statute as a single event. A statute has a commencement, a political history and a formal repeal or lapse, and a provision inside that statute has a separate biography that may begin earlier and end later than the parent text. The method therefore follows the provision rather than the title page. A definition, a custody period, a bail condition or a rule of evidence is traced from instrument to instrument, and at each move the comparison asks whether the words travelled intact, whether the words travelled with alteration, or whether the words disappeared while a functional substitute appeared elsewhere in the legal order.

The first test is textual. The wording of a provision is placed beside the wording of the claimed successor, and resemblance is measured at the level of phrases, thresholds and consequences rather than at the level of general purpose. Two provisions may share a purpose and still differ in every operative particular, while two provisions in differently named statutes may share the decisive phrase that determines custody, bail or admissibility. Where the decisive phrase survives with minor rearrangement, the transplant is direct. Where the phrase changes but the threshold remains, the transplant is textual in substance and structural in form.

The second test concerns machinery. A provision does not operate through words alone, and a definition without a listing process, a custody rule without a remand court, or a scheduled offence without an investigating agency remains incomplete. The method therefore asks which institution gives the provision effect at each stage: the executive that notifies a ban, the tribunal that confirms that decision, the agency that investigates, the prosecutor who seeks extension of custody, and the court that hears the case. A transplant may preserve the text and change the machinery, and that change can alter practical meaning more deeply than a verbal amendment.

The third test concerns safeguards. Every departure from ordinary procedure carries a stated justification and, in most instruments, a stated limit: a review committee, a tribunal hearing, a judicial extension order, a sanction requirement before prosecution, a time ceiling, or a special rule for recording a confession. The method records the safeguard that accompanied a device in the parent statute and then records the safeguard that accompanied the same device after transfer. A provision that moves with its safeguard intact presents a different case from a provision that moves while the safeguard weakens, disappears or changes hands from a judicial body to an executive body.

The fourth test concerns forum. The place of trial shapes the meaning of every other device, since custody, bail, presumptions and admissibility are argued before a particular court under a particular procedure. Designated courts under the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, special courts under the Prevention of Terrorism Act, courts constituted through the National Investigation Agency Act and ordinary sessions courts trying cases under state legislation do not form a single undifferentiated venue. The method therefore records whether a transferred provision entered a special forum, an ordinary forum or a hybrid arrangement in which a central agency investigated and a designated court tried.

These four tests together produce a stricter vocabulary than survival or continuity alone. A device may be carried, narrowed, widened, relocated, renamed or lost, and each term answers a different factual pattern. Carried means that text, machinery, safeguard and forum remained substantially aligned. Narrowed means that the device remained but its trigger, scope or evidentiary effect contracted. Relocated means that the substance remained while the statutory home, the administering institution or the forum changed. Renamed means that a familiar function continued under a new label without substantial operative change. Lost means that no successor performed the function.

The chart that follows is the working tool of the article because the chart holds those distinctions in a form that argument alone cannot sustain across six instruments and four decades. Prose can explain a single transfer with care, and prose can compare two statutes in sequence, but prose loses precision when seven devices move at different speeds through the same repeal. The chart fixes each device in a row and each instrument in a column, so a horizontal reading gives the biography of a device and a vertical reading gives the legal order at a chosen date. The argument depends upon holding both readings together.

The chart also disciplines the claims made around the chart. A cell that records absence imposes a burden on any sentence that asserts continuity, and a cell that records presence imposes a burden on any sentence that asserts abolition. The ratchet thesis, the repeal thesis and the emergency thesis each gain or lose strength at particular cells rather than across the whole table. Extended detention supports continuity after 2008, but the intervening absence after 2004 matters to chronology. Proscription supports a longer continuity that begins before the terrorism chapters entered the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act.

The sunset argument turns on a distinction between the life of an instrument and the life of its substance. A sunset clause disciplines a temporary statute in a real sense: the legislature must return, debate and re-enact, or the instrument expires, and expiry carries legal consequences for new prosecutions and for official authority. The discipline is temporal and political, and the lapse of 1995 and the repeal of 2004 show that the discipline could bite. Yet a sunset disciplines only the container when a standing statute stands ready to receive the contents. If definitions, listing machinery, custody periods and bail rules can move into a permanent Act through an ordinary amendment, the temporary character of the source statute no longer governs the duration of the substance. The substance escapes the discipline through the structure of the receiving law, which requires no periodic renewal.

That escape changes the politics of accountability in a specific way. Under a temporary statute, continuation requires an affirmative legislative act, and opponents can organise around a known date and a known question, namely whether the exceptional regime deserves further life. Under a permanent statute, termination requires an affirmative act of repeal, and supporters of continuation need only prevent that act. The burden of legislative initiative reverses, and with that burden the burden of public argument also reverses. A sunset therefore remains valuable where the substance shares the fate of the container, but the Indian lineage shows a repeated separation between fate at the level of title and fate at the level of provision. The lesson is narrow: a sunset attached to a statute, without attention to the receiving capacity of permanent law, protects against the wrong unit of survival.

## The Counter-Reading: What the Ratchet Thesis Gets Wrong

The ratchet thesis holds that Indian anti-terror law moves in one direction, accumulating powers through each emergency and retaining those powers after each repeal, so that every temporary statute leaves the permanent order more coercive than the order that preceded it. The counter-reading begins with a subtraction. The police confession clause died with the Prevention of Terrorism Act in 2004, after a life that ran through the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act and the later statute, and no successor clause entered the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act in 2004, in 2008, in 2012 or in 2019. A ratchet that loses a central evidentiary tooth at the decisive repeal requires a more exact description than accumulation alone.

The weight of that subtraction deserves plain statement because the clause had occupied a distinctive place in the exceptional architecture. Ordinary Indian evidence law distrusted confessions made to police officers, for reasons rooted in the risk of coercion within custody, and the anti-terror statutes reversed that distrust by admitting confessions recorded by senior officers under stated conditions. Repeal removed that alteration entirely. Investigators retained extended custody after 2008 and prosecutors retained a demanding bail test, but a statement made to a police officer returned to the ordinary rule of inadmissibility. Any account that treats 2004 as a mere change of label must carry the weight of that return, because a central prosecution tool vanished and stayed vanished through every later amendment.

The presumptions tell a second story of narrowing rather than simple transfer. Under the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, Section 21 supplied broad presumptions that could arise from recovery of arms or explosives, from fingerprints at a scene and from other specified circumstances, and the breadth of those presumptions shifted the practical burden inside a trial. The presumption that entered the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act through Section 43E after 2008 was narrow, tied to recovery of arms, explosives or other specified material from the possession of the accused and to expert evidence connecting that material with the offence. The device moved, contracted and changed its trigger, and a thesis that records only movement misses the contraction.

The association side of the lineage supplies a third correction, centred on the tribunal. From the original Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act onward, a ban on an unlawful association required confirmation through a tribunal presided over by a judge of a High Court, with notice, hearing and a reasoned determination within a statutory structure. Successive amendments altered periods, procedures and surrounding definitions, and the terrorism chapters added a scheduled list for terrorist organisations with review arrangements, but the association ban at the heart of the 1967 Act retained a judicial confirmation stage throughout the lineage. The point concerns location of authority, and on that question the lineage shows continuity of judicial control rather than steady executive capture of the listing function.

The sunset supplies a fourth correction. A sunset clause is not a nullity merely because substance may travel after expiry or repeal. The temporary character of the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act forced a public reckoning in 1995, when Parliament allowed the Act to lapse after years of controversy over misuse, low conviction figures and detention on a large scale. The temporary character and repeal politics of the Prevention of Terrorism Act forced another reckoning in 2004, when a new government carried repeal as an electoral commitment and placed surviving provisions into permanent law through a separate amendment that opponents could contest on separate terms.

A serious defence of the transplant also exists, and the counter-reading gains strength by stating that defence in a strong and respectable form. Terrorism did not lapse when the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act lapsed in 1995, and terrorist violence did not repeal itself when the Prevention of Terrorism Act fell in 2004. A legislature that believed the offences real, the investigative difficulties genuine and the ordinary law inadequate faced a practical question of housing: where should definitions of terrorist acts, proscription of terrorist organisations, extended investigation periods and special trial arrangements reside if the temporary container had become politically or constitutionally untenable. Housing those provisions in a permanent statute, subject to ordinary amendment politics, judicial review and electoral accountability, carries an argument of honesty. Serial temporary statutes can normalise emergency while pretending impermanence, whereas codification in permanent law states openly that the legislature regards the offences and the machinery as part of the settled legal order.

That defence deserves respect because it identifies real costs in the alternative that critics sometimes leave unexamined. A permanent home allows courts to develop stable interpretation over time, allows practitioners and accused persons to know the governing text without tracking ordinances and renewal Acts, and allows amendment through the ordinary legislative calendar rather than through the compressed politics of expiry. On that view, the transplant of 2004 represented not concealment but translation, from an emergency framed statute associated with a particular government and a particular controversy into a general statute that had existed since 1967 for the regulation of unlawful associations.

The answer to the defence begins with a concession and then turns on burden and visibility. The concession is that housing offences in permanent law is not illegitimate in itself, and a legislature may rationally decide that terrorism requires standing definitions and standing investigative machinery. A repeal presented to the public as the end of a discredited statute carried a political meaning larger than a relocation of provisions, and the relocation preserved the most contested custody and bail features after a short interval while removing the confession clause and narrowing the presumptions. Democratic honesty therefore depends upon the clarity with which the receiving amendment is described at the moment of transfer.

A further answer concerns asymmetry after codification. Ordinary amendment politics operates under no deadline for the exceptional provisions inside a permanent Act, so inertia favours retention regardless of the original justification, changed threat assessments or accumulated evidence about use and misuse. A legislature persuaded by the honesty argument could meet the objection through periodic review clauses, reporting duties and tribunal transparency inside the permanent statute, preserving codification while restoring moments of scrutiny. The absence of a comparable general review discipline in the receiving structure remains the point at which the defence weakens.

The counter-reading therefore refines the thesis rather than replacing one slogan with another. The lineage shows retention in definitions, listing, custody and bail, subtraction in confessions, contraction in presumptions, judicial continuity in association tribunals, genuine reckoning under temporary status and genuine addition in individual designation in 2019. A ratchet that sometimes slips, narrows and pauses is still a powerful pattern, but the pattern is uneven in ways that matter to courts, legislators and accused persons. The thesis survives the counter-reading in a disciplined form, as an account of direction with exceptions that carry legal and political weight.

## The Lineage on One Page: A Chronology in Prose

The lineage begins with the Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act of 1963, which added sovereignty and integrity of India as grounds for reasonable restrictions on freedoms of speech, assembly and association. That amendment supplied constitutional room for legislation aimed at secessionist and disruptive associations, before any terrorism statute existed in the later sense. The association became the first legal unit of concern: the state identified collective activity said to threaten sovereignty or integrity, restricted the collective form and attached criminal consequences to membership and support after prohibition.

Parliament enacted the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act in 1967 as a permanent statute for declaring associations unlawful, constituting a tribunal under a High Court judge to test a declaration, and penalising membership and support after a ban took effect. The Act contained no terrorism chapters of the later kind, no schedule of terrorist organisations in the later form and no designation of individuals as terrorists. The original design nevertheless established three durable elements: executive initiation of a ban, judicial confirmation through a tribunal and criminal liability organised around association rather than around a completed violent act alone.

The Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act arrived first in 1985 as a temporary response to terrorism centred heavily on Punjab, and Parliament replaced and strengthened the experiment through the Act of 1987, which continued through successive extensions until 1995. TADA supplied a definition of terrorist acts, designated courts, extended custody that could reach one year, a stringent bail condition in Section 20(8), admissibility of confessions made to senior police officers under Section 15 and broad presumptions under Section 21.

In Kartar Singh v State of Punjab in 1994, the Supreme Court upheld the broad constitutional validity of TADA while reading safeguards into several provisions and limiting particular elements. The judgment accepted legislative competence and the exceptional character claimed for terrorist offences, yet the reasoning insisted upon procedural conditions around confessions, custody and designated courts. Kartar Singh therefore occupies an ambivalent place: the decision preserved the statute and legitimated a distinct anti-terror procedure, and the decision simultaneously recorded the constitutional price of that distinctness in safeguards and interpretive limits.

TADA lapsed in 1995 when Parliament declined to extend the Act further, and the lapse ended authority to invoke TADA provisions for fresh conduct, while trials already constituted continued under savings arrangements. The government promoted a Criminal Law Amendment Bill as a successor that would place selected anti-terror provisions inside ordinary criminal law, but the Bill failed to secure enactment. The failure matters because the interval after 1995 was not an interval without violence or without state response: ordinary penal provisions, explosives legislation, arms legislation and state laws carried the load.

Maharashtra enacted the Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act in 1999, and that state statute became a major link between the lapse of TADA and the arrival of POTA. MCOCA addressed organised crime and the syndicate form, supplied stringent bail conditions, extended custody, special courts and admissibility of confessions to senior police officers under safeguards, and later supplied a model that other states adapted.

The Prevention of Terrorism Ordinance of 2001 and the Prevention of Terrorism Act of 2002 restored a central anti-terror statute after the attack on Parliament in December 2001 sharpened legislative demand. POTA defined terrorist acts, created a schedule of terrorist organisations, established special courts, permitted custody up to 180 days, restricted bail, admitted confessions to senior police officers under Section 32 and supplied presumptions under Section 53, alongside review committees. Passage required a joint sitting of Parliament after disagreement between the Houses.

In PUCL v Union of India in 2003, the Supreme Court upheld POTA against constitutional challenge while interpreting provisions and emphasising safeguards, a posture that recalled Kartar Singh in broad outline. The decision treated the legislative judgment about terrorism with deference and sustained the special framework, yet the political life of POTA continued through review committee findings, state level disputes and opposition commitments.

Repeal arrived in 2004 through the Prevention of Terrorism (Repeal) Act, and the same legislative moment carried the transplant through amendments to the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act that inserted chapters on terrorist acts, terrorist organisations, forfeiture and related offences. The schedule moved, the definitions moved in adapted form and the association statute acquired a second identity as the central anti-terror law, while the police confession clause and the broad presumptions did not move. Custody and bail in the receiving Act initially followed ordinary code periods and ordinary tests, a detail that chronology must preserve because the harder regime returned only in stages.

In 2005 the Committee on Review of Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, chaired by Justice B P Jeevan Reddy, recommended repeal of [AFSPA](/2010/08/15/afspa-1958-explained/) with its essential provisions incorporated into the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, and the government did not accept that recommendation. The episode belongs in the chronology because the proposal treated the UAPA as the natural receiving statute for exceptional powers drawn from a different and older security law, exactly the receiving capacity that the transplant of 2004 had demonstrated.

The amendments of 2008, enacted after the Mumbai attacks of November 2008 together with the National Investigation Agency Act, hardened the receiving statute and built the investigative institution that earlier transplants had lacked. Section 43D supplied custody up to 180 days through judicial extension, a demanding bail rule in Section 43D(5) and a narrow presumption through Section 43E, while definitions expanded. The National Investigation Agency Act created a central agency for scheduled offences, empowered the centre to direct investigation of cases with inter-state or international dimensions and provided for special courts.

The amendment Act passed in 2012, cited as Act 3 of 2013, extended the duration of a ban on an unlawful association from two years to five years and widened the field through offences connected with economic security, including provisions addressed to counterfeit currency and material damaging to the monetary stability of India, together with liability addressed to companies and other entities. The five year ban altered the rhythm of tribunal confirmation and renewal, reducing the frequency of the exercise that the original design had imposed at two year intervals.

The amendments of 2019 completed the period covered by the chart through two connected movements: individual designation under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act and expansion of the National Investigation Agency. The amendment Act empowered the government to designate an individual as a terrorist and place that individual in the Fourth Schedule, extending a listing logic previously applied to organisations into a personal status carrying grave reputational, financial and legal consequences. The National Investigation Agency (Amendment) Act widened scheduled offences, extended agency reach to offences committed outside India in defined circumstances and adjusted the special court framework.

## 2008 and After: The Settlement That Followed the Mumbai Attacks

The attacks in Mumbai between 26 and 29 November 2008 became the hinge on which the later history of Indian anti-terror law turned. Ten attackers arrived by sea and moved across the city in separate groups, striking a major railway terminus, hotels, a hospital, a cafe, a Jewish centre, and other public places, and the violence unfolded as a siege rather than as a single explosion. Firing, hostage taking, and prolonged encounters continued over three nights and into a fourth day, and the siege was broadcast live, with television cameras carrying the operations of the security forces, the burning of hotel floors, and the uncertainty of families outside cordons into homes across the country. Earlier bombings had been sudden and, however devastating, had ended at the moment of detonation. Mumbai presented armed men holding ground inside the financial capital, communicating with handlers, selecting targets in sequence, and resisting assault for an extended period while coordination among police, commando, intelligence, and naval elements appeared improvised. In the reading that governed the legislative response, the attacks exposed a gap between a criminal law built around offences within a single state and a form of terrorism planned abroad, financed across borders, executed by a small team, and directed in part from outside Indian territory. The response did not create a new standalone code of the kind represented earlier by the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act or the Prevention of Terrorism Act. It amended the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, a permanent statute of long standing, and created a federal investigative agency beside that statute. That choice of form mattered, because amendments to a permanent law carry no expiry date and accumulate layer upon layer until the amended text bears little resemblance to the text as first enacted.

The first layer was the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act 2008, Act 35 of 2008, which inserted Sections 43A to 43F. Section 43A conferred powers of arrest, search, and seizure adapted to terrorist offences, enabling an officer within the scope of the provision to arrest a person believed to have committed an offence under the Act, to search persons, premises, and conveyances in connection with such an offence, and to seize material relevant to the investigation. The provision placed the urgency of the first hours of a terrorism investigation, when documents, devices, weapons, and substances must be preserved, inside the permanent statute rather than in temporary legislation.

Section 43D governed custody and bail. Section 43D(2) extended the period within which investigation could be completed and the charge-sheet filed from 90 days to 180 days, where the court was satisfied, on the report of the public prosecutor indicating the progress of the investigation and the specific reasons for detention beyond the initial period, that extension was warranted, and police custody could extend to 30 days within that scheme. The combined effect was longer investigative detention before charge, reflecting the official view that terrorist conspiracies with dispersed participants, foreign links, and layered finance could not be unravelled within a shorter span. Section 43D(4) barred anticipatory bail in cases within the specified scope, so that a person apprehending arrest could not obtain a direction that arrest, if it occurred, should be followed by release on bail. Section 43D(5) provided that an accused person was not to be released on bail, or on personal bond, if the court, on a perusal of the case diary or the police report, found reasonable grounds for believing that the accusations were prima facie true. The test altered the accustomed approach, because the court was required at the threshold to form a view, on material collected by the investigating agency and not tested by cross examination, about the apparent truth of the accusation itself. In practice the clause made the case diary and the charge-sheet the centre of the bail hearing and lengthened pre-trial detention where bail was refused.

Section 43E introduced a presumption confined to defined recovery situations. Where arms or explosives or other substances specified for the purpose were recovered from the possession of the accused and there was reason to believe that such arms, explosives, or substances of a similar nature were used in the commission of a terrorist act, or where fingerprint evidence linked the accused to the offence by being found at the site or on connected material, the court was to presume, unless the contrary was shown, that the accused had committed the offence. The provision was deliberately narrower than Section 21 of the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, which had raised presumptions from a far wider set of triggers and had drawn sustained criticism for that breadth. Section 43E allowed the legislature to place distance between the new settlement and the repealed special laws while still shifting, in the specified situations, an evidential burden onto the accused at trial.

Section 43F imposed an obligation to furnish information, so that a person aware of information relevant to an offence or to the activities of an offender could be required to supply it to the investigating officer, with penal consequences for failure to furnish information or for furnishing information known to be false, subject to the protections stated in the provision. The amendment also inserted Section 51A, which empowered freezing of funds and assets, and related prohibitions, in implementation of the framework of the United Nations Security Council for the prevention and suppression of terrorism financing, so that financial restriction could operate on designation and executive action without awaiting conviction. The substantive definition was widened at the same time. Section 15 was expanded by the words by any other means of whatever nature, so that the means by which the listed harm was caused were not confined to bombs, firearms, lethal weapons, gases, chemicals, or biological, radioactive, or nuclear substances, and the interests that a terrorist act might threaten were enlarged by the addition of economic security alongside the unity, integrity, security, and sovereignty of India. Economic security entered the definition in 2008 in general form, and later amendments returned to the phrase and gave it specific content.

The second institution of the settlement was created by the National Investigation Agency Act 2008, Act 34 of 2008, which received assent on 31 December 2008. The Act established the National Investigation Agency as a federal investigative agency for scheduled offences, a list that included offences under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act together with other offences affecting the sovereignty, security, and integrity of India, and it provided for special courts for the trial of scheduled offences. The agency was constituted in January 2009. Its creation was the institutional answer to investigations that cross state lines, because an investigation spanning several states, international borders, and multiple agencies lacked a single authority able to take charge, coordinate intelligence and evidence, and pursue a case from first information through charge-sheet to trial without repeated negotiation among jurisdictions. Under the scheme of the Act, the central government could direct the agency to investigate a scheduled offence, state governments could refer cases, and officers of the agency exercised powers of investigation across India. The constitutional setting remained that of a Union law operating in a field where public order and police ordinarily fall to the states, and the justification rested on the inter-state and international character of the offences. After January 2009 India possessed, on a permanent footing, a central agency whose ordinary business was the investigation of terrorist and related scheduled offences, and that fact altered the practical meaning of the substantive statute, because a wide definition of terrorism operates differently when a specialised agency exists to invoke it and to carry cases before special courts.

The next layer arrived with the amendment passed in December 2012 and numbered Act 3 of 2013. The phrase economic security was elaborated through the counterfeit currency clause in Section 15, so that damage to the monetary stability of India by the production, smuggling, or circulation of high quality counterfeit Indian currency fell within the description of a terrorist act where the other elements of the definition were present. The elaboration identified debasement of the currency through counterfeiting of a quality capable of affecting confidence and stability as terrorist harm rather than as a mere offence against coinage and currency, and it tied the abstract phrase economic security to monetary stability as its organising idea. It reflected an official assessment that counterfeit currency networks were connected with hostile agencies and terrorist finance, and that the harm inflicted was systemic rather than confined to the loss suffered by an individual holder of a forged note.

The same amendment extended and widened the organisational provisions. The period of an unlawful association declaration under Section 6 was extended from two years to five years, so that a declaration confirmed by the tribunal operated for five years unless revoked earlier, reducing the frequency with which the executive had to return to the tribunal process and lengthening the interval during which membership, support, fundraising, and other connected offences attached to the declared association. Sections 22A to 22C dealt with offences by companies, societies, and trusts, making the entity itself, together with persons in charge of its conduct in the manner provided, answerable where an offence was committed by or through such a body, and recognising that terrorist finance, logistics, recruitment, and property holding frequently move through incorporated or registered forms and through accounts and premises held in an organisational name. The amendment also enlarged proceeds of terrorism, so that property derived or obtained from a terrorist act or a terrorist offence, property connected with terrorist funding, and property representing or traceable to such value fell more completely within attachment and forfeiture, giving the investigative authority a firmer basis for following value through substitutions and transfers. The direction of travel was consistent, treating terrorism not only as violence by an individual but as an enterprise sustained by money, by entities, and by property that could be identified and immobilised.

The third layer arrived with the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act 2019, Act 28 of 2019. Its central innovation was designation of individuals as terrorists through Section 35 and the Fourth Schedule, so that the central government could add the name of an individual believed to be involved in terrorism to the Fourth Schedule by notification, with a procedure for application for removal and for review in the manner provided. Before 2019 the listing power had operated in its principal form on organisations, which could be named in the Schedules or declared unlawful, with consequences flowing to members and supporters. After 2019 the listing power reached the person and not only the organisation, without the mediation of membership, and designation could attach stigma, restrictions, and legal consequences to a named individual on executive assessment, subject to the review arrangements in the statute, rather than only after conviction by a court. Supporters argued that prominent terrorists operated across organisational labels and founded new groups when old groups were listed. Opponents argued that executive designation of a person as a terrorist, in a statute governing bail, finance, and property, placed a grave determination in administrative hands and altered the relationship between accusation and adjudication. The amendment was enacted in that contested form, and the Fourth Schedule became a permanent feature of the Act.

Other provisions of the 2019 amendment adjusted investigation and property control. Approval of the Director General of the National Investigation Agency was required for seizure and attachment of property in cases investigated by the agency, aligning the authority to approve property measures with the authority conducting the federal investigation. Investigation by officers of inspector rank was permitted, enlarging the pool of officers competent to investigate offences under the Act and reflecting the volume and dispersal of such cases. The Second Schedule was enlarged by the addition of the nuclear terrorism convention, the International Convention for the Suppression of Acts of Nuclear Terrorism, connecting domestic offences more fully with that international instrument. The National Investigation Agency Amendment of the same year widened the scheduled offences of the agency and gave it reach over offences committed outside India in defined circumstances, including offences affecting Indian citizens and interests abroad where an Indian connection justified investigation and trial in India. The pairing of the two amendments of 2019 was characteristic of the settlement, because the substantive statute was widened at the point of the person while the investigative agency was widened at the point of subject matter and territory.

The significance of the successive amendments lies also in the manner in which the devices interlock in a single prosecution. A case could begin with arrest, search, and seizure under Section 43A, proceed through an investigation extended to 180 days with police custody of up to 30 days, encounter the bar on anticipatory bail and the prima facie test at the stage of regular bail, rest at trial in part on a presumption arising from the recovery of arms or explosives or from fingerprint evidence, draw information from persons obliged under Section 43F to furnish it, and run in parallel with the freezing of funds and assets under Section 51A and with attachment of property treated as proceeds of terrorism. Where the National Investigation Agency conducted the investigation, the same case could be tried before a special court, with property measures approved by the Director General of the agency and investigation carried out by an officer of inspector rank. Where an organisation stood declared unlawful for five years, or an individual stood designated in the Fourth Schedule, offences of membership, support, and funding acquired a defined statutory anchor independent of proof of a particular violent act. No single provision created that combined effect. The effect was produced by accumulation, as each amendment answered a perceived gap left by the previous text and left the previous text otherwise untouched, so that a practitioner reading the Act in 2019 confronted a statute whose centre of gravity had shifted entirely from the suppression of unlawful associations, the original concern of the legislation, toward the investigation, detention, trial, financing, and designation architecture of counter-terrorism, without any single enactment having presented that architecture to Parliament as a whole for approval as a complete code.

The shape of the settlement is therefore unmistakable. Every device in the standing statute arrived by amendment after a crisis, and none of the amendments carried a sunset. The extended charge-sheet period, the restriction on bail, the confined presumption, the obligation to furnish information, the freezing of funds and assets, and the federal agency arrived after Mumbai. The elaboration of economic security through counterfeit currency, the five year declaration for unlawful associations, the offences by companies, societies, and trusts, and the enlargement of proceeds of terrorism arrived in the amendment passed in December 2012. Individual designation, inspector rank investigation, Director General approval for property measures in agency cases, and the wider reach of the agency arrived in 2019, again by addition and again without expiry. Earlier generations of anti-terror legislation in India had been temporary in form, had lapsed or been repealed, and had forced Parliament, however imperfectly, to decide whether exceptional powers should continue. The settlement that followed the Mumbai attacks abandoned that discipline. It placed exceptional procedure, an enlarged definition, organisational proscription for extended periods, entity liability, asset freezing under an international framework, executive designation of organisations and then of individuals, and a permanent federal investigative agency inside, or alongside, a permanent statute, and left each addition to endure until a future Parliament chose affirmatively to remove it. Its consequences for detention, bail, finance, association, and the distribution of investigative authority between the centre and the states flow less from any single section than from the cumulative fact that the sections were never required to justify themselves again by surviving a sunset.

## The Constitutional Machinery That Permits All of This

The lineage traced through preventive detention, TADA, POTA, the state organised crime statutes and the successive enlargements of the UAPA rests on a constitutional foundation laid before any of those statutes existed, and the foundation explains why the argument in India has so rarely been about whether Parliament may legislate against terrorism at all. Entry 9 of the Union List places preventive detention for reasons connected with defence, foreign affairs or the security of India within exclusive Union competence, and Entry 1 of the same list, covering defence of India and every part thereof, together with the residuary power under Article 248 and Entry 97, supplies a broad base for legislation directed at organised violence aimed at the Union itself. Entry 1 of the Concurrent List covers criminal law, including all matters included in the Indian Penal Code at the commencement of the Constitution, and Entry 2 covers criminal procedure, including all matters included in the Code of Criminal Procedure at that commencement. Terrorist offences, conspiracies, membership offences, financing offences, special courts, altered bail rules, extended remand periods and modified evidentiary provisions all fall naturally inside those two entries, and Parliament legislating under them legislates on subjects on which state legislatures may also legislate, subject to the repugnancy rule in Article 254. The result is a field concurrent in form but central in gravity, because a central law prevails over an inconsistent state law to the extent of the inconsistency, and because the definition of the offence and the procedure of the special court, the subjects that matter most in a terrorist prosecution, are precisely the subjects on which the central statute speaks in exhaustive detail.

State legislation on organised crime fits into the same machinery through a deliberate constitutional opening. Article 254(2) provides that where a state law on a Concurrent List subject is repugnant to an earlier central law, the state law shall nevertheless prevail in that state if it has been reserved for the consideration of the President and has received presidential assent. The Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act, 1999 travelled exactly that route, and the state statutes modelled upon it in Karnataka, Gujarat and Uttar Pradesh did the same, each obtaining assent and each therefore operating inside the state despite provisions on interception, confessions and bail that cut across the central procedural code. The assent mechanism performs two functions at once. It preserves a formal federal check, because the centre must examine a state design that departs from the central template, and it simultaneously authorises departure, because assent, once given, cures the repugnancy that would otherwise have displaced the state provision. Federalism in this area has therefore not operated as a barrier to the special law model. It has operated as a second channel through which the model could flow when the central channel was closed by a sunset or a repeal.

The substantive restriction ground that sustains bans and designations has its own constitutional history. The Sixteenth Amendment of 1963 inserted the words sovereignty and integrity of India into Article 19(2), Article 19(3) and Article 19(4), so that reasonable restrictions could be imposed on the freedoms of speech, assembly and association in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India. A law penalising advocacy of cession or secession, or action intended to disrupt the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India, could thereafter be defended as a restriction referable to an enumerated ground rather than as a general public order measure, and the definition of unlawful activity in the UAPA, with its references to cession, secession and disaffection, draws directly on that vocabulary.

Against that enabling structure stands one principal procedural floor, and its limits are as instructive as its protections. Article 22 guarantees to a person arrested under an ordinary law the right to be informed of the grounds of arrest, the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner, and the right to be produced before a magistrate within twenty four hours. For preventive detention, Article 22 substitutes a different regime: communication of grounds, an opportunity to make a representation, and review by an Advisory Board for detention beyond three months, with express permission in Article 22(7) for Parliament to prescribe longer periods and classes of case in which board consultation may be dispensed with. The critical point is that Article 22 governs detention, and its most elaborate safeguards attach to detention without trial. A prosecution under TADA, POTA or the UAPA is not preventive detention in the constitutional sense, even when the accused spends long periods in custody because bail is restricted and trial is slow. The detainee under a preventive detention order obtains the Article 22 machinery of grounds, representation and board review, while the undertrial under a special statute obtains the protections of the procedural code as modified by that statute, together with Articles 20 and 21.

The courts, presented repeatedly with challenges to statutes built on this machinery, settled early upon a posture that has held across four decades: uphold and regulate rather than strike down, read safeguards into elastic provisions, narrow definitions by construction, and leave the legislative architecture standing while disciplining its application. Kartar Singh v State of Punjab, decided in 1994, is the fullest expression of the method. The Constitution Bench upheld the core of TADA, including the special courts and the admissibility, under conditions, of confessions made to senior police officers, while surrounding that admissibility with guidelines on recording, warning, reflection time and magisterial production, and while reading down provisions whose literal breadth would have captured conduct far removed from terrorism. PUCL v Union of India, decided in 2004 in the challenge to POTA, repeated the pattern with a similar result. The Supreme Court upheld the Act in substance, sustained the review committee machinery, interpreted the support and membership provisions with qualifications designed to require culpable intent, and declined to treat abuse in administration as a ground for invalidation of the law itself. A legislature enacting a successor statute could therefore proceed on the settled expectation that the court would preserve the framework and correct at the margins, and that expectation lowered the constitutional risk of each successive transplant.

## Unlawful Association and Terrorist Organisation: Two Machines, One Statute

The UAPA contains within a single statute book two distinct machines for proscription, built in different decades for different purposes, operating through different procedures, and attaching different safeguards to the act of listing. The older machine is the unlawful association route, inherited from the original design of 1967. Under Section 3, the central government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, declare an association to be unlawful if, in its opinion, the association has for its object any unlawful activity, or any activity punishable under the provisions the section specifies, or if the association encourages or aids persons to undertake such activity, or if its members undertake such activity. The declaration does not rest on executive opinion alone for its duration. Section 4 requires the notification to be referred, within thirty days of publication, to the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Tribunal, constituted by a sitting judge of a High Court, which must inquire whether there was sufficient cause for the declaration and must either confirm the notification or cancel it. A declaration so confirmed endures, since the amendment of 2012, for a period of five years, extended from the earlier two year term, so that a single confirmation sustains a long ban subject to fresh declaration and fresh confirmation thereafter. The offences that follow are principally those in Section 10, which penalises membership of an unlawful association, attending its meetings, contributing to it or soliciting contributions for it, and Section 13, which penalises the commission, advocacy, abetment or incitement of an unlawful activity itself. The underlying concept, defined in Section 2(o), is unlawful activity in relation to an individual or association, meaning action intended, or supported, to bring about the cession of a part of the territory of India or the secession of a part of that territory, action intended to disrupt or question the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India, or action intended to cause disaffection against India.

The second machine is the terrorist organisation route, introduced when the UAPA was recast in 2004 as the vehicle for the substance of the repealed POTA, and elaborated thereafter. Under Section 35, the central government may, by order published in the Official Gazette, add an organisation to the First Schedule as a terrorist organisation, or add a name by which such an organisation is known, where it believes the organisation is involved in terrorism. No tribunal confirmation follows. The listing takes effect on the executive act, and the remedies lie afterwards, by application and review rather than by prior judicial inquiry. Section 36 permits the listed organisation to apply to the central government for removal from the Schedule, and provides for an application to be placed before a review committee where the government does not grant it, while Section 37 governs the constitution and procedure of review committees for this purpose, chaired by a sitting or retired judge of a High Court and empowered to examine the material and report on whether the listing should stand. The committee model differs from the tribunal model in sequence, in initiative and in effect. The tribunal under Section 4 must be moved by the government within a fixed period and its confirmation is a condition of the declaration continuing; the review committee is engaged after listing, commonly on the application of the affected organisation, and the listing operates in the interval regardless of the pace or outcome of review. The offences attached to the second machine sit principally in Chapter IV and in the cognate provisions on membership and support. Section 38 penalises membership of a terrorist organisation, with a proviso directed at a person who became a member before the organisation was listed and who has not taken part in its activities after listing, and Section 39 penalises support given or invited for a terrorist organisation, whether by seeking support, addressing a meeting for the purpose of encouraging support or inviting persons to support, or arranging a meeting knowing that it is to support the organisation or further its activities.

The doctrinal consequence of housing both machines in one statute became sharper with the amendment of 2019, which extended the second machine from organisations to individuals. The amendment empowered the central government to designate an individual as a terrorist and to insert the name in a new Fourth Schedule, applying the Section 35 listing logic and the Sections 36 and 37 application and review logic to a named person. Individual designation carries consequences that organisation listing distributes across a collectivity: a designated person bears the terrorist label personally, faces the stigma and the practical disabilities that flow from notification, and becomes the reference point for offences committed by others in support of or association with that person, all on the basis of an executive satisfaction reviewed after the fact rather than confirmed in advance by a tribunal of the Section 4 kind. An association accused of secessionist unlawful activity receives tribunal scrutiny as part of the original design, whereas an individual accused of terrorism receives executive designation with post listing review as the principal remedy. The difference between the two machines is therefore not a drafting accident. It is the difference between the 1967 design, in which proscription was conceived as an exceptional restraint on association requiring judicial confirmation, and the 2004 design, in which proscription was conceived as one instrument among several in a counter terrorism code, to be deployed at executive speed and corrected through committees, applications and judicial review in the ordinary courts. The statute book holds both conceptions without reconciling them, and every prosecution that begins with a listing begins by choosing which conception of state power the case will inhabit.

## What the Lineage Leaves Open

A lineage can explain how a body of law arrived at its present arrangement without settling whether that arrangement should continue, and the Indian anti-terror sequence leaves a set of questions open precisely because its history answers the question of origin so completely. The first concerns the sunset clause, the device that disciplined TADA and POTA by forcing Parliament to confront expiry, to debate renewal, and in the case of POTA to accept repeal after a change of government and a record of documented misuse. TADA was allowed to lapse in 1995 after successive extensions had become politically costly, and POTA was repealed in 2004 within its own short life, and in each instance the sunset or the repeal performed a clearing function, terminating the special confession and bail regimes and requiring any successor policy to be argued afresh. The UAPA carries no sunset. Its permanence was a deliberate feature of the transplant strategy of 2004, in which the substance of a temporary law was relocated into a permanent law in order to escape the cycle of extension debates. The alternative view holds that the transplant route has made sunsets pointless in substance even where they survive in form, because a legislature determined to preserve a regime can always migrate its provisions into a permanent host before the temporary vehicle expires, as happened in 2004, and can rely on state legislation under Article 254(2) assent to hold the ground in the interval. A sunset that can be defeated by relocation disciplines only a legislature that accepts the discipline, and the history offers evidence for both readings: expiry did end TADA as a statute, yet much of TADA reasoning on confessions, bail and special courts survived in MCOCA and in the enlarged UAPA.

The second open question concerns individual designation and the proportionality of its safeguards to its consequences. Designation of an organisation disperses its burdens across members, donors and sympathisers, but designation of an individual concentrates the burden in a single legal identity, and the concentration changes the procedural calculation. A person so designated faces reputational destruction that no later removal fully repairs, exposure of associates to investigation by reason of the association itself, and a practical reversal of initiative in which the designated person must apply for removal and pursue review against a decision taken on material that may not be fully disclosed. The safeguards in Sections 36 and 37, application to the government that made the order and consideration by a review committee, were designed for organisations that possess offices, records and legal representatives, and their adequacy for an individual, who may be outside the jurisdiction, in custody, or without the resources to contest classified material, remains untested at the scale the power permits. The lineage supplies no answer, because the individual power arrived in 2019 as an extension of the organisation power, justified by analogy rather than by a fresh procedural design fitted to the individual case.

The third open question runs in the federal direction and asks whether the state statutes are converging on the central template or drifting from it. Convergence is visible in the shared inheritance: special courts, restrictive bail formulations, provisions on interception and on the evidentiary use of intercepted material, and definitions of organised crime or terrorist activity that track central language closely enough to permit coordinated investigation across jurisdictions. Drift is visible in the particulars that matter at trial: variations in the definition of organised crime and in the requirement of prior charge sheets, differences in the seniority of officers competent to record confessions or sanction interception, divergent appellate routes, and uneven amendment histories in which one state updates its statute after a central amendment while another retains an older text. Assent under Article 254(2) freezes a state design at the moment of approval, while the central template continues to move, so divergence accumulates by the ordinary operation of time even where no legislature intends it.

The fourth observation concerns the direction of reform proposals in the field, and it may be the most revealing evidence for the thesis that runs through the whole sequence. Proposals from law commissions, parliamentary committees, bar associations, civil liberties organisations and academic commentators differ sharply in ambition, yet they cluster with notable consistency around three aims: narrowing the definitions, in particular the definitions of terrorist act, unlawful activity and membership, so that intention and knowledge requirements are stated expressly and peripheral conduct is excluded; restoring an ordinary bail jurisprudence, by amending or removing the prima facie bar in Section 43D(5) and returning the special court to the considerations that govern bail elsewhere in criminal law; and strengthening listing review, by introducing prior judicial confirmation, time bound committee decisions, fuller disclosure, or an independent reviewer with access to the complete file. Repeal of the UAPA as a whole, by contrast, occupies a marginal place in serious legislative discussion, advanced by some campaigns and manifestos but seldom translated into a bill with prospects of passage, and almost never accompanied by a worked alternative for the financing, listing and cross border investigation provisions that even critics concede address real phenomena. Each predecessor law ended, and each ending was followed not by an interregnum of ordinary law but by a successor that preserved the core in altered form, until permanence itself became the premise shared by proponents and by most critics. To read the next amendment with that history in mind is to apply a simple discipline before accepting any claim made for it, whether the claim is novelty, necessity or moderation. The reader of the next amendment should ask what it moves, what it retires, and where the retired substance goes.

## The Operational Case, Stated at Full Strength

The operational case begins, in the telling of successive governments and of security officials speaking in the parliamentary debates, with the condition of the ordinary trial in an area of organised insurgency. Witnesses in such conditions do not face a private fear alone. A witness faces a structured threat, delivered through an organisation with local knowledge, local presence, and a memory longer than any single prosecution. Intimidation produces silence, silence produces hostile witnesses, and hostile witnesses produce acquittals that have little relation to the underlying facts. Protection under the ordinary process, designed for the isolated dispute between individuals, offers a witness little shelter against a network able to reach a family, a village, or a place of work. From this starting point, the state case treats special procedure not as a preference for severity but as a response to a documented collapse, in which the forms of a fair trial remained intact while the substance of testimony disappeared.

The second strand concerns the character of the violence itself. Successive governments argued in Parliament, and the Law Commission work on terrorism legislation recorded in similar terms, that organised and externally supported violence differs in kind from ordinary crime. Ordinary policing is built around an incident, a complaint, an investigation, and a charge against identified persons for identified acts. A terrorist campaign reverses that order. Finance moves through informal channels, training and direction move across borders, material moves through couriers who may know little of the final act, and responsibility is deliberately fragmented so that no single participant carries the whole design. A process confined to the final incident therefore reaches the least important figure in the chain while leaving untouched the structure that recruited, funded, equipped, and directed that figure.

It follows, in this argument, that the organisation and not only the act must be the unit of legal attention. Listing machinery exists because a campaign is sustained between incidents, through recruitment, fundraising, propaganda, safe housing, and the maintenance of a name under which all of that activity coheres. A law that waits for a completed act before intervening treats the interval between attacks as a legally empty space, although that interval is precisely where the next attack is prepared. Listing an association or an organisation, with the consequences that follow for membership, support, and funds, is presented by its defenders as the only device that matches the temporal shape of the threat, reaching the organisation during the long periods of preparation rather than only at the moment of violence.

Custody and bail form the fourth strand, and the argument is stated frankly by its makers. Rules of bail and custody designed for ordinary crime assume a defendant whose capacity to interfere with the process is limited and whose associates, if any, are few. Against a network, release is not a neutral restoration of liberty pending trial. Release returns a trained and connected person to the structure that sustained the offence, restores a channel of instruction to witnesses and co accused persons, and in documented instances has allowed flight across borders that ordinary sureties cannot prevent. Extended custody before charge is defended on a matching ground, namely that investigation across jurisdictions, languages, financial trails, and encrypted or fragmented records cannot be completed within periods fixed for a local investigation, and that premature charge on incomplete material serves neither the prosecution nor the accused.

The fifth strand is historical and, for its makers, decisive. Every device in the standing statute is said to answer a specific and documented failure of the ordinary process rather than a general appetite for power. Special courts answered delay and the scattering of connected trials across distant forums. Modified rules on confession and evidence in the repealed statutes answered the refusal of witnesses to repeat in open court statements given to investigators under threat. Restrictions on bail answered intimidation mounted from custody through associates at liberty. Provisions on forfeiture and attachment answered the ease with which funds raised for violence moved beyond reach while proceedings continued. The argument invites assessment device by device, against the failure each device was built to meet, rather than assessment of the statute as an undifferentiated whole.

A further element concerns deterrence and signalling, although officials usually place it after the practical arguments. A special statute announces that the state regards organised political violence as a distinct category of wrong, graver than ordinary homicide or destruction because the target extends beyond the immediate victims to the public order itself and to the willingness of citizens to use public space, public transport, and public institutions without fear. The listing of an organisation carries a signal to foreign governments, financial institutions, and intermediaries as well, marking the point at which diplomatic and banking cooperation can be requested on a shared legal basis. Without a formal designation, cooperation depends on informal assurance, and intermediaries handling funds or travel for a violent organisation can claim ignorance of its character.

The operational case also includes an argument about democratic authorisation. Successive governments have pointed to the parliamentary record itself, to the debates in which safeguards were inserted, clauses were modified, and sunset provisions were accepted for the temporary statutes, as evidence that the exceptional law passed through ordinary democratic scrutiny rather than around it. Judicial review forms part of the same claim, since the Supreme Court in Kartar Singh v State of Punjab examined the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act provision by provision, struck down little, read safeguards into much, and left a framework that later drafting treated as a constitutional floor. On this view, a statute that has survived parliamentary debate and constitutional challenge occupies different ground from an executive decree, and criticism must engage the safeguards as enacted rather than the powers as imagined in the abstract.

Finally, the case rests on a comparison that its makers regard as unavoidable. The alternative to a special statute is not the absence of state action under insurgency conditions. The alternative, in the historical record of disturbed areas, has often been heavier reliance on preventive detention laws, on special powers for the armed forces, on informal custody, and on encounter deaths that leave no trial record at all. A codified anti terror statute, with defined offences, designated courts, recorded reasons, and appellate routes, is presented as the more accountable of the available instruments, because each exercise of power under the statute creates a file, a charge sheet, and a judgment that can be examined. The strongest form of the argument therefore does not claim that the statute is gentle. The claim is that organised violence will be met by organised law in any event, and that a defined statute, debated in public and tested in court, is preferable to discretion exercised without a text.

## The Liberty Case, Stated at Full Strength

The liberty case, as developed by civil liberties organisations including the Peoples Union for Civil Liberties, begins with a reversal of the official sequence. The official account treats arrest, custody, and trial as stages leading to a punishment imposed only after conviction. Critics answer that under the anti terror statutes the process itself has become the punishment. Custody before charge extends far beyond the ordinary period, the bail test in Section 43D(5) of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act requires a court to refuse bail where the accusation appears prima facie true on the material in the case diary and the charge sheet, and a trial in a special court may take years to conclude. A person held through that period has served, in substance, a long sentence of imprisonment, loss of livelihood, separation from family, and public stigma, irrespective of the verdict that eventually arrives.

Academic studies of the working of the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, including the study of anti terror laws by Ujjwal Kumar Singh, supply the empirical spine of this argument. Singh traced the distance between the declared target of the legislation, namely organised terrorism in defined regions, and the recorded use of the legislation against a far wider population that included trade union activists, political opponents, members of minority communities, and persons caught in local disputes that bore no relation to any campaign of violence. The breadth was not an accident of a few errant officers, in this reading, but a predictable consequence of drafting that defined the terrorist act through intention and effect in language wide enough to cover ordinary disorder, and of a statute that offered investigators procedural advantages unavailable under the ordinary law.

Manisha Sethi, in Kafkaland, extended the argument from drafting to institutional culture. Sethi examined the production of terror cases through investigation, media circulation, and trial, and recorded a pattern in which suspicion attached to identity and association hardened into charge, in which evidence that would have collapsed under ordinary scrutiny survived for years inside a special process, and in which acquittal at the end brought neither apology nor restoration. The significance of that account for the liberty case lies in the location of the harm. Harm did not depend on conviction. Harm accumulated through arrest, remand, headlines, and the slow calendar of the special court, and acquittal merely closed a file whose contents had already done their work on employment, housing, family standing, and health.

The record of the repealed statutes gives the argument its figures, and each figure requires attribution because each rests on a distinct source. Human Rights Watch recorded over 76,000 arrests under the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act across its life, with a recorded conviction rate under one per cent. The Peoples Union for Civil Liberties and other civil liberties organisations cited that scale of arrest alongside the small number of convictions to argue that the statute functioned in practice as a detention instrument rather than a prosecution instrument, sweeping a population into custody from which the state eventually extracted very few findings of guilt. A detention rate of that order, critics argue, cannot be explained as investigative error at the margins. A detention rate of that order describes the ordinary operation of the law as enacted and administered.

The Prevention of Terrorism Act record carries the same structure in a shorter life. Sethi, in the account given in Kafkaland of the review reporting on the Prevention of Terrorism Act, records 1,031 arrests under that statute, with 18 completed trials and 13 convictions at the time of the review reporting. Civil liberties organisations placed those figures beside the composition of the arrested population in Gujarat and elsewhere, and beside the findings of the review committee process that followed the change of government in 2004, to argue that communal and political selection shaped enforcement from the start. Defenders of repeal treated the figures as a verdict on the design itself, since a statute that produced so few completed trials in relation to arrests had inverted the proper order, in which arrest is the exceptional opening of a process that normally ends in a prompt and public determination.

Breadth of definition forms the doctrinal core of the liberty case. The standing statute reaches beyond the completed terrorist act to membership of a listed organisation, to support for such an organisation, to fundraising, to conspiracy and preparation, and to conduct adjacent to speech, including advocacy and incitement framed in wide terms. Each extension moves the point of criminal liability further from a proved act of violence and closer to association, belief, and expression. Critics argue that association based offences reverse the ordinary discipline of criminal law, under which liability follows a proved act joined to a proved intention, and replace that discipline with status, under which the listing of an organisation converts a relationship with that organisation into the central fact to be proved.

Designation sharpens the objection. A listing decision, whether of an organisation in the First Schedule or of an individual under the power inserted in 2019, is an executive act taken on material that the designated party has limited means to test, and consequences follow at once for travel, funds, employment, association, and reputation. Those consequences arrive before any court has found anything against the named person, and removal from the schedule requires the designated party to seek review through committees and procedures in which the evidential basis may remain partly undisclosed. For critics, designation is the point at which the architecture most clearly departs from the criminal model, because a severe public status with punitive effects rests on executive satisfaction rather than on a charge proved in open proceedings.

The final strand concerns normalisation. Each repealed statute was defended in its time as temporary and exceptional, fitted to a named emergency in Punjab, in the North East, in Kashmir, or after a particular attack. Critics observe that the devices survived every repeal through transplant into the standing statute, that grounds once confined to disturbed areas became available across the whole country, and that a population accustomed to special procedure in terror cases became readier to accept similar procedure elsewhere. The liberty case therefore resists assessment of any single clause in isolation. The question, for the Peoples Union for Civil Liberties and for the academic literature in this field, is cumulative and constitutional, concerning the kind of criminal law a republic normalises when emergency drafting becomes the permanent template.

## Reading the Next Amendment: A Field Guide in Prose

Amendments to anti terror law rarely arrive as calm technical revisions, and the lineage supplies a disciplined method for reading the next amendment whenever a text appears. The first step is identification of the trigger event and of the political settlement that the event produced. A bombing, an assassination, an attack on Parliament, or a prolonged insurgency creates a demand for visible legislative action, and the resulting amendment usually embodies a bargain among the government of the day, opposition parties seeking either stronger powers or stronger safeguards, state governments guarding policing authority, and courts whose earlier judgments fixed the boundaries of permissible drafting. Reading the trigger and the settlement together explains features that the bare text conceals, including clauses inserted to secure passage, clauses dropped to avoid a defeat, and safeguards phrased as assurances from the ministerial bench rather than as operative provisions.

The second step is location of the device under change within the vocabulary of the lineage. The transplant chart developed across this series distinguishes the recurring devices with some precision, namely the definition of the terrorist act, the custody period before charge, the bail test, the treatment of confession, the special court, the forfeiture power, and the listing machinery for associations, organisations, and individuals. An amendment that appears novel frequently alters the setting of one familiar device rather than inventing a fresh instrument, and the comparison with [TADA 1987 explained](/2011/04/15/tada-1987-explained/) shows how often a device presented as a response to a fresh emergency reproduces, with modifications, a mechanism tested under the repealed statutes and assessed in the judgments that followed. The working papers of that comparison, amendment bills, committee reports and the chart itself, circulate as office documents, and the [Office File Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) opens the Excel, Word and PowerPoint files in which such records travel without requiring local software.

The third step is classification of the movement involved, and the lineage offers three possibilities that cover most cases. A change may move substance, by extending a period, widening a definition, lowering a threshold, or adding a scheduled offence, and such a change alters outcomes in real cases. A change may retire substance, as the repeal settlement of 2004 retired the admissibility of confession to a police officer, and such a change removes a device from the working law even where surrounding provisions survive. A change may rename substance, by transferring a device from a temporary statute into the standing statute, by altering the label attached to a listed entity, or by recasting a power in administrative language, and the comparison in [the TADA, POTA and UAPA comparison](/2011/08/01/tada-pota-uapa-comparison/) demonstrates how renaming has repeatedly preserved effect while changing presentation.

Safeguards supply the fourth step, and the benchmark remains the list of protections articulated in Kartar Singh v State of Punjab, where the Supreme Court sustained the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act while reading in requirements concerning recorded reasons, seniority of the recording officer, communication of rights to the accused, and screening of prosecutions. Measurement of an amendment against that list involves concrete enquiries, concerning the rank of officer authorised to act, the obligation to record reasons in writing, the point at which judicial scrutiny first enters, the availability of review for a designated party, and the sanction requirement before prosecution. A safeguard present in a speech but absent from the enacted text carries no weight in a courtroom, and the history traced in [POTA 2002 and its repeal explained](/2011/05/01/pota-2002-repeal-explained/) records how review machinery promised in debate operated in practice.

Listing machinery requires separate attention as the fifth step, because amendments have widened that machinery more steadily than any other part of the architecture. Enquiry under this head identifies the entity or person newly capable of designation, the authority empowered to designate, the schedule or notification through which designation takes effect, the consequences that follow automatically, and the route and burden for removal, and the full exposition in [the UAPA complete guide](/2011/05/15/uapa-1967-complete-guide/) sets out the standing machinery against which any extension falls to be measured. Particular care attaches to amendments affecting individuals, since designation of a named person without a prior judicial finding raises the questions of disclosure, hearing, and duration in their sharpest form.

A further check concerns commencement and transition, matters often buried at the end of an amending Act and frequently decisive in litigation. Attention falls on the date from which a change operates, on the treatment of investigations and trials pending at commencement, on the fate of designations and attachments made under the earlier text, and on the saving language that preserves or extinguishes accrued procedure. Transitional drafting reveals intention with unusual clarity, because a legislature intending a merely clarificatory change ordinarily protects pending proceedings in express terms, while a legislature intending a break with earlier practice addresses retrospectivity directly. Silence on transition, in a field where custody and bail turn on the applicable text, generates years of argument before appellate courts.

The closing step concerns time. Every temporary statute in the lineage carried a sunset clause, and Parliament repeatedly debated renewal against evidence of use and misuse, while the standing statute carries no equivalent expiry. Examination of a fresh amendment therefore records whether a sunset clause or a periodic review clause was offered, in the bill as introduced, in committee, or through an opposition amendment, and records also what happened to that proposal in debate, whether rejection was reasoned on the merits, deferred to a later review, or accomplished without discussion. The fate of a review clause often reveals the intended permanence of a change more reliably than the ministerial speech introducing the change. This article is legal information for study and examination purposes, not legal advice, and a person facing proceedings under any of these statutes needs a practising advocate.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act is the constant of the lineage because that statute alone carries no sunset clause.** Temporary statutes expired or faced repeal, while amendments accumulated inside the standing statute across five decades.
2. **Repeal in this field has operated as transplant rather than subtraction, with the confession clause as the great exception.** Devices from the repealed statutes migrated into the standing statute in 2004, while admissibility of confession to a police officer ended with the Prevention of Terrorism Act.
3. **The hardest procedural devices arrived by amendment after crises, in 2008 above all.** Extended custody, the stringent bail test in its applied form, and the investigative architecture surrounding the National Investigation Agency followed the Mumbai attacks.
4. **The listing power has widened in stages from associations to organisations to individuals.** Each stage extended designation to a broader class, and the 2019 amendment brought named persons within a machinery first built for collective entities.
5. **The courts have regulated the architecture rather than demolished that architecture, from Kartar Singh to the Peoples Union for Civil Liberties judgment.** Judgments read in safeguards, required intention for membership offences, and sustained the statutes under challenge instead of striking down the framework.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which anti-terror laws has India had since independence?

The central lineage contains the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act of 1967 as the standing statute, the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Acts of 1985 and 1987 as temporary statutes that lapsed in 1995, and the Prevention of Terrorism Act of 2002 as a temporary statute repealed in 2004. Around that lineage stand state organised crime statutes, preventive detention legislation with a separate history, and the National Investigation Agency Act of 2008 as an investigative statute rather than a code of offences. Earlier colonial and early republican preventive laws form background, although the recognisable anti terror architecture of definitions, special courts, modified bail, and listing begins with the standing statute of 1967.

### Q: Why does India keep replacing its anti-terror laws?

Replacement has followed a repeating pattern of crisis, enactment, controversy, and political change. A major attack or insurgency produced a demand for a visible legislative response, a temporary statute followed with a sunset clause, recorded misuse and low conviction outcomes weakened the defence of that statute, and a change of government or the arrival of the sunset date ended the experiment. Replacement never meant a return to the ordinary law alone, because the standing statute remained in force throughout and received, at each settlement, devices transferred from the departing statute. The cycle therefore reflects political rhythm and constitutional pressure as much as legal design, with permanence residing in the statute that attracted least attention.

### Q: What makes an anti-terror law different from ordinary criminal law?

Difference lies in accumulation rather than in any single provision. An anti terror statute defines offences through the intention and likely effect of an act as well as the act itself, extends liability to membership, support, fundraising, and preparation, permits a longer period of custody before charge, applies a more stringent test for bail, provides special courts with modified procedure, attaches property connected to proceeds or use in an offence, and maintains machinery for listing organisations and persons outside a trial. Ordinary criminal law contains some of these elements in isolation, for offences such as organised crime or unlawful assembly, although the combination, the breadth of definition, and the listing power together mark the distinct character of the anti terror form.

### Q: Which anti-terror laws remain in force in India?

The operative central framework consists of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act of 1967 in amended form, supplying the offences, the listing schedules, the custody and bail rules, and the special procedure applied in prosecutions, together with the National Investigation Agency Act of 2008, supplying the federal investigative agency and its scheduled offences. State organised crime statutes remain operative in several states, with the Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act of 1999 as the earliest and most litigated example, and counterpart statutes in other states modelled in part on that design. The Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act and the Prevention of Terrorism Act form no part of the operative framework, although proceedings validly begun under those statutes generated a long tail of litigation.

### Q: Do Indian states have their own anti-terror laws?

States have legislated principally through organised crime statutes rather than through statutes labelled as anti terror laws, a distinction shaped by the constitutional distribution of legislative power, under which public order and police fall largely to the states while Parliament legislated on terrorism through entries concerning defence, foreign affairs, and criminal law and procedure. The Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act of 1999 supplied the template, with features including stringent bail, admissibility of confession to a senior police officer, and extended custody, and states including Delhi, Karnataka, Gujarat, and others adopted or adapted comparable frameworks. Some state enactments required presidential assent because of repugnancy with central law, and litigation over competence and safeguards has accompanied the state statutes throughout their history.

### Q: How does the Constitution permit special anti-terror legislation?

Parliament draws competence from several legislative entries read together, including defence of India, foreign affairs, inter state and international obligations, and the concurrent entries for criminal law and criminal procedure, while the state legislatures hold public order and police subject to central legislation in the concurrent field. Competence alone does not settle validity, since a special statute must also satisfy Part III, including equality before the law in Article 14, protection in respect of conviction in Article 20, protection of life and personal liberty in Article 21, and safeguards on arrest in Article 22, together with freedoms of speech and association in Article 19 subject to reasonable restrictions. Courts have tested anti terror legislation against those provisions and have generally sustained the framework while reading in procedural safeguards.

### Q: What is the difference between an unlawful association and a terrorist organisation?

An unlawful association under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act is a body declared unlawful through notification after a process involving a tribunal, on grounds that include activity prejudicial to sovereignty and territorial integrity, and declaration carries consequences for membership, meetings, and funds. A terrorist organisation is an entity listed in the First Schedule to the statute, or an organisation found to be involved in terrorism through the procedures of Chapter III, and listing engages the specific offences of membership, support, and fundraising in Chapter IV and related provisions. The distinction matters in practice because the route of designation, the confirming authority, the available offences, and the path to removal differ between the two categories, although a single body may in principle attract attention under either head.

### Q: Has any Indian anti-terror law ever been struck down as unconstitutional?

No central anti terror statute has been struck down as a whole. In Kartar Singh v State of Punjab in 1994, the Supreme Court upheld the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act in substance, invalidated or qualified particular aspects, and read in safeguards concerning confession, review, and procedure. In Peoples Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India in 2003, the Court upheld the Prevention of Terrorism Act against a broad constitutional challenge, while reading a requirement of intention into the membership offence and sustaining the review and safeguard provisions as constitutionally significant. Individual provisions and particular applications have failed in litigation at various points, although the judicial pattern across the lineage is regulation of the architecture through interpretation, severance, and safeguard, rather than invalidation of an entire statute.

### Q: What was the sunset clause and why did it matter?

A sunset clause fixes the date on which a statute or a set of provisions ceases to operate unless Parliament renews the legislation. The Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act began with a limited life and received extensions through amendment, until Parliament allowed the statute to lapse in 1995, and the Prevention of Terrorism Act carried a three year life that the repeal of 2004 anticipated. The clause mattered because expiry forced a parliamentary reckoning on evidence, compelling a government to defend recorded use, arrest figures, and conviction outcomes in order to obtain renewal. The standing statute carries no sunset clause, and amendments to that statute therefore accumulate without any equivalent moment of compulsory reconsideration.

### Q: How did the transplant of 2004 change the standing statute?

The repeal settlement of 2004 ended the Prevention of Terrorism Act and simultaneously amended the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act to receive much of the substance of the departing statute. Chapters on terrorist acts, terrorist organisations, fundraising, and forfeiture entered the standing statute, the First Schedule listing machinery expanded, and punishment and procedure provisions modelled on the repealed statute became permanent law without any sunset clause. One major device did not travel, namely admissibility of confession made to a police officer, and that omission marks the clearest substantive concession within the settlement. The transplant changed the politics of the field as well, since opposition to a permanent statute proved harder to organise than opposition to a temporary statute facing expiry.

### Q: Why is 2008 treated as the turning point in the lineage?

The amendments of 2008 followed the Mumbai attacks of November in that year and altered the standing statute more deeply than any single earlier amendment. Custody before charge extended to a maximum of 180 days under judicial authorisation, the bail provision in Section 43D(5) acquired the form in which courts have since applied a prima facie test at the bail stage, provisions on freezing and forfeiture widened, and the National Investigation Agency Act created a central agency with authority over scheduled offences across state boundaries. Speed of enactment formed part of the significance, since Parliament legislated within weeks of the attacks, and the settlement reached in that atmosphere, with limited committee scrutiny, set terms that later litigation and debate have worked within ever since.

### Q: What changed in 2019 and why did the change attract attention?

The amendment of 2019 empowered the central government to designate an individual as a terrorist through notification in the official gazette, extending a listing machinery previously directed at organisations and associations. Attention concentrated on designation before trial, since listing carries consequences for reputation, movement, association, and property related dealings from the moment of notification, while the designated person bears the initiative in seeking review and removal. The amendment also adjusted provisions concerning attachment of property and the investigative authority of the National Investigation Agency, including rank requirements for officers in certain enquiries. Defenders presented individual designation as a necessary answer to persons operating outside formal organisations, while critics treated the change as the point at which executive listing reached the named citizen directly.

### Q: How does listing an organisation work under the standing statute?

Listing operates through inclusion of an organisation in the First Schedule to the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, by notification of the central government on the ground of involvement in terrorism, with related provision for organisations operating under different names. Consequences follow from listing itself, since membership of a listed terrorist organisation, support invited or given for such an organisation, and fundraising connected to terrorism constitute offences under the statute, and property and funds become exposed to prohibition and forfeiture procedures. Removal requires application and governmental or judicially supervised review under the mechanisms provided in the statute, and the practical burden of initiating that process rests on the listed party. Courts have examined listing in challenges concerning particular organisations, generally within a deferential frame on security assessment.

### Q: What role does the National Investigation Agency play in the lineage?

The National Investigation Agency, created by the statute of 2008, supplies the investigative and prosecutorial machinery for scheduled offences, principally offences under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act and connected offences concerning explosives, atomic energy, hijacking, and related subjects. The agency may take over investigation on direction of the central government, officers of the agency exercise powers across state boundaries without the consent based frictions that attend ordinary inter state investigation, and trials proceed before designated special courts. Within the lineage, the agency represents a shift from statute drafting toward institutional design, since earlier phases concentrated on definitions and procedure within existing police structures, while the settlement of 2008 built a permanent central institution whose existence shapes which cases enter the anti terror track.

### Q: Why did the confession device disappear while other devices survived?

Confession to a police officer occupied a singular position in the repealed statutes, since both the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act and the Prevention of Terrorism Act made such confession admissible under conditions concerning seniority of the recording officer, recorded warnings, and production before a magistrate. The device attracted the strongest judicial anxiety in Kartar Singh, the heaviest criticism in the repeal debates, and a long historical suspicion in Indian evidence law, which ordinarily excludes confession to police because of the risk of coercion in custody. Survival of other devices in 2004 reflected a political calculation that custody, bail, and listing could be defended as procedural regulation, while confession admissibility had become the symbol of the exceptional statute and the concession most capable of marking repeal as real.

### Q: What safeguards did Kartar Singh read into the law?

The Constitution Bench in Kartar Singh sustained the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act while attaching conditions to the operation of its strongest provisions. Safeguards articulated in the judgment included recording of confession by a senior officer with a warning that the statement could be used in evidence, prompt production of the maker before a magistrate, medical examination on complaint of torture, screening of prosecutions through review mechanisms at senior level, and insistence on strict compliance as a condition of reliance on special procedure. Later drafting and later judgments treated those conditions as a constitutional floor for exceptional criminal procedure, and measurement of subsequent statutes against the Kartar Singh list became a standard move in litigation and in parliamentary criticism alike.

### Q: How have courts treated the membership offence over time?

Treatment of membership illustrates the judicial method across the lineage, namely regulation through interpretation rather than invalidation. The Prevention of Terrorism Act membership provision survived challenge in the Peoples Union for Civil Liberties judgment with intention read in, so that innocent or passive association fell outside the intended reach of the offence. Under the standing statute, litigation produced conflicting lines of authority, with one line treating mere membership of a banned organisation as sufficient and a later line, affirmed on review in 2023, sustaining that broader reading for organisations declared unlawful, while debate continues on the relation between membership, intention, and the freedom of association in Article 19. The sequence shows courts adjusting the boundary of a status based offence across decades without removing the offence from the statute book.

### Q: Do special courts change the character of a trial?

Special courts under the lineage retain the structure of a criminal trial, with charge, evidence, cross examination, defence, and judgment, while altering the setting and the calendar in significant respects. Designation concentrates terror cases before particular courts, provisions have at various points permitted proceedings in camera, protection of witness identity, trial in absence in limited circumstances under the repealed statutes, and day to day hearing obligations intended to counter delay. In practice, concentration has produced expertise in some jurisdictions and heavy backlog in others, and the promise of speed has frequently failed against the volume of accused persons, witnesses, and documentary material in a single case. The character of the trial therefore changes less through any single rule than through accumulation of custody, bail restriction, and duration.

### Q: What happens to pending cases when an anti-terror statute is repealed?

Repeal and lapse have generally preserved pending proceedings through saving provisions, on the principle that acts done and liabilities incurred under a statute survive the end of that statute unless the repealing legislation directs otherwise. Trials under the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act continued for years after lapse in 1995, and the repeal of the Prevention of Terrorism Act in 2004 included a distinctive review mechanism under which a committee examined pending cases and many prosecutions were withdrawn or transferred where the review found no prima facie case under the repealed statute. Accused persons in transferred cases faced trial under the standing statute or the ordinary law for the underlying acts, so repeal altered the procedural track and the available offences rather than erasing the underlying allegations.

### Q: Why do conviction figures feature so heavily in the debate?

Conviction figures carry weight because both sides accept, at least in argument, that a prosecution statute falls to be judged partly by outcomes in court. A high arrest volume joined to a very low conviction outcome supports the critics in a specific and limited way, suggesting that arrest operated as an end in itself or that investigation gathered persons faster than evidence could be gathered against those persons. Defenders answer with the conditions of insurgency, witness intimidation, and the preventive purpose of arrest and disruption, and with the observation that acquittal may reflect the failure of proof rather than innocence of design. Figures cannot settle the constitutional questions concerning definition, bail, or designation, although figures discipline the debate by replacing competing impressions of use and misuse with a record open to examination.
