The rose line is the most quoted sentence in *Romeo and Juliet*, and it does not exist. Not in the form the world quotes, at any rate. "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44) stands in no book printed in Shakespeare's lifetime: the first quarto of 1597 gives the sentence one shape, the second quarto of 1599 gives it another, and the First Folio of 1623 manages to garble the famous question itself while keeping a reading at odds with the one the public has memorized. Inside the divergence sits a single contested syllable. The second quarto, the authoritative early text from which modern editions descend, prints "by any other word." The first quarto prints "by any other name." One of those readings is what Shakespeare's text, on the documentary evidence, most probably said; the other is what four centuries of quotation have taught the world to say. This article examines the crux as a problem in documents: what each early witness actually prints at the rose line and in the sentences that carry it, how editors from Edmond Malone onward have chosen between the rival forms, what hangs on the choice for Juliet's argument about the detachability of labels, and which reading an honest edition should put on the page.

The verdict can be stated at the outset, since the evidence is unusually compact. An editor should print "word." The reading "word" is carried by the second quarto, the copy-text for the play, and by the Folio tradition that descends from it; "name" is carried by the first quarto alone, a text whose authority at this passage is compromised by everything else it does to the speech around the line. Yet the verdict comes with a rider that matters more than the verdict itself. The speech the world knows, the smooth dozen lines that begin "'Tis but thy name that is my enemy," is an editorial construction assembled in the eighteenth century from pieces of both quartos, as the textual essay in the Pelican Shakespeare puts the point with unusual bluntness: there is in fact no early text that reads as the modern text does, and this is the most famous speech in the play. The crux is therefore double. There is the small crux of one syllable, word against name, and there is the large crux behind it: the most famous line in English drama is a conflation, and every quotation of it silently ratifies a set of editorial decisions that the quotation never discloses. Restoring the crux does not diminish the line. It restores the line's history, and with it the reader's right to know what kind of object a famous quotation is.

## The Three Witnesses at the Rose Line

Three books printed between 1597 and 1623 transmit the balcony scene, and a reader who sets their versions of the name speech side by side meets three different passages that are unmistakably one passage. The transcriptions quoted in this article follow the old-spelling texts as reproduced in the Pelican Shakespeare's essay on the play's texts, checked against the apparatus of the Cambridge edition of the play edited by G. Blakemore Evans, the fullest compact record of what each witness reads at each disputed point.

The first quarto, printed in 1597, gives the speech in its shortest form. Juliet's catalogue of what a Montague is not runs cleanly: "Whats Montagne? It is nor hand nor foote, / Nor arme, nor face, nor any other part." The question follows at once, pointed as a question: "Whats in a name? That which we call a Rose, / By any other name would smell as sweet." The reading at the crux is "name." The second quarto, printed in 1599 and advertised on its title page as newly corrected, augmented, and amended, gives a fuller speech and a disturbed one. Its catalogue breaks down in the middle: "Whats Mountague? it is nor hand nor foote, / Nor arme nor face, o be some other name / Belonging to a man." The half-line "Belonging to a man" hangs stranded after the exclamation it should precede, and the sentence that carries the crux arrives without punctuation to mark its famous question: "Whats in a name that which we call a rose, / By any other word would smell as sweete." The reading at the crux is "word." The First Folio, printed in 1623 from the third quarto, which itself reprints the second, preserves "word" and mangles the question into something no one has ever quoted on purpose: "What? in a names that which we call a Rose, / By any other word would smell as sweete." S. W. Reid, in a study of the Folio text of the play published in *Studies in Bibliography*, cites the Folio's lines by their through line numbers, 837 and 838, and delivers the judgement around which this whole subject turns: these famous lines appear correctly in no early text.

Three witnesses, then, and three states of the sentence. The short quarto reads "name" inside a tidy speech. The long quarto reads "word" inside a damaged one. The Folio reads "word" inside a garbled question. Every later state of the line is an editor's negotiation among those three, and the negotiation has to settle two separate disputes that careless discussion habitually merges: the dispute about one word at the end of the sentence, and the dispute about the shape of the whole passage that carries the sentence to its close.

### Which witnesses agree with each other at the crux?

Only the Folio agrees with the second quarto, and the agreement proves nothing, because the Folio was set from the third quarto, a reprint of the second. At the crux the early books divide one tradition against one: the long quarto and its descendants reading "word," the short quarto alone reading "name."

Agreement among witnesses means less than it appears to mean, because two of the three are not independent. The Folio text of *Romeo and Juliet* was set from the third quarto of 1609, a reprint of the second quarto, so the Folio's "word" is the second quarto's "word" at one remove, transmitted, not corroborated. The true division is two against one in appearance and one against one in substance: the second quarto tradition on one side, the first quarto on the other. Any argument that counts the Folio as a second vote for "word" double-counts a single act of printing. The case for "word" must rest on the authority of the second quarto itself, not on a tally of books.

That discipline, refusing to count derivative witnesses as independent evidence, is the first tool the crux teaches. It applies with equal force on the other side. The countless modern books that print "name" are not witnesses at all; they are descendants of editorial choices and of the quotation tradition, and their number proves nothing about what stood in any early copy. Against a documentary question, only documents vote. At the rose line the documents divide evenly, one early tradition against the other, and the division has to be broken by arguments about the character of the two quartos and about the sense of the passage, not by counting. The sections that follow take those arguments in order: first the witnesses one by one, then the repair work of the editors, then the rival cases for the two readings, and finally the strange afterlife in which the weaker reading conquered the world.

## The First Quarto's 'Name': A Short Speech, Cleanly Printed

The first quarto of *Romeo and Juliet* appeared in 1597, printed in London in circumstances the series has examined in the study of that edition as an object: an unentered text, published without the author's name, presenting the play in a version roughly a quarter shorter than the one that followed it two years later. Its title page promises the play as it had been often played publicly, and whatever route brought its copy to the printing house, the balcony scene it preserves is a working theatrical shape of the scene: compressed, swift, and at the name speech noticeably tidier than the longer text's version of the same lines.

The tidiness deserves emphasis, because it is the strongest card the first quarto holds at this passage. In the first quarto the name speech runs without dislocation. Juliet asks what a Montague is, answers with the catalogue of body parts, and the catalogue closes with "nor any other part" before the argument moves to its question and its flower. Nothing dangles. The question is pointed as a question. A reader given only this page would find the speech plain, short, and rhetorically complete: the enemy named, the body catalogued, the label interrogated, the rose produced as proof. Where the longer text stumbles over its own fullness, the short text walks straight. That is precisely the profile the first quarto shows across the play, and it cuts both ways as evidence. A text tidied by transmission, whether by actors recalling a performance, by an adapter fitting the play to a touring company, or by a reporter smoothing what memory blurred, will often read more cleanly than the author's papers at exactly the points where the author's papers preserve the disorder of composition.

At the crux itself the first quarto reads "name," and the reading has an obvious surface logic. The speech is a speech about names. Its first sentence declares the beloved's label to be the only enemy; its question asks what is in a name; its bargain, when it comes, is transacted in the vocabulary of naming, doffing, and baptism. A version of the rose sentence that answers the question with the question's own word has a rhetorical snap to it: what is in a name, the sentence says in effect, when a rose by any other name keeps its sweetness. The repetition binds question and proof into one gesture. Defenders of the first quarto's reading, and editors who have adopted it, have always been able to point to that fitness. The line as the first quarto gives it is the line as a proverb wants it: symmetrical, self-sealing, quotable.

Against the fitness stands the witness's general character. The first quarto's text of the balcony scene is not merely shorter at the speech; it is shorter around it, compressing Romeo's answering speeches, thinning the scene's exchanges, and showing throughout the scene the paraphrastic drift that marks its text wherever the longer quarto allows comparison. At the lines immediately following the speech, the Cambridge apparatus records the pattern in miniature: where the second quarto and its descendants give Romeo's self-description in full, the first quarto's version runs the lines together and omits words the fuller text preserves. A witness that demonstrably rewrites its surroundings earns no presumption of accuracy at the one word where its reading happens to please. Its "name" may be what the author wrote; it may equally be the tradition's own improvement, the substitution a memory makes when it reaches for the obvious word, the word the sentence has been talking about for six lines. Synonym substitution of exactly that kind, the replacement of a less expected word by the word the context keeps suggesting, is the commonest documented failure of reported texts, and "name" for "word" is a textbook shape for it: the speech says name, name, name, and the reported sentence, arriving at its climax, says name once more.

### How much of the speech does the first quarto actually carry?

Less than the familiar dozen lines, in a concentrated form: the enemy sentence, the catalogue of body parts, the question, and the rose, without the longer text's expansions. The short speech is complete as an argument and actable as a moment, but its compression is exactly why its testimony about single words carries reduced weight.

Less than the familiar version, and the difference matters for how its testimony is weighed. The first quarto's name speech is a concentrated form of the argument: it keeps the enemy sentence, the catalogue, the question, and the rose, and it moves through them without the longer text's expansions and second thoughts. What it does not carry, at this point in the scene, is the full architecture of plea and bargain that the modern text builds out of the longer version's materials combined with repairs. Judged as a performance script, the short speech is actable and sufficient. Judged as a witness to what the author set down, its compression is the problem: a text that has cut cannot be cross-examined about what it cut, and a text that smooths cannot certify the one smooth reading it offers at the crux. The first quarto tells a reader what the speech felt like in the theatre. The question before an editor is what the speech said on the page from which the theatre's version ultimately derived, and on that question the short quarto is a witness of the second rank, here as everywhere in the play.

There is a further caution, and it concerns the first quarto's own material state. The edition survives in a handful of copies, five by the usual count, and its text at any given line is the text of a single hurried printing, never corrected by a second edition in its own line. The second quarto's tradition, by contrast, was reprinted, corrected in places, and carried into the Folio; its readings can be tracked across a transmission. None of that makes the longer text right at the crux. It makes the contest unequal in kind: a considered documentary tradition on one side, a single theatrical snapshot on the other, and the crux has to be decided by asking which kind of witness errs in which kind of way at a word like this one.

## The Second Quarto's 'Word': Authority Inside a Damaged Passage

The second quarto of 1599 is the foundation of the modern play. Printed from copy that the scholarly consensus, argued through the second quarto's own study in this series, identifies as the author's foul papers, it preserves the fullness and the untidiness of a working manuscript: variant speech prefixes, descriptive stage directions, passages of false start and second thought. Editors choose it as copy-text because its accidentals and most of its substantives stand closest to the author's hand. At the rose line it reads "word," and the presumption that attends copy-text readings applies: a substantive in the copy-text stands unless it can be shown to be wrong. The burden of proof lies on "name," not on "word."

The passage around the reading, however, is one of the places where the second quarto's copy visibly failed its printers, and the failure has shaped everything editors have done since. The catalogue of body parts runs: "Whats Mountague? it is nor hand nor foote, / Nor arme nor face, o be some other name / Belonging to a man." Read as printed, the lines say that a Montague is neither hand nor foot nor arm nor face, then break into the exclamation "O be some other name," then trail a fragment, "Belonging to a man," that belongs grammatically to the catalogue it follows too late. The sentence's skeleton is dislocated: a phrase has slipped its joint. The likeliest account, and the one editorial practice has ratified, is that the copy at this point preserved the author's own process, a phrase written, reconsidered, and repositioned in a way the printing house could not follow, or that a compositor's eye displaced a half-line in setting. Either way, the printed sequence is not a sequence any author intended as it stands, and every modern edition repairs it, openly or silently.

The repair matters to the crux for a reason discussions of the crux often miss. The same passage that requires surgical repair at the catalogue requires none at the rose line itself. "Whats in a name that which we call a rose, / By any other word would smell as sweete" is, apart from its missing punctuation, a complete and grammatical utterance: the words stand in their places, the syntax closes, the sense lands. The damage in the second quarto's speech is local and mechanical, a displaced half-line and dropped pointing; it is not a general corruption that would license an editor to treat every word in the passage as negotiable. An editor who accepts the copy-text's "word" is not endorsing a broken text. The text at the crux is whole. It is the lines four places earlier that are broken, and they are broken in a way that has no tendency to turn "word" into "name" or the reverse.

The missing punctuation deserves its own moment, because it changes what the famous question is on the second quarto's page. In that printing, "Whats in a name that which we call a rose" runs forward without stop or question mark, one breath from question to evidence. The Cambridge apparatus condenses the fact into a single entry: at this line, the reading "What's in a name?" with its pointing is credited to the fourth quarto and the first; the second and third quartos print the words unpunctuated; the Folio prints its garble. The question mark that every modern reader takes for granted, the mark that makes the sentence an interrogative performance rather than a statement unfolding, enters the second quarto's line of descent only in 1622, a generation after the copy-text. Nothing about the crux of word and name turns on the pointing, but the pointing is a useful discipline for the imagination: the sentence the world quotes is pointed by editors, spelled by editors, lined by editors, and, at one word, chosen by editors from between two early forms. The quotation is a made object all the way down.

### Why does the copy-text's reading carry the presumption?

Because the second quarto was printed from the author's working papers, its words stand as the author's unless shown to be wrong, while a reported text's words arrive through memory and adaptation. "Word" is grammatical and pointed, so the burden of proof rests entirely on the rival reading, and "name" has never discharged it.

Because of what the copy is. A text printed from the author's working papers transmits the author's words with the author's disorders; a text printed from any later or theatrical source transmits the words after other hands and memories have had their turn. Where the two kinds of witness offer different substantives, editorial method since the mid-twentieth century, the method associated with W. W. Greg's rationale of copy-text, instructs the editor to follow the documentary authority for the words themselves unless the copy-text reading is manifestly erroneous. "Word" is not manifestly erroneous. It makes complete sense, arguably finer sense than its rival, as the section on the sense of the passage will show. A reading that is documentary, grammatical, and pointed does not yield to a reading whose chief assets are smoothness and fame. The presumption, in short, is not a courtesy extended to the second quarto. It is the conclusion of the argument about where the second quarto came from.

## The First Folio's Garbled Question: 'What? in a Names'

The First Folio of 1623 is the most prestigious book in English literature, and at the most famous question in *Romeo and Juliet* it prints nonsense. The Folio's text of the play was set from a copy of the third quarto, the 1609 reprint of the second quarto, annotated to some degree against playhouse copy, as the study of the Folio text in this series sets out. At the name speech, the Folio's compositors produced: "What? in a names that which we call a Rose, / By any other word would smell as sweete." The question has collapsed into a stammer: an interrogative word, a stray letter, a plural noun that no syntax can house. Reid's examination of the Folio text takes these two lines as an exhibit of how little editorial supervision the play received in that volume, and the lines repay the attention. Generations of readers who encountered the play first in Folio-derived texts were reading a speech whose climax had been assembled by nobody: inherited from the quartos, degraded in the Folio, and awaiting the eighteenth century for its familiar repair.

For the crux, the Folio contributes two facts, one about each dispute. On the word itself, the Folio reads "word," agreeing with its copy. The agreement, as the opening section warned, adds no independent weight: it is the second quarto's reading transmitted through the third quarto, one tradition voting twice in appearance only. On the question, the Folio demonstrates something more interesting, the vulnerability of fame. A line does not become safer in transmission by becoming famous, because fame in 1623 was not yet attached to these words in the way it now is; the compositors who garbled the question were setting type at the speed of piecework, and a speech's later celebrity offers its syllables no protection at the moment of setting. The garble "in a names" has even a certain accidental wit, a printing house stumbling over the very word the speech interrogates, but as evidence it is simple: the Folio, at this passage, is a careless descendant, and its testimony counts only where its copy's testimony is independently in question.

There is a temptation, in writing about this passage, to treat the Folio's blunder as comedy and move on. The blunder matters more than that, because the Folio is the channel through which the play reached the Restoration stage and the early eighteenth-century editors. The texts from which Rowe, Pope, and Theobald worked stood downstream of this page. When those editors confronted the name speech, they confronted a tradition in which the question was garbled in the Folio, unpunctuated in the second and third quartos, dislocated in the catalogue in every quarto descendant of the second, and clean only in the short first quarto whose authority their century had not yet learned to discount. The familiar speech had to be built because no surviving book offered it ready-made. Understanding that the builders faced a ruin, of modest proportions but a ruin all the same, is the precondition for judging what they built.

It is worth pausing on the mechanics of the Folio's error, since the crux tradition is full of errors whose mechanics decide their meaning. "Whats in a name" in the copy became "What? in a names" on the Folio page: the compositor, or the annotating hand behind the copy, divided the words wrongly, supplied a question mark in the wrong place, and pluralized the noun. Errors of division and pointing are the small change of hand-press printing, and Charlton Hinman's compositor studies of the Folio, pursued in the Folio study in this series, taught the discipline to read such errors as evidence about the printing house rather than about the author. No one supposes Shakespeare wrote "What? in a names." Everyone supposes a workman nodding over a forme produced it. The lesson transfers directly to the smaller crux. If the Folio's gross error is discounted as mechanical, then mechanical error is a live category at this passage in every witness, and the question for word and name becomes which witness's error profile better explains the rival reading: the foul-papers quarto whose compositors demonstrably stumbled over this very speech's pointing, or the reported quarto whose whole method invited synonym substitution. That question is taken up in the section on what kind of error "name" might be.

## Q3 and Q4: The Rose Line in Transmission, 1609 to 1622

Between the second quarto and the Folio stand two reprints that the crux's history cannot skip, because the state of the sentence in each marks a stage in the sentence's long repair. The third quarto of 1609 reprints the second quarto's text, dislocation, missing punctuation, and "word" included; it is less a new edition than a fresh impression of an existing one, and its chief role in the play's history is the passive one of having been chosen, fourteen years later, as the Folio's copy. Whatever the third quarto prints at the rose line is the second quarto at one remove, and its "word" is accordingly the reading the Folio's compositors had before them when they set the garbled question around it.

The fourth quarto, printed in 1622, is the first book in the second quarto's line to present the famous question as a question. The Cambridge apparatus entry is terse, as apparatus entries are: the pointed form "What's in a name?" is credited to the fourth quarto and the first, against the unpunctuated run-on of the second and third and the Folio's garble. A question mark is a small thing, and editors rightly treat punctuation as accidental, the layer of the text most subject to printing-house habit. Yet the fourth quarto's pointing records a readerly act: someone in the transmission, compositor or corrector, heard the sentence as the interrogative the world now hears, and marked it. The history of the line's punctuation is a miniature of the history of its text. The words had to wait for their pointing, their pointing for their repair, and their repair for an editor willing to use both quartos at once. Fame, in print, is an accumulation of such small decisions, each one invisible in the finished quotation.

The transmission also frames the crux's sharpest irony. The reading "name" never travels in the authoritative line at all. It exists in the first quarto of 1597 and then vanishes from the printed tradition of the play for more than a century, while the second quarto's descendants carry "word" through 1609, 1622, 1623, and 1637. When "name" returns to printed texts of the play, it returns not through documentary descent but through editorial election: eighteenth-century and later editors, collating the first quarto and preferring its word at this point, or following the quotation tradition that had independently preserved the smoother form, put "name" back into the line. A reading can thus be at once the earliest printed form of the sentence and a latecomer to the edited text, and the rose line's "name" is exactly that: first in the bookstalls, last in the editions, and first again in the public memory, which never consulted the editions at all.

### What do the later quartos add to the evidence?

Nothing at the crux word, since reprints transmit rather than judge, and both later quartos simply repeat the second quarto's "word." Their contribution is diagnostic: the third quarto carried the reading to the Folio as its copy, and the fourth, in 1622, first pointed the famous question in this line of descent.

Nothing at the crux word, and something at everything around it. The third and fourth quartos cannot corroborate "word," being reprints, and they cannot introduce "name," being reprints of the wrong side. Their value to the crux is diagnostic. They show the second quarto's text stabilizing into the form in which the seventeenth century knew the play: dislocation unrepaired, question eventually pointed, "word" unchallenged. Every reader of the play between 1599 and the age of Malone who read the scene in a quarto or the Folio read "by any other word." If the public memory of the line says "name," that memory was not formed from the printed play in its first century and a half. It was formed elsewhere, and the section on the popular form takes up the question of where.

## Before Malone: A Century and a Half of 'Word'

Between the last early reprint and Malone stands the long eighteenth century of Shakespeare editing, and the rose line's state across that century explains both what Malone inherited and why his intervention was needed. The century's first editors worked inside the Folio tradition. Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709, the first to present itself as an edited Shakespeare, descended from the Fourth Folio of 1685, and the texts of Pope and Theobald that followed it were, for a play like this one, Folio texts corrected by taste, by conjecture, and increasingly by learning. At the name speech, that inheritance meant a particular configuration of problems. The Folio tradition carried "word" at the crux, so the century's readers, when they read the play in an edition, read the documentary reading without knowing it was one. The same tradition carried the second quarto's dislocated catalogue through its quarto ancestry, overlaid with the Folio's own carelessness at the question, so the speech those readers met was at once right at the disputed syllable and wrong in its architecture, the reverse of the modern situation in every particular.

The crux, as a crux, could not exist for those editors, because a crux requires two witnesses in active comparison and the first quarto was not yet a working document of the editorial trade. The short quarto survived in its handful of copies, known to antiquaries, occasionally consulted, but not yet installed as the systematic second authority that later method would make of it. Edward Capell's editions in the middle of the century mark the turn: Capell collated the early quartos with a patience none of his predecessors had applied, and built his text on the earliest substantive editions, a reorientation whose effects ran through everything after. By the time Malone came to the play, the documentary materials for the crux were all in play: a copy-text tradition reading "word," a short quarto reading "name," a dislocation demanding repair, and a scholarly culture newly persuaded that such things were decided by evidence rather than by elegance. Malone's genius at the passage was to act on every element of that inheritance at once, repairing the catalogue from the first quarto while leaving the crux to the tradition, an editor's discrimination that his successors have mostly preserved even where they have revised his details.

The century also fixed the quotation tradition's independence. While the editors' line descended through the Folio, the proverb descended through the culture, and the two lines crossed without merging: anthologies of beauties, dictionaries of familiar quotations in their early forms, school extracts, and the common memory of the stage all carried "name" forward on a track the editions neither fed nor corrected. When later editors, collating the first quarto in earnest, found "name" waiting for them in a document of 1597, the discovery felt like a vindication of the popular memory, and in a narrow sense it was: the memory and the document agreed. What the agreement could not show was the one thing an editor needs, the reading's authority, because a memory and a reported text are the same kind of witness wearing different clothes, and their concurrence is the concurrence of an error with its own echo. The eighteenth century's bifurcated inheritance, documentary "word" in the editions and memorial "name" in the culture, is the crux's modern form in embryo; Malone settled the passage's architecture, and the syllable was left for the next two centuries to argue, which they have done in the apparatus of every serious edition since.

## Malone's Conflation: How the Familiar Speech Was Assembled

The speech the world quotes was built by Edmond Malone. The Irish scholar-editor, whose 1790 edition of Shakespeare set new standards for documentary rigour, confronted at this passage a copy-text whose catalogue was dislocated and a first quarto whose catalogue was sound. His solution, in the standard account of the passage's editorial history, was to splice: to take from the first quarto the arrangement in which "nor any other part" completes the catalogue and "Belonging to a man" attaches to it, and to set the exclamation "O be some other name" after the repaired lines, producing the sequence every modern reader knows: "What's Montague? It is nor hand nor foot, / Nor arm nor face, nor any other part / Belonging to a man. O be some other name!" The lines are a conflation, a made text, assembled from two witnesses neither of which contains them in that form.

The Pelican Shakespeare's essay on the texts of the plays chooses this very passage to teach the general lesson, and its sentence deserves quotation at the length the subject warrants: there is in fact no early text that reads as the modern text does, and this is the most famous speech in the play; the passage readers prize is an editorial construction, conflated and revised from the early versions into its recognized greatness. The point is not that Malone cheated. The point is that editing, at a damaged passage, is authorship under discipline, and the discipline's honesty consists in recording what was done. Malone's repair has been generally ratified by subsequent editors, the Cambridge edition among them, whose text prints the transposed block within markers that flag the passage as editorially rearranged, a practice this article commends as the honest minimum: repair the dislocation, and show the reader the scaffolding.

Malone's splice bears on the word crux in a way that is easy to miss. The editor who imports the first quarto's arrangement at the catalogue has already exercised judgement about the first quarto's local reliability: at the catalogue, the short text preserves the sense the long text garbles, because dislocation is a mechanical accident and the undislocated form is likely to stand nearer the copy's intention. That judgement, sound in itself, creates a gravitational pull toward the first quarto's other local reading. An editor who has just taken the catalogue's shape from the first quarto faces the rose line four lines later with the first quarto open and newly vindicated, and its "name" sits there offering the same service of smoothness. The history of the edited text at this passage shows the pull operating unevenly: many editions that accept Malone's arrangement decline his neighbour reading and print "word"; others take both. The two decisions are independent, and the article's verdict keeps them so: the transposition repairs a demonstrable mechanical failure, while "name" replaces a sound substantive with a smoother one, and the cases are not parallel. Conflation is justified where the copy-text fails to make sense. At "word," the copy-text makes excellent sense, and the warrant for conflation fails with it.

It is equally important to acquit Malone of the popular form. The speech he assembled is not the speech the public quotes, because the public's version says "name" where the documentary tradition, and many editions since, say "word." The public version is a further conflation, Malone's architecture carrying the first quarto's word, filtered through quotation books, anthologies, and memory. Three agencies have thus shaped the famous twelve lines: the author, whose papers underlie the second quarto; the editor, who repaired the papers' damage by borrowing the short text's arrangement; and the quoting culture, which substituted the short text's climactic word into the repaired frame and then forgot every step. The crux, properly understood, is the record of all three agencies, and its study is the study of how a line becomes a quotation.

## Word or Name: What Each Reading Does to the Argument

A crux is worth an article only if the rival readings genuinely differ, and these do, though the difference is finer than the fame of the line suggests. The speech around the sentence is the play's most concentrated piece of reasoning about labels, and its terms are set before the rose appears. Juliet has established that the beloved's designation, not the beloved, is her enemy; that a Montague is no body part; that the designation is a title, a thing worn like clothing and capable of being doffed. The rose sentence is the proof offered for the whole position, the appeal to a case where designation and thing can be inspected separately. What the proof asserts depends, at the margin, on its final disputed word.

With "name," the proof is exact and narrow. The question has asked what a name contains; the answer instances a thing whose properties survive every change of name, because the properties were never in the name. The sentence's logic is substitution within a single category: names are interchangeable labels, and the rose, renamed at will, keeps its scent because scent is not a naming fact. The reading makes the sentence a perfect miniature of the speech's thesis, thesis and proof sharing a term, and it explains the reading's tenacity. It is the version a memory retains, because it is the version whose rhetoric is circular in the satisfying way: name in the question, name in the answer, nothing left over.

With "word," the proof widens by one register. A word is not only a name; it is any utterance, any unit of language, the category under which names fall along with everything else that can be said. The sentence then asserts that the rose's sweetness survives not merely renaming but rewording, that no verbal form of any kind touches the thing's nature. The claim is the stronger and more philosophical of the two, and it is the claim the scene goes on to dramatize: Romeo answers the speech in its own widened currency, taking Juliet at her word, offering to be new baptized under a fresh designation, and protesting that, had his designation been written, he would tear the word. The word "word" tolls through the exchange that follows the speech, three times in the mouths of the lovers within twenty lines, each time meaning the spoken or written token by which a person is summoned and known. The second quarto's reading plugs the rose sentence into that verbal network. The first quarto's reading seals the sentence shut as a self-contained aphorism. Both are intelligible. They are not the same sentence, and the difference is the difference between a proverb about names and an argument about language.

Period usage supports the wider reading's availability. In the English of the 1590s, "word" ranged across senses that modern English has parcelled out: a spoken utterance, a promise, a report, a password, a term, and yes, in contexts of naming, the name itself. Juliet uses the word in the promise sense within the same scene when she asks to be sent word of the marriage's time and place, and Romeo uses it in the token sense when he offers to tear the written word that names him. An audience hearing "by any other word" would not have stumbled; the phrase said, in the idiom of the time, by whatever other term of address or description. The crux is therefore not a choice between a right word and a wrong one in the language's terms. It is a choice between two authorial possibilities that the language equally permits, which is why the documents, and only the documents, can arbitrate it.

Catherine Belsey's essay "The Name of the Rose in Romeo and Juliet," published in *The Yearbook of English Studies* in 1993, remains the most searching account of what the speech's philosophy assumes, and it bears on the crux without being an essay about the crux. Belsey sets Juliet's question against the period's unsettled debate about whether names belong to things by nature or by convention, the debate that runs from Plato's *Cratylus* through Renaissance speculations about the Adamic language in which the first names fitted their creatures exactly. Juliet speaks as a conventionalist: the label is arbitrary, imposed, detachable, and the thing is itself regardless. Belsey's argument, developed across the speech's terms, is that the play neither simply endorses nor simply refutes that position; it shows a world in which the conventional label has acquired the force of a natural fact, because kinship, law, and vengeance are transacted through it. Romeo cannot doff Montague, because Montague is not a coat but a position in a structure that kills. The beloved's hope that the designation is no part of the man is refuted by every event that follows the speech, and the refutation is the tragedy.

What does Belsey's reading do to the choice between word and name? It diminishes the choice's power to settle the speech's meaning, and in diminishing it, clarifies what the choice is actually about. On either reading, Juliet asserts detachability; on either reading, the play answers with a corpse-strewn demonstration that the label is load-bearing. The semantic difference between the narrower and wider claim is real but it is not the difference between a true speech and a false one; both speeches are false in the way the tragedy requires, as the delivered study of [the balcony's big question](/2023/08/04/whats-in-a-name-romeo-and-juliet/) establishes at length. What the crux decides is not the play's philosophy but the text's wording, the prior question of what an edition is to print before interpretation begins. That ordering, documents before meanings, is the discipline the crux exists to teach, and Belsey's essay, by showing how much interpretation can be built on either form, inadvertently demonstrates why the documentary question cannot be settled by interpretive preference. An editor who chooses "name" because the narrower claim seems more poignant, or "word" because the wider one seems more philosophical, has the procedure backwards. The documents vote first; the meanings are counted after.

The opposing pole in the criticism is occupied by Murray J. Levith, whose *What's in Shakespeare's Names* (1978) takes Juliet's speech as its opening exhibit for the contrary conviction: that in Shakespeare, names are anything but arbitrary, that the playwright chose and inherited designations dense with significance, and that Juliet's doctrine is the one position the plays everywhere refute. Levith prints the rose sentence with "word" and records the first quarto's "name" in a bracket, a small typographical act that enacts the documentary verdict in passing. The bracket is worth dwelling on as a model of how the crux should live in print: the reading text carries the authoritative form, the rejected form is visible at the point of decision, and the reader loses nothing. Between Belsey's demonstration that the speech's thesis fails in the play's world and Levith's demonstration that the playwright's practice belies the thesis, the speech stands doubly ironized, and the crux stands where it belongs, as a question of evidence about two letters' difference in a printing house, not as a referendum on the philosophy. The philosophy is secure under either reading. The text is not, and securing the text is the editor's narrower, harder duty.

### What does 'that which we call' commit the speaker to?

It commits the speaker to performing the act the sentence declares irrelevant: the rose reaches the argument only under a description, "that which we call a rose," so naming is already at work inside the proof that naming does not matter. The circularity is the speech's open secret on either reading of the crux.

More than either reading's partisans usually notice. The phrase builds the act of calling into the sentence's grammar: the rose enters the argument not as a thing in a garden but as a thing under a description, "that which we call a rose," the naming already performed inside the clause that will pronounce naming irrelevant. Critics of the speech's logic have long marked the circularity: the proof that designation does not matter must use the designation to produce its example. The crux sharpens the circularity differently on each side. With "name," the sentence answers a question about names with a clause about calling, and the slippage between calling and naming passes unnoticed in the smoothness. With "word," the slippage is the sentence's open subject: calling is a verbal act, the rose is held under a word, and the assertion is that the verbal act, any verbal act, leaves the scent untouched. The second quarto's reading makes the sentence argue about the very medium in which it is itself an event. The first quarto's reading makes it argue about a social institution. Editors choosing between them are choosing, whether they know it or not, between a sentence about language and a sentence about society, and the documentary evidence relieves them of the burden of that choice by making it for them.

## What the Editors Print, and What the Apparatus Confesses

The modern editorial map at the rose line is a study in near-consensus with one distinguished defection. The Folger Shakespeare, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, prints "word" and keeps the second quarto's own arrangement at the catalogue, letting the dislocated half-line stand in its printed place with pointing to guide the sense. The Cambridge edition prints "word" and Malone's repaired arrangement, marking the transposed passage so that the repair is visible on the page. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition prints "word" within the speech as Malone arranged it, and numbers the scene 2.1, so that the famous question carries the address 2.1.86 in that edition against the 2.2.43 of the Folger, Cambridge, and Arden texts, a reminder that even the line's coordinates are editorial. René Weis's Arden edition, the third series text of 2012, prints "word" in Malone's arrangement. David Greenham's *Close Reading: The Basics*, taking its text from Brian Gibbons's Arden edition, quotes the sentence in the "word" form and builds a discussion of the word "word" upon it. The Pelican Shakespeare prints "name," standing with the first quarto and, consciously or not, with the quotation tradition, while its own textual essay supplies the most quoted modern statement that the familiar speech is a conflation. The split is therefore not between careless and careful editions. It is between editions that follow the copy-text through this crux and an edition that exercises, at this one word, the editor's liberty to prefer the rival witness's reading on grounds of sense and fitness.

The apparatus is where editions confess what the reading text conceals, and at this passage the confessions repay reading as literature in their own compressed form. The Cambridge apparatus at the speech's opening records, in a single line of sigla, the whole documentary situation of the question: the pointed form is credited to the fourth quarto and the first; the second and third quartos stand unpunctuated; the Folio stands garbled. At the transposed block, Cambridge's asterisks perform the same office for Malone's surgery. An edition's honesty is measurable by what its small print admits, and by that measure the crux is well served by the scholarly tradition: a reader who consults the notes at 2.2.43-44 in any of the documentary editions will find "word" in the text and "name" in the record, the decision taken and the evidence preserved in the same opening. What no apparatus can preserve is the quotation tradition's version, because that version was never edited; it was remembered, and memory keeps no apparatus.

One presentation escapes the choice altogether, and the passage has attracted it oftener than most: the parallel text, in which the two quartos' speeches are printed facing one another and the reader performs the collation with the eye. Parallel presentation is the honest format for a crux whose interest lies in the divergence itself, and teachers reach for it at this speech instinctively, setting the short quarto's dozen tidy lines against the long quarto's fuller, damaged splendour. Its cost is the surrender of the edited text's chief service, a single page to read and perform from, and its benefit is the one this article has pursued throughout: at the rose line, uniquely, the divergence is the meaning's documentary foundation, and a reader who has watched the two speeches differ at the catalogue, the pointing, and the climactic word has learned the passage more completely than a reader of either form alone. The editions that print one text and confess the other in small type are not evading the parallel presentation. They are compressing it into a page, and the compression is legible to anyone the crux has taught to read small print as the record of a decision rather than as furniture.

The disagreement among the editors is genuine and deserves adjudication rather than a tally. The Pelican's "name" rests on an intelligible principle: that where the copy-text's reading and a rival witness's reading are both possible, the editor may choose the one that makes the passage cohere most completely with its own rhetoric, and the speech is about names from its first line to its last bargain. The documentary editions' "word" rests on the opposed principle: that coherence of that kind is precisely what transmission manufactures, especially in a reported text, and that an editor who rewards it systematically will gradually replace the author's characteristic roughness with the tradition's polish. Adjudicated on the principles, the documentary position wins, and not narrowly. The whole evidentiary basis for trusting the second quarto more than the first is that the second preserves the author's papers, false starts and all, while the first preserves a performance's memory of the play. To prefer the first quarto's climactic word because it fits better is to prefer, in the one place where the two witnesses' characters differ most consequentially, the witness whose known talent is for fitting things. "Name" fits the speech the way a remembered punchline fits a story. That is what memories do to lines, and it is why editors are not licensed to follow them at a crux where the copy-text is sound.

### Which form should a student quote in an essay?

The essay should quote its own edition and no other: the reading on that edition's page, under that edition's scene and line numbers, with one clause acknowledging the variant if the wording matters to the argument. Fame is not an edition, and a quotation that answers to fame while citing a book answers to neither honestly.

The form in the edition the essay is working from, cited to that edition, with the variant acknowledged in a phrase if the argument touches the line's wording. Quoting "name" from a documentary edition because the phrase is famous, or "word" from the Pelican because an article recommended it, confuses the two authorities the crux keeps separate: the edition governs the text, the tradition governs the fame, and an essay that knows which of its sentences answers to which authority has understood the passage better than one that merely quotes it correctly. The one indefensible practice is silent mixture, printing the famous form while citing an edition that prints the other, a small dishonesty that the crux exists to expose and that markers, like editors, are entitled to penalize.

## The Popular Form: How 'Name' Won the World Without Winning the Text

The quotation tradition's preference for "name" is usually treated as a mere error, the public misremembering its Shakespeare. The documents suggest a more interesting account. "Name" is not a corruption that crept into the line in the age of greeting cards; it is the line's first printed form, published in 1597, two years before "word" existed in print, and performed, if the first quarto's title page is believed, often and with great applause before either printing. The form of the sentence that the culture retains may descend, at whatever number of removes, from the form in which the sentence was first heard and first read. The documentary tradition that carried "word" through the seventeenth century was a tradition of books, a few thousand copies in scholars' and players' hands. The theatrical and proverbial tradition that carried "name" was a tradition of mouths. When the two traditions met in the eighteenth century's editions, the editors negotiated; when they meet in the modern memory, the mouths win, because the sentence is retained by people who have heard it oftener than they have read it, and what they heard was the proverb.

The proverb's mechanics favour "name" at every joint. The sentence is almost always quoted in its stripped form, "a rose by any other name would smell as sweet," detached from the question that precedes it and the speech that surrounds it, and in the stripped form the word "name" does double duty: it is both the sentence's subject and its frame, allowing the fragment to mean its whole doctrine without context. Stripped of its frame, "by any other word" sounds, to an ear trained by the proverb, like a mistake, because the fragment's internal echo has been lost. Quotation dictionaries, anthologies of familiar sayings, schoolroom posters, and the vast secondary literature of the phrase, the territory the series surveys in its study of ['a rose by any other name' as an everyday phrase](/2029/08/24/rose-by-any-other-name-idiom/), transmit the self-sealing form because the self-sealing form is what a proverb is for. An idiom that required its apparatus would not be an idiom.

There is no scandal in this, and the article's verdict does not pretend otherwise. Proverbs are not editions, and a culture that says "name" is not committing an error against a text it never claimed to be quoting exactly; it is using a sentence the play released into the language, and the sentence as released carried, from its first printing, the "name" form. The scandal, such as it is, runs the other way: readers who meet the line in a documentary edition and find "word" often assume the edition is wrong, and teachers who meet it in the proverb assume the documents can be ignored. Both assumptions mistake the crux's lesson. The documents establish what the edited text should print; the proverb establishes what the language has done with the line since; and the two establishments are compatible the moment each is confined to its jurisdiction. An editor prints "word." A speaker quoting the proverb says "name." A scholar writing about the line says which jurisdiction the sentence on the page is answering to, and the whole four-hundred-year confusion dissolves into a footnote that the Cambridge apparatus, in its way, already writes.

## Fame as an Editorial Problem: Why the Weaker Reading Feels Right

Every crux is an argument about evidence, but this crux is also an argument about a feeling: the documentary reading strikes most readers, on first encounter, as wrong. The feeling deserves analysis rather than dismissal, because it is not ignorance. It is the predictable product of the same cognitive machinery that produced the variant in the first place. A sentence encountered a thousand times in one form acquires the form's familiarity as a property of the sentence itself; the familiar version does not merely seem commoner, it seems truer, the way a face seen daily seems the standard against which portraits are judged. When an edition prints "word," the reader's memory files a protest in the vocabulary of textual criticism: surely the book has made a mistake. The protest feels like knowledge. It is actually recognition, and recognition is not a documentary faculty.

The symmetry with the first quarto's probable error is exact and worth stating plainly. Whoever stands behind the short text's "name," reporter, adapter, or revising author setting down a first thought, the reading's power comes from the speech's own repetitions recruiting the sentence's climax into their pattern. The modern reader's feeling that "name" must be right is that same recruitment operating across centuries: the speech says the noun four times, the culture has repeated the sentence a million times in the noun's form, and the two repetitions conspire to make the documentary reading sound like a slip. Textual criticism exists, as a discipline, precisely for the class of cases where the better-attested reading sounds wrong, because a method that only ratified what readers already believed would have no function. The rose crux is the discipline's most public test case, the one where the gap between feeling and evidence can be demonstrated to any reader in five minutes with three old books, or three transcriptions, open on the table.

### Why do readers trust the familiar form over the documents?

Because familiarity is the only credential most quotations ever present. A famous line arrives without its apparatus, carrying no signal of the choices that produced it, and the mind mistakes the absence of visible doubt for the presence of certainty in the form the memory already holds.

The documentary reading, announced with an editor's hedges and a note, looks tentative beside the proverb's confidence, although the confidence is unearned and the hedges are the marks of the only process that ever checked. Readers trust the familiar form for the reason audiences trust a confident witness: fluency reads as truth. The crux's whole pedagogy consists in reversing that inference at one small, safe, verifiable point.

The editorial consequences of the feeling are measurable in the history the earlier sections traced. Editors are readers first, and the pull toward "name" has operated on them as on everyone: the reading's appearances in edited texts across the centuries cluster where an editor's method licenses preference among viable readings, and vanish where method binds the choice to the copy-text. The Pelican's defection is the honourable form of the pull, exercised openly by editors whose own essay taught the conflation lesson. The dishonourable form is silent: the school text, the anthology, the quotation collection that prints "name" without any documentary act at all, inheriting the proverb while wearing the edition's authority. Between those poles the crux does its sorting work. It cannot make the feeling go away, and should not try: the feeling is the record of the line's life in the language, and a criticism that cannot honour the life while correcting the text has misunderstood both. What the crux can do is assign the feeling its proper evidentiary weight, which is none, and its proper cultural weight, which is enormous, and keep the two ledgers separate. That separation, maintained in the face of the most familiar sentence in the language, is the whole art the passage teaches.

## The Look of the Sentence: Spelling, Capital Letters, and Lineation

A crux is usually discussed as if texts were made of meanings, but the early pages are made of letters, and the letters at this passage carry their own evidence about the sentence's travels. Begin with the flower. In the first quarto's line, as the Pelican transcription gives it, the rose is capitalized: "That which we call a Rose." The second quarto prints the flower small: "that which we call a rose." The Folio, following its copy's tradition at one remove and its compositors' habits at another, restores the capital: "that which we call a Rose." Capitalization in hand-press books is an accidental, governed by compositor taste and by the copy's own inconsistency, and no editor would build an argument on it. Yet the accidentals map the transmission with accidental fidelity: the sentence's spelling records which shop set it and from what copy, and the modern text's uniform lower case is a modernization that erases the map. A reader of the modern page sees a sentence that appears to have descended through history without touching it. A reader of the early pages sees the touching everywhere.

The spelling of the contested sentence's other words tells the same story in smaller compass. The second quarto's "sweete" keeps its final vowel; the first quarto's "sweet" does not; the Folio's "sweete" matches its copy's line. The beloved's designation appears as "Montagne" in the first quarto's catalogue and "Mountague" in the second's, spellings that reflect the copy behind each book and the habits of the men who set them. Speech prefixes in the second quarto at this scene abbreviate and vary in the pattern that students of that edition, Eric Sams among them in his reappraisal of the first quarto, have catalogued across the play: the heroine's prefix runs through forms from the full designation to a bare initial pair of letters. None of these features decides the crux. All of them belong to the crux's proper study, because they demonstrate the article's recurring claim in its most material form: the sentence has a documentary body, and the body has a history that the quotation, stripped and modernized, is designed to make its users forget.

Lineation performs the same erasure at the level of the verse. In the modern text the sentence occupies the ends of two lines, numbered 43 and 44 of the scene in most editions, the question at the close of the first, the proof completing the second, the whole couplet-shaped unit set off by its sense. In the second quarto the passage is set as verse in a scene whose lineation the printing house elsewhere in the speech disturbs, part of the general untidiness of copy set at speed; in the first quarto the shorter speech is set the more cleanly for being shorter. The Folio's through line numbers, the coordinates by which Reid cites the garbled question at 837 and 838, belong to a system invented for a book with no act and scene divisions reliable enough to cite by, Charleton Hinman's great finding aid turned accidental monument: the most famous question in the play is most precisely locatable, in its most prestigious early book, at an address where it is misprinted. There is a parable in that for every reader who believes that canonical status and textual accuracy keep house together. They are neighbours. They are not the same house.

Even the speech's position in the scene shifts its address between editions, as the Oxford numbering shows: scene 2 of act 2 in the Folio-derived tradition, scene 1 in editions that do not count the Chorus's second appearance as a scene in its own right. The line has, in short, no single stable citation, no single stable spelling, no single stable pointing, and no single agreed climactic word. What it has is a rhythm and a sense that survive every instability, and a fame that the instabilities have never once impeded. Critics who find the documentary study of such a line pedantic might consider what the survival proves: the culture transmits the music and the doctrine and lets the documents fall where they may. The editor's task is to be the one agent in the system whose job is the documents, and the crux is the place where that task becomes visible to everyone.

## What Kind of Error Is the First Quarto's 'Name'?

Suppose, as the verdict holds, that the author's sentence said "word." The first quarto's "name" then requires explanation, and the available explanations sort into three, each implying a different account of what the first quarto is. The general comparison of the two quartos' characters, pursued in the series' study of [Q1 vs Q2: what the differences reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/), supplies the framework; the rose line is the framework's most famous test case.

The first explanation is memorial substitution. On the account of the first quarto as a reported text, reconstructed from performance by participants or auditors, its variants should cluster where memory predictably fails: at connectives, at unstressed syllables, and at words for which the context supplies a seductive synonym. "Name" for "word" fits the profile exactly. The speech has repeated the noun "name" through its opening sentences; a reporter carrying the speech away in the ear arrives at the climax with the noun ringing, and produces the ringing noun where the author wrote the quieter one. The substitution improves the line's local symmetry while diminishing its sense, the signature of memorial error, which optimizes for recall rather than for argument. On this account "name" is precisely the kind of reading the first quarto would generate if it were not authorial at this point, and its attractiveness is evidence against it.

The second explanation is deliberate adaptation. On the account of the first quarto as a theatrical version, cut and adjusted for performance by the company or its bookkeeper, some variants are not failures but decisions. An adapter shortening the balcony scene for the stage might well prefer the self-sealing form of the sentence, which lands faster in a theatre and asks less of an audience following an argument at speaking speed. The rose line in performance is a laugh-line's neighbour, a moment of delighted recognition in the house, and "name" is the form an audience can applaud without parsing. On this account the reading is a genuine theatrical improvement and a documentary irrelevance: what plays best and what the author wrote are different questions, and the adapter's business was the first.

The third explanation is authorial priority. Eric Sams's reappraisal of the first quarto argued, against the whole reported-text tradition, that the short quarto represents the author's own first version of the play and the long quarto his revision of it. On that model, "name" is what Shakespeare wrote first, and "word" is his improvement, the reviser's widening of the claim from a social category to a verbal one as the speech's design clarified in his mind. The model cannot be dismissed by pointing to the first quarto's roughness, since first drafts are rough, and it gives "name" the one pedigree that would make it genuinely authorial. Its consequence for editors is nonetheless the same as the other models' consequences, and the convergence is the strongest structural argument the crux affords. If the first quarto is a report, "name" is a reporter's error. If it is an adaptation, "name" is an adapter's improvement. If it is a first draft, "name" is a superseded reading, the form the author himself replaced when he revised the speech into the version the second quarto transmits. Under every theory of the first quarto that scholarship has seriously entertained, the edited text of the play as revised and published in the author's maturity prints "word," and "name" belongs in the apparatus, honoured, visible, and not promoted. A crux in which all roads of transmission criticism lead to the same reading is as settled as cruxes get; the wonder is not that editors print "word" but that the displaced reading ever found, in fame, a second kingdom where no editor's writ runs.

The sister case in this run of textual studies confirms the method by contrast. The "Gallop apace" crux, examined in Article 228, presents a passage where the first quarto cannot arbitrate because its scene is so compressed, where the second quarto and Folio divide over a single word of the invocation, and where centuries of editors have proposed emendations none of which commands the field. There, no reading earns the presumption cleanly and the crux remains genuinely open. Here, the presumption is clean, the rival is explicable under every model, and the verdict follows. Setting the two side by side shows that textual criticism is not a machine for manufacturing doubt. It is a method for distinguishing the passages where doubt is the honest conclusion from the passages where it is merely the fashionable one.

### Could the second quarto's 'word' be the error instead?

In principle a compositor could substitute any word, but no known printing-house failure fits: the documented errors at this passage are mechanical, stranding half-lines and dropping punctuation, while replacing the obvious "name" with the subtler "word," against six repetitions pulling the other way, would be an error that improves the argument it corrupts.

The question must be asked, because the second quarto's compositors demonstrably mishandled this very speech: they dislocated its catalogue and dropped its pointing, and a compositor who could strand "Belonging to a man" could in principle substitute a word. The mechanism, though, does not fit. Compositor error of the documented kinds at this passage is mechanical: eyeskip, transposition, misdivision, the sort of slip that garbles without improving. "Word" for "name" would have to be a semantic substitution, a compositor replacing the obvious word with a subtler one against the pull of six preceding repetitions, an error that sharpens the argument it corrupts. Printing houses do not err in that direction; memories do, and memories err the other way, from the subtler word to the obvious one. The asymmetry of the two hypothetical errors is the crux in miniature. "Name" is explicable as the corruption of "word" by any of the known agencies of corruption. "Word" is explicable as the corruption of "name" by none of them. Direction of error, the oldest test in the discipline, decides what the tally of witnesses could not.

## The Verdict on the Rose Crux, and How to Quote the Line

The verdict follows from the documents, the error profiles, and the sense, in that order. Print "word": the second quarto reads it, the Folio tradition transmits it, the direction-of-error test supports it, every theory of the first quarto leaves it standing as the revised text's reading, and the wider claim it makes for Juliet's argument, that no verbal form touches the thing itself, is the claim the scene's own language of words and word-tearing sustains. Record "name" in the apparatus at the line, without apology and without promotion: it is the first printed form of the sentence, a reading of genuine historical dignity, and the ancestor of the proverb. Retain Malone's repair of the catalogue, because the dislocation is a demonstrable mechanical failure, and mark the repair as the Cambridge edition marks it, so that the reader can see the joint. Point the question as the fourth quarto and the first point it. Modernize the spelling, since modernization is the declared convention of a modern-spelling edition, and let the notes carry the old forms for any reader who wants the sentence's documentary body back.

For the common reader, the practical conclusions are three, and they are worth stating without sigla. First, an edition that prints "word" is not mistaken, eccentric, or modernized beyond recognition; it is printing the authoritative text, and the famous form is the variant. Second, an edition that prints "name" is not illiterate; it is exercising an editor's liberty in favour of the earliest printed form and the passage's surface rhetoric, and its choice is defensible exactly as far as its notes disclose it. Third, the quotation in the wild, "a rose by any other name," is neither edition's text but the proverb's, a fourth state of the sentence with its own legitimacy in its own jurisdiction, the jurisdiction surveyed in the series' account of the line's life as an everyday phrase. The confusions begin only when jurisdictions are crossed in silence: when the proverb is cited as the play, when an edition is cited for a reading it does not print, when the line's authority in an argument depends on a form its speaker's author probably never finally wrote.

What the crux finally exposes is the structure of textual authority itself, at the point where that structure supports the most weight. The most famous line in the play survives in three early states, none of them the familiar one; the familiar one was built by an editor from the wreckage of a damaged passage; the building was so skilful that the world mistook it for the author's unaided work; and inside the built passage, one syllable continued to arrive from the wrong witness, carried by fame past every documentary checkpoint. If that is true of this line, guarded by four centuries of editors and quoted by millions, it is true in some degree of every line that reaches a reader through print. The rose crux is therefore not a curiosity for specialists. It is the general condition of reading Shakespeare, made visible at the one point where every reader can be brought to see it, because every reader arrives already knowing the line by heart, and knowing it, in the documentary sense, wrong. Restoring the crux does not take the line away from the reader who loves it. It gives that reader the line's whole biography, and a sentence with a biography is a richer possession than a sentence with only a reputation.

### Does restoring the crux change how the line should be taught?

It changes the lesson's order, not its destination: before the speech is read as the play's great statement about names, it is collated as a made object with a history, so that students learn to ask of any famous sentence how it reached the page. The thematic reading then proceeds on a text whose credentials are known.

It changes the order of the lesson, not its destination. The speech remains what the thematic criticism has always found in it, the play's great assertion about names and identity, whose full treatment belongs to the series' study of [names and identity](/2024/04/12/names-and-identity-romeo-and-juliet/) in the lovers' lives and to the balcony articles' readings of the argument. What the documentary lesson adds is a prior question that students can be taught to ask of any famous sentence: in what form has this reached the page, and whose hands are on it? A class that collates the three early states of the rose sentence learns more about what a text is in twenty minutes than a term of paraphrase can teach, because the variants are small enough to see whole and large enough to matter. The line's fame, so often the obstacle to reading it freshly, becomes the instrument: nobody needs to be persuaded that the sentence matters, and the discovery that it also has a history lands with the force of a plot twist in a story the class thought it knew.

## Counting the Speech: How Its Own Words Vote

One further body of evidence sits inside the text itself, and it is the kind a reader can verify with a finger on the page: the distribution of the two contested terms across the speech and the exchange that answers it. The name speech proper, in the modern conflated text, speaks its key noun four times in ten lines. The designation is the enemy; the question asks what is in a name; the beloved is begged to doff his name; the name is declared no part of him. Around those four occurrences the speech builds its subsidiary vocabulary of titles, baptism, and calling, and its presiding noun is unmistakably the one the first quarto's reading repeats a fifth time at the climax. Read in isolation, the speech's internal statistics plead for "name" with real force: the sentence that asks what is in a name sits inside a paragraph that can think of nothing else, and a fifth occurrence completes the pattern with an almost musical inevitability.

The exchange that follows the speech reverses the statistics completely. Romeo's answers run on the other noun. He takes Juliet at her word, the word meaning at once her utterance and her pledge. He declares his designation hateful to him because it is an enemy to her, and wishes that, had it been written, he would tear the word, the name imagined as a physical inscription that can be ripped from a page. Juliet, within the same scene, has not yet drunk a hundred words of his utterance, and will close the scene's arrangements by asking to be sent word of the marriage's time and place. Across the balcony scene as the second quarto transmits it, "word" is the term in which the lovers actually transact their business: pledges are words, messages are words, the hated designation is a word that can be torn. The rose sentence, on the second quarto's reading, is the thesis of which those later usages are the working out: the scene announces that things survive their words, and then spends two hundred lines demonstrating how men and women live and die by words all the same. On the first quarto's reading, the sentence is sealed off from the scene's working vocabulary, a jewel of a proverb set in a scene that then proceeds to talk about something else.

Internal evidence of this kind cannot outweigh documentary evidence, and the article's verdict does not ask it to. What it does is remove the last plausible motive for overriding the documents. The case for importing "name" against the copy-text was always a case from fitness: the speech is about names, so its climax should say name. The fuller count shows the fitness argument to have been conducted on too small a sample. The scene is about names and words in a sustained counterpoint, the social fact and the verbal token, and the climax sentence belongs to the counterpoint, not to one side of it. An editor who once saw the count whole would need a positive reason to prefer the first quarto's sealed aphorism to the second quarto's connected argument, and no such reason has been produced in four centuries of the crux's discussion. The presumption for the copy-text stands, and the internal evidence, called as a witness for the rival, ends by testifying for the defence.

There is also a lesson in the count for readers of the apparatus. Variants are usually presented as isolated points, a word here, a word there, each decided on its local merits. The rose crux shows a variant whose meaning is distributed across a scene: change the syllable and the scene's verbal economy changes with it, tilting the balance between the institution the lovers are fighting and the medium they are fighting in. Editors know this, which is why cruxes at famous lines attract disproportionate print; the New Variorum tradition of commentary, which at other cruxes in this play has filled pages over single words, rests on the recognition that in a text this dense, no variant is only local. The syllable is small. The economy it tips is the whole scene's.

A parallel from the speech's other end confirms the pattern's reach. The bargain that closes the speech is struck in the vocabulary of clothing and worship, doffing, title, baptism, saint, and the name is the garment throughout; yet the sentence that has attracted the crux sits at the exact hinge where the speech stops inventorying the garment and starts theorizing the wardrobe's irrelevance. A reading that keeps the hinge word inside the garment's vocabulary, as "name" does, makes the speech a list with a moral. The copy-text's reading makes the hinge swing: at the crux the speech changes its subject from one family's nomenclature to the nature of verbal signs as such, and the bargain that follows is then negotiated, fittingly, in words about words, pledges, messages, and torn inscriptions. Editors rarely state the stakes at that size. The size is the passage's own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch rose crux, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch rose crux is the comparison table in this article setting the rose line's documentary career in one view: the three early witnesses, the eighteenth-century repair, and the modern editions, with each text's reading at the catalogue, the question, and the sentence. The pattern it exposes is a split between descent and fame. Every book in the line of transmission from the second quarto reads "word," and nearly every scholarly edition follows it, while the first quarto's "name" holds no documentary descendants at all yet owns the public memory outright. The table also exposes the deeper pattern that no early witness contains the speech in its familiar form, so the most quoted sentence in the play is an editorial assembly that the table takes apart into its components.

### Q: Which word does the second quarto print at the end of the rose line, word or name?

The second quarto prints "word": its line runs, in the original spelling, "By any other word would smell as sweete." The 1599 quarto is the copy-text from which modern editions of the play descend, printed from copy the scholarly consensus identifies as the author's working papers, and its reading at the crux is the one the documentary tradition has carried ever since. The third quarto reprinted the reading in 1609, the fourth quarto repeated it in 1622, and the First Folio, set from the third quarto, transmits it in 1623. A reader who meets "word" in a modern edition is meeting the reading of the play's authoritative early text, not an editorial novelty. The same quarto page shows its provenance's untidiness: the catalogue four lines earlier stands dislocated, a half-line stranded out of its place, so the witness that authorizes "word" is visibly a working text rather than a polished one, its authority residing in descent from the author's papers, not in surface perfection.

### Q: What does the first quarto read where the second quarto prints "word"?

The first quarto reads "name": "By any other name would smell as sweet." The 1597 quarto, the earliest printed text of the play, gives the name speech in a shorter and tidier form than the second quarto's, with the catalogue of body parts running cleanly to "nor any other part" and the famous question pointed as a question. Its reading at the crux is the form the world quotes, and it is the only early witness that has it. The reading's isolation is the heart of the crux: the first quarto is a text whose authority is everywhere qualified by its compression and paraphrase, so its unique reading at the famous line must argue its way past the copy-text's presumption, and on the evidence assembled in this article it cannot.

### Q: What exactly does the First Folio print at "What's in a name?"

The First Folio prints a garble: "What? in a names that which we call a Rose." The 1623 text of the play, set from a copy of the third quarto, breaks the most famous question in English drama into pieces, misdividing the words, planting a question mark in the wrong place, and pluralizing the noun the speech exists to interrogate. S. W. Reid's study of the Folio text cites the lines by their through line numbers, 837 and 838, and treats them as evidence of how little supervision the play received in that volume. The Folio keeps the second quarto's "word" in the sentence that follows, but its handling of the question disqualifies it as an independent witness at this passage: its testimony is its copy's, degraded.

### Q: Why do most scholarly editions print "word" in the rose line?

Most scholarly editions print "word" because editorial method follows the copy-text where the copy-text makes sense. The second quarto, printed from the author's papers, reads "word"; the reading is grammatical, pointed, and arguably the finer sense, widening Juliet's claim from names to all verbal forms; and the rival reading comes from a witness whose known habits include exactly the synonym substitution that would produce "name" from "word." The Folger, Cambridge, Oxford, and Arden editions all print "word" on this reasoning. The burden of proof in textual criticism lies on the reading that would displace the copy-text, and at this crux the displacement case, built on smoothness and fame, has never discharged it.

### Q: Which well-known modern edition prints "name" instead?

The Pelican Shakespeare prints "name." Its reading text gives the sentence in the form the public knows, standing with the first quarto against the copy-text tradition its own documentary situation would predict. The choice is the more striking because the Pelican's textual essay chooses this very passage to demonstrate that the familiar speech is an editorial conflation found in no early text. The edition thus holds both ends of the crux at once: its essay teaches that the famous lines are a made object, and its text makes them in the popular form. The defection is distinguished rather than careless, an editor's liberty exercised openly, and it usefully proves that the crux is a genuine choice and not a mechanical consequence of the evidence.

### Q: Who assembled the familiar form of the name speech, and from what materials?

Edmond Malone assembled it, in his 1790 edition, from the two quartos. The second quarto's catalogue of body parts is dislocated, with the half-line "Belonging to a man" stranded after the exclamation "O be some other name." The first quarto's catalogue is sound, completing the list with "nor any other part." Malone spliced the first quarto's arrangement into the longer text, producing the sequence every modern reader knows, in which the catalogue closes, the fragment takes its grammatical place, and the exclamation follows. The repair has been generally ratified since, and the Pelican essay's verdict stands over the result: no early text reads as the modern text does, and this is the most famous speech in the play.

### Q: What is dislocated in the second quarto's catalogue of body parts?

The half-line "Belonging to a man" is dislocated. As the second quarto prints the passage, Juliet's catalogue runs "nor hand nor foot, nor arm nor face," then breaks into the exclamation "O be some other name," and only then delivers the fragment "Belonging to a man," which belongs grammatically to the catalogue it now follows too late. The sentence's skeleton has slipped a joint, most plausibly because the author's papers at this point preserved a phrase repositioned in revision, or because a compositor displaced the half-line in setting. The damage is local and mechanical. It requires the repair Malone eventually supplied, and it has no tendency to cast doubt on the sound sentence that follows four lines later, where the crux of word and name resides.

### Q: When did the question mark enter the famous question in the quarto line?

The question mark enters the second quarto's line of descent in the fourth quarto of 1622. The Cambridge edition's apparatus records the situation in one entry: the pointed form "What's in a name?" is credited to the fourth quarto and to the first quarto, while the second and third quartos print the words unpunctuated, running the question straight into its evidence, and the Folio prints its garble. The mark is an accidental, the layer of text most subject to printing-house practice, and no argument at the crux turns on it. It nonetheless disciplines the imagination: the interrogative flourish every reader takes for granted was supplied to the authoritative tradition a generation after the copy-text, by hands the quotation never mentions.

### Q: Does any book printed in Shakespeare's lifetime contain the speech in exactly the form in which it is quoted?

No lifetime printing contains the speech in its familiar form. The first quarto has the tidy catalogue and the pointed question but reads "name" and carries a shorter speech. The second quarto has "word" and the fuller matter but a dislocated catalogue and no punctuation at the question. The Folio has "word" and a garbled question. The smooth dozen lines the world quotes, tidy catalogue, pointed question, and all, exist only from the eighteenth-century editions that assembled them, Malone's of 1790 chief among them. The Pelican Shakespeare's textual essay makes the point in its canonical form: there is no early text that reads as the modern text does, at the most famous speech in the play.

### Q: What does the Cambridge edition's apparatus record at the rose line?

The Cambridge apparatus, edited by G. Blakemore Evans, records the whole documentary situation in compressed sigla. At the famous question it credits the pointed reading to the fourth quarto and the first, notes the unpunctuated run-on of the second and third quartos, and registers the Folio's "What? in a names." Around the speech it marks Malone's transposed block with asterisks so the repair is visible in the text, and at the crux word itself the text prints "word" with the first quarto's "name" consigned to the record. The apparatus is the edition's confession and its conscience: everything the reading text decides, the small print preserves, and a reader who consults it at 2.2.43-44 holds the entire crux in a dozen abbreviations.

### Q: How does S. W. Reid describe the state of the famous lines in the early texts?

Reid describes the lines as appearing correctly in no early text. His study "The Editing of Folio Romeo and Juliet," published in *Studies in Bibliography*, volume 35, exhibits the Folio's version, "What? in a names that which we call a Rose, / By any other word would smell as sweete," cited by through line numbers 837 and 838, as part of a larger demonstration that the play received little editorial supervision in the First Folio. The judgement generalizes beyond the Folio. Correctness at this passage, in the form posterity quotes, is not a property any of the three early witnesses possesses; it is a property conferred later, by editors, and Reid's sentence is the scholarly warrant for the article's central claim that the famous line is a made object.

### Q: Why does the Folio's agreement with the second quarto add no extra weight to "word"?

The Folio's agreement adds no weight because the Folio is not an independent witness for this play. Its text was set from a copy of the third quarto, which is a reprint of the second, so the Folio's "word" is the second quarto's "word" transmitted through two further printings, not a second judgement formed on separate evidence. Counting the Folio as a corroborating vote double-counts a single act of printing, the elementary error the discipline of recension exists to prevent. The genuine division at the crux is one tradition against one: the second quarto and its descendants on one side, the first quarto on the other, and the contest must be settled by the witnesses' characters and the direction of error, not by a tally of books.

### Q: What kind of error would "name" be if the first quarto is a reported text?

It would be a memorial substitution, the classic error of reported texts. A reporter carrying the speech away from performance has heard its key noun four times before the climax: the name that is the enemy, the name in the question, the name to be doffed, the name that is no part of the man. Arriving at the rose sentence, memory supplies the ringing word, the context's own suggestion, in place of the quieter word the author wrote. The substitution improves the line's symmetry while narrowing its sense, and that profile, optimized for recall rather than argument, is the signature of memorial transmission. On the reported-text account, "name" is therefore not merely explicable as an error; it is the precise error the theory predicts at this precise point in the speech.

### Q: How does Eric Sams's account of the first quarto change the status of "name"?

Eric Sams's reappraisal of the 1597 quarto argues that the short text is not a report at all but Shakespeare's own first version of the play, with the second quarto representing his revision. On that account "name" changes status from a reporter's error to an author's first thought, genuinely Shakespearean and genuinely superseded. The change dignifies the reading without promoting it. An edited text of the play prints the work in its revised form, the form the second quarto transmits, and a first-draft reading displaced by the author's own revision belongs in the apparatus however distinguished its pedigree. Sams's model thus joins the memorial and adaptation models at the same editorial conclusion, reached by three different roads: print "word," record "name."

### Q: Which later words in the exchange echo the second quarto's "word"?

Romeo's answering speeches echo it three times within the scene. He takes Juliet at her word, the word as pledge and utterance in one. He wishes his written designation could be destroyed as a physical thing: had it been written, he would tear the word. Juliet herself measures the encounter in the same currency, protesting that her ears have not yet drunk a hundred words of his utterance, and closes the scene's business by asking to be sent word of the marriage. The second quarto's rose sentence, on its own reading, opens the theme those usages work out: the scene announces that things survive their words, then spends its length showing two people transacting their fate entirely in words. The first quarto's "name" leaves the sentence outside that economy.

### Q: What happened to the rose line in the third and fourth quartos?

The third quarto of 1609 reprinted the second quarto's sentence entire: the dislocation in the catalogue, the unpunctuated question, and the reading "word," passing the whole state of the passage unchanged into the copy from which the Folio would be set. The fourth quarto of 1622 reprinted the reading as well, but added the sentence's first piece of modern dress in this line of descent, the punctuation that turns "Whats in a name" into the question "What's in a name?" Neither reprint contributes evidence at the crux word, since reprints transmit rather than judge. Together they show the second quarto's form of the line stabilizing as the seventeenth century's received text, "word" unchallenged in print for over a century.

### Q: Can the choice between word and name be heard in performance?

The choice cannot be heard as a difference of sound, because the two words are metrically identical: single syllables of the same weight, occupying the same position in the line, leaving the verse's rhythm untouched whichever an actor speaks. No audience has ever detected the crux by ear, and no actor's choice between the forms can be faulted on delivery. What can be heard is the sentence's shape, and there the forms diverge in effect if not in sound. "Name" closes the question's circle audibly, the same noun returning like a rhyme. "Word" opens the sentence outward, toward the scene's later tearing and sending of words. The crux is thus a reader's and editor's crux in the strictest sense: it exists on the page, in the apparatus, and in the argument, and vanishes in the air of the theatre.

### Q: Why does the proverb keep the "name" form when editions print "word"?

The proverb keeps "name" because proverbs are transmitted by hearing and memory, not by collation, and the "name" form is engineered for memory. It is also the line's first printed form, published in 1597 and, on the first quarto's title page, much performed before that, so the form the culture retains may descend from the form in which the sentence was first heard. Stripped of its speech, the fragment "a rose by any other name" seals itself: the noun frames and fills the saying, requiring no context to deliver its doctrine. "Word" in the stripped fragment sounds to an ear trained by the proverb like an error. Quotation books, anthologies, and schoolrooms therefore transmit the self-sealing form, and the documentary form survives where documents are consulted: in editions. The two survivals compete only when a reader forces them to. The proverb's business is to be repeatable at a wedding, in a headline, on a valentine; the edition's business is to be answerable to 1599. Each form performs its own institution's duty, and confusion begins only when one borrows the other's credentials, when the proverb claims the document's authority or the edition silently borrows the proverb's word.

### Q: How do the editions disagree about where the rose line is numbered?

The editions disagree because they divide the act differently. In the Folio-derived scene division followed by the Folger, Cambridge, and Arden texts, the balcony scene is Act 2, Scene 2, and the rose sentence stands at lines 43 and 44, cited as 2.2.43-44. In Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition, which does not count the Chorus's speech opening the act as a separate scene, the balcony scene is 2.1, and the famous question stands at 2.1.86. The Folio itself offers a third address, the through line numbers 837 and 838 by which Reid cites its garbled version. A citation of the most famous line in the play is therefore incomplete without the edition it cites, one more sense in which the sentence exists only as editors have framed it.
