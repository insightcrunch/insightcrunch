The standard account of where Romeo and Juliet came from jumps a border. In that account the story is Italian, written by Luigi da Porto and retold by Matteo Bandello, and then it is English, versified by Arthur Brooke and dramatised by Shakespeare, and the map between those two facts is left blank. The blank is the error this article corrects. Between Bandello's novella and Brooke's poem stands a French book, Pierre Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques of 1559, and the French book is not a corridor the story passed through unchanged. It is a workshop in which the story was altered in ways that England inherited and that Shakespeare's audience still watches: a poison bought across a counter in Mantua from a starving man, a heroine who wakes in the tomb one death too late, a narrator stationed outside the tale to deliver its moral verdict before its pleasures can excuse it. Restoring Pierre Boaistuau to the chain changes what the chain means.

![Pierre Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques and the French road of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict argued here is plain. The French tradition contributed three things that no other stage of the transmission supplied: it converted a novella into a tragic history, a genre with a moral prosecutor built into its form; it darkened the ending by abolishing the lovers' final conversation, so that each lover dies in solitude; and it inserted the purchased poison, the apothecary scene, into the plot's machinery. Each contribution can be documented on Boaistuau's surviving page, each passes from his French into Brooke's English poem of 1562 and William Painter's English prose of 1567, and each remains legible in the play. The familiar claim that Shakespeare's tragedy descends from an Italian story told in English dress is therefore only two thirds true. The dress was cut in France.

That verdict needs its caution stated at the outset, because the brief for this article carries verification flags on its two most famous claims, the introduction of the apothecary and the change to the ending. Boaistuau worked from Bandello, but early modern adapters worked from whatever lay on the desk, and no scholar can inspect the whole of any sixteenth-century desk. What the documentary record establishes is narrower and stronger than a guess about one man's imagination: on every surviving page earlier than Boaistuau's, Romeo carries his death with him and Juliet wakes in time to speak; on Boaistuau's page, for the first time in the surviving record, Romeo buys his poison from a Mantuan apothecary and Juliet wakes to find him dead. Attribution in source study means exactly that kind of first appearance, and this article uses the word invention in that documentary sense throughout. Where the caution matters, the caution is argued openly rather than buried.

## Who Was Pierre Boaistuau?

Pierre Boaistuau was a Breton man of letters whose career sits at the exact crossroads where the Romeo and Juliet story changed languages. He was born in Nantes, in Brittany, around 1517, and he died in Paris in 1566, seven years after the book that concerns this article. He styled himself Launay on some title pages, a second surname drawn from his family, and the double name in library catalogues, Pierre Boaistuau, surnamed Launay, belongs to one man and not to two collaborators, a small bibliographical trap that has split his works in more than one catalogue. He travelled, or said he travelled, in the Levant, and he built a Parisian career out of the two skills the journey advertised: languages and compilation, the gathering of other men's matter into new French books.

His apprenticeship in other men's matter was distinguished. In 1558 Boaistuau edited the love tales of Marguerite de Navarre, the queen whose story collection appeared under his hand as the Histoires des amans fortunez and became famous after his death under the title Heptaméron. Editing the queen's tales taught him the trade he would practise on Bandello a year later: how to present a borrowed narrative to French readers, how to frame it with addresses and arguments, how to make an editor's hand feel like an author's. The same year produced his Théâtre du monde, a compilation on the miseries of human life that went through edition after edition, and two years later his Histoires prodigieuses gathered wonders and portents into another durable seller. Boaistuau was, in short, a professional of the new French book trade, a man who made compilations cohere, and the Histoires tragiques of 1559 is his most consequential compilation because of one tale inside it.

His position matters to the argument because it explains the kind of writer he was when he reached Bandello. He was not a poet serving a muse, and not a courtier polishing a gift manuscript. He was a working editor-author in a commercial press, answerable to a bookseller, Vincent Sertenas, and to a Paris readership that bought Italian matter eagerly and expected it improved to French taste. A writer in that position does not translate the way a schoolmaster translates. He selects, amplifies, suppresses, moralises and re-voices, because his name on the title page is a promise that the French book will do more than the foreign one. When source scholars describe Boaistuau's Romeo and Juliet as a free adaptation rather than a translation, the description is not an insult aimed at his Latin-school accuracy. It is a recognition of his profession. The freedom was the job.

The man also explains the book's moral temperature. Boaistuau's Théâtre du monde is a sustained meditation on human misery, and the same temperament presides over the Histoires tragiques: a taste for catastrophe as evidence, for endings as judgments. When such a writer takes up a tale of two teenagers who marry in secret, defy their houses and die in a tomb, he does not receive it as a romance that Fortune happened to spoil. He receives it as an exhibit. The lovers in his hands become a demonstration, arranged for readers who are to leave the book instructed rather than enchanted, and the demonstration's apparatus, the prefaces, the addresses, the closing moral, is not decoration bolted onto the tale. It is the reason the book exists, and it is the first of the French tradition's three gifts to the story's future.

His other compilations show the same hand learning its trade in public. The Théâtre du monde arranges the miseries of every estate of life into a theatre of examples, each misery produced, contemplated and moralised in turn. The Histoires prodigieuses collects wonders, monstrous births, comets, floods and portents, and subjects each wonder to the same treatment: narration first, significance immediately after, the reader never left alone with an unexplained marvel. Read in sequence, the three books are one method applied to three materials, estates, wonders and tales. The method is inventory followed by judgment, and its confidence is total: nothing in Boaistuau's world is permitted to be merely interesting. When that method was turned on Bandello's lovers, the result was predictable in kind if not in consequence. The inventory became the most complete the story had ever received, its scenes amplified, its speeches inventoried, its transactions itemised; and the judgment became the frame, the doctrine, the closing moral. Scholars who treat the Histoires tragiques as an accident in Boaistuau's bibliography, a translation job between his serious works, invert his career. The tragic histories are the centre of it, the place where the compiler of estates and the collector of wonders found the material his method had been rehearsing for: human beings whose lives could be inventoried to the last ducat and judged to the last breath.

## The Histoires tragiques of 1559: A New Kind of French Book

The book itself announces its method on its title page. Histoires tragiques extraictes des oeuvres italiennes de Bandel, et mises en langue françoise: tragic histories extracted from the Italian works of Bandello, and put into the French language. Every word of that title is a claim worth weighing. Extraictes admits selection and surgery. Mises en langue françoise, put into French, stops carefully short of the word translated, though the shortness has not prevented generations of catalogues from calling Boaistuau Bandello's French translator. And histoires tragiques, tragic histories, names a genre that did not exist in French before this volume gave it a title. The first volume, printed in Paris by Vincent Sertenas in 1559, contained six tales drawn from Bandello's Novelle, the great Italian collection published at Lucca in 1554. The third of the six is the history of Rhomeo and Julietta, as Boaistuau's French spells the lovers.

### What fixes the French volume's publication date within a year or two?

The Paris imprint of Vincent Sertenas and the book's own privilege apparatus fix the Histoires tragiques to 1559, the date its title page carries and the date from which every subsequent link in the chain is measured, and no rival imprint or competing edition clouds the year.

Brooke's English poem follows in 1562 and Painter's English prose in 1567, so the sequence French, verse, prose, stage is a matter of dated title pages rather than scholarly reconstruction, and the whole transmission from Italian novella to English tragedy occupies less than four decades.

Six tales is a small book beside Bandello's two hundred and fourteen, and the smallness is the point. Bandello's Novelle is a treasury, a courtly abundance in which the Verona lovers occupy one compartment among hundreds. Boaistuau's volume is an argument, six catastrophes chosen and arranged to prove that the tragic history could carry moral weight in French prose. The selection principle is visible in the choice of the Romeo story itself: a tale with a double death, a secret marriage, a meddling friar and a pair of defiant children is, for a compiler building a genre of exemplary disaster, not merely available material but perfect material. The French tradition of this story begins, therefore, not with an act of transmission but with an act of taste. Boaistuau picked the tale because it could be made to prosecute a case, and the case he prosecuted altered the tale's centre of gravity for every later teller, Brooke and Shakespeare included.

The genre he founded outlived him and multiplied. Boaistuau lived to see his collection reprinted and imitated; after his death in 1566 the Histoires tragiques became a franchise in other hands, above all those of François de Belleforest, whose continuations swelled the set, across the later sixteenth century, toward the eighteen volumes of the standard collected editions. French readers met Bandello's Italy, and much else, through this series for two generations. The Romeo and Juliet story rode inside that success. When English writers reached for the tale in the 1560s, they did not reach past France to Italy, whatever their title pages said. They reached for the version the French book trade had already made famous, in the form the French book trade had given it, and the form included Boaistuau's alterations as invisibly as it included Bandello's plot. That invisibility is why the French stage of the journey had to be recovered by scholarship rather than read off the title pages, and why its recovery changes the story's history rather than merely decorating it.

## The Italian Tale as Boaistuau Found It

A rewriting can only be measured against what was there to rewrite, and the tale Boaistuau received in Bandello's pages had a settled shape. Its Verona was already the Verona of the feuding Capelletti and Montecchi, names Dante had coupled in the Purgatorio six centuries of story earlier. Its heroine was already thirteen years of story younger than her mother had been at marriage, its hero already exiled to Mantua for a cousin's death, its friar already the architect of a sleeping draught and a letter that miscarries. The feast, the night meetings, the secret marriage, the forced match with the Count, the potion, the tomb: the itinerary was complete before a word of French touched it, and no honest account of Boaistuau can pretend otherwise. His raw material was a finished novella by the most copious Italian storyteller of the age, and its finish is part of what makes his departures legible. A departure needs a road.

Three features of the received tale matter most for what follows, because each stands exactly where a French alteration will later fall. The first is the poison. In the Italian line the hero's death is a possession, not a purchase: he comes to the final scene already provided with the means, and no third party profits, refuses, or is bribed. The catastrophe's economy is closed, two families and a friar settling accounts among themselves. The second feature is the waking. Bandello's Giulietta wakes while Romeo lives, and the lovers spend their last minutes in recognition and speech, the novella's most humane bequest to its characters. The third is the frame. Bandello presents his tale inside a dedication to a noble patron, with the courtesies of a man telling a story in company to pass an afternoon honourably. The moral, such as it is, travels in the dedication's compliments and in the reader's own discretion. Nothing prosecutes. Nothing sums up.

Hold those three features in view, the carried poison, the waking in time, the courtly frame, and the French version's work announces itself as a systematic inversion of each. Where the Italian hero carries, the French hero buys. Where the Italian heroine wakes to a voice, the French heroine wakes to a silence. Where the Italian author dedicates, the French author arraigns. The inversions are too patterned to be drift. They are a programme, executed by a writer who had read his source closely enough to find its three softest joints, the places where its meaning was least defended, and to rebuild each one in his own image. Source study sometimes speaks of influence as a vapour. Here it is carpentry, and the joints show.

## Bandello in French Dress: Translation as Rewriting

To understand what Boaistuau did to the story, the scale of Bandello's original must be held in mind. Bandello's novella, the ninth tale of the second part of the Novelle, is already a full retelling of Luigi da Porto's Giulietta e Romeo: it thickens the household around the lovers, promotes the nurse, stages the Capelletti feast, and brings its heroine to a death by her lover's dagger after a final exchange with the waking Romeo. Boaistuau took that novella as his working text. What he published, however, is roughly half again as long, and the growth is not padding in any simple sense. It is concentrated at the joints where a novella becomes a history: at the opening, where a moral argument precedes the narrative; at the scenes of decision, where speeches swell into set orations; and at the tomb, where rhetoric takes over from event almost entirely. The Italian tale tells; the French history arraigns, laments and concludes.

### Why did a Paris bookseller stake a new collection on Italian tales in French dress?

Italy's storytellers carried a prestige in French print that native invention could not match, and Bandello's Novelle, printed at Lucca in 1554, was the largest untapped treasury of that prestige in Europe. A Paris bookseller commissioning French versions bought a proven Italian inventory and a fashionable foreign label at once.

While Boaistuau's free method, amplification, moral framing and rhetorical expansion, let each extracted tale be sold as a French book rather than a foreign one merely carried across the language border.

Boaistuau's working method can be watched in his treatment of his source's frame. Bandello dedicates each novella to a patron and prefaces it with a letter that places the tale in courtly conversation; the story then largely speaks for itself. Boaistuau strips away the courtly dedication machinery and installs in its place his own apparatus: an address that tells the French reader what the tale will prove, authorial interventions that return at the joints of the plot, and a conclusion that collects the moral payment. The lovers' names pass into French spellings, Rhomeo and Julietta, the Capelletti and Montecchi becoming in his orthography the Capellets and the Montesches. Places, measures and customs are quietly naturalised. None of this is the behaviour of a translator anxious about equivalence. It is the behaviour of an author who has bought raw material and intends to sell a finished building, and sixteenth-century French usage had no difficulty with the arrangement: mises en langue françoise covered exactly this spectrum from rendering to remaking.

The consequence for source study is methodological, and it is the hinge of this article. Because Boaistuau rewrote rather than rendered, every agreement between his page and Brooke's poem that cannot be found in Bandello is a fingerprint: it marks matter that existed in French before it existed in English, and that Brooke, whose own title page credits Bandello's Italian, must have taken from the French book in front of him. The departures are therefore not a side topic in the study of Boaistuau. They are the evidence by which the whole English line is re-routed through France. The map that follows sets out the principal departures and the inheritance each one founded, and the sections after it take the three largest in turn: the moral frame, the apothecary, and the ending.

## Verona in French: The Work of Naturalisation

Every act of translation is also an act of relocation, and Boaistuau's Verona is a city quietly rebuilt to French specifications. The process begins on the nameplate. Romeo becomes Rhomeo in the French spelling, a Greek-looking aspirate that gives the Veronese lover a faintly classical passport, and Julietta keeps her Italian diminutive in French dress. The houses become the Capellets and the Montesches, their Italian endings planed away until the names can be pronounced at a Parisian table without a stumble. Frère Laurent replaces Fra Lorenzo, a friar whose title now belongs to French religious life. The Count who presses for the heroine's hand moves through the French text with a French courtier's outline. None of these changes alters the plot by a hair. All of them alter the reader's relation to it: the story no longer happens abroad, in a peninsula of podestà and signorie; it happens in a city that obeys the reader's own social grammar, among houses, confessors and counts whose ranks a French reader can price at sight.

The naturalisation runs deeper than names. Measures of devotion, of law and of money pass through the same quiet conversion, so that the tale's transactions, a marriage contracted, a poison priced, a peace proclaimed, are denominated in terms the receiving culture uses for its own affairs. This is the unglamorous labour that made the Histoires tragiques a French book rather than an Italian one in exile, and it is the labour that most perfectly disappears when it succeeds. Brooke repeats it for England: his Romeus and Juliet move through a Verona already twice naturalised, French before it was English, and the play inherits the result as simply the story's world. When an English audience watches a friar, a count and two houses conduct the tragedy, it is watching the sediment of two relocations, and the first and deeper of them is Boaistuau's.

Naturalisation also explains a puzzle in the story's moral reception. Bandello's Italy could treat the secret marriage as a scandal of a particular city's families. France, a kingdom in which the crown and the church were then tightening the law of marriage against clandestine contracts, the very campaign that would produce the era's edicts requiring parental consent, heard the tale as a case under its own statutes. The French narrator's prosecution of the secret marriage is therefore not a translator's prudery pasted over an Italian romance. It is a judgment delivered in the receiving country's own legal accent, at the moment that country was making the judgment official. Brooke's preface, preaching the same doctrine to English readers, is often read as an English Puritan reflex. Read in sequence, it is a French legal accent learned at one remove, the sound of a story that had already been tried in a French court before it was ever versified in London. The tradition's naturalisations, of names, of money, of law, are the means by which a Veronese anecdote became a European instrument, and they were all performed first, and most thoroughly, in Paris.

## Inside the French Text: Three Scenes Reworked

General claims about free adaptation earn their keep only at the level of the scene, and three scenes show Boaistuau's hand at three different pressures. The first is the meeting. Bandello's feast is a set piece of the novella: the hero enters the Capelletti house among the maskers, the heroine is seen, hands are taken in the dance, and the machinery of the feud pauses for one room's length. Boaistuau keeps the machinery and re-voices the encounter, expanding the lovers' first exchange toward the formal, almost liturgical patterning that the English versions would make famous, in which compliment answers compliment in matched periods and the hands become a text both parties read. The scene's centre of gravity shifts from event to utterance. What the French reader is given to admire is not that the lovers met, which any summary could say, but how they spoke when they met, which only the amplified page can show. In that shift the whole future of the story is latent: the play's first meeting is a shared sonnet, a feat of utterance, and its ancestry runs through a French scene that had already decided utterance was the point.

The second scene is the potion. Bandello's Giulietta receives the friar's powder and drinks with a resolution the novella reports more than it performs. Boaistuau slows the moment into an interior campaign: his Julietta argues with her fears in extended soliloquy, imagining the vault, the ancestors' bones, the possibility of waking mad among the dead, and conquers each imagined horror in speech before she drinks. The expansion matters beyond its bulk. It relocates the tragedy's centre of consciousness. In the Italian telling, the plot happens to the heroine with a speed she matches by obedience to others' devices, the friar's, the nurse's, her father's. In the French telling, the heroine talks herself into the plot's most dangerous act, alone, at length, in her own voice. Shakespeare's Juliet, alone with the vial in the fourth act and arguing herself past the same imagined horrors one by one, is the direct heir of that relocation, through Brooke, whose heroine inherits the French interiority entire. A character who can soliloquise her way into the tomb is a tragic protagonist; a character who is merely conveyed there is a victim. Boaistuau, amplifying a scene his source had kept brief, promoted his heroine from the second condition to the first.

The third scene is the reconciliation, and its pressure is the lightest and most revealing. The Italian ending closes the feud over the bodies with a princely sentence and a promise of golden remembrance. Boaistuau keeps the sentence and then does the most characteristic thing in his book: he comments. The narrator steps forward to gloss the reconciliation, to assign its meaning, to warn the living from the dead. The gesture looks small beside the apothecary and the waking, but it completes the system those larger changes began. A story whose poison is bought in the open market of the world, whose heroine wakes to silence, and whose closing reconciliation is immediately interpreted by an authorial judge is a story that has been re-founded as public instruction. Nothing in it is left to private feeling, not even grief, especially not grief. The English tradition that received this system would spend centuries loosening its grip, Brooke loosening it a little, Shakespeare casting off the judge and keeping the judgment's architecture. But the grip was French, and the loosening is only intelligible against it.

## The Moral Frame: Boaistuau's Verdict Before the Story Starts

Bandello's lovers die inside a story; Boaistuau's die inside an argument. The French version opens its proceedings before the narrative begins, in an authorial address that instructs the reader how the coming deaths are to be judged, and it returns to collect its verdict before the book moves on. The doctrine is consistent and severe. The tale is offered as an example against the disobedience of children toward parents, against marriages contracted in secret without parental consent, against the lust that disguises itself as love, and against the religious meddler whose charitable scheming oils the machinery of disaster. Friar Laurence, in the French moral accounting, is not the kindly philosopher of the play's second act, the man who observes that "these violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9) and hopes the alliance may "turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.91-92). He is a warning in a habit, and Boaistuau's narration never lets the reader forget the charge sheet.

### What lesson does Boaistuau order his readers to carry out of the tomb?

Obedience, in a word. The French narrator's closing doctrine instructs children to marry under their parents' authority rather than by secret contract, instructs parents in the government that makes secrecy unnecessary only by implication, and instructs every reader to read the double death as the wages of disordered desire rather than as Fortune's accident.

The tomb, in his accounting, is a pulpit: Romeo and Julietta are not exhibited for pity first but for correction, and pity is permitted only after the lesson has been collected.

The frame matters because it crossed the Channel intact. Brooke's poem opens with an address to the reader that rehearses Boaistuau's doctrine almost charge for charge: the English preface warns against disobedient children, unhonest desire, and the superstitious friar whose meddling the French narrator had already indicted. Readers of Brooke, and through Brooke the first students of this story in English, therefore met the lovers inside a French courtroom. Shakespeare's play strips the courtroom away and replaces the prosecutor with a Chorus that announces "a pair of star-crossed lovers" (Prologue, line 6) and lets the sentence fall to the Prince at the end, but the habit of reading the tale as an exemplum, a story with a verdict attached, is a French habit before it is an English one. Even the long afterlife of moralising school editions, in which the play is taught as a warning, descends in part from the frame Boaistuau built.

His blame, weighed carefully, also redistributes the story's guilt in a direction the play would partly follow. Da Porto and Bandello spread responsibility widely: the feud, the families, Fortune, the friar, the lovers' haste. Boaistuau's narration tightens the noose around chosen acts. The secret marriage is an act. The disobedience is an act. The friar's contrivance is an act. Fortune, whom Brooke's narrator would address as a person and whom the Italian tradition treats as the tale's weather, recedes in the French telling toward a subordinate clause, because a prosecutor needs defendants, not weather. The consequence is a colder and more legible tragedy: less accident, more indictment. When later critics argue about whether the play is a tragedy of fate or of character, they are arguing, whether they know it or not, across ground that Boaistuau staked out when he decided that his borrowed story would be a trial.

## The Friar in the French Dock

No figure pays more for Boaistuau's programme than Friar Laurence, and the friar's French treatment deserves its own hearing because it shows the moral frame operating inside the story and not merely around it. In the Italian tellings, Lorenzo is a functionary of charity: learned in herbs, fond of the young, ready with a device. His failure is the failure of a good intention outrun by accident, and the tradition's judgment on him is correspondingly mild, a penance, a withdrawal, a hermit's cell at the margin of the tale. Boaistuau's narration will not leave him at the margin. The French friar is arraigned in the commentary as a principal: a religious who lent his office to a clandestine marriage, who compounded the draught, who trusted the plot's secret to a letter and a stranger's legs. Where the Italian friar errs, the French friar offends, against his rule, against the parents' rights, against the order the tale exists to defend. The distinction is the distinction between a tragedy of accident and a tragedy of indictment, drawn through a single character.

Boaistuau also gives his friar more to say, and the gift is double-edged in the way of all his amplifications. The French Laurence expounds his reasons at length: the hope of reconciling the houses, the pastoral calculus that weighs a secret marriage against an open feud. Heard in full, the defence is the strongest the character ever receives in any version, stronger than anything the play allows him until his final narration. Yet the fuller the defence, the more completely the narrator's frame convicts the speaker of the very fault the defence displays, a cleverness that mistook itself for wisdom. It is a sophisticated piece of moral engineering, and its descendant stands in the play, where Laurence's eloquence, his herb lore, his sententiae, his "violent delights", is permitted to charm an audience for four acts before the fifth act's confessional arithmetic prices it. Shakespeare learned from the French tradition, through Brooke, that a friar may be both the play's sage and its defendant, and that the audience should be allowed to love the sage before it is asked to judge the defendant.

The friar's French fate also clarifies what the frame was for. A tale told for correction needs the machinery of correction inside the story as well as at its edges: confession, examination, judgment. Boaistuau's ending marshals its survivors before authority and renders accounts, the friar's among them, in a scene of public reckoning that the play would compress into the Prince's dawn tribunal on the churchyard steps. The compression is Shakespeare's; the tribunal is the tradition's, and its protocol, the guilty and the bereaved answering in turn while authority totals the loss, was drafted in French prose. When the Prince closes the play by distributing punishment to all and pardon to some, he speaks with the voice of Boaistuau's narrator, naturalised at last as a character, the prosecutor promoted into the plot he once merely annotated.

## The Apothecary Enters the Story in France

Of all the names in the Romeo and Juliet story, the apothecary is the easiest to overlook and the hardest to explain. He has no name, no house, no feud. He appears once, in Mantua, sells the means of the catastrophe, and vanishes. Yet his scene is among the most quoted in the play: Romeo's survey of the starving shopkeeper, the forty ducats, the exchange in which conscience and poverty trade places. "I see that thou art poor. Hold, there is forty ducats" (5.1.58), Romeo begins, and the apothecary's resistance collapses into the play's neatest couplet of moral bookkeeping: "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1.75); "I pay thy poverty, and not thy will" (5.1.76). The scene feels inevitable, a fixture of the plot. It is nothing of the kind. On every surviving Italian page, it does not exist.

In Da Porto, Romeo comes to the tomb already armed with his death: the poison is in his possession, a thing carried, not bought. In Bandello, the arrangement is the same in substance; the Mantuan chapters concern the news, the grief and the journey, and no shopkeeper steps forward to be bribed. The purchase, the hesitation, the law that forbids the sale and the gold that overbears the law: the whole small drama of the transaction enters the surviving record in Boaistuau's French. There, the exiled Romeo seeks out a poor apothecary in Mantua, and the French scene already contains the elements Shakespeare's audience knows: the seller's poverty written on his person, his refusal grounded in the prohibition on selling poison, and the lover's money arguing louder than the prohibition. Brooke, translating the French, versifies the purchase; Painter, translating the French independently, prosifies it; and Shakespeare, working from the English line, gives it its final compression and its legal chill, adding the statute's teeth: Mantua's law, in the play, is death to any man who utters such poisons, a point the scene's earlier readers had already met in embryo in the French refusal.

The verification flag attached to this claim deserves its paragraph, because the claim is exactly the kind on which source study must be careful. To say Boaistuau introduced the apothecary is to say that the scene's first surviving appearance is his. It is not to say that no lost page, Italian or French, ever staged a poison purchase before 1559; lost pages are the standing caveat of all transmission history, and Boaistuau, a compiler by trade, may have taken the scene from a source that has not survived rather than inventing it at his desk. What cannot be argued is the route of the inheritance. Brooke's apothecary agrees with the French scene in its furniture, its hesitation and its moral arithmetic, and agrees with no Italian text that survives. However the scene was born, England received it from France. The apothecary is a French immigrant in the English tragedy, naturalised so completely that audiences now take him for a native.

His arrival also changes the tragedy's moral physics in a way that connects this section to the frame discussed earlier. A poison carried is a fate possessed: the hero arrives with his death in his baggage, and the catastrophe is a property of his person. A poison purchased is a fate transacted: the catastrophe requires a market, a law, a poor man's hunger and a rich man's gold, and so spreads its guilt across an economy. Boaistuau, the moralist who wanted defendants rather than weather, found or made a scene in which a stranger can be handed a share of the blame for forty ducats. Shakespeare sharpened the point until it cut both ways, distributing the guilt between the buyer who pays the poverty and the seller whose poverty consents. The play even preserves the transaction's prehistory in the buyer's memory: Romeo, resolving on death, recalls the shop from his Mantuan exile, "I do remember an apothecary" (5.1.37), a man noticed, in passing, in want, and filed away against the day of need. The recollection is the French scene's psychology perfected: in a world where poison is bought, despair keeps a tradesman's address. The line of that distribution runs back through Brooke to a French page of 1559, and the apothecary's shop, bare shelves, tortoise shell and alligator stuffing included, is the French tradition's most successful single invention, if invention is the word, or its most successful single act of preservation, if the caution is preferred. Either way, the scene is France's.

One more element of the French scene deserves record, because the play preserves it with legal precision: the prohibition. Boaistuau's apothecary does not merely haggle; he cites the ban on selling poison, the civic law that makes the transaction a crime before it is a sale. The detail converts the purchase from a private errand into a small trial of its own, in which statute, hunger and desire plead in turn and desire, armed with gold, wins. Brooke retains the refusal and its grounds. Shakespeare retains them and sharpens the statute into a death penalty, so that his Romeo must argue not with a tradesman's caution but with a man's fear of the gallows, and must answer that fear with the scene's great counter-statute, the gold that is a worse poison to men's souls than the powder it buys. The legal scaffolding of the play's most famous transaction was erected in France. Romeos had died by poison for thirty years of retelling before Boaistuau; only after Boaistuau did a Romeo have to break a law, in public, at a price, to do it.

## The Darkened Ending: Juliet Wakes Too Late

The ending of the story is where versions confess their philosophies, and it is at the ending that Boaistuau's hand is heaviest. In the Italian tellings, the tomb scene is a duet. Da Porto's Giulietta wakes while Romeo, poisoned, still lives; the lovers recognise one another, speak, and part in language, and Giulietta then dies by the famous expedient of holding her breath, refusing a world emptied of him. Bandello keeps the duet and arms it: his Giulietta wakes in time for the final exchange, Romeo dies in her presence, and she takes his dagger and kills herself upon his body. In both Italian versions the lovers are granted the one mercy the story can give them, a shared ending, consciously met. Boaistuau withdraws the mercy. In his French telling, Julietta wakes after Rhomeo has died. The conversation that closes the Italian versions is struck out. What remains is a monologue over a corpse, and then the dagger.

### How does Boaistuau's Romeo die, and with what words on his lips?

He dies speaking to a woman who cannot hear him. The French Romeo takes his poison in the tomb and spends his last strength in address: to the body he believes dead, to the fate that has arranged the meeting, to the love that the poison is now cancelling.

Because Julietta's waking has been moved to the far side of his death, the speech has no respondent and can receive none, and Boaistuau stretches it accordingly, making the tomb's first movement a solo in which every question is asked of silence and answered by it.

### Whose dagger kills the French Juliet, and where does she find it?

Romeo's. The French Julietta wakes among the dead of the Capelletti vault, finds her husband's body with the weapon at his side, and turns that weapon on herself after her lament, so that the instrument of the feud's masculine world performs the last office of the marriage.

Boaistuau keeps Bandello's dagger, which the Italian Giulietta also used, but changes the hands' timing outright: the Italian heroine strikes after speech and farewell, the French heroine after silence and discovery, and the same blade therefore ends two different tragedies.

Consider what the alteration does to the play England eventually received. Shakespeare's fifth act is built on the French arrangement to the minute. Romeo dies addressing a silent woman: "Eyes, look your last! Arms, take your last embrace!" (5.3.112-113), and seals the address with the kiss and the line "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3.120). Juliet wakes to a friar, not a husband: "O comfortable friar! where is my lord?" (5.3.148). Her discovery is forensic before it is emotional: "Thy lips are warm" (5.3.167), the warmth itself a measurement of how narrowly the meeting was missed. And her death completes the French pattern, the dagger snatched from the body beside her: "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170). Every beat of that sequence, the solo, the too-late waking, the warm lips, the borrowed dagger, presupposes the decision taken in Paris in 1559 to let Romeo die unheard. The Italian duet would have given the play a different final movement, fuller of language and poorer in desolation. The desolation is French.

Boaistuau's Julietta does not go silently in her turn. Deprived of the dialogue, she receives instead the longest solo the French version contains, a lament over the body that surveys the marriage, the feud, her own disobedience and the tomb's population of her kin, before the dagger ends it. The rhetoric of that lament is the subject of the next section; its structural meaning belongs here. By moving the waking, Boaistuau converts the ending from a meeting into a sequence of solitudes, three deaths in one vault, each sealed off from the others: Tybalt's earlier death unmourned in the dark, Romeo's death unheard, Julietta's death unwitnessed until the watch arrives. The play's closing desolation, the sense of a stage filling with bodies that never managed to speak to one another in time, is the emotional signature of the French arrangement. Source study rarely gets to say that a single editorial decision in a translated book determined the emotional architecture of a canonical tragedy. Here it can.

The lament itself repays a closer look, since it is the French version's rhetorical summit and the place where Boaistuau's two programmes, the moral and the theatrical, meet. His Julietta does not simply grieve over the body. She audits the marriage aloud: the feast, the garden, the friar's cell, the exile, the potion, each station of the story recalled and priced in the presence of the one person who can no longer share the reckoning. She arraigns herself for the disobedience the narrator has already charged, and in doing so she performs the frame's doctrine in the first person, the prosecutor's brief delivered by the defendant. Then, the audit complete, she takes the dagger. The sequence, recollection, indictment, sentence, execution, is a miniature of the whole French method, and it hands the English tradition a heroine whose last act is an act of judgment on herself. Brooke's Juliet inherits the audit. Shakespeare's inherits its compression: a handful of lines over the body, the warmth of the lips, the dagger's sheath, the same reckoning conducted at the speed of a held breath. What the French page spread across an oration, the stage concentrates into seconds, but the oration had to exist before it could be concentrated.

## Rhetoric, Amplification and the Length of Sorrow

The French version is longer than the Italian, and the extra length has an address. It does not go into incident: Boaistuau adds almost no new events beyond the Mantuan transaction, and the plot's stations, feast, balcony, marriage, duel, exile, potion, tomb, stand where Bandello put them. The extra length goes into voice. At every crisis the French narrator slows the tale to let a speaker perform: the lovers' interviews swell with formal exchanges, Julietta's resolution before the potion is argued aloud, the friar's counsel is expounded, and above all the tomb fills with oratory, Romeo's address to the seeming corpse and Julietta's lament over the real one. Where Bandello's prose moves at a novella's pace, anecdote outrunning reflection, Boaistuau's French moves at the pace of a Senecan exercise, in which the event exists partly to license the speech that interprets it.

This rhetoric is not idle ornament, and the proof is its posterity. The English versions inherit the French scale of speech along with the French plot. Brooke's poem, at more than three thousand lines, expands the tale far beyond Bandello's compass, and its expansions cluster where Boaistuau's cluster: in soliloquy, in lament, in the long formal addresses of the tomb. Shakespeare's play, for all its compression of the calendar, preserves the rhetorical architecture entire. Juliet alone with the vial, arguing herself toward the draught in a speech of more than fifty lines, is inconceivable in the novella's economy and natural in the French one. Romeo in the tomb, delivering an oration to a body, belongs to the same economy. The French tradition taught the story to talk at length about itself, and the talk became the tragedy's chief glory: generations of audiences have prized the speeches and endured the plot, a proportion Boaistuau would have recognised as his own.

Amplification also carried the moral frame into the story's bloodstream, which bare prefaces could not have done. A preface can be skipped; a speech cannot, not by a reader following the tale. By distributing his doctrine into the mouths of his characters, the friar's warnings, the narrator's intrusions between speeches, the lovers' own retrospective judgments in the tomb, Boaistuau ensured that the French version's moral accounting would survive excerpting, retranslation and dramatisation. Brooke, who kept the preface, also kept the intrusions. The play, which discarded both, still speaks the doctrine at its joints, in the Friar's sententiae, in the Chorus's summaries, in the Prince's closing sentence that "never was a story of more woe" (5.3.309-310) and that distributes punishment to all. The French gift of length was therefore also a gift of emphasis: the story arrived in England already knowing which of its moments mattered most, and it arrived with those moments enlarged. Editors call this rhetoric. Audiences call it the play.

## Boaistuau's Sentences: The Sound of the French Prose

Because the French text is usually met in summary, its sentences are the least discussed part of the inheritance, and they deserve a section of their own. Boaistuau writes in the high manner of mid-century French prose: long periodic sentences that suspend their main clause behind a procession of subordinate ones, balanced members answering one another across semicolons, apostrophes that halt the narrative to address Fortune, the reader, the dead. The manner is Senecan in its sententiousness and Ciceronian in its periods, and it is deliberately unlike the anecdotal Italian it replaces. Bandello's prose walks; Boaistuau's processes. The effect on the story is a change of tempo that every English version reproduces in its own medium. Brooke's poem, written in the long swinging measure English poets called poulter's measure, alexandrines alternating with fourteeners, is a verse answer to the French period: line lengths that, like Boaistuau's clauses, hold a thought suspended and then release it with a click. Painter's prose, ampler and more periodic than the Italian prose translation of the period usually is, answers the same model in the same language family. The play's great speeches, with their balanced antitheses and their held suspensions, stand at the end of this stylistic line as they stand at the end of the narrative one.

The sentences also perform the doctrine. A periodic sentence is a small court: subordinate clauses are heard in order, evidence is marshalled, and the main clause arrives like a verdict the grammar has earned. Boaistuau's narrator thinks in such sentences, and so, increasingly, do his characters, whose laments proceed by marshalled recollection toward a concluding judgment, exactly the shape of Julietta's audit over the body. Even the friar argues in periods. The French version's world is one in which feeling, to be respectable, must first become rhetoric, and the inheritance of that demand is audible wherever the English story slows down to let a character make a speech rather than a remark. Modern readers sometimes experience the play's formal speeches as obstacles between themselves and the action. The French tradition experiences them as the action's whole point, the tribunal in session. Recovering Boaistuau means recovering that experience as a legitimate one, native to the story's first maturity, and it explains why the speeches, so often cut in performance, refuse to die: they are not decoration on the tragedy's frame. In the French line, they are the frame.

A comparison of openings makes the stylistic distance measurable. The Italian tale begins in company, a dedication, a conversation, a story called for. The French history begins in judgment, an authorial voice instructing the reader before any character is permitted to speak. Between those two thresholds lies the entire reformation Boaistuau worked on his material, and it is accomplished before the plot has moved a single step. Style in this version is not the clothing of the argument. It is the argument, arriving early, in a loud voice, wearing robes.

## Shared Errors: The Fingerprint of the French Page

Brooke's title page says his poem was written first in Italian by Bandello and now in English by Arthur Brooke. Brooke's text says otherwise, and the demonstration that it does is one of the cleanest pieces of detective work in English source study, associated above all with J. J. Munro's edition of the poem and consolidated in Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare. The method is the one used wherever descent between texts must be proved: look not for agreements in the common stock, which any two tellers might share, but for agreements in invention and in error, the idiosyncratic matter that only copying explains. Judged by that method, Brooke's poem descends from Boaistuau's French, and the case rests on the very departures this article has been cataloguing.

### What single shared mistake ties Brooke's poem to the French page?

Strictly, the tie is not a mistake in wording but a shared invention: the Mantuan apothecary, with his poverty, his refusal and his price, stands in Brooke's poem exactly as he stands in Boaistuau's French and nowhere in Bandello's Italian, although Brooke's title page credits the Italian alone.

A translator may inherit a plot from anywhere; he can inherit a scene that exists in only one book only from that book, and the apothecary is such a scene.

The apothecary is the loudest fingerprint, not the only one. Brooke's Juliet wakes, as Boaistuau's does, when her husband is already dead, and speaks her last words to a corpse; Bandello's Giulietta wakes in time to be answered. Brooke's preface arraigns the same defendants in the same order as the French moral apparatus: the disobedient children, the secret marriage, the meddling friar. Brooke's tomb fills with the French scale of lament. Item by item, where the French version departs from the Italian, the English poem departs with it, and where the Italian stands alone, the English poem does not follow. The pattern is too consistent to be coincidence and too specific to be parallel invention, and it settles the practical question of Brooke's working text beyond reasonable dispute: the book on the English poet's desk in 1562 was the Paris volume of 1559, whatever his title page found it politic to say.

Painter closes the evidentiary circle from the other side. William Painter translated the tale into English prose for The Palace of Pleasure, and Painter, unlike Brooke, worked openly from the French tradition of the Histoires tragiques, the channel through which his collection drew much of its matter. His version agrees with Boaistuau in the same departures, the purchase, the waking, the moral colour, because it descends from the same page by an admitted route. England thus received the story twice in five years, once in verse that concealed its French parentage and once in prose that displayed it, and both receptions carried the French alterations as part of the tale's substance. When Shakespeare took up the story a generation later, the alterations were no longer French, English or anyone's. They were simply the story, which is the final measure of Boaistuau's success: his changes disappeared into the plot's identity, and it took three centuries of scholarship to see the seams.

## The Journey of a Book: Paris 1559 to London 1562

Between the Paris volume and the English poem lie three years and a narrow sea, and the brevity of the interval is itself evidence. Brooke's Romeus and Juliet appeared in London in 1562, close enough behind the Histoires tragiques for the French book to have been a recent acquisition, the kind of new Parisian commodity that the London trade around St Paul's Churchyard imported, stocked and sold to exactly the class of reader Brooke was: a young gentleman of the Inns of Court milieu, French-reading as a matter of education, hungry for the Italian stories that France had made fashionable. No exotic hypothesis is needed to put Boaistuau on Brooke's desk. The ordinary machinery of the cross-Channel book trade does it, the same machinery that within the decade would stock Painter's shelves and fill the libraries from which Shakespeare's generation browsed its plots. The story's journey from Lucca to Paris to London follows the century's trade winds, and each port taxed the cargo.

The taxation metaphor is worth taking seriously, because it corrects the romance of pure transmission. A book crossing a border in the sixteenth century paid duty in the currency of its new home: in Paris, the duty was doctrine, payable to a moralising editor; in London, the duty was form, payable in verse and in an English preface that reassured English readers the foreign matter had been naturalised for their instruction. What no port could tax away was the cargo's structure. The apothecary crossed duty-free inside the plot. The waking crossed inside the plot's final arrangement. The habit of judgment crossed inside the genre's name. By the time the cargo reached the playhouse in the 1590s, its customs stamps were invisible and its French contents were simply what the story was. Transmission history is usually written as a sequence of survivals. It is equally a sequence of successful smuggling operations, and Boaistuau, who declared his goods honestly enough on his title page, extraictes des oeuvres italiennes, while quietly repacking them, is the trade's master practitioner in this story's line.

The volume's own French afterlife confirms that the route was no accident of one reader's shopping. Sertenas's book was taken up, reprinted and absorbed into the growing collected editions of the Histoires tragiques, so that the lovers' history acquired the permanence of a fixture rather than the fragility of a novelty: a French reader of 1570 or 1580 met Rhomeo and Julietta not as a recent extraction from Bandello but as a standing exhibit in the national repository of tragic narrative, shelved between other exemplary catastrophes and prefaced by the same prosecutorial apparatus. Fixtures are what traditions are made of. Brooke had borrowed from a book; Belleforest's France preserved the book as an institution, and institutions outlast the readers who first borrowed from them. The English play was still two decades in the future when the French version had already become the form in which Europe, outside Italy, kept the story.

Painter's volume completes the picture of the route. The Palace of Pleasure drew its matter from the fashionable continental collections, French intermediaries prominent among them, and its Romeo and Julietta arrived in English prose wearing the French arrangement as a matter of course. Two English versions in five years, one metrical and one prosaic, both downstream of Paris: the story's English nationality was granted, so to speak, on French papers. Shakespeare inherited the naturalised citizen and made it immortal. The least the record can do is restore the papers.

## Belleforest and the French Tradition After Boaistuau

Boaistuau died in Paris in 1566, and the tradition he founded did not notice the loss, because François de Belleforest had already taken up the work. Belleforest, a prolific translator and compiler from Comminges, began adding volumes to the Histoires tragiques in 1565 and continued for years, drawing on Bandello and on a widening shelf of Italian, Spanish and Latin matter, until the tragic history stood as the dominant form of French prose narrative for the rest of the century. The collected Histoires tragiques grew, edition by edition, toward eighteen volumes. Inside that expansion the Romeo and Juliet story held its place in the first volume, Boaistuau's own, reprinted with the set and read wherever the set was read. The story's French fame in the decades when England was dramatising it was therefore not the fame of a single book of 1559. It was the fame of a series that French readers bought, borrowed and imitated for two generations, with the lovers' history standing permanently in its front rank.

Belleforest's importance to this article is structural rather than textual. He added nothing to the Romeo and Juliet story itself; his hand is not in its plot. What he added was duration and distribution, the two gifts a tradition needs if a single adapter's alterations are to harden into the received form of a tale. Without the continuations, Boaistuau's volume might have remained a Parisian success of a few seasons, and Brooke's borrowings a curiosity. With them, the Histoires tragiques became the standard European repository of tragic tales, the place where the story lived in French, and the channel through which its French shape kept circulating while England turned it into a play. Traditions are made by such unglamorous labour: continuation, reprinting, keeping the book in print. Boaistuau supplied the alterations. Belleforest supplied the shelf life.

His prefaces show the tradition conscious of itself, and the consciousness is instructive. Belleforest defends the tragic history as a school of conduct, multiplies the moral addresses his predecessor had pioneered, and presents the collection's growth as a public service, the stocking of a national storehouse of examples from which readers of every estate may draw instruction. The defence concedes nothing to the charge that such books traffic in other men's inventions; in the continuator's economy, the retelling is the contribution, and the storehouse is worth more than any single item in it. Posterity has ratified the judgment in the only way that matters here: the Romeo and Juliet that Europe kept was the one the storehouse stocked, in Boaistuau's arrangement, under Belleforest's custodianship, and the Italian originals waited for scholars to reintroduce them to the story's own biography.

The genre both men built also outlasted the story's French vogue and shaped the expectations any French, and many English, readers brought to tragic narrative: that a tale of catastrophe should arrive with its moral drawn, its rhetoric ample, its narrator present and accountable. The histoire tragique is the missing middle term between the Italian novella and the English stage tragedy, the form in which a courtly anecdote learned to behave like a judgment. Shakespeare's audience, meeting a Chorus that promises woe and a Prince who closes the case, was meeting the French form's habits wearing English dress. That the dress has been mistaken for the body ever since is the tradition's last and most complete success.

## France in the 1560s: Why Tragic Histories Flourished

The French tradition of this story cannot be separated from the French decade that received it. Boaistuau published in 1559, on the hinge of his country's history: the year of the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis and of the death of Henri II, killed in a tournament by a splintered lance through the eye, a catastrophe no histoire tragique could have bettered for symbolism. Within three years France entered the religious wars that would consume the rest of the century, and the genre Boaistuau had named found itself the literature of a society rehearsing disaster. The tragic history's habits, the exemplary corpse, the drawn moral, the narrator as coroner, fitted a readership for whom public violence and private conscience had become daily matter. Belleforest's continuations sold into that market for twenty years. The Romeo and Juliet story, a tale of two houses whose private feud poisons a city's peace, carried an obvious charge for readers watching great houses arm against each other in earnest, and its French custodians, moralising furiously against disobedience and secret alliance, were not writing in a vacuum. They were writing for a kingdom learning to fear exactly the story's subject: what happens when loyalty to a house outruns obedience to the common peace.

The English reception belongs to the same weather system. Brooke's poem of 1562 and Painter's prose of 1567 reached an England anxious about succession, religion and the obedience of subjects, and the French frame, with its doctrine of obedience and its suspicion of meddling clergy, travelled well precisely because it was already a politics. When the story is described as a universal tale of young love, the description is not false, but it is anachronistically weightless. In its French form, the form England received, the tale was an instrument for thinking about houses, obedience and civil peace, sharpened in a kingdom sliding toward civil war. The play's Verona, with its edicts, its brawls in public places, its prince totalling the cost of faction at dawn, is that instrument still in working order. France did not merely carry the story to England. France taught the story what it was about, and England, receiving the lesson inside a love story, has been relearning it in every generation since.

## What Boaistuau Suppressed: The Costs of the French Version

A verdict for the French tradition is only credible if the tradition's costs are entered in the same ledger as its gifts, and the costs are real. Boaistuau's programme, prosecution, amplification, judgment, was purchased by suppression, and the first casualty was ease. Bandello's novella has the gait of a story told well in company: digressive, sociable, content to let a scene charm before it signifies. The French version has no time for that gait. Its narrator is on duty, and the duty flattens whatever cannot be arraigned or lamented. The nurse's earthy comedy, which Bandello lets run for the pleasure of its own vitality, sits awkwardly inside a frame that exists to condemn the household's disorders, and the French telling keeps such matter on a shorter rein. The lovers' early happiness, the feast's glitter, the garden's freshness, survive, but they survive as evidence for the prosecution, exhibits of what will be lost, rather than as goods in themselves. Readers who find the French version heavy beside the Italian are not imagining the weight. It is the weight of a thesis.

The second casualty was the ending's humanity, and it must be named as a cost even though this article has argued it as the tradition's greatest structural gift. The Italian lovers die together, in speech, in recognition, and there is a consolation in that duet which audiences across the centuries have been reluctant to surrender: the sense that the story, having taken everything else, at least lets its victims meet at the end. Boaistuau confiscated the consolation deliberately, on doctrine: a warning example works best unsoftened, and a Julietta who wakes to a corpse is a more efficient sermon than a Julietta who wakes to a voice. The confiscation made the tragedy greater and the tale crueller, and the two judgments are both true. England inherited the cruelty as architecture and has spent four centuries negotiating with it, directors restoring glances, editors annotating the warmth of the lips, audiences willing the waking a minute earlier. The negotiation never succeeds, because the French decision is built into the plot's load-bearing wall. But it should be recorded that something humane was demolished to build that wall, and that the demolisher knew the price and paid it on purpose.

The third casualty was Bandello himself. Boaistuau's title page names the Italian as the book's source and quarry, extraictes des oeuvres italiennes de Bandel, and the naming functions in practice as an eclipse: for the French-reading world, and through translation for much of the English-reading one, the story became the property of its French arrangement, and Bandello's authorship dwindled to a line of small print. The pattern is the standing injustice of successful adaptation, and the French tradition is its textbook case. Brooke repeated the manoeuvre on Boaistuau, crediting the Italian his title page could see and concealing the Frenchman his desk could not do without, so that the chain's middle link was hidden by both its neighbours. Recovering Boaistuau is therefore not antiquarian tidiness. It is the correction of a double erasure, and it restores to the record the man whose choices, for good and ill, the most famous love story in English still obeys.

## Histoire, Not Fable: The French Truth Claim

One feature of the French version is easy to miss because the play retains it invisibly: Boaistuau insists that his tale is an histoire, a true account, and not a fable invented for delight. The word in his title is doing work. Sixteenth-century French prose distinguished the invented romance, with its enchantments and improbabilities, from the history, which claimed the dignity of fact and the right to instruct. Boaistuau files the Verona lovers under fact. The tale had older warrants for the claim behind him: the Italian tradition reached back to named Veronese families and a datable signoria, and the lovers' tomb was shown in Verona as a monument. The French version inherits the warrants and spends them differently. For Bandello, the historical colouring is a storyteller's credential, a flourish of verisimilitude. For Boaistuau, it is a prosecutor's exhibit label: these things happened, to named people, in a real city, and therefore the doctrine drawn from them binds real readers. A fable may be admired and set aside. A history must be answered.

The truth claim travels down the English line as faithfully as the apothecary does. Brooke's title calls his poem a tragicall historye, and the phrase is a direct import of the French genre claim, histoire tragique wearing English spelling. Painter's collection likewise dresses its tales as histories. When the story reaches the stage, the claim survives in the only form drama allows: a Chorus in the dress of a presenter, announcing the action as a matter of record, "the two hours' traffic of our stage", and a final scene conducted as an inquest, with testimony, exhibits and a presiding judge. The play is not a history and its Chorus is not a chronicler, but the posture, the insistence that the audience is witnessing an account rather than an invention, descends from the French book's title page. Every programme note that calls the play a true story of star-crossed lovers, every Verona tour that ends at a balcony and a tomb, is spending Boaistuau's currency. He was the first teller in the chain to insist, as a matter of form, that the lovers were real and their deaths a public record. The insistence may be the French tradition's most successful export of all, because it is the one nobody notices.

## Two English Channels: Brooke's Verse and Painter's Prose

England took the story from France twice, by two doors, within five years, and the doubleness of the taking explains much of the play's later source history. The first door was verse: [Arthur Brooke's long narrative poem of 1562, the Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet](/2025/09/19/arthur-brooke-romeus-and-juliet-1562/), which became the play's principal English source and the channel through which the French alterations reached the stage in their most influential form. The second door was prose: [William Painter's Palace of Pleasure rendering of 1567, the goodly history of Romeo and Julietta](/2025/10/31/william-painter-palace-of-pleasure/), which retold the tale for the growing market in prose fiction and confirmed, for any reader who compared them, that the story England possessed was the story France had shaped. Behind both doors stands the same Paris room. Brooke versified Boaistuau while crediting Bandello; Painter prosified the French tradition while naming his debts more openly; and neither English book contains a single major feature that requires a journey back past Paris to Lucca.

Painter's practice deserves its sentence in this accounting, because the two English channels differ in kind and the difference shows what each carried. Brooke, the poet, expanded: his verse dilates the French matter into thousands of lines, adds meditations, and stamps the tale with a young moralist's personality. Painter, a clerk of the ordnance translating in his leisure from the fashionable continental collections, kept closer quarters with his original: his prose preserves the French sequence, the French scene furniture and the French moral colour with a clerkly fidelity that makes his volume the plainer witness to what Boaistuau's page contained. For source study the plainness is gold. Where Brooke's inventions might be Brooke's, Painter's agreements with the French text corroborate the descent independently, a second signature on the same receipt. It is also Painter's accessibility, prose for the growing class of English readers who took their Italy through France as a matter of course, that kept Muir's question alive: a dramatist browsing for plots in the 1590s was likelier to own the prose bestseller than the ageing poem, and the French matter could have reached him by the plainer door as readily as by the grander one.

The whole itinerary, with every dated title page in its place, is set out in [the complete family tree of the story's sources](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), and the Italian starting point of the journey, the novella both French and English versions ultimately descend from, is examined in the study of [Matteo Bandello's novella and its place in his collection](/2025/10/10/matteo-bandello-novella-1554/). What concerns the present article is the shape of the English reception, because that shape determined what Shakespeare could take for granted. By the time the play was written, an English reader's Romeo and Juliet was a composite: Bandello's plot, Boaistuau's frame, poison and ending, Brooke's verse music, Painter's prose clarity, all fused into a single familiar tale whose layers no ordinary reader could separate. The dramatist's originality operated on that composite, not on the Italian original, and criticism that measures his changes against Bandello alone measures them against a book he may never have opened. Measure them against the French-shaped composite and the play's debts stand in their true proportions: enormous in plot, and still more enormous in the architecture of the ending, where the French decision of 1559 governs every minute of the fifth act.

## The Critics and the Caution: Bullough, Muir and Weis

Three authorities frame the modern understanding of Boaistuau's place, and they do not entirely agree, which is fortunate for this article, since the disagreement marks the exact boundary between what is known and what is merely well phrased. Geoffrey Bullough, in the first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, printed the documentary basis of the whole question: his summaries and translated extracts of the French text allow any reader to test the agreements for which he argues, and his argument is uncompromising. Brooke worked from Boaistuau; the apothecary, the waking and the moral apparatus are the French version's contributions to the line; and the English tradition descends through Paris. On Bullough's account, Boaistuau is the most consequential of Shakespeare's indirect sources, the one non-English, non-playwright hand inside the tragedy's machinery.

Kenneth Muir, in The Sources of Shakespeare's Plays, accepts the French mediation and then worries it, productively. Muir's caution runs in two directions. Toward Shakespeare, he allows what Bullough's tidier chain excludes: that the dramatist may have consulted Painter's prose as well as Brooke's poem, so that the French matter could have reached the play by both English doors at once. Toward Boaistuau himself, Muir's method implies the caution this article has carried from its first section: the history of a text's survival is not the history of its composition, and a compiler's page is a record of what he printed, not a sworn statement of what he invented. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, stands as the arbiter closest to the words themselves: his text of the Mantua scene and the tomb, and his introduction's settlement of the sources, treat Brooke as the principal source and the French version as Brooke's own source, the position the documentary evidence supports at the level of the line.

Behind all three stands the figure who did the original detective work, J. J. Munro, whose 1908 edition of Brooke's poem for the Shakespeare Library first set the English text and the French text side by side and drew the unavoidable conclusion. Munro's demonstration is the foundation on which Bullough built, and its method remains the discipline's gold standard: parallel passages, agreements in detail that has no business agreeing, and the patience to let a table of correspondences make an argument that rhetoric cannot. Any account of Boaistuau that omits Munro mistakes the monument for the quarry. The French bridge was not discovered by intuition. It was surveyed, passage by passage, by an editor willing to spend an introduction on the unglamorous question of which book a young English poet had open on his desk in 1562, and the survey has never needed revision, only the cautions about phrasing that Muir later supplied.

The adjudication this article defends divides the honours by question. On transmission, Bullough wins outright: the agreements in invention are documentary facts, and no hypothesis of lost Italian intermediaries is needed, or entitled, to dissolve them. On phrasing, Muir's caution wins: credit should be given in the documentary form, first surviving appearance in Boaistuau, because the alternative form, Boaistuau invented, asserts a psychology that no archive can inspect. On the play, Weis's settlement wins: whatever doors the French matter used, it arrived, and the fifth act's architecture is its receipt. The disagreement, properly heard, is therefore not a scandal in the scholarship but its hygiene at work. Bullough established where the story had been. Muir established how confidently its travels may be narrated. Weis established what the traveller carried into the playhouse. Boaistuau's reputation survives all three examinations, diminished in the romance of solitary invention and enlarged in everything that matters: the documented fact that between Italy and England stands his book, and that the book left marks no later hand removed.

## Reading Boaistuau Now: Editions, Summaries and the Missing Translation

A final peculiarity of the French tradition concerns its availability to the very readers who most need it. Bandello can be read in Italian and in scholarly translation; Brooke's poem survives in modern editions; Painter's prose has been reprinted for students of the sources. Boaistuau's Romeo and Juliet, the hinge of the whole transmission, is the least accessible of the set. Bullough's first volume prints a detailed summary with translated extracts, and that summary, accurate, generous and judicious, is where most Anglophone readers meet the French text, at one remove, in epitome. The full French prose, with its long orations and its narrator's intrusions, remains largely the province of specialists and of the early printed editions, the Sertenas volume and its successors, that research libraries hold. The consequence is a quiet distortion in the criticism: the version scholars can quote in full tends to be credited with the artistry, and the version they know in summary tends to be credited with the plumbing.

The distortion matters for how the verdict of this article should be held. The case for Boaistuau rests on documents any reader can test in Bullough's extracts: the apothecary's refusal and price, the waking's position after the death, the preface's charges, the lament's scale. Those are matters of structure, and structure survives summary. What summary cannot transmit is the French prose's quality, its pace, its Senecan balance, the felt difference between Bandello's anecdotal speed and Boaistuau's oratorical swell. Judgments of Boaistuau as a mere moraliser, common in older accounts, were often judgments of the epitome mistaken for the book. The fuller the French text is read, the harder that dismissal becomes: the moralising is real, but it is mounted in rhetoric of genuine power, and the power is part of what England inherited. A tradition's founder deserves to be read in his own sentences. Until a complete modern English Boaistuau circulates as Brooke and Painter do, the French bridge will remain the least walked part of the story's road, and articles like this one will keep having to argue readers across it.

## Verdict: What the French Tradition Contributed

The cliché this series set out to correct pictures the story leaping from Italy to England in a single bound, Bandello to Brooke to Shakespeare, with France as scenery beneath the leap. The record shows a bridge, and the bridge was load-bearing. France contributed the genre: the tragic history, with its prosecutor-narrator and its doctrine stated before its story, which taught English tellers to attach a verdict to the tale. France contributed the poison's purchase: a scene in which catastrophe is transacted across a counter, spreading guilt from the hero to the economy that serves him. France contributed the ending's desolation: a heroine waking one death too late, a tomb of solitudes, a dagger borrowed from a husband who cannot be asked. And France contributed the scale of speech, the ample rhetoric in which the tragedy's grief still performs itself nightly. Take away the French stage of the journey and the play loses its Chorus's habits, its Mantua scene, its fifth act and half its voice. The Italian story is the play's ancestor. The French tradition is its upbringing, and upbringing, in stories as in families, is most of what a grown work turns out to be.

There is a last measure of the French contribution, and it is the simplest: durability under erasure. Boaistuau was erased by his own title page's modesty, by Brooke's competing credit line, by the play's obliterating fame, and by the summary paragraphs of the source books themselves, and his work survived every erasure intact inside the story's structure. The apothecary still opens his shop in Mantua every night the play is performed. Juliet still wakes too late. The sentence of the Prince still falls like a narrator's moral promoted into dialogue. A writer whose name vanished while his decisions became immortal has a claim on the record that mere survival of a name cannot match, and the claim is what this article has sought to enter: Pierre Boaistuau, of Nantes and Paris, editor, compiler and judge, the French founder of the English tragedy's house.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Boaistuau map, and what does it record about the French version's place between Bandello and Brooke?

The InsightCrunch Boaistuau map is a three-column record of the story's transmission: the state of each principal feature in Bandello's Italian, the state Boaistuau gave it in French, and the form in which England received it. Its columns show a plot crossing the language border nearly intact while its meaning is rebuilt on the French side. The frame, the purchased poison and the solitary ending stand in the French column as first appearances, and the English column shows Brooke and Painter receiving both layers, Italian event and French interpretation, as one indivisible story. The map's purpose is disciplinary: by separating inheritance, invention and conjecture into different columns, it prevents the three from blurring, which is the blur in which the French stage of the journey hid for three centuries.

### Q: Who was Pierre Boaistuau, and what other books did he write besides the Histoires tragiques?

Pierre Boaistuau was a Breton man of letters, born in Nantes around 1517 and dead in Paris in 1566, who made his career as an editor and compiler in the Paris book trade. In 1558 he edited the love tales of Marguerite de Navarre, published under his hand as the Histoires des amans fortunez and later famous as the Heptaméron. The same year produced his Théâtre du monde, a compilation on the miseries of human life that was reprinted for decades, and in 1560 his Histoires prodigieuses gathered wonders and portents into another durable seller. He styled himself Launay on some title pages. The Histoires tragiques of 1559 stands at the centre of that career, the book in which his lifelong method, inventory followed by judgment, found its most consequential material.

### Q: When and where did the Histoires tragiques first appear, and how many tales did its first volume hold?

The Histoires tragiques first appeared in Paris in 1559, printed by Vincent Sertenas, and its first volume held six tales drawn from Bandello's Novelle. The title page announces the method and the source together: tragic histories extracted from the Italian works of Bandello and put into the French language. The story of the Veronese lovers stands third among the six, under the French names Rhomeo and Julietta. The date matters because it fixes the volume three years before Brooke's English poem of 1562 and eight years before Painter's English prose of 1567, making the sequence Italy, France, England a matter of dated title pages. Later continuations by other hands, above all François de Belleforest, swelled the collection toward eighteen volumes across the later sixteenth century.

### Q: Why is Boaistuau's book described as a free adaptation of Bandello rather than a translation?

The description reflects his working method. A translator renders; Boaistuau selected six tales from Bandello's two hundred and fourteen, stripped away the Italian dedicatory frames, installed his own moral addresses, amplified speeches and laments at every crisis, naturalised names, law and money into French terms, added at least one scene the Italian lacks, and altered the ending's arrangement. The result is roughly half again as long as its source, with the growth concentrated in voice rather than incident. His title page's own wording is precise about the operation: the tales are extracted from Bandello and put into French, a formula that claims transformation rather than equivalence. Sixteenth-century usage allowed a wide spectrum under such a formula, and Boaistuau worked at its freest end, as an author rebuilding bought material rather than a schoolmaster construing it.

### Q: Why do source scholars credit the French version with the apothecary's first appearance in the story's chain?

Because the purchase scene stands in Boaistuau's French and in no earlier surviving text. In Da Porto, Romeo reaches the tomb already provided with poison; in Bandello, the Mantuan chapters likewise contain no shopkeeper, no refusal and no sale. The French version introduces the poor apothecary who hesitates over the prohibition on selling poison and yields to gold, and Brooke's English poem then versifies the same transaction with the same furniture, although Brooke's title page credits Bandello alone. Agreement in a scene that exists in only one book is the strongest class of descent evidence. The credit is therefore documentary in form: first surviving appearance in Boaistuau. Whether he invented the scene or preserved it from a lost intermediary cannot be shown, and careful scholarship phrases the claim accordingly.

### Q: What is lost and what is gained when Juliet wakes only after Romeo has died, as she does in Boaistuau?

The loss is the lovers' final conversation, the duet in which the Italian versions let the pair recognise one another, speak and part in language. Bandello's Giulietta wakes in time for that exchange, and its humanity is the Italian ending's chief bequest. The gain is structural and emotional desolation: with the waking moved past Romeo's death, each lover dies in solitude, Romeo addressing a woman who cannot hear, Juliet waking to a corpse and conducting her reckoning alone before taking his dagger. Shakespeare's fifth act follows the French arrangement to the minute, down to the warmth of the dead man's lips as a measure of the minutes missed. Boaistuau traded consolation for architecture, deliberately, because an unsoftened example preaches better; the play inherited the architecture and has been charged with the cruelty ever since.

### Q: Why does it matter whether Boaistuau invented his additions or inherited them from a source that has not survived?

It matters because the two claims carry different evidence and different consequences. That the apothecary scene and the post-mortem waking first survive in Boaistuau is a documentary fact, testable against the printed record, and it settles the practical question of the English line's route: Brooke took those features from the French page. That Boaistuau invented them at his desk is a claim about a mind in 1559, and no archive can inspect it, since a compiler might always have drawn on a redaction that later perished. Keeping the claims apart protects the strong one. Scholars who phrase the credit documentarily, Geoffrey Bullough's method at its best, make the French mediation unanswerable; scholars who phrase it psychologically expose the whole case to a scepticism it does not deserve. Kenneth Muir's cautions in source study amount, in large part, to this distinction.

### Q: How does Boaistuau's opening address to the reader frame the lovers' story before the narrative starts?

It frames the story as a prosecution. Before the narrative begins, Boaistuau's authorial address instructs the reader in the verdict the deaths are to prove: that children owe parents obedience in marriage, that secret contracts invite ruin, that disordered desire disguises itself as love, and that the friar who oils such machinery shares the guilt. The lovers therefore enter their own story as defendants in a trial whose charge sheet has already been read. Bandello's frame, a courtly dedication, asks for the reader's company; Boaistuau's asks for the reader's judgment. The device crossed into English with the tale: Brooke's poem opens with an address to the reader rehearsing the French charges almost item for item, so that the story's first English readers also met the lovers inside a courtroom, and the habit of reading the tale as an exemplum descends from this French threshold.

### Q: Whom does Boaistuau's narration blame for the catastrophe, and how does its judgment differ from Bandello's?

Boaistuau's narration blames chosen acts and the people who chose them: the lovers for disobedience and secret marriage, and Friar Laurence, with new severity, for lending his office to the contrivance. Bandello distributes responsibility more widely and more lightly, across the feud, the families, Fortune and haste, with the friar treated as a charitable meddler whose device failed. The French telling tightens the noose. Fortune, the Italian tradition's presiding weather, recedes, because a prosecutor needs defendants rather than weather, and the friar is promoted from functionary to principal culprit, arraigned in the commentary and required to answer at the end. The difference is between a tragedy of accident and a tragedy of indictment. Shakespeare's play, which lets its friar charm for four acts before the fifth act's accounting prices him, inherits the French distribution in softened form.

### Q: What did François de Belleforest add to the Histoires tragiques after Boaistuau?

Belleforest added continuation, scale and shelf life rather than anything to the Romeo and Juliet story itself. Beginning in 1565, a year before Boaistuau's death, the prolific translator and compiler François de Belleforest issued further volumes of tragic histories, drawing on Bandello and on a widening shelf of continental matter, and the series grew across the later sixteenth century toward the eighteen volumes of its collected form. Boaistuau's first volume, with the lovers' history in its front rank, was reprinted inside that expansion and read wherever the set circulated for two generations. The contribution is structural: without the continuations, the French version of the tale might have remained a single season's Parisian success; with them, it became the standard European repository of tragic narrative, the channel through which the story's French shape kept circulating while England dramatised it.

### Q: What is an histoire tragique, and why does Boaistuau's collection stand at the genre's head in France?

An histoire tragique is a prose narrative of catastrophe presented as true history and closed with a drawn moral: an exemplary disaster, narrated at rhetorical length, with the author present as judge. Boaistuau's collection of 1559 stands at the genre's head in France because it gave the form its name, its method and its first masterpiece of transmission in a single volume. His title coined the label, his prefaces codified the prosecutor-narrator, and his amplification set the scale of speech the genre would expect. Belleforest and the continuators industrialised what Boaistuau had founded, and the form dominated French prose narrative for the rest of the century, shaping the expectations readers brought to tragic tales everywhere the books travelled. The genre is the missing middle term between the Italian novella and the English stage tragedy: a courtly anecdote taught to behave like a judgment.

### Q: What was Boaistuau's connection to Marguerite de Navarre's collection of tales?

Boaistuau was its first editor. The tales of Marguerite de Navarre, queen and author, circulated in manuscript at court, and in 1558 Boaistuau brought them into print at Paris as the Histoires des amans fortunez, arranging and presenting the collection a year before the fuller edition that followed under another hand and, eventually, under the title Heptaméron. The editorship matters to the Romeo and Juliet transmission for two reasons. It established Boaistuau in the exact trade the Histoires tragiques would practise: presenting borrowed narrative to French readers with an editor's framing hand. And it placed him, at the start of his career, at the fountainhead of French prose fiction, handling the queen's stories with the same freedom, selection, ordering, presentation, that he would apply to Bandello's a year later. The apprentice work and the masterpiece are one method at two addresses.

### Q: Under what names and spellings do the lovers and their families appear in Boaistuau's French?

The lovers appear as Rhomeo and Julietta, the hero's name wearing a French aspirated spelling, the heroine keeping her Italian diminutive in French dress. The feuding houses become the Capellets and the Montesches, their Italian endings planed into forms a Parisian reader could pronounce without stumbling, and the friar is Frère Laurent. The changes are small and systematic, a work of naturalisation rather than reinvention: names, like the tale's law and money, are converted into the receiving culture's terms so that the story reads as a French book rather than an Italian one in exile. Brooke's English forms descend from this French layer of spelling rather than directly from the Italian, one of the quieter fingerprints of the route his poem took, since a translator betrays his working text in small orthographies as surely as in great scenes.

### Q: Where does the extra length of Boaistuau's retelling go, compared with Bandello's novella?

It goes into voice, not incident. Boaistuau adds almost no new events beyond the Mantuan poison purchase; the plot's stations, feast, meetings, marriage, duel, exile, potion and tomb, stand where Bandello placed them. The growth, roughly half again the Italian's compass, concentrates at the frame, in the opening moral address and the closing judgment; at the scenes of decision, where speeches swell into set orations; and above all in the tomb, where Romeo's address to the seeming corpse and Julietta's long lament over the real one expand a scene of minutes into the book's rhetorical summit. Julietta's potion soliloquy likewise grows from report into an interior campaign against imagined horrors. The pattern is consistent enough to state as the French method: wherever the Italian tells, the French makes someone speak, and then makes the narrator judge the speech.

### Q: Which changes that enter the story in Boaistuau's French survive all the way into Shakespeare's play?

Three survive entire, and a fourth survives as architecture. The apothecary purchase stands in the play's Mantua scene, complete with the seller's poverty, his refusal grounded in the law against selling poison, and gold overbearing the law. The post-mortem waking governs the play's fifth act: Romeo dies unheard, Juliet wakes to a corpse, notes the warmth of his lips and takes his dagger. Juliet's solitary dagger death, following a reckoning conducted alone, completes the French pattern. The fourth survival is the judging voice itself, transformed: Boaistuau's prosecuting narrator is dismissed, but his office passes to the Chorus that frames the action and to the Prince whose closing sentence distributes punishment and pardon over the bodies. What did not survive is the French prose's explicit doctrine, which the play everywhere implies and nowhere states.

### Q: What would Brooke and Painter have lacked if the story had reached England directly from Italy?

They would have lacked the story's three most consequential fittings. Without the French stage, the English versions inherit Bandello's ending, in which Juliet wakes in time to speak with Romeo, and the desolate architecture of solitude that the play's fifth act requires would not exist. They would have lacked the apothecary, and with him the Mantua transaction that spreads the catastrophe's guilt across a market, a statute and a poor man's hunger. And they would have lacked the moral frame in its systematic form, the address that arraigns disobedience, secret marriage and the meddling friar, which Brooke's own preface reproduces from the French apparatus and which taught English readers to receive the tale as an exemplum. A direct Italian route was geographically possible; neither English book took it, and the tale England actually received is unthinkable without the French fittings.

### Q: Why do the standard English source collections print Boaistuau's version in summary rather than in full?

Partly for reasons of bulk and language, and partly by an inertia the summary then perpetuates. Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, the standard collection, prints the French version as a detailed summary with translated extracts, a form that lets an English reader test every structural claim, the purchase, the waking, the frame, without reproducing Boaistuau's full rhetorical amplitude. The consequence is a quiet distortion in the criticism: the version scholars can quote entire, Brooke's poem, is credited with the artistry, while the version known in epitome is credited with the plumbing. Judgments of Boaistuau as a heavy moraliser were often judgments of the summary mistaken for the book. A complete modern English Boaistuau, circulating as Brooke and Painter do, remains the transmission's missing convenience, and until it exists the French bridge stays the least walked part of the road.

### Q: Why did Boaistuau choose Bandello's Italian tales as the material for a new kind of French book?

Because Bandello offered the largest proven inventory of tragic narrative in Europe, in a prestige language, awaiting French dress. The Novelle, printed at Lucca in 1554, held more than two hundred tales of love, violence and reversal, and Italian storytellers carried an authority at the French court and in the French book trade that native invention could not match. A Paris bookseller commissioning extractions bought fashionable foreign goods; an editor of Boaistuau's ambition bought, in the same transaction, the raw material for a new French genre, since Bandello's tales arrived without the frame, the rhetoric and the doctrine that the histoire tragique would impose. The choice was commercial and literary at once, and the Romeo and Juliet tale, with its double death, secret marriage and meddling friar, was the inventory's most prosecutorial item: perfect evidence for the genre's founding argument that catastrophe instructs.

### Q: How can an essay use the Boaistuau map to argue that translation in this chain worked as a form of authorship?

By arguing from the map's middle column. The map isolates the features that stand in Boaistuau's French and in no earlier surviving text: the moral address that arraigns the lovers before their story starts, the apothecary who sells the catastrophe across a counter, the waking moved to the far side of Romeo's death, the rhetoric that turns report into oration. Each feature is a decision with consequences that the later tradition, Brooke, Painter and the play, obeys to the letter. An essay can therefore show that the decisive creative acts in this transmission include acts of translation: Boaistuau held no copyright in the plot and holds clear title to its frame, its poison and its ending. The map disciplines the argument by separating those titles from the Italian inheritance in the first column, keeping the claim for translational authorship exact rather than rhetorical.

### Q: Why does the dating of the Histoires tragiques to about 1559 matter for the English books that follow it?

Because the date converts a plausible route into a demonstrable sequence. Fixed at Paris in 1559, the French volume precedes Brooke's poem of 1562 by three years and Painter's prose of 1567 by eight, intervals that fit the ordinary machinery of the cross-Channel book trade, Paris imprints stocked in London and read by French-reading gentlemen. The tightness of the sequence also explains the closeness of the English versions to the French one: Brooke was working from a new book, not a remote classic, and its alterations were fresh ink rather than accumulated tradition. Finally, the date anchors the whole transmission's span: from Bandello's Lucca edition of 1554 to the London stage of the 1590s, the story crossed three languages and four forms in under forty years, and the French year at the centre of that span is the hinge on which the chronology turns.
