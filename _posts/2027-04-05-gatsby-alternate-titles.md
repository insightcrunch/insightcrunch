The title Fitzgerald almost used was not The Great Gatsby at all, and for most of the two years he spent making the novel he expected the cover to read Trimalchio in West Egg. That fact changes how you read the book. A title is the first argument a novel makes about itself, the frame it hands the reader before a single sentence of story arrives, and Fitzgerald tried on at least half a dozen frames before he settled on the three plain words we now treat as inevitable. Recovering the names he discarded is not literary trivia. It is a way of seeing the choice behind the choice, because each rejected option would have told the reader something different about what kind of book this is and what kind of man Jay Gatsby is meant to be.

![The alternate titles of The Great Gatsby and the Trimalchio draft explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-69.webp)

Read the discarded names and the final one stops looking obvious. Trimalchio in West Egg would have promised a satire of a vulgar arriviste. On the Road to West Egg would have promised a journey narrative. The Gold-Hatted Gatsby and The High-Bouncing Lover, both drawn from the novel's own epigraph, would have promised a book more playful and more openly ironic than the elegy Fitzgerald wrote. Under the Red, White, and Blue, the name he wanted at the very last minute, would have made the whole thing a verdict on America before the reader met a single character. Set these against The Great Gatsby and you can measure exactly what the final choice gained, which is the argument this article defends.

## The choice behind the name: why the alternate titles change the reading

Titles do quiet, powerful work. They set expectation, they cue genre, and they tell the reader how to hold the story before the story starts. When a novel is as canonical as this one, the name feels welded to the book, as if Fitzgerald reached into the air and pulled down the only possible label. He did nothing of the kind. He agonized. He proposed name after name to his editor, changed his mind, changed it back, and even after the book was set in type he tried to swap the words on the cover for something else entirely. The history of that indecision is one of the best-documented episodes in his correspondence with Maxwell Perkins at Scribner's, and it survives because Fitzgerald put his second thoughts in writing.

The stakes of recovering that history are interpretive, not antiquarian. If you believe the final name was inevitable, you treat it as a neutral label and you stop thinking about it. If you see it as a choice made against real alternatives, you have to ask what the choice does, and the moment you ask that question you are reading the novel more closely than most of its readers ever will. The word great in particular is not decoration. It is the hinge on which the book's irony turns, and the alternatives make that irony visible by showing what the novel would have lost without it. This is close reading applied to the cover, and it is exactly the kind of attention that separates a reader who has absorbed the plot from a reader who can argue about the design.

There is a second reason the alternate names matter. Two of them, The Gold-Hatted Gatsby and The High-Bouncing Lover, are lifted straight from the epigraph poem that still opens the novel, the verse Fitzgerald signed with the invented name Thomas Parke d'Invilliers. Those phrases did not vanish when he abandoned them as titles. They stayed, embedded on the first page, so that the rejected names haunt the finished book like a watermark. A reader who knows the history of the wavering reads that epigraph differently, hearing in it the ghost of the book Fitzgerald almost published. The final wording of the guide to what the finished name signifies belongs to the [master reading of what the title means](/2022/04/04/great-gatsby-title-meaning/); this article stays with the road not taken, the names he weighed and let go.

## The title Fitzgerald almost used: the names he weighed

Fitzgerald's list of candidates was not a tidy shortlist he ran through once. It was a moving target that shifted across the composition and the production of the book, and the names cluster into families that each imagine a different novel. Understanding those families is the substance of the context, so it is worth walking through them in the order of what each would have imposed on the reader.

The name he lived with longest was Trimalchio, sometimes standing alone and sometimes expanded to Trimalchio in West Egg. This was his working label for most of the drafting, and he was genuinely attached to it. The early typescript that scholars now call the Trimalchio draft, an earlier and materially different version of the novel, carries the mark of that preference, which is why the two are easy to confuse. They are not the same thing. The [Trimalchio draft](/2030/12/16/gatsby-trimalchio-draft/) is a distinct textual object, an earlier state of the manuscript with real differences from the published book. The alternate titles are a separate matter, a question of what to call the finished novel, and Trimalchio happens to sit at the center of both stories because Fitzgerald loved the allusion enough to reuse it.

A second family pointed at place and journey. On the Road to West Egg framed the book as a movement toward a destination, a narrative of arrival, with the geography of the invented Long Island towns doing the naming. This family kept the setting Fitzgerald had built, the fictional Great Neck and Manhasset Neck he renamed West Egg and East Egg, but it traded the protagonist for the map. It would have been a novel named after where the characters go rather than who they are, and that shift alone tells you how differently the reader would have entered the story.

A third family came out of the epigraph. The Gold-Hatted Gatsby and The High-Bouncing Lover both borrow their images from the four lines of verse that still open the novel, the little poem about wearing the gold hat and bouncing high to win the beloved. These names would have leaned into whimsy and irony, announcing from the cover that the book knew its hero was performing, straining, trying too hard to be seen. They are the most openly playful of all the candidates, and they are the ones the finished book keeps closest, because the poem survived even though the names drawn from it did not.

A fourth family, the one Fitzgerald reached for at the end, went national and abstract. Under the Red, White, and Blue turned the whole novel into a statement about America, folding the private tragedy of one man into the colors of the flag. It is the grandest of the alternatives and the least specific, and Fitzgerald wanted it badly enough to try to make the change after the book was already in type, a timing that tells you how far he was still willing to gamble on the frame.

### Who is Trimalchio and why did Fitzgerald borrow the name?

Trimalchio is a character in the Satyricon, a Roman work attributed to Petronius, a freed slave who grows enormously rich and throws grotesque, showy banquets to prove his arrival. Fitzgerald borrowed the name because Gatsby, too, is a self-made man buying status through spectacle, and the allusion cast the parties as vulgar theater staged by an outsider desperate to belong.

The trouble with the allusion is that it presumes a classically educated reader who will recognize Petronius on sight, and it fixes the reader's judgment before the novel earns it. Trimalchio in the Satyricon is a figure of satire, comic and repellent, and to name the book after him is to tell the reader in advance that Gatsby is a fool with money and nothing more. Fitzgerald's finished novel refuses that verdict. It lets Gatsby be ridiculous and then, at the very end, insists on his greatness anyway, and a Trimalchio title would have closed off that late reversal before it could land. The name that survives, [How Fitzgerald wrote the novel](/2027/03/01/how-fitzgerald-wrote-gatsby/) over two hard years, is one that keeps the question open.

Fitzgerald did not throw the allusion away entirely. It surfaces once inside the finished text, at the moment in the seventh chapter when the parties stop and Nick observes that "his career as Trimalchio was over." That single sentence is the fossil of the discarded name, a fossil deliberately preserved. Fitzgerald let the reader meet Trimalchio exactly once, at the point where Gatsby abandons the performance to pursue Daisy directly, so that the allusion marks the end of the spectacle rather than defining the whole book. It is a precise piece of craft. The name he rejected for the cover became a hinge inside the plot.

## Trimalchio's feast: what the Roman allusion actually promised

To feel the weight of the name Fitzgerald nearly kept, it helps to know what a reader of 1925 with a classical education would have heard in it. The Satyricon survives in fragments, and its most famous episode is the Cena Trimalchionis, the dinner of Trimalchio, a long set piece describing a banquet of staggering excess. Trimalchio is a freedman, a former slave who has amassed a fortune, and the feast is his instrument of self-advertisement. Courses arrive disguised as other foods, a boar is carved to release live birds, the host recites bad verse and boasts of his wealth, and the whole evening is a performance of arrival by a man who cannot buy the one thing money will not purchase, which is the ease of those born to it. The scene is comic and grotesque, and its satire falls squarely on the vulgarity of new money straining to look like old.

The parallel to Gatsby is exact enough to see why Fitzgerald reached for it. Gatsby's parties are a freedman's feast in modern dress. The orchestra, the crates of oranges and lemons, the buffets glistening with spiced hams, the guests who arrive uninvited and leave without meeting their host, all of it is spectacle staged by a self-made man to draw one particular guest across the water. Gatsby, like Trimalchio, has bought his way up from nothing and cannot quite disguise the nothing he came from. Tom Buchanan sneers at the pink suit and the drugstore fortune precisely as a Roman aristocrat would have sneered at the freedman's gold. The allusion, then, is not lazy. It is a genuine reading of Gatsby as a type the ancient world already knew, the outsider who mistakes display for belonging.

But the allusion delivers a fixed verdict, and that is the trouble. In Petronius the freedman is an object of ridicule and nothing more; the reader is invited to laugh at him and feel superior. To name the novel after Trimalchio is to instruct the reader to take that same superior stance toward Gatsby from the first page, and Fitzgerald's finished book will not permit it. The whole force of the ending depends on the reader having come to feel, against Tom's contempt and against Nick's better judgment, that Gatsby's straining is somehow finer than the carelessness of the people he strains toward. Nick says as much when he tells Gatsby that the whole rotten crowd is not worth him. A Trimalchio on the cover would have poisoned that late tenderness by promising a satire the novel outgrows, and Fitzgerald seems to have sensed the mismatch even as he clung to the name he loved.

### What did a 1925 reader hear in the name Trimalchio?

An educated reader in 1925 would have recognized Trimalchio immediately as the grotesque nouveau-riche host from Petronius's Satyricon, and would have expected a satire of a vulgar upstart. The name promised comedy at the expense of new money, a book that mocked its central figure rather than mourning him, which is precisely the expectation Fitzgerald's finished novel works to overturn.

That gap between the promise and the book is why the allusion could not carry the cover. Fitzgerald wrote much of the novel abroad, among the wealthy American expatriates whose world he was anatomizing, and the distance sharpened his eye for the difference between mockery and elegy. The classical frame belonged to a satirist, and Fitzgerald had written something closer to a tragedy with a satirist's surface. He needed a name that could hold both registers, and Trimalchio could hold only one. The allusion is exact as a description of Gatsby's parties and wrong as a description of the reader's final feeling, and a title has to answer to the ending, not just the middle.

The persistence of the name in Fitzgerald's imagination is itself telling. He did not abandon Trimalchio easily or early; he defended it, expanded it, and let it govern his working sense of the book for a long stretch, which suggests he first conceived the novel closer to satire than the finished elegy. The distance he traveled from Trimalchio to The Great Gatsby is the distance the book itself travels from mockery to grief, from a party seen from outside as absurd to a life mourned from inside as significant. Reading the naming history, one watches Fitzgerald grow past his own first idea of the book, and the survival of the Roman name in that single seventh-chapter sentence is his acknowledgment of where he began, a quiet salute to the satire the novel outgrew on its way to something larger.

## On the Road to West Egg: the geography Fitzgerald almost put on the cover

The place names Fitzgerald invented were not neutral scenery, and one discarded candidate would have promoted them to the marquee. On the Road to West Egg would have named the book for its setting and its shape, framing the story as a movement toward a destination rather than a portrait of a person. West Egg is the fictional stand-in for the part of Long Island where new money built its mansions, the raw, showy enclave across the bay from the older, quieter money of East Egg. Nick lives in West Egg, in the small house beside Gatsby's enormous one, and the whole social geography of the novel runs along the axis between the two Eggs and the road that leads through the Valley of Ashes into the city.

A geographic name would have done something specific to the reader's expectations. It would have foregrounded the journey and the map, promising a novel about arriving somewhere, and there is a version of Gatsby that fits that frame, since the book is full of driving, of cars moving between the Eggs and the city, of the fatal road where Myrtle dies. The road is where much of the plot's violence happens, and the phrase on the road carries a forward motion that suits a story building toward catastrophe. Yet the frame is finally too external. It names the setting and loses the man, and the novel is not really about West Egg as a place. It is about a single figure standing on his lawn reaching toward a green light across the water, and no map can hold that image.

The geographic candidate also risked a smaller, drearier book. A story named for its destination invites the reader to track progress toward that destination, to read for plot and arrival, and Gatsby is not built for that kind of reading. Its power is retrospective and lyrical, filtered through Nick's memory and grief, and its most important movements are internal rather than geographic. To name it for the road would have been to promise the wrong genre, a picaresque or a travel narrative, when Fitzgerald had written an elegy disguised as a summer's social comedy. The setting matters enormously to the book, but it works best as the ground beneath the characters rather than as the thing the cover points at.

There is a subtler cost as well. The two Eggs are a symbol before they are a setting, twin shapes identical in outline and opposite in meaning, new money and old, and the novel uses that near-identity to make its argument about class. West Egg alone, promoted to the title, would have split the pair and lost the contrast, naming one half of a symbol that means nothing without its other half. The Buchanans' East Egg is the standard Gatsby's West Egg can never meet, the older, surer wealth that regards the new arrivals with contempt, and a name that pointed only west would have quietly taken Gatsby's side and flattened the social geometry the book keeps in tension. The final name sidesteps the problem by naming neither shore, keeping the reader's attention on the man who stands between them, reaching across the water from the wrong Egg toward a light on the right one.

## The gold-hatted lover: how the epigraph keeps the rejected names alive

The epigraph that still opens the novel is four lines of verse signed Thomas Parke d'Invilliers, and the signature is itself a small joke, because d'Invilliers is a character from Fitzgerald's first novel and the poem is Fitzgerald's own. The lines read, in part, "Then wear the gold hat, if that will move her," and "If you can bounce high, bounce for her too," and they build to the cry of the "gold-hatted, high-bouncing lover." Two abandoned titles live inside those lines. The Gold-Hatted Gatsby and The High-Bouncing Lover were never arbitrary inventions; they were phrases Fitzgerald already had on the page, and he considered promoting them from epigraph to cover.

That the poem stayed while the names it might have supplied were dropped is one of the most revealing facts about the whole affair. Fitzgerald kept the images and refused the labels. He wanted the reader to encounter the gold hat and the high bounce, to feel the strain and the performance those metaphors describe, but he did not want to announce that reading from the cover. A title is a promise; an epigraph is a hint. By demoting the phrases from promise to hint, Fitzgerald preserved their irony while withholding the verdict, letting the reader arrive at the sense of Gatsby as a straining performer through the story rather than being told it in advance.

The gold hat and the high bounce also encode the novel's central action in miniature. Gatsby wears the gold hat, the display of wealth, precisely to move Daisy, and he bounces high, exerts himself past the point of dignity, for her too. The epigraph is a compressed statement of the whole plot, and it survives because it works better as a frame than as a name. As a title it would have flattened Gatsby into a clown. As an epigraph it deepens him, because the reader watches him actually wear the gold hat and actually bounce high, and the performance becomes moving rather than merely absurd. The ironic edge of the final naming belongs to the same instinct that governs [irony throughout the novel](/2026/03/30/gatsby-irony/), the refusal to let any single tone hold the whole book.

Consider how the epigraph would have landed as a name against how it lands as a frame. Printed on the spine, The High-Bouncing Lover would have been a comic label, the kind of phrase that invites a smirk, and every reader would have carried that smirk into the first chapter and measured Gatsby against it. Buried in the epigraph, the same phrase does patient work. The reader meets it before knowing who Gatsby is, files it away half-understood, and only later, watching Gatsby stretch and strain toward Daisy across the bay, feels the phrase click into place as an accurate and suddenly poignant description. The demotion from name to epigraph converts mockery into recognition, and the timing is everything. A title is read first and colors all that follows; an epigraph is read first and understood last, which is why Fitzgerald could risk the same words in the smaller position that would have sunk the book in the larger one.

The signature deepens the effect further. By attributing the lines to Thomas Parke d'Invilliers, a poet he invented for an earlier novel, Fitzgerald frames his own summary of the plot as a found quotation, an epigraph borrowed from a minor authority, which gives the gold-hatted image the air of a proverb the story will test. The reader is invited to take the verse as a wise old saw about love and display, and then to watch the novel both confirm and complicate it, since Gatsby does everything the poem prescribes and still loses. Had the phrase been the title, this layered irony would have collapsed into a joke. Kept as a signed epigraph, it becomes a small oracle, and the discarded titles are the paths not taken away from that oracle toward something blunter.

### Why did Fitzgerald sign the epigraph with an invented poet's name?

Thomas Parke d'Invilliers is a minor character from This Side of Paradise, Fitzgerald's debut novel, a poet within that book. Attributing the epigraph to him lets Fitzgerald quote himself while pretending not to, a private wink that also lends the verse a borrowed authority, as though the lines came from some established minor poet rather than from the novelist himself.

The device matters for the alternate titles because it shows how comfortable Fitzgerald was hiding his own hand. He was willing to plant his verse under a mask, to let the reader assume an outside source, and that same playfulness runs through his title-making. He tried names that quoted his own poem, names that quoted a Roman satirist, names that quoted the flag. Each was a way of borrowing a frame from somewhere else and setting it over Gatsby's head. The final name borrows nothing. The Great Gatsby is the only candidate that does not reach outside the novel for its authority, and that self-sufficiency is part of why it holds.

## Under the Red, White, and Blue: the wavering that ran to the last

The most dramatic part of the story is how late it went. Fitzgerald did not settle his mind and then move on. He agreed to The Great Gatsby with visible reluctance, prodded by Perkins and by Zelda, both of whom preferred it, and then, with the book already set in type and moving toward publication, he wired his editor wanting to change the name to Under the Red, White, and Blue. By then it was effectively too late. The machinery of production had turned too far, and the novel went out under the name he had grudgingly accepted rather than the one his last-minute nerve reached for.

This wavering is not a footnote. It tells you that Fitzgerald himself did not experience the final name as inevitable, which is the strongest possible corrective to the reader who treats it that way. He thought the national frame might be grander, might make the book feel like the American statement he sensed it could be, and he was willing to gamble the whole cover on that hunch at the eleventh hour. That he lost the gamble to a printing schedule rather than to a settled conviction is worth sitting with. The name we canonize is partly an accident of timing, and it is a better name than the one Fitzgerald wanted at the end, which is a strange and instructive thing to know about a masterpiece.

Under the Red, White, and Blue would have done real damage. It would have told the reader before the first page that the book is a verdict on the nation, and it would have made the private ache of one man's longing feel like a mere illustration of a thesis. The finished novel earns its national resonance slowly, through Nick's closing meditation on the green light and the old island that flowered once for Dutch sailors' eyes, and to announce that meaning on the cover would have spoiled the slow build. The abstraction that would have felt grand on the title page would have felt like padding in an essay, and the same is true of the reader who reaches for a grand national reading too early. Context works as analysis only when it stays tied to the specific scene, and the discarded flag title is a warning about starting with the abstraction instead of ending there.

### How late did Fitzgerald try to change the title?

Fitzgerald tried to change it at the last possible moment, after the novel was already in proof and set in type, wiring Perkins to ask for Under the Red, White, and Blue. The production schedule had advanced too far to accommodate the switch, so the book appeared as The Great Gatsby, the name he had accepted earlier with reluctance rather than conviction.

The lateness is the point. A writer who settles a title early and forgets it experiences the name as natural. A writer who is still trying to change it as the presses warm up experiences it as contingent, one option among several that happened to win. Fitzgerald belonged to the second kind. His indecision is documented in the same body of letters that records the [editing partnership with Maxwell Perkins](/2027/03/29/maxwell-perkins-editing-gatsby/), the editor who steadied the book through its final shaping and who preferred the name that stuck. Perkins's preference, set against Fitzgerald's late doubt, is part of why the final choice reads as a collaboration rather than a lone stroke of genius.

## The naming as collaboration: Zelda, Perkins, and the pressure of the press

The received image of the solitary genius naming his masterpiece does not survive contact with the record. The name The Great Gatsby emerged from a small negotiation among three parties: Fitzgerald, who kept reaching for Trimalchio and then for the flag; Perkins, his editor at Scribner's, who wanted something clear and saleable; and Zelda Fitzgerald, whose taste her husband trusted and who favored the plain, character-centered name. The final wording won because two of the three people closest to the book preferred it and because the calendar of production eventually removed the option of further change. It is a made thing, shaped by more than one hand and by the ordinary constraints of publishing, and knowing that deepens rather than diminishes it.

Perkins's role is easy to underrate because good editing is invisible by design. He was the steadying professional across Fitzgerald's career, the reader who could tell an author which of his instincts to trust, and on the naming he trusted the instinct that produced clarity over the instinct that produced allusion. A commercial editor in 1925 had reason to fear a name that would baffle browsers in a bookshop, and Trimalchio would have baffled most of them. But Perkins's preference was not merely commercial. The plain name is also the better literary choice, and part of an editor's craft is knowing when the marketable option and the artful option are the same option. Here they were, and Perkins saw it while Fitzgerald was still half in love with his Roman.

Zelda's part is quieter in the record but real. Fitzgerald submitted much to her judgment, sometimes to a degree that complicated their marriage, and her preference for the character-centered name aligned with Perkins's and told against her husband's attachment to Trimalchio. The result is that the title we treat as inevitable was in fact ratified by the two readers Fitzgerald most needed to please, working against his own stated preference, and finalized by a deadline neither he nor they controlled. There is a lesson in that for how meaning gets made. The novel's most famous three words are the product of pressure, taste, and timing, not of a single confident vision, and the book is no less great for having been named by committee and by clock. If anything the collaborative origin is fitting, because a novel so preoccupied with performance and reputation, with how a name can be built and sold, was itself named through exactly the mix of judgment and accident it describes. This whole story sits inside the larger account of the [1925 publication](/2027/03/08/gatsby-1925-publication/), the release that sent a negotiated name into an indifferent market.

## The difference the title makes to interpretation

Set the alternatives aside and ask what The Great Gatsby actually does. The word great is the whole engine. Applied to a man who is a bootlegger, a fabricator of his own past, and a romantic willing to take the blame for a killing he did not commit, the adjective is unstable. It can be read as sincere, an honest tribute to the largeness of Gatsby's longing and his capacity for hope. It can be read as ironic, the flat American idiom of the carnival barker, the great this and the great that, applied to a showman whose greatness is a performance. The novel sustains both readings at once and never resolves them, and that irresolution is the book's deepest effect.

None of the rejected names could do this. Trimalchio fixes the verdict at contempt. The Gold-Hatted Gatsby fixes it at whimsy. Under the Red, White, and Blue fixes it at national allegory. Each is legible, and legibility is exactly the problem, because a title that tells the reader what to think closes the interpretive space the novel needs open. The Great Gatsby is the only candidate that poses a question instead of delivering an answer. It calls Gatsby great and dares the reader to decide whether the word is earned, and that dare is renewed every time the reader reaches the final page and has to weigh Gatsby's foolishness against his grandeur one more time.

The choice also names the man rather than the place, the allusion, or the nation. On the Road to West Egg would have foregrounded geography. Under the Red, White, and Blue would have foregrounded the country. The Great Gatsby foregrounds a single human being, and it does so with a definite article and an adjective that together promise significance. The is not a. This is not a Gatsby among many but the Gatsby, the definitive one, the one worth a book, and that grammatical confidence is part of the irony, because the man the sentence elevates is in most respects a failure. The title's structure enacts the novel's argument, insisting on the importance of a life the world would call small.

The doubleness pays off most sharply at the very end. Nick's closing meditation reaches past Gatsby to the Dutch sailors who once saw the fresh green breast of the new world, and he ties Gatsby's wonder at the green light to that older, national wonder, so that the small man's longing becomes an image of the whole American reaching after something already behind it. A name that had already announced the national theme, as Under the Red, White, and Blue would have done, would have robbed that ending of its surprise. The reader would have been waiting for the flag from page one. Because the cover named only the man, the widening at the close, from Gatsby to America to every reader who has ever believed in a receding green light, arrives as a discovery rather than a fulfilled promise. The title's restraint is what makes the ending's reach possible.

### Does the title's irony depend on knowing Gatsby's crimes?

The irony sharpens as the reader learns what Gatsby is. Called great on the cover, he turns out to be a bootlegger tied to organized crime, a man who invented his name and his past and who lets Daisy escape responsibility for a death. Each revelation puts pressure on the adjective, so the word grows more unstable the deeper the reader goes, and the cover's promise is retested with every chapter.

By the final page the reader holds two Gatsbys at once, the criminal fraud and the incorruptible dreamer, and the single word great is the container that refuses to let either cancel the other. This is why the name rewards rereading in a way the alternatives could not. A satirical or a national frame settles on a first reading and stays settled. The Great Gatsby reopens every time, because the tension the adjective holds is never resolved by the plot, only deepened. The title is not a summary the reader outgrows but a question the reader carries back into the book.

## The word great and the American grain

There is a specifically American flavor to the irony that the classical and national alternatives would have missed. Great is the great American adjective, the word that fronts a thousand advertisements and civic boasts, the Great White Way, the great outdoors, the greatest show on earth. It is the language of the pitch, the carnival, the salesman, and Gatsby is, among his other selves, a salesman of an image. To call him great in that idiom is to place him in a line of American self-promoters and to hear, under the tribute, the hollow ring of the marketplace. Fitzgerald had a fine ear for that ring, and the name lets the reader hear it without insisting on it, so that the same word praises the dreamer and diagnoses the huckster in one breath.

Yet the word also carries its older, graver sense, the greatness of scale and soul that belongs to tragic heroes. Gatsby's capacity for hope is genuinely large, larger than anyone else's in the book, and Nick's whole narration is an effort to honor that largeness against the evidence of the man's absurdity and crime. The adjective therefore straddles two registers of American speech at once, the cheap superlative and the true measure of a great heart, and the novel never tells the reader which register governs. Held between the sideshow and the elegy, the word does what no alternative could. It makes the reader supply the tone, and in supplying it the reader takes a position on Gatsby that the book then complicates. The finished [meaning of the title](/2022/04/04/great-gatsby-title-meaning/) lives in that unresolved doubleness, which the discarded names throw into relief precisely because each of them resolved it.

### What does the word great in the title actually do?

The word great does double duty. Read straight, it honors Gatsby's outsized capacity for hope and his refusal to accept the ordinary. Read ironically, it echoes the hollow American idiom of the sideshow and the advertisement, the cheap superlative slapped on everything. The novel keeps both readings live, so the adjective never settles into pure praise or pure mockery.

That instability is the reason the title outperforms every alternative. A great many titles can praise a hero, and a great many can mock a fraud, but very few can do both in a single word and leave the reader to arbitrate. Fitzgerald found one, and the fact that he found it partly by exhausting the alternatives, by trying and discarding names that each did only one of those jobs, suggests that the doubleness was not luck. He kept rejecting names that answered the question until he found one that asked it. Reading the discarded list is a lesson in how a writer arrives at ambiguity on purpose.

## Myths and oversimplifications about the title to avoid

The first myth is that the name was obvious. It was not. Fitzgerald preferred Trimalchio for most of the writing and tried to change the accepted name at the last minute, and only the pressure of his editor, the taste of his wife, and the deadline of the press produced the version we know. Treating the name as inevitable erases a real history of doubt and, worse, stops the reader from asking what the name accomplishes. The inevitability is an illusion created by fame. A book we have all known by one name for a century feels as though it could never have been called anything else, but the manuscript record says otherwise.

The second myth is that the alternate titles and the Trimalchio draft are the same subject. They are not. The draft is an earlier version of the novel, a different arrangement of the text with real differences in scene and structure, and it is a genuine object of study in its own right. The alternate titles are a separate question about what to call the finished book. Trimalchio bridges the two because Fitzgerald favored it as both a working label and a candidate name, but a careful reader keeps the draft and the naming distinct. Conflating them muddles two different kinds of evidence, one textual and one paratextual.

The third myth is that the discarded names were bad and the final one simply better, a straightforward story of a writer improving. The truth is subtler. Some of the alternatives were genuinely strong. The epigraph titles are witty and the national one is ambitious, and Fitzgerald wanted the national one at the end. The final name is not better because the others are worthless. It is better because it alone holds the novel's irony open, and recognizing the real appeal of the rejected names is what makes the final choice's superiority visible. A comparison that stacks the deck teaches nothing. A fair one, which grants each alternative its virtue and then shows what it would have cost, teaches everything.

The fourth myth is that the naming history is a curiosity with no bearing on how the book means. On the contrary, it is one of the clearest windows the novel offers onto its own method. The choice among the names is a choice among readings, and by watching Fitzgerald weigh satire against elegy against national allegory, a reader watches him decide, in miniature, what kind of book to write. The wavering is not noise around the signal; it is the signal, the record of an author sorting through the possible meanings of his own material and settling on the one that stays unsettled. To dismiss the alternate names as trivia is to miss that the title is the novel's thesis in three words, arrived at by elimination, and that the eliminations are as instructive as the survivor.

### Why does the naming history matter to how the book means?

The naming history matters because each candidate name is a compressed reading of the novel, so watching Fitzgerald choose among them is watching him choose among interpretations. The move from Trimalchio to The Great Gatsby is a move from satire to something that holds satire and elegy together, and that shift on the cover mirrors the doubleness the whole book sustains inside.

Treating the history this way turns a biographical fact into a critical tool. Instead of a list of quaint rejected labels, the alternate names become evidence for an argument about the book's refusal to settle, and that argument is stronger for resting on Fitzgerald's own documented process. The reader who knows only the finished name can assert the novel's ambiguity; the reader who knows the discarded names can demonstrate it, showing that Fitzgerald reached the ambiguous frame by trying and rejecting every unambiguous one. That is the difference between claiming a reading and proving it.

### What is the difference between the alternate titles and the Trimalchio draft?

The alternate titles are the various names Fitzgerald considered for the finished novel, from Trimalchio in West Egg to Under the Red, White, and Blue. The Trimalchio draft is a distinct, earlier version of the manuscript, materially different in places from the published book. One concerns the cover; the other concerns the contents.

Keeping them apart matters for accuracy and for essay writing. A student who writes that Fitzgerald almost called the book Trimalchio is correct. A student who writes that the Trimalchio draft and The Great Gatsby are merely two names for one text is wrong, because the draft differs from the published novel in substance, not only in its label. The name Trimalchio sits at the junction of the two stories, which is precisely why the confusion arises and why a precise reader takes care to specify which one is meant.

## The title as analysis: using the alternate names in an essay

The alternate titles are one of the most efficient tools a student can carry into an essay, because they turn a paratextual fact into an interpretive lever. The move is simple and powerful. State what Fitzgerald almost called the book, name what that title would have imposed, and then argue that the final choice does something the alternative could not. In two sentences you have shown that the title is a choice with consequences, and you have earned the right to read the word great as an argument rather than a label. That is a stronger opening than the usual thesis about the American Dream, because it grounds a claim about meaning in a documented decision.

The key is to use the context as analysis, not as decoration. A weak essay reports the alternate names as a fun fact and moves on. A strong essay makes the names do work. If your thesis is that the novel refuses to settle its judgment of Gatsby, the discarded titles are your first evidence, because each of them settles the judgment and the final one does not. If your thesis is about performance and display, the gold-hatted and high-bouncing titles from the epigraph are your evidence, because they name the performance the novel dramatizes. The alternate names are not a digression from your argument; they are a way to prove it from the outside, using the cover to confirm what the close reading finds inside.

There is a research-grade version of the move as well. Because the naming history is documented in Fitzgerald's correspondence, a student writing at length can treat the title as a case study in composition, showing how the finished meaning emerged through revision and negotiation rather than arriving whole. This connects the cover to the larger story of how the book was made, from the drafting through the [1925 publication](/2027/03/08/gatsby-1925-publication/) that sent it into a world not yet ready for it. A worked opening shows how compact the move can be. A student might write: Fitzgerald almost called this novel Trimalchio in West Egg, a name that would have fixed Gatsby as a vulgar upstart and told the reader to laugh at him before the story began. By choosing The Great Gatsby instead, Fitzgerald swapped a verdict for a question, since the word great can honor Gatsby's hope or mock his performance and the novel never decides which. That refusal to decide is the book's central technique, and it begins on the cover. Three sentences, and the essay has already staked a real claim, grounded it in a documented fact, and named the interpretive stakes. That is genuine scholarship, built on primary evidence, and it distinguishes a writer who has thought about the book from one who has merely read about it. The alternate names reward attention at every scale, from a snappy essay opening to a chapter of archival argument, because in every case they convert the paratext into a lever on the text.

### How should a student use the alternate titles in an essay?

Open by naming what Fitzgerald almost used and what it would have imposed, then argue that the final name does something the alternative could not. Use Trimalchio to show the verdict the book avoids, use the epigraph titles to show the performance it dramatizes, and use the word great to show the irony it holds open. Keep every claim tied to a scene.

The discipline is to make the fact argue. A title dropped into an essay as trivia earns nothing, but a title set against the finished name to reveal a choice earns a paragraph of genuine analysis. The best student essays treat the alternate names the way the novel treats the epigraph, as a frame that deepens the reading rather than a label that settles it. Done well, the move signals to an examiner that the writer understands the book as a made thing, shaped by decisions, rather than as a fixed object that fell from the sky already titled.

## Written abroad: naming the American novel from a distance

There is a further irony worth naming, one the discarded titles help expose. The most American of novels, the book that ends by folding one man's dream into the whole nation's, was written largely abroad, on the French Riviera, among the wealthy American expatriates whose careless glamour Fitzgerald was studying at close range. He named it, and renamed it, and tried to rename it again, from that distance. The national candidate, Under the Red, White, and Blue, was in a sense a homesick name, a way of pinning the flag to a book composed in exile from the country it anatomizes, and its abstraction may owe something to the vantage of the expatriate who sees his homeland as a color scheme rather than a set of streets. The plain name he settled on is more grounded precisely because it names a man he could imagine rather than a country he was viewing from across an ocean.

The distance sharpened the very quality the final name preserves. Watching rich Americans abroad, Fitzgerald could see both their allure and their emptiness at once, hold admiration and judgment in a single gaze, and that double vision is exactly what the word great encodes. A writer fully inside the world he described might have tipped into satire or into celebration; the expatriate, half in and half out, could sustain the tension that the finished cover holds. The alternate titles that resolve the tension, whether toward mockery or toward patriotism, read almost like moments when Fitzgerald lost his footing and grabbed for a simpler frame. The name that survived is the one that keeps the expatriate's doubled sight, admiring and diagnosing in the same breath, and refuses to come down on either side.

To see the naming this way is to understand it as inseparable from the conditions of composition rather than as a late cosmetic decision. The book's meaning, its geography, its cast, and its cover were all being shaped in the same foreign rooms, under the same pressures of money, marriage, and ambition, and the wavering over the name is of a piece with the labor of the whole. A reader who traces the alternate names back to the desk where they were weighed sees the title not as a stamp applied at the end but as the last and most public of the thousand choices that made the novel, the one choice printed large enough for everyone to read.

## Closing verdict

The title Fitzgerald almost used is a doorway into how the novel thinks. For most of its making the book was Trimalchio in West Egg, a satire announced in advance, and at the very end its author reached for Under the Red, White, and Blue, a national verdict announced in advance. Between those poles lay the epigraph names, playful and ironic, and the plain geographic one, and every last option shared a single flaw. Each told the reader what to think before the reader had met Gatsby. The name that won does the opposite. It calls a bootlegger great and then spends nine chapters making the reader decide whether the word is a tribute or a taunt, and it never lets the reader off the hook.

That is the title that chose irony over allusion, and the rejected names are how we know the choice was deliberate. Fitzgerald did not stumble onto ambiguity. He tried every legible frame, found each of them too settled for the book he had written, and accepted, with a reluctance that outlasted the presses, the one frame that keeps the question open. Read the discarded list and you cannot go back to treating the cover as a neutral label. You see it as the novel's first sentence, the first place Fitzgerald stages the argument he sustains to the last line, and you carry into the book a sharper sense of how much a name can do. The greatness of the title is that it withholds its own meaning and hands the work of judgment to you, which is exactly what the greatest of the alternate titles could never have done.

Keep the list of what he almost used close at hand as you read, because it is the most portable proof of the novel's method. Trimalchio in West Egg, On the Road to West Egg, The Gold-Hatted Gatsby, The High-Bouncing Lover, Under the Red, White, and Blue: five settled verdicts, each a fine name for a lesser book, and none of them able to do the one thing the survivor does, which is to refuse to tell you what to feel about the man on the cover. Fitzgerald spent two years and a fistful of rejected names learning that the strongest frame was the one that framed a question, and the reader who learns the same lesson from the same list reads the whole novel as he finally wrote it, great and ironic and unresolved in a single breath.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What titles did Fitzgerald almost use?

Fitzgerald considered a cluster of names before settling on The Great Gatsby. The one he lived with longest was Trimalchio, often expanded to Trimalchio in West Egg, after the vulgar new-rich host in Petronius. He also weighed On the Road to West Egg, a journey-and-place name, and two phrases lifted from the novel's own epigraph, The Gold-Hatted Gatsby and The High-Bouncing Lover. At the very end, with the book already set in type, he tried to switch to Under the Red, White, and Blue. Each name imagined a slightly different novel, from classical satire to national verdict, and the fact that he cycled through so many shows how unsettled the choice remained until deadline and editorial pressure closed it.

### Q: What was the Trimalchio in West Egg title?

Trimalchio in West Egg was Fitzgerald's preferred working name for most of the composition, joining a classical allusion to the novel's invented Long Island setting. Trimalchio is a character in Petronius's Satyricon, a freed slave grown rich who throws lavish, tasteless banquets to display his status, and Fitzgerald saw Gatsby's parties in the same light, as spectacle staged by an outsider. West Egg locates that spectacle in the new-money enclave Gatsby inhabits. The name would have told the reader in advance that the book satirizes a vulgar upstart, which is why Fitzgerald eventually let it go: the finished novel insists on Gatsby's greatness as well as his absurdity, and the Trimalchio frame would have foreclosed the sympathy the ending demands. The allusion survives once inside the text, in the seventh chapter.

### Q: What did the final title gain over the alternatives?

The final name gained irony and openness. Every alternative delivered a verdict before the story began: Trimalchio pronounced Gatsby a vulgar fool, the epigraph names pronounced him a comic performer, and Under the Red, White, and Blue pronounced the book a statement on America. The Great Gatsby pronounces nothing. The word great can be read as sincere praise for Gatsby's capacity to hope or as the hollow superlative of the carnival, and the novel sustains both at once without resolving them. That doubleness is the book's deepest effect, and only this name preserves it. The alternatives were all legible, and legibility was the flaw, because a title that answers the central question closes the interpretive space the novel needs to keep open across all nine chapters.

### Q: What would the rejected titles have lost?

Each rejected name would have surrendered the novel's central ambiguity. Trimalchio in West Egg would have lost the sympathy the ending earns, telling the reader to despise Gatsby before meeting him. On the Road to West Egg would have lost the protagonist himself, foregrounding geography over the man. The Gold-Hatted Gatsby and The High-Bouncing Lover would have lost the tragic register, flattening Gatsby into a clown by announcing his performance as comedy. Under the Red, White, and Blue would have lost the private ache of one man's longing, folding it into a national thesis stated too early to be earned. In every case the loss is the same in kind: a settled meaning imposed on a book whose power depends on refusing to settle. The final name alone keeps every reading available.

### Q: Was the final title inevitable?

No, and treating it as inevitable is the most common error readers make about it. Fitzgerald preferred Trimalchio for most of the writing, accepted The Great Gatsby with reluctance under pressure from his editor and his wife, and then tried to change it to Under the Red, White, and Blue after the book was already in production. The name we canonize won partly because a printing schedule ran out before Fitzgerald's last-minute switch could take effect. A book known by one name for a century feels welded to it, but the manuscript and correspondence record a genuine history of doubt. Recognizing that the choice was contingent, one option among several that happened to prevail, is what allows a reader to ask the more valuable question of what the chosen name actually accomplishes.

### Q: How does the final title both elevate and question Gatsby?

The name elevates through its grammar and questions through its irony. The definite article and the adjective together promise significance: this is the Gatsby, the definitive one, a life worth a whole book, and that confidence honors a man the world would call small. At the same time the word great is unstable when applied to a bootlegger who invents his own past and takes the blame for a killing he did not commit. It can ring as sincere tribute to his outsized hope or as the flat American idiom of the sideshow, the cheap superlative attached to everything. The novel keeps both meanings live to the final page, so the reader must weigh Gatsby's foolishness against his grandeur and decide whether the elevation is earned, which is the judgment the book refuses to make for them.

### Q: Who is Trimalchio and why did Fitzgerald borrow the name?

Trimalchio is a figure in the Satyricon, a Roman work attributed to Petronius, a freed slave who becomes fabulously rich and hosts grotesque, ostentatious feasts to advertise his new status. Fitzgerald borrowed the name because Gatsby follows the same pattern, a self-made outsider buying belonging through spectacle, and the allusion cast the mansion's parties as vulgar theater. The problem is that the allusion presumes a classically trained reader and delivers a verdict of contempt before the story earns anything, and Fitzgerald's finished novel wants to complicate that verdict rather than confirm it. He kept the allusion for a single sentence in the seventh chapter, where Nick notes that Gatsby's career as Trimalchio was over, using the discarded name to mark the end of the performance instead of defining the entire book.

### Q: What is Under the Red, White, and Blue?

Under the Red, White, and Blue was the name Fitzgerald wanted at the very last moment, after he had accepted The Great Gatsby and the book was already set in type. The phrase folds the novel into the colors of the American flag, turning the private story of one man's longing into an explicit statement about the nation. Fitzgerald wired Perkins to make the change, but the production schedule had advanced too far and the switch never happened. The name would have made the book a national verdict announced on the cover, sacrificing the slow, earned resonance the finished novel builds through Nick's closing meditation. It is the grandest and least specific of all the candidates, and Fitzgerald's desire for it so late shows that his own sense of the book remained unsettled right up to publication.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald like the title The Great Gatsby?

Fitzgerald accepted The Great Gatsby without loving it. He preferred Trimalchio for most of the composition and agreed to the name we know under pressure from Maxwell Perkins and from Zelda, both of whom favored it. His reluctance was real and durable, strong enough that he attempted a last-minute change to Under the Red, White, and Blue when the book was already in production. So the writer who gave us one of the most admired titles in American literature did not himself regard it as the obvious or the best choice at the time. This is a useful corrective, because it separates the title's later reputation from its author's actual feelings and reminds us that the greatness of the name is something the book earned in reception, not something Fitzgerald confidently declared.

### Q: How late did Fitzgerald try to change the title?

Extremely late, after the novel was already in proof and set in type. Fitzgerald wired Perkins asking to rename the book Under the Red, White, and Blue, but by that point the production process had moved too far to reverse economically, and the novel appeared as The Great Gatsby. The lateness is significant because it reveals that Fitzgerald never experienced the final name as settled. A writer who fixes a title early treats it as natural, while one still trying to change it as the presses warm up treats it as one contingent option among several. Fitzgerald belonged firmly to the second group, and the name we canonize won as much by the pressure of a deadline as by his conviction, an accident of timing that produced a better result than the change he was reaching for.

### Q: What does the word great in the title actually do?

The word great performs two opposite jobs at once and never chooses between them. Read sincerely, it honors Gatsby's enormous capacity for hope and his refusal to accept an ordinary life, treating his longing as a genuine kind of largeness. Read ironically, it echoes the empty American idiom of the advertisement and the sideshow, the superlative attached to anything for sale, and it mocks a man whose greatness is a performance. The novel refuses to settle which reading is right, keeping both active from the cover to the final sentence. That is why the word outperforms every alternative Fitzgerald considered. Most titles can praise a hero or mock a fraud, but very few can do both in one word and hand the decision to the reader, and Fitzgerald arrived at this one by exhausting the names that did only one job.

### Q: How does the gold-hatted lover epigraph connect to the discarded titles?

The epigraph that opens the novel supplied two of the abandoned names directly. Its four lines, signed with Fitzgerald's invented poet Thomas Parke d'Invilliers, describe wearing the gold hat and bouncing high to win the beloved, and the phrases gold-hatted and high-bouncing lover became the candidate titles The Gold-Hatted Gatsby and The High-Bouncing Lover. Fitzgerald kept the poem but rejected the names it might have furnished, demoting those images from a promise on the cover to a hint on the first page. The choice preserved their irony while withholding the verdict, letting the reader feel the strain and performance the metaphors describe through the story rather than being told about it in advance. The epigraph therefore functions as a museum of the road not taken, keeping the discarded titles alive inside the finished book.

### Q: Why did Maxwell Perkins prefer The Great Gatsby?

Maxwell Perkins, Fitzgerald's editor at Scribner's, favored The Great Gatsby over the allusive and abstract alternatives because it was clear, marketable, and centered squarely on the character readers would remember. The classical Trimalchio names risked baffling ordinary buyers, and the national one was vague, while The Great Gatsby named the man and promised significance in three plain words. Perkins's judgment, joined by Zelda's, was decisive in overcoming Fitzgerald's own preference for Trimalchio. The episode belongs to the larger record of their collaboration, in which Perkins repeatedly steadied the book through its final shaping. It is a reminder that the title we treat as a stroke of solitary genius was in fact a negotiated outcome, produced by an editor's commercial and literary instinct working against an author's more esoteric attachment.

### Q: What is the difference between the alternate titles and the Trimalchio draft?

The alternate titles are the various names Fitzgerald weighed for the finished novel, a question about the cover. The Trimalchio draft is an earlier version of the manuscript itself, materially different from the published book in scene and structure, a question about the contents. They are frequently confused because Trimalchio sits in both stories: Fitzgerald favored it as a working name and the early typescript carries that preference. But a student should keep them distinct. Saying Fitzgerald almost called the book Trimalchio is accurate, while saying the Trimalchio draft is just an alternate name for The Great Gatsby is wrong, because the draft differs from the published novel in substance. One is paratext, the wording on the outside, and the other is text, the words inside, and precise analysis depends on knowing which is meant.

### Q: Does the title reference to Trimalchio survive inside the finished novel?

Yes, once, and deliberately. In the seventh chapter, at the moment when Gatsby stops throwing his parties to pursue Daisy directly, Nick observes that his career as Trimalchio was over. That single sentence is the fossil of the discarded name, preserved at the exact point where the spectacle ends. Fitzgerald let the reader meet the allusion he had rejected for the cover, but he placed it so that it marks the close of the performance rather than defining the whole book. It is a precise piece of craft: the name that would have pre-judged Gatsby as a vulgar host instead becomes an internal marker for the end of that hosting. A reader who knows the naming history hears the whole abandoned title compressed into that one line.

### Q: Why does the plain surname Gatsby carry so much weight?

The surname carries weight because the title frames it with a definite article and an adjective that together insist on significance. The Great Gatsby is not a Gatsby among many but the Gatsby, singular and definitive, and great promises that this life matters enough to fill a book. That grammatical confidence is itself part of the irony, because the man the sentence elevates is in most worldly respects a failure: his fortune is criminal, his past is invented, and his dream collapses. The novel spends nine chapters testing whether the confidence of its own name is warranted. Set against the alternatives, which named a Roman satirist, a stretch of geography, or a flag, the choice to build everything on one plain surname foregrounds the human being over every abstraction, which is the novel's fundamental commitment.

### Q: What did Fitzgerald mean by wanting a title with no allusion?

The pattern of Fitzgerald's rejected names suggests a growing wish to escape borrowed frames. Trimalchio quoted Petronius, the epigraph titles quoted his own verse, and Under the Red, White, and Blue quoted the flag, so each reached outside the novel for its authority. The Great Gatsby borrows nothing. It is the only candidate that stands on the book's own materials, the character and an adjective, without leaning on a source the reader must recognize. Whether or not Fitzgerald articulated this as a principle, the outcome is a name that is self-sufficient, requiring no classical education or national symbolism to work. That independence is part of its durability, because a title that depends on an allusion ages with the allusion, while one built from the novel's own name and a single loaded word renews its question for every new reader.
