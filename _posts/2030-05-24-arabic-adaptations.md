Romeo and Juliet arrived in Arabic before the Arab world had a word for what it was doing with the play, and that early arrival is the key to the whole reception. The verdict of this article is that the Arabic life of the tragedy is not a footnote to European Shakespeare but a second life with its own chronology, its own canon, and its own verdicts: translation made the play classical Arabic literature, relocation made it political evidence, and the standing comparison with Layla and Majnun both dignified it and distorted it. The play mattered most in Arabic where it stopped being an import and became a local instrument, and the parallel with Layla and Majnun is most useful when it is read as a measure of distance rather than as a declaration of identity. Everything that follows tests that verdict against the documented record: the printed versions of 1898 and 1901, the blank-verse experiment of the 1930s, the classroom standard of the late twentieth century, and three stagings in Jerusalem, Beirut, and Baghdad that each rewrote the feud in a different local grammar.

The play itself supplies the machinery that Arab adapters kept returning to, and it is worth stating that machinery plainly before following it across languages. The Prologue opens with a civic formula: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue, 1 to 3). The feud is symmetrical, public, and inherited. The lovers meet at a masked feast and exchange a sonnet, a piece of verse built so that the two voices complete a single fourteen-line form. Juliet asks the question that organizes the whole tragedy: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43 to 44). The answer the play gives is that a name is everything in Verona, because the name is the feud, and the feud is the law of the city. The deaths end the play not with a wedding but with an accounting, and the Prince closes it with a verdict on the city rather than a blessing on the couple: "A glooming peace this morning with it brings; / The sun, for sorrow, will not show his head" (5.3.304 to 305). Arab adapters kept all of that machinery and changed the city. That is the single most important sentence in the Arabic reception, and every section below is a variation on it.

## How Romeo and Juliet Reached the Arabic Stage

Shakespeare reached Arabic through theatre, not through the study, and through Beirut and Cairo rather than through London. The Arabic theatre itself was a nineteenth-century creation, built in the Levant by men who had seen European companies and wanted a local equivalent. Marun al-Naqqash staged his first Arabic play in Beirut in 1847, working from Moliere rather than from Shakespeare, and his nephew Salim al-Naqqash carried a troupe to Egypt in 1876, where the Khedive's patronage and the commercial stage of Alexandria and Cairo gave it a paying public. The first Arabic Shakespeare belongs to that commercial world, and its habits explain much of what followed: the troupes were businesses, the audiences were mixed and middle class, and a play survived if it sold. Translation in that world was not a scholarly act. It was a stage act, and the word the period used for it was Arabization, ta'rib, a term that promised a text made Arabic in idiom, setting, and sensibility, not a text carried across languages with its joints intact. The distinction between translation and Arabization is the axis on which the whole early history turns, and the two first Arabic versions of the tragedy sit on opposite sides of it.

The intermediary language mattered as much as the destination language. The earliest Arabic Shakespeare characteristically arrived through French versions, because French was the European language of the Levantine and Egyptian commercial theatre, and because the French adaptations of the period already did some of the work of domestication for the translator. A French source meant that a play could arrive with its edges already smoothed, its verse already tamed, and its characters already recognizable to an audience that knew French melodrama better than English blank verse. Khalil Mutran's celebrated Othello of 1912, the most famous Arabic Shakespeare translation of its generation, rested on Georges Duval's French text rather than on the English original, and the point is worth stating plainly because it corrects the assumption that the great names of the early tradition worked from English. The record shows a chain of mediations, French to Arabic, commercial stage to literary canon, and the tragedy of Romeo and Juliet passed through every link of that chain. To write its Arabic history as though a single authoritative translation existed is to misdescribe the field. There were many versions, of different kinds, serving different masters, and their coexistence is the history.

## The First Arabic Versions: Martyrs of Love and the Arabization Trade

The documentary record begins with a title that is itself an interpretation. Najib al-Haddad's Arabic version of the tragedy, titled Martyrs of Love, was staged in Cairo in 1891, a decade before the text was published in 1901. The title tells the reader what kind of play the adapters thought they were selling: not a tragedy of two houses, but a martyrology of love, in which the lovers die as witnesses to a feeling rather than as casualties of a civic war. That framing was the commercial Arabization in miniature. The play was made legible to a Cairo audience by being renamed in the idiom of popular romance, and the renaming worked, because the play was performed regularly in Egypt from that staging onward. A second version followed quickly, by Tanyus 'Abdu, printed in 1898, and the two versions together mark the tragedy's entry into Arabic as a stage commodity rather than as a literary monument. The chronology is precise and worth keeping straight: al-Haddad on the stage in 1891, 'Abdu in print in 1898, al-Haddad in print in 1901. Three dates, two men, and a play that was already a repertoire staple before it was a book.

### Q: Was the play among the first Shakespeare works to reach Arabic?

Yes, the tragedy stood among the first Shakespeare plays adapted and translated into Arabic in the late nineteenth century, alongside Hamlet, and both entered through the same commercial channels, French intermediaries, and troupes. The priority matters because the play was Arabicized before Arabic Shakespeare had a literary standard to judge it by.

The commercial stage that produced these versions had its own poetics, and the scholarship on it is blunt about the method. 'Abdu was an entrepreneur of translation, a man whose versions were written to be performed and sold, and his practice included the liberties the box office rewarded: compression, embellishment, and a willingness to adjust an ending to the taste of the house. The later generation of literary translators treated that practice as a scandal, and the tension between the two generations became the defining argument of Arabic Shakespeare for half a century. But the commercial versions did the work that the literary versions inherited. They put the lovers' names into Arabic circulation, they established the story as a known quantity for audiences who had never read a line of English verse, and they gave the play a local title, Martyrs of Love, that framed it as romance before the canon reframed it as tragedy. The scholarly habit of dismissing the early Arabizations as corruptions misses the mechanism: the corruption was the introduction. A play that arrives only in perfect translation arrives late, and this play arrived early precisely because it arrived loosely.

The critical question for the early period is therefore not whether the versions were faithful, because they were not, but what their unfaithfulness bought. What it bought was speed, range, and a public. By the time the literary translators began their work in the 1910s and 1920s, the tragedy already belonged to the Arabic stage in the way a folk story belongs to its tellers, and the new translators were not introducing a stranger but reclaiming a familiar figure. That is why the history of the Arabic Romeo and Juliet reads so differently from the history of the Arabic Hamlet, the play that dominates the scholarship. Hamlet became the Arab play of power and delay, the vehicle for political allegory in Egypt, Syria, and Iraq, and Margaret Litvin's landmark study follows that career in detail. Romeo and Juliet took the other road. It became the play of love against division, the title that a company reached for when it wanted to say that a local hatred had made young people its victims. The two careers are complementary, and the tragedy's Arabic career is best understood as the sentimental counterpart to Hamlet's political one. Where Hamlet asked who should rule, Romeo and Juliet asked who was allowed to love, and the second question turned out to be the more portable one.

## Khalil Mutran and the Translation That Never Was

### Q: Did Khalil Mutran translate Romeo and Juliet?

No, Mutran never translated the tragedy, and the correction matters because popular summaries hand him the whole of early Arabic Shakespeare. His versions were Othello, Hamlet, Macbeth, and The Merchant of Venice, the four plays the catalogue credits to him, and his Othello of 1912, rendered from Duval's French, became the celebrated standard. Verona's tragedy is absent from his list.

Mutran's importance to this article is therefore negative in the most productive sense: he set the standard that the tragedy's translators had to answer, without ever touching the play himself. His version of Othello, titled Utayl, was celebrated as the great Arabic Shakespeare translation of its generation, and his idiom, an elevated literary Arabic prose, became the language that the Egyptian stage accepted as the sound of Shakespeare. When the Higher Institute for Dramatic Arts opened, aspiring actors learned Mutran's prose by heart, and the language of the stage became, for a generation, the language of Mutran's Shakespeare. The tragedy's translators inherited that standard and had to decide whether to meet it or to break it. Ali Ahmed Bakathir broke it deliberately, and Mohamed Enani spent a career negotiating with it, and neither negotiation is intelligible without Mutran standing just offstage, the standard-bearer of a play he never translated.

The absence also corrects a lazy pattern in reception history, the habit of assigning every early Arabic Shakespeare achievement to the single most famous name. Mutran's fame has a gravitational pull, and lesser-known translators have been pulled into his orbit by summaries that credit him with the whole canon. The scholarly catalogue resists that pull. It assigns the tragedy's early versions to al-Haddad and 'Abdu, its verse experiment to Bakathir, and its modern standard to Enani, and it leaves Mutran with the four plays he actually made. A reception history that respects its own documents keeps the names separate, because the separation is the story: the tragedy reached Arabic through commercial adapters and experimental poets, not through the grand literary translator, and its Arabic life was therefore more various, more theatrical, and less respectable than the canon that later claimed it. The play's Arabic translators were, from the first, men working at the edges of literary prestige, and the play stayed at those edges for a long time.

There is a second reason the Mutran correction matters, and it concerns the source language. Mutran worked from French, and his celebrated translations were therefore second-hand in a literal sense, yet they became the standard of fidelity for the next generation. That paradox, a translation from a translation becoming the measure of faithfulness, tells the reader something essential about what fidelity meant in the early Arabic field. It meant elevation of register, dignity of diction, and suitability for the national stage, not closeness to the English line. The commercial versions of 'Abdu were judged not against Shakespeare's text but against Mutran's idiom, and the judgment was about class as much as accuracy. Sameh Hanna's sociology of the translation field makes exactly this point, and it is taken up in the critics' section below: the literary translators won their authority by changing the rules of the game, not by playing it better. Mutran's absence from the tragedy's record is thus doubly instructive. It shows both that the play's translators were not the grand names, and that the grand names themselves were translators from French whose authority rested on register rather than on source fidelity.

## Bakathir, Enani, and the Long Search for an Arabic Verse

If the early period gave the tragedy a stage life, the middle period gave it a literary argument, and the argument was about verse. Ali Ahmed Bakathir, the Yemeni-born Egyptian playwright, translated the tragedy in blank verse, a version made in 1936 and published in 1942, and the experiment was a deliberate provocation. Arabic poetry had its own metres and its own prestige system, and the claim that Shakespeare's unrhymed iambic line could be carried into Arabic was a claim about the capacity of the Arabic language itself. Bakathir's version was designed to demonstrate that Arabic could sustain Shakespeare's blank verse without breaking, and the demonstration mattered to a generation of writers who were testing whether Arabic literature could absorb European forms without surrendering its own. The version entered the canon that the Egyptian stage and school system carried into the 1950s, when the standard repertoire of Arabic Shakespeare was reckoned to include Hamlet, Othello, and Macbeth, The Merchant of Venice in Mutran's version, Julius Caesar in Muhammad Hamdi's version, and the tragedy in Bakathir's blank verse. That list is a snapshot of a canon under construction, and the tragedy's place in it, in verse where its neighbours stood in prose, is a mark of Bakathir's ambition.

Bakathir's later Shakespeare work shows the other face of the Arabic reception, the frankly political adaptation, and it must be reported plainly because it conditions everything said below about the play's political uses. His adaptation of The Merchant of Venice, titled The New Shylock, bent the play into a statement about the Arab-Israeli conflict, and the scholarship records that it drew on the stereotypical notions traditionally associated with Jews in English literature. The point is not to condemn a dead playwright in passing but to establish that the Arabic Shakespeare tradition, from an early date, treated the plays as material for political argument, and that the argument was not always elevated. The tradition's political edge cut in several directions, and the tragedy of Romeo and Juliet inherited that edge when later adapters relocated its feud. Bakathir's own tragedy translation, by contrast, was a literary rather than a political act, and the distinction between the two Bakathirs, the verse translator and the political adapter, is the distinction between the two careers of Arabic Shakespeare in general. The same writers did both, and the same plays served both purposes.

The search for an adequate Arabic idiom continued after Bakathir, and its most persistent worker was Mohamed Enani of Cairo University. Enani translated the tragedy twice, a fact that is itself the argument. His first version, dated 1965 in Shetawi's catalogue, was in prose, and he found it unsatisfying, as he had found his prose Midsummer Night's Dream unsatisfying, and he retranslated the tragedy in a mixture of prose and verse. Enani's own testimonial on translating Shakespeare describes the problem as one of language and tone, and his solution, a mixed idiom that let verse carry the poetry and prose carry the plain speech, became, in the judgment of the scholarship on his work, the contemporary language of Arabic Shakespeare translation. The claim is large, and it rests on a simple observation: Enani's idiom is the one that later translators and teachers actually used, because it solved the problem that Mutran's elevated prose and Bakathir's blank verse had each left unsolved. Mutran's prose was dignified but antiquated, and Bakathir's verse was ambitious but isolated, and Enani's mixture was usable. The history of the Arabic tragedy is, at this level, a history of usable idioms, and Enani's is the one that lasted.

Enani's career also illustrates the institutional turn in the reception. The 1950s saw Taha Hussein's project, under the aegis of the Arab League, to translate the whole of Shakespeare into Arabic prose, and the project changed the status of translation from a commercial or personal act into a national cultural enterprise. The tragedy's later versions belong to that institutional world: Mu'nis Taha Husayn's version of 1960, Enani's of 1965, Ghazi Gamal's of 1978, and three anonymous translations between 1959 and 1990, all catalogued by Shetawi. The anonymous versions are a useful check on the romance of the named translator, because they show a field in which the play circulated in versions whose makers were not recorded, copied, staged, and taught without an author to venerate. A reception history that only follows famous names misses the anonymous circulation that kept the play alive between the landmarks, and the catalogue's three anonymous versions are a reminder that the tragedy's Arabic life was wider than its bibliography.

## Layla and Majnun: The Parallel That Measures Distance

### Q: Is the Layla and Majnun story older than the play?

Yes, the legend is older than the play by nine centuries in its anecdotal form, since its lovers belong to seventh-century Arabia and its episodes were gathered in tenth-century collections, and older by four centuries in its fixed literary form, since Nizami composed his Persian poem in 1188. The chronology reverses the usual direction of influence claims in the comparison.

Every Arabic account of the tragedy eventually arrives at Layla and Majnun, and the arrival is not decorative. Arab readers did not meet the play in a vacuum; they met it with a native story of thwarted love already installed at the centre of their literary tradition, a story told wherever Arabic and Persian poetry travelled, and the meeting shaped both readings. The legend, in outline, is quickly told. Qays, a poet of the Banu Amir tribe in seventh-century Arabia, loved his kinswoman Layla and proclaimed the love in verse so openly that the proclamation itself ruined his suit, because a love announced in public became a scandal that no father could bless. Her family refused him and married her to another man. Qays lost his reason in the world's eyes and gained his name from the loss: Majnun, the madman, in the Arabic idiom a man possessed by jinn. He withdrew to the wilderness, lived among animals, composed verses to an absent beloved, and was found dead at her grave after her death, with verses cut into a rock nearby. The episodes circulated for centuries as anecdotes, short and loosely connected reports gathered in Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani's great tenth-century anthology, the Book of Songs, and in Ibn Qutaybah's work on poets, before the Persian poet Nizami Ganjavi fixed them in 1188 as a single long narrative poem, the third book of his Khamsa. Nizami's fixing was not the legend's end but its launch into a second, Persianate career. Later poets across the Persian, Turkish, and Urdu literary worlds rewrote the poem in homage and rivalry, Jami in the fifteenth century and Fuzuli in the sixteenth among the most celebrated, and the lovers became a shared property of Islamic literatures from Anatolia to India, their story carried in courtly epics, folk performance, and eventually opera and film. The itinerary matters for the comparison this article is grading. When Arab readers set the play beside Layla and Majnun, they were not setting a world text beside a local curiosity; they were setting one travelling classic beside another, older one whose route through languages and empires resembled the play's own later route in reverse. Each story had survived by being rewritten, and each had accumulated, along the way, a halo of retellings that made the original hard to see. The parallel is between two reception histories as much as between two plots, and that is the level at which it holds up best.

The differences begin at the root, with the kind of love each story imagines. Layla and Majnun belongs to the Udhri tradition of Arabian love, named for the Banu Udhra tribe proverbial for lovers who loved chastely and died of it, and its love is defined by non-arrival. The lovers never marry, never meet as equals in dialogue, and barely meet at all after the separation; the love is a condition of the lover, not a relationship between two people. Majnun's beloved becomes an idea that his verses refine, and the tradition itself recognized the refinement, because Sufi interpreters took Majnun over as a model of annihilating devotion, the lover consumed by a love whose object points beyond itself. In the Sufi register, Layla is a veil for the divine, and Majnun's madness is a spiritual achievement. Nothing in the play works that way. The Verona lovers are neither chaste nor veiled; they marry within a day of meeting, consummate the marriage, and conduct their love as a partnership of plans, messages, and decisions. When Juliet asks what a name is worth, she is reasoning, and the play's love is unintelligible without her reasoning. The Udhri lover's love deepens as the beloved recedes; the play's love intensifies as the beloved comes closer. The two traditions even disagree about which direction love travels.

The obstacles differ just as sharply, and the difference decides what each story can be used to say. In the play, the obstacle is a system: two households, equal in dignity, locked in an inherited public violence, with a city government trying and failing to hold them apart, so that the lovers die as casualties of a civic order and the city is left to count the cost. Readers who want the full anatomy of that system in the play can follow [the account of why the Capulets and Montagues hate each other](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/), which traces the grudge from the Prologue's civic formula to the Prince's final accounting. In the legend, the obstacle is a household decision and a code of honour: one father's refusal, one arranged marriage, one reputation destroyed by publicity. There is no second house of equal dignity, no feud, no prince, and no civic reckoning at the end. Majnun's world is not indicted by his death; his tribe grieves and moves on, and the desert that receives him asks no questions. The play's ending is a tribunal. The legend's ending is a grave in the wilderness with verses on a rock. A story that ends in a tribunal can be used to argue about a society; a story that ends at a grave can only be used to argue about love itself, and the Arabic reception of the play gains its political reach precisely from machinery the native legend does not possess.

The legend also possessed what the play had to earn: a canonical Arabic stage version of its own, and the two canons grew up side by side. Ahmad Shawqi, the Egyptian poet laureate known to his public as the Prince of Poets, fixed the legend for the Arabic theatre in his verse drama Majnun Layla of 1931, one of the poetic plays with which he set out to give Arabic a classical repertoire of its own. The date deserves emphasis. Shawqi's Majnun Layla appeared in the same decade in which Bakathir was rendering the tragedy into Arabic blank verse, and the coincidence defines the parallel's real function in the period. Arabic literary culture was not choosing between a native story and a foreign one; it was canonizing both at once, in verse, as twin pillars of a modern repertoire, one inherited and one imported. A Cairo audience of the 1930s could meet Majnun in Shawqi's lines and the Verona lovers in Bakathir's, and the comparison between them was not a scholar's conceit but a playgoer's experience, renewed every season. The parallel lived in the repertoire before it lived in the criticism, which is why it has never been dislodged by the criticism's objections since. Generations of Arab spectators have seen both stories staged, and a comparison anchored in shared spectatorship survives any number of structural refutations, because it records a fact about audiences rather than a claim about texts.

The women of the two stories differ in a way that Arab adapters felt keenly, because it made the imported play do work the native legend could not. Juliet speaks. She speaks the balcony scene's arguments, proposes the marriage, rebukes delay, drinks the potion with a soliloquy that inventories her fears, and dies by her own hand on her own decision. Layla, in the anecdotes and largely in Nizami, is spoken about. Her love is reported, her suffering is described, her death is narrated, but her words are few, and her interior life reaches the reader through the lover's verses rather than through her own. The asymmetry is not a failure of the legend; it is the legend's design, in which the beloved must stay an icon for the lover's madness to keep its purity. But an Arab stage that wanted a heroine who argued had to import one, and the import helps explain why the play, rather than a staging of the native legend, kept being chosen by companies with something to argue. The comparison therefore cuts both ways. The play gained dignity in Arabic by standing beside a classical legend, and it gained employment by supplying what the legend withheld: a heroine with a voice and a city with a verdict.

Byron's famous tag for the legend, the Romeo and Juliet of the East, completes the picture by getting its direction wrong, and the error is instructive. The label measures the older story by the newer one, Verona supplying the standard and Arabia the instance, although the Arab anecdotes predate the play by some nine hundred years and Nizami's poem predates it by four hundred. A label that reverses nine centuries is not a comparison but a claim of ownership, and Arab writers who used the parallel were often quietly reversing it back, implying that Verona had produced a local instance of a story Arabia had long possessed. The parallel's real work in the reception is that negotiation over seniority, conducted under the cover of a compliment. Nor is the manoeuvre unique to the Arab case: China performs the same operation when it reads the imported play beside the Butterfly Lovers, and [the play's career in Chinese theatre](/2030/05/17/romeo-and-juliet-chinese-theatre/) shows the same pairing of an incoming tragedy with an indigenous legend of parted lovers, with the same negotiation underneath. The pattern is global: wherever the play travelled, a native doomed-love story was waiting, and the meeting of the two became the local criticism's first subject. The Arab case is the best documented of those meetings, and its verdict, argued here, is that the parallel is real as reception and limited as analysis. It explains how Arab audiences received the play. It does not explain the play, and adapters who leaned on it hardest produced the weakest versions, because they translated the play into the legend's terms and lost the tribunal at the end.


## Jerusalem and Ramallah: One Play, Two Languages

The most consequential Arabic staging of the tragedy was not, strictly, a translation at all, because half of it was in Hebrew. In 1994 the Khan Theatre of Jerusalem and the Al-Kasaba Theatre of Ramallah mounted a joint production in which the two households were divided by language and nation: the Montagues were played by Palestinian and Israeli-Arab actors speaking Arabic, directed by Fuad Awad of Al-Kasaba, and the Capulets by Israeli actors speaking Hebrew, under Eran Baniel of the Khan, with the shared scenes directed by both men. Romeo was played by Khalifa Natour, a Palestinian actor, and Juliet by Orna Katz, an Israeli actress, and Mohammed Bakri played Mercutio. The production had been approved on 13 September 1993, the day of the Oslo handshake in Washington, and it opened in Jerusalem in 1994 in a converted hall behind the railway station, before travelling to European stages including the festival at Lille, one of its sponsors. George Ibrahim, Al-Kasaba's general director, stood behind the Palestinian side of the enterprise. On paper, the design reads like a diagram of the peace process itself: two peoples, two languages, one stage, one pair of lovers crossing the line. The record of what happened inside that diagram is more interesting than the diagram.

The first thing the design did was to distribute understanding unevenly, and the unevenness was the meaning. A Hebrew-speaking spectator followed the Capulets in full and met the Montagues through gesture, tone, and the fragments of Arabic that Jerusalem life supplies; an Arabic-speaking spectator received the mirror image. The lovers' scenes, directed jointly, were the only fully shared territory in the performance, and the audience could hear the play's argument in acoustic form: two households intelligible to themselves and half-strange to each other, with comprehension complete only where the young people met. No relocation of the feud in the Arab record states its thesis so economically, because no other production made the audience live inside the feud's epistemology, knowing one house from within and the other from across a barrier of language. Freddie Rokem, reviewing the production for the Palestine-Israel Journal, resisted the design's self-congratulation, and his resistance is the necessary counterweight. Rokem argued that the casting said nothing unexpected about the conflict, that the tragic love story had been privatized into a human-interest story, and that the production demonstrated coexistence rather than examining it. The charge is serious and partly just: a Palestinian Romeo and an Israeli Juliet announce their meaning before a word is spoken, and a play whose meaning is exhausted by its casting has surrendered the text to the gesture.

The production's troubled rehearsal history supplies the evidence that adjudicates between the gesture and the charge. Rokem himself records that rehearsals were repeatedly interrupted by political and economic difficulties, and the production's histories fill in the sequence: after the massacre of Palestinian worshippers in Hebron in February 1994, joint rehearsals halted, the Palestinian cast withdrew, several Israeli actors quit, and the company resumed only after the whole cast and crew met together. A gesture that survives its own interruption has become something more than a gesture. The companies were not performing reconciliation for an evening; they were practising it under the conditions that made it nearly impossible, and the practice, not the poster, is the production's content. The documentary film Compromise, which followed the production, reached a similar doubleness from the other side: Yael Munk's analysis argues that the film's documentation subverted its makers' peace-building intentions by exposing the gaps the staging hoped to bridge. Between the stage event and its film, the production's meaning split, and the split is the most honest record any Arabic Romeo and Juliet has left. It showed two companies building one play and two audiences taking home two plays, and it preserved both facts without resolving them.

The afterlife of the production is a measure of its moment. Baniel later reported that Al-Kasaba no longer displayed a photograph of the production in its public gallery, and that a Palestinian designer on the production had fled Gaza under threat. Whether every detail of that report is complete is less important than what the report itself registers: the co-production became an artefact of a closed political season, the Oslo years, whose terms neither side any longer inhabited. The production is therefore the Arab record's clearest demonstration of the difference between a staging and a headline borrowing. A headline borrows the lovers' names to caption a division. The 1994 production borrowed nothing; it built the division into the means of production, rehearsed under it, nearly collapsed under it, and left an archive that contradicts its own publicity. That is what a documented adaptation can do that a headline cannot, and it is the standard against which the next case, which announced its meanings far more loudly, has to be judged.

## Baghdad: The Feud as Sect, the Stage as Witness

### Q: Did the Baghdad production name the two sects aloud?

No, Monadhil Daood's adaptation never speaks the words Sunni and Shia, encoding the division instead in costume and circumstance that an Iraqi audience could read at sight: a red-and-white checked keffiyeh on the Sunni Capulet, a black-and-white scarf on the Shia Montague, and a Paris drawn as a foreign al-Qaeda militant rather than as an Iraqi suitor.

Romeo and Juliet in Baghdad, written and directed by Monadhil Daood for the Iraqi Theatre Company and co-produced with the Royal Shakespeare Company, opened in Baghdad in April 2012 and travelled to the Swan Theatre at Stratford-upon-Avon and the Riverside Studios in London for the World Shakespeare Festival, and onward to Doha. Daood, a veteran Iraqi actor and playwright who had left Iraq in the 1980s after a play of his angered the government, spent two years on the script, and the adaptation is systematic in a way the Jerusalem production never tried to be. The feuding houses become two half-brothers divided by sect. Romeo is a Shia and Juliet a Sunni, childhood sweethearts separated for nine years by the violence, meeting again as adults inside the sectarian geography of post-2003 Iraq. The dialogue, in Iraqi dialect, admits the occupation's furniture directly: references to Blackwater, to Iranians, to the American reconstruction effort. The old Montague survives on an intravenous drip attended by an Anglo-American soldier, a small and eloquent stage picture that gives the occupying power a trivial and permanent role at the family's sickbed. Nothing in the design is left ambiguous, and the ambiguity's absence is the production's aesthetic signature and its critical problem.

The ending rewrites the play's machinery at its two load-bearing points. The deaths are removed from the economy of private error, the missed message and the mistimed waking, and reassigned to the public violence that the adaptation takes as its subject: Paris, recast as an al-Qaeda fighter and pointedly not an Iraqi, storms the church of Our Lady of Salvation where the lovers have taken refuge and detonates a belt of explosives, killing both. The church is a real address in Baghdad's memory, the site of the attack of 31 October 2010 in which militants killed forty-four worshippers and two priests, and the adaptation's climax quotes that atrocity as deliberately as a text quotes a line. The reconciliation, in turn, is reassigned from the Prince to a history professor, who talks the two fathers into ending their feud. The substitutions are exact inversions of the original's distribution of agency. In Verona, private mistakes kill the lovers and public authority pronounces the lesson; in Baghdad, public violence kills the lovers and a teacher, the custodian of the past rather than the wielder of power, pronounces the lesson. Daood's stated message was simple, that love is better than the conflict between the families, and the adaptation builds a stage world in which that simplicity is earned by horror rather than asserted by sentiment.

The production's documentary apparatus deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is where the two contracts, with Baghdad and with the festival, were written down. The company prepared performance notes that addressed its two audiences in two distinct messages. To British spectators, the notes argued, in effect, that Iraqis were complex and fallible human beings rather than the savages of the news frame; to Iraqi spectators, they argued that unless Sunni and Shia talked, the next generation's story would be a tragedy. The notes are an extraordinary artefact of the festival economy: a production so aware of its double address that it drafted its two meanings separately and printed both. Deborah Shaw of the Royal Shakespeare Company assisted with the surtitling and the production's presentation to anglophone audiences, which placed the machinery of translation at the service of the export frame even as the performance itself spoke Iraqi dialect. None of this discredits the work. It specifies the work's conditions, and it explains, better than any review, why the same evening could be received in Baghdad as recognition and in Stratford as explanation. A production that writes its own divergent programme notes has already conceded the frame-dependence that its critics and defenders would later argue about as if it were a discovery.

Inside Iraq, the production was received as testimony. Tim Arango's report for the New York Times recorded a university student in the audience, Senan Saadi, saying that cloaked as a Shakespearean classic was a rendering of Iraqi lives across the previous nine years, and that the play was about the reality, the killing between Sunnis and Shias. Sarwa Rasool, who played Juliet, spoke of neighbourhoods whose sectarian character had inverted after 2003 and of couples divided by the new geography. Explosions were heard in Baghdad the morning after the opening, and attacks across the country left nearly three dozen people dead by the end of that day, a juxtaposition the press coverage noted because the production's conditions of performance were part of its meaning. The Baghdad audience did not need the diagram explained, because the diagram was the street outside the theatre. Samir Talib Dawood's scholarly reading of the production extends that reception into a postcolonial argument: the adaptation writes back to the cultures that took part in the 2003 war, appropriating the invaders' classic to arraign the invasion's consequences, and stands as the first internationally recognized attempt to set the play directly inside post-2003 Iraq.

The export reception divided against itself, and the division is the critical fact about the production. Margaret Litvin, Saffron Walkling, and Raphael Cormack, surveying the Arabic productions of the 2012 festival in the journal Shakespeare, judged Romeo and Juliet in Baghdad the closest of the three to a charge they call forensic fascination, and described it as a conscious attempt to use Shakespeare as a cultural bridge, a rendering of the Iraqi situation so legible that its meanings were engineered for two audiences at once. The production's own performance notes support the description, since they spelled out separate messages addressed to British and to Iraqi spectators, and left, in the scholars' phrase, nothing open to misinterpretation. Read from Stratford, the production could look like a diagram of Iraq drawn for export. Read from Baghdad, the same legibility was recognition, the naming of things the audience lived among but rarely saw staged. The adjudication offered here is that both readings are true of different performances of the same text: in Baghdad the production functioned as witness, and its simplifications were the shorthand of shared knowledge; on the festival stage the same simplifications functioned as explanation, and explanation of a war to the countries that waged it will always sit closer to the diagram than to the event. The play Daood wrote did not change between the two cities. The contract between stage and audience changed, and the production's divided reputation is the receipt for that change.


## Beirut: The Last Day and the Feud in a Minor Key

### Q: Is Akhir Yom a translation of the play?

No, Akhir Yom is a localized musical adaptation, a Lebanese work by Oussama al-Rahbani built on the tragedy's armature, premiered at the Casino du Liban in 2004, with the author's own score. Its script remains unpublished; its record survives through Yvette Khoury's study and the Lebanese press, a thinner archive than the Baghdad production's, and claims are graded to it.

Lebanon gave the tragedy its most intimate Arabic staging, because Lebanon's division is not between states or sects at war across a border but within one small country's families, neighbourhoods, and institutions. Akhir Yom, The Last Day, brought the play home to the Rahbani family, whose musicals, built by Assi and Mansour Rahbani and sung by Fairuz, had functioned for decades as Lebanon's unofficial national theatre, the genre in which the country imagined itself whole. Oussama al-Rahbani, of the family's second generation, made the first Shakespeare adaptation by a Rahbani, and the choice of play was itself a statement: the tragedy of two houses, retold inside the musical form that had always promised one house. Khoury's study of the production reads it as a meeting of eastern and western theatrical traditions and as an examination of the Other inside Arabic drama, with the conflict turned inward, intracultural, a matter of Lebanese against Lebanese rather than of Arab against foreigner. The lovers' obstacle in Beirut was not an occupying power or a neighbouring nation. It was the country's own arrangement of itself.

The Lebanese press read the production in exactly those terms, and the reviews are the evidence for the localization's mechanism. Dima Sa'd, writing in al-Balad, called the piece a love story that exposes the fragility of the civil peace, al-salm al-ahli, the settled and uneasy order that followed the civil war of 1975 to 1990. A headline in al-Diyar captured the localization's most original move, reporting that the production put al-Hikmeh and al-Riyadi onstage: Beirut's two great rival basketball clubs, whose fixtures carried for decades the sectarian charge of their support, one historically anchored in Christian Beirut and the other in Muslim Beirut. The feud, translated into sport, became a fixture list. The choice is a small masterpiece of adaptation logic. A Lebanese audience did not need the clubs explained; it needed only to hear the rivalry named in the lovers' story to understand that the play's two households had been mapped onto a division the country rehearsed weekly, safely, in stadiums, because rehearsing it elsewhere had once cost a war. Where Baghdad encoded sect in costume and Jerusalem encoded nation in language, Beirut encoded confession in allegiance, the lightest of the three codes and in some ways the most Lebanese: a feud everyone could laugh about in public because its public form was a game.

The musical form carries the production's second meaning. The Rahbani musical was a technology of national feeling long before it was a commercial genre, its songs constructing a Lebanon of villages, seasons, and shared sentiment that the country's politics never managed to build. To place the tragedy inside that form was to ask whether the form's promise could survive the plot's refusal: a Rahbani evening conventionally ends in communal harmony, and this plot ends in a tomb. Khoury's account emphasizes the production's questioning of the civil peace rather than its celebration of unity, and the press response suggests that audiences received it in that spirit, as an exposure rather than a reassurance. The Last Day is therefore the opposite pole of the Arabic record from Baghdad's production. Daood rebuilt the play as an indictment with a message spelled out for export; al-Rahbani rebuilt it as a musical that asks a question its own genre cannot answer, for an audience that needed no explanation at all. The two productions, eight years apart, mark the range of the Arabic relocation: the feud as evidence in a war-crimes brief, and the feud as a fixture in a national diary.

## Three Registers: Translation, Adaptation, and the Borrowed Name

The Arabic record divides into three registers, and confusing them is the commonest error in accounts of the reception. The first register is translation proper, the work of al-Haddad and 'Abdu in their different modes, of Bakathir, of Enani, and of the anonymous versions in Shetawi's catalogue: texts that claim to carry the play into Arabic and submit, in principle, to comparison against the original. The second register is the staged adaptation, the work of Daood and al-Rahbani and the Khan and Al-Kasaba companies: performances that claim the play's armature and discard its letter, relocating the feud and sometimes rewriting the ending, and that submit instead to comparison against the local reality they map. The third register is the borrowed name, the journalistic and conversational formula by which any two lovers divided by sect, village, family, or politics are captioned as an Arab Romeo and Juliet. The registers have different evidentiary standards, and an account that grades a headline by the standards of a translation, or a translation by the standards of a headline, will misjudge all three.

The registers also have a history, and the history runs downhill in formality and uphill in reach. The borrowed name is the register with the widest circulation and the least content: it requires of its user no knowledge of the play beyond the lovers' names and their deaths, and it flatters a local story by attaching it to a global one. Arabic journalism's use of the formula, like its use everywhere, tells the reader more about the prestige of the play than about the couple in the story, and its evidentiary value for the reception is confined to that prestige. The staged adaptation stands in the middle: it requires a company, a text, and an audience, and it produces evidence in proportion to its archive, which is why the productions of 1994, 2004, and 2012 can be argued about in detail while the borrowed names cannot. The translation stands at the top of the evidentiary scale and at the bottom of the circulation scale, the least read and the most durable register, the one that decides what the other two can borrow. A company in 2012 could assume that its audience knew the lovers' story because translators and adapters had kept that story in Arabic circulation since 1891. The registers feed each other in that order, and the reception's health can be measured by whether all three are alive at once. In the Arabic case, for most of the period since 1891, they were. The Arabic borrowed name has one feature found nowhere else in the play's global travels, a native competitor. An Arabic headline writer confronting a pair of lovers divided by family, village, or sect disposes of two formulas, not one: the imported names, and Qays and Layla, the legend's own pair. The choice between the formulas is a small act of cultural positioning performed daily in the region's press. The imported formula claims world recognition for a local story and quietly subordinates the story to Verona; the native formula claims the opposite, that the local archive already owns the pattern and needs no passport. Which formula a given newspaper reaches for says something real about its cultural politics, and the coexistence of the two is the borrowed-name register's Arabic signature: in the Arab world, the play's name never held a monopoly on doomed love, because the competition was twelve centuries old and still in print.

Distinguishing the registers also disciplines the claims made for the play's political use, which the brief for this article rightly flags as the reception's standing complication. A translation used in a classroom is a political fact of a quiet kind, since canons are chosen. A staged adaptation that maps the feud onto sect or nation is a political act of a loud kind, since it names the division in public. A headline borrowing is a political gesture of the cheapest kind, since it spends the play's prestige without risking anything the play contains. The Arabic record contains all three, and its distinction is that its loud acts were made by people with standing in the places they named: an Iraqi company in Baghdad, a Palestinian and an Israeli company in Jerusalem, a Rahbani in Beirut. The political uses that matter in this reception were not metaphors imposed from outside. They were wagers made by local companies with their own audiences, and they stand or fall by the local record, not by the approval of the festival circuit that later carried two of them abroad.

## The Play in the Arab Classroom

The classroom is the quietest site on the map and the one that fixed the reception's canon. The Egyptian stage made the play famous; the Egyptian educational order made it permanent, and the mechanism was the same institutional machinery that enshrined Mutran's idiom when the Higher Institute for Dramatic Arts set his prose as the actors' memorized standard. By the early 1950s the Arabic Shakespeare canon, as the record of the period reckons it, had settled into a short list: three of the great tragedies, The Merchant of Venice in Mutran's version, Julius Caesar in Muhammad Hamdi's, and the tragedy of Verona in Bakathir's blank verse. Taha Hussein's Arab League project of the 1950s, which set out to put the whole of Shakespeare into Arabic prose, gave translation a state-sponsored frame, and the versions of the following decades, Mu'nis Taha Husayn's of 1960 and Enani's of 1965 and after, belong to that frame. Enani's own base was Cairo University, and his testimonial on translating the play is a professor's document, written from inside the institution that taught the text. The Arab classroom's Shakespeare was therefore a translated Shakespeare for most students and an English Shakespeare for the English departments, and the tragedy circulated in both, a doubleness that [the play's place in classrooms around the world](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/) shows to be the play's standard condition in education systems far beyond the Arab world.

The classroom also explains the reception's characteristic distribution of attention. Litvin's work on the Arab Hamlet makes the point for the neighbouring play that most Arabs met Shakespeare on the popular stage rather than in elite colonial classrooms, the famous schoolboy performances of the colonial colleges being the exception that memoirs remember. The tragedy fits that distribution even more snugly than Hamlet, because its first Arabic life was a commercial stage success a decade before it was a printed book, and its classroom canonization came two generations later, with Bakathir's verse and the institutional projects behind him. The sequence matters for the verdict. Arab audiences did not receive the play as a colonial school text and then rebel against it; they received it as popular entertainment, naturalized it as classical literature, and only then installed it in the syllabus. The postcolonial story often told about Shakespeare in the region, of an imposed canon later appropriated, runs in the wrong order for this play. The appropriation came first, and the canon was built out of it. The university form of the reception preserved the doubleness in institutional form. The play was studied in English in the region's English departments, where Enani himself taught at Cairo University, and in Arabic in translation programmes and Arabic departments, where his version and Bakathir's served as the set texts, so that a single university could house two tragedies that were nominally one: the English text as an object of literary study, and the Arabic text as an object of translation study, each with its own scholarly apparatus and its own examination questions. Comparative literature, the discipline that Litvin and Hanna both inhabit, grew up in the space between those two texts, and its characteristic questions, about idiom, register, mediation, and audience, are the questions the Arabic reception had been posing in practice since 1898. The academy did not import its agenda into the reception. The reception supplied the agenda, a century deep, and the academy arrived to find its subject already in session.


## A Repertoire, Not a Monument: The Egyptian Stage Century

Between the landmarks stands a fact so plain that scholarship states it almost in passing: the play has been performed regularly in Egypt from the staging of Martyrs of Love in 1891 onward. Yvette Khoury's study concedes that no full history of Arabic performances of the tragedy exists, and offers the regularity itself as the known quantity. That concession deserves to be read as a finding rather than as a gap. A play performed regularly for a hundred and thirty years inside a mixed commercial and state theatre system is not a monument visited on ceremonial occasions. It is a repertoire utility, a title that companies reach for to open seasons, to give young actors their first classical parts, to fill the respectable slot in a programme, and to remind an audience that the house can do the classics. The tragedy's Egyptian century is best imagined as a plateau rather than as a range of peaks: a high, continuous level of performance from which the documented summits, 1891 itself and the later festival productions elsewhere, happen to rise into the archive.

The company system that sustained the plateau can be sketched from its institutions. Georges Abyad, trained in Paris and the Arab stage's first great tragedian, built a company in the 1910s for which Mutran translated Shakespeare directly, and although the plays documented for that channel are Mutran's own list, Othello above all, the channel itself mattered to every play in the repertoire, because it proved that translated Shakespeare could anchor a serious company's season rather than decorate it. Youssef Wahbi's Ramsis troupe, founded in 1923, professionalized the Egyptian stage a stage further, and its actors moved between theatre and the young cinema, carrying the repertoire's habits with them; the 1942 film of the tragedy belongs to that crossover generation. Mutran himself, in his capacity as an early director of the Egyptian National Theatre Company, supervised Shakespeare productions that competed, with uneven success, against a commercial theatre judged too melodramatic or too farcical by the literary party, and the competition between the national stage and the commercial stage became the field in which the repertoire's standards were fought out. The state's authorization of a co-educational drama institute in 1944 completed the apparatus: training, a national company, a commercial sector, and a reviewing press, all turning over a stock of plays in which the tragedy held a permanent place.

What the plateau means for the reception's interpretation is easily missed, because scholarship is drawn to events and the plateau is not an event. The regular Egyptian performances did the slow work that the famous productions get credit for. They kept the lovers' names in oral circulation across generations that never opened a translation; they trained the audiences who would later receive Jerusalem, Beirut, and Baghdad as variations on a known quantity rather than as novelties; and they maintained the pool of actors and directors for whom the play was a tool ready to hand. When Daood reached for the tragedy in 2010 to write about Iraq, he was reaching for an instrument that a century of Arab stages had kept in tune, and the instrument's readiness is the plateau's achievement. The archive records the summits because summits generate paper. The plateau generated competence, which leaves less paper and matters more.

The same point disciplines the map's silences. For most Egyptian productions across the twentieth century, the record preserved neither the version spoken, nor the cast, nor the house, and the catalogue's anonymous translations hint at the scale of the unrecorded base: if printed versions could circulate without their makers' names, performances could certainly vanish without their programmes. The honest response to that condition is the one this article has practised throughout, grading each claim to its archive and treating the undocumented regularity as a single large fact rather than as a licence to invent instances. The tragedy's Arabic biography is unusually well evidenced at its turning points and almost unevidenced in its long middle, and a reader who understands that distribution understands the reception better than a reader who knows three more production dates. The play lived in Arabic the way plays live anywhere: mostly in the dark, at work, in front of audiences who left no reviews, and the famous stagings are the moments the lights happened to be on.

## Why the Tragedy, and Not Another Play

The selectivity of the Arabic reception deserves an explanation, because the region had the whole canon to choose from and distributed its attention unevenly. Hamlet took the politics, Othello took the repertoire's centre in Mutran's version, and the comedies, as Mahmoud Shetywi's bibliographical work observes of The Merchant of Venice's exceptional status, were generally less received than the tragedies. The tragedy of Verona took the subject that the region's own literature had prepared for a thousand years: young love destroyed by the arrangements of the old. The explanation lies in a fit between the play's machinery and the receiving culture's inheritance, and the fit has three parts. First, the feud is an empty form. The Prologue calls it an ancient grudge and never states its cause, and a cause-free grudge is the most portable object in world drama: an adapter needs to supply only a local division, Sunni and Shia, Arabic and Hebrew, Hikmeh and Riyadi, and the play's structure closes around it. Hamlet's machinery, by contrast, requires a court, a succession, and a ghost, and travels only where those can be rebuilt. The tragedy's requirements are two families, one city, and a law that fails, and every society in the region could meet them from stock.

The second part of the fit is the Udhri inheritance. Arab audiences possessed, in the Layla tradition and its kin, a millennium of poetry about love perfected by obstruction, and the imported play arrived as a foreign instance of a native obsession, which is the easiest kind of foreign work to naturalize. But the energy of the reception lay in the differences the inheritance made visible, not in the resemblance. The native tradition gave the Arab audience a lover who goes mad alone in the desert; the play gave that audience two lovers who conspire together in a city, a heroine who argues her case in soliloquy, and an ending in which the community, not the beloved, stands accused. Each of those differences was a tool. The heroine's voice let companies argue about women and obedience without naming a local target. The civic ending let companies indict a social order while appearing to stage a romance. The conspiracy of the young let companies stage generational revolt in the safest available costume. A reception is a set of uses, and the tragedy's uses in Arabic were supplied by its differences from the legend it was shelved beside.

The third part of the fit is generational, and it is the Arab reception's signature reading of the play. In production after production, the emphasis fell on the direction of the killing: the elders' quarrel, executed by the young. Daood's Baghdad adaptation states the reading as its message, that the families' conflict murders the generation that did not start it. The Jerusalem production cast the reading across a national line during a peace process whose architects were old men. Al-Rahbani's Beirut musical framed it as the fragility of a peace that the young had inherited without making. Even the commercial Arabizations leaned the same way, since Martyrs of Love frames the lovers as victims offered up to a world's arrangements rather than as agents of their own ruin. The play supports the reading without strain: Romeo kills Tybalt in a quarrel he tried to refuse, Juliet is traded in a marriage market by a father who calls her disobedience a fault to be corrected by force, and the Prince's closing accounting fines every elder in the play. The Arabic reception did not distort the tragedy by stressing the generational indictment. It isolated the tragedy's most exportable argument and ran it, for a hundred and thirty years, in every register from the school text to the festival stage.

## What the Critics Disagree About

The scholarship on the Arabic reception argues about three things: whether the early Arabizations were a corruption or a foundation, whether the reception's history ascends from loose adaptation to faithful translation, and where the meaning of a relocated production resides. Each argument has named parties, and each admits an adjudication on the evidence assembled in this article. The first argument is the oldest. The literary generation that followed the commercial stage treated 'Abdu's versions as a scandal of the marketplace, and Muhammad Taymur, the critic and playwright, praised Mutran's translations precisely as a counterweight to the commercial versions that had held the stage. The fidelity framing survived for decades: the early versions were free, the later versions were faithful, and the history was a purification. Sameh Hanna's sociology of the translation field, worked out in his study of Mutran's Othello as an act of decommercialization, dismantles the framing without denying its facts. Hanna shows a field in which translators competed with different currencies: 'Abdu's currency was box-office success and newspaper fame, displayed on his title pages, where he styled himself the skilled writer and proprietor of the well-reputed al-Sharq newspaper; the literary translators' currency was institutional and educational capital, displayed on theirs, where Muhammad Hamdi, the translator of Julius Caesar in 1912, introduced himself as a teacher of translation at the Higher School of Teachers. Mutran's elevated idiom, on Hanna's reading, was a strategy within that competition, a bid to redefine what counted as legitimate translation so that the literary men would win by the new definition.

The adjudication on the first argument is that Hanna explains the field and the fidelity critics describe the texts, and both are needed because the two claims operate at different levels. As texts, the early versions are free and Mutran's are second-hand, made from French; no sociology changes the distance between 'Abdu's Martyrs of Love machinery and the English lines. As a field, the triumph of the literary translators was a social event before it was a textual one, and the tragedy is the proof, since its canonical version was written by neither party to the quarrel: not by 'Abdu, whose version served the stage, and not by Mutran, who never touched the play, but by Bakathir, a playwright pursuing a verse experiment, and later by Enani, a professor pursuing an idiom. The quarrel's winner, for this play, was a third party, and a history organized as the quarrel's two sides cannot see him. Hanna's framework can, because it predicts that positions in a field get taken by whoever finds an unoccupied one, and the tragedy's Arabic standard was an unoccupied position for half a century.

The second argument concerns the shape of the history. Mohammed Naser Hassoon's survey of the reception in Multicultural Shakespeare describes a long movement from Arabization through domestication to translation proper, an ascent from the commercial liberties of the 1890s to the disciplined versions of the later twentieth century, and the description fits the printed record: the versions do grow closer to the English text across the catalogue's dates. The adjudication qualifies the ascent with the stage record, because the line bends backward whenever performance reclaims the play. Enani's mixed idiom of prose and verse was already a negotiation rather than a terminus, and the great stage events of the reception, Jerusalem in 1994, Beirut in 2004, Baghdad in 2012, are all acts of domestication as thorough as anything 'Abdu attempted, relocations of setting, language, and ending that the 1890s adapters would have recognized as their own method. The history is therefore not an ascent but an oscillation: print culture pulls the play toward the original, stage culture pulls it toward the locality, and the reception lives in the alternation. Hassoon's periodization remains the best map of the printed record, and the stage record stands beside it as its necessary correction.

The third argument, over where a relocated production's meaning resides, has already been adjudicated case by case in this article, and the general verdict can be stated. Rokem located the Jerusalem production's meaning in its performance text and found the text thin; Baniel located it in the act of co-production and found the act eloquent; Munk located it in the gap between the event and its documentation. Samir Talib Dawood located the Baghdad production's meaning in its address to Baghdad and found testimony; Litvin, Walkling, and Cormack located it in its address to the festival and found a diagram. The pattern across both quarrels is that each critic fixed one frame, the text, the act, the home audience, the export audience, as the real one, and judged the others as noise. The productions themselves refute the fixing, because both were built to operate in several frames at once, and did. The sound conclusion is that a touring adaptation's meaning is frame-dependent in fact, not as an evasion but as a description of the object, and that criticism serves the record best when it specifies its frame and argues within it, as Rokem does for the text and Litvin and her co-authors do for the festival. Where this article has adjudicated, it has done so by that rule: the Jerusalem production was stronger as an act than as an interpretation, and the Baghdad production was stronger as testimony at home than as explanation abroad, and those are verdicts about frames, delivered plainly, rather than verdicts about essences.


## Cairo's Monopoly and the Migrant Makers

The map's Cairo-heavy first century invites a question the map alone cannot answer: why did one city hold the reception for so long, and whose work was it? Cairo held it because Cairo held the means of production. Egypt had the troupes, the theatres, the publishers, the newspapers that reviewed and advertised, and the audiences with money for tickets, and after Salim al-Naqqash's arrival in 1876 it also had the Khedive's example of state patronage for performance. Alexandria, cosmopolitan and francophone, supplied the first stages; Cairo supplied the scale. A play became Arabic in Egypt because Egypt was where Arabic plays became property, reviewed in print, fought over by critics, and taught in institutes. The other Arab capitals entered the tragedy's story when their own institutions matured: Beirut through the Rahbani musical empire, Jerusalem and Ramallah through companies built in the teeth of occupation, Baghdad through a national company and a festival invitation. The geography of the reception is therefore an institutional history in disguise, and the sites on the map mark the places where an Arab stage existed that was strong enough to wager on the play.

The makers complicate the map in a second way, because the Cairo canon was substantially built by arrivals. Al-Haddad was a Lebanese man of letters working in Egypt. 'Abdu's career ran between the Levantine press and the Egyptian stage. Mutran, the standard-setter of the whole tradition, was born in Baalbek and made his career in Cairo, the poet of two countries in the phrase his admirers used. Bakathir was of Hadrami Yemeni origin, formed as a writer in Cairo. The pattern is the nahda's own pattern, in which the Levant supplied the journalists, translators, and teachers and Egypt supplied the market and the institutions, and it has a consequence for how the reception should be described. Calling the early Arabic Romeo and Juliet an Egyptian achievement is accurate about its stages and incomplete about its personnel; calling it a Levantine export is accurate about its makers and incomplete about its public. The play's Arabic life was a joint product of migration, the work of men who carried a literary language into a richer market and were changed by the carrying. Even the tragedy's standard-setter paradox belongs to the pattern: the canon of Arabic Shakespeare was codified in Cairo by a Lebanese poet translating from French, and the tragedy entered that canon through a Yemeni playwright's verse. The Arab world's Romeo and Juliet was cosmopolitan at birth, and its later relocations to Jerusalem, Beirut, and Baghdad were returns to cities its first makers had come from.

The institutional frame also explains the reception's gaps, which are as instructive as its landmarks. Shetawi's catalogue records three anonymous translations of the tragedy between 1959 and 1990, versions whose makers went unrecorded, and the map lists a 1978 version by Ghazi Gamal with no place attached in the catalogue, an honest blank. The performance archive is similarly uneven: the Jerusalem production left reviews, a scholarly essay, and a documentary film; the Beirut production left a journal article and newspaper notices; dozens of Egyptian stagings across the twentieth century left little beyond the fact of their regularity. A reception history written only from its best-documented cases would mistake the archive for the life. The play was performed in school halls, provincial theatres, and amateur seasons that no journal catalogued, in versions no library kept, and the catalogue's anonymous entries are the visible edge of that larger, unrecorded circulation. The map in this article is therefore offered as a map of the documented reception, and its silences are part of its evidence: they show where the play lived below the waterline of print, which is where most theatre lives.

## Verdict: An Arab Play by Adoption

The evidence supports the verdict stated at the outset, and it can be stated again with its supporting counts. The Arabic reception is a second life, not a shadow: it has its own first night (Cairo, 1891, before its first book), its own canon (settled by the early 1950s, with the tragedy in Bakathir's verse), its own standard idiom (Enani's mixed prose and verse, won after Mutran's prose and Bakathir's verse each failed to be usable enough), its own classical parallel (Layla and Majnun, nine centuries senior to the play and structurally its opposite in agency, obstacle, and ending), and its own masterpieces of relocation (Jerusalem in 1994, Beirut in 2004, Baghdad in 2012). The pattern is not the Arab world's alone: [African stagings and adaptations](/2030/05/31/african-stagings-adaptations/) redraw the same feud across clan, ethnic, and political lines on the neighbouring continent, and the Indian and East Asian records rhyme with both. What distinguishes the Arabic case is the completeness of its arc. The play was commercial entertainment before it was literature, literature before it was a syllabus, and a syllabus before it was a festival export, and each stage of that arc left documents that the next stage argued with.

The verdict on the parallel follows from the comparison's own evidence. Layla and Majnun dignified the play in Arabic by granting it a classical neighbour, and distorted it whenever adapters or commentators collapsed the two stories into one, because the collapse discards exactly what the play adds to the world's stock of doomed-love stories: a heroine who reasons and acts, a feud between equals, a marriage, and a city put on trial at the end. The parallel earns its keep as a measuring instrument. Set beside Majnun's solitary madness, the lovers' partnership becomes visible; set beside the grave in the wilderness, the tomb in Verona and the Prince's accounting become visible. Arabic criticism's best work on the play, from Bakathir's verse wager to Khoury's reading of The Last Day, proceeds by that measurement. Its weakest work, the borrowed-name register of journalism, proceeds by the collapse. The distinction drawn in this article between the parallel as distance and the parallel as identity is therefore not a nicety. It is the difference between the reception's criticism and its publicity.

The verdict on the political uses follows the same line. The play served Arab stages as an instrument for naming division because its feud is an empty form and its victims are the young, and the uses that honoured the instrument were the ones that paid for it in specificity: a sect encoded in a keffiyeh, a nation encoded in a language split, a confession encoded in a basketball fixture. The uses that spent it cheaply, the headline borrowings, left nothing behind them. And the verdict on the translation history is the Mutran correction generalized. The Arabic Romeo and Juliet was not made by the tradition's most famous translator, who never touched it, nor by its most faithful one, since fidelity was not the field's early currency. It was made by commercial adapters who renamed it for love's martyrs, by a playwright who wagered that Arabic could carry blank verse, by a professor who translated it twice because the first attempt did not satisfy him, and by companies who rebuilt its feud out of their own cities' materials. A reception with that roster is not a footnote to anyone's Shakespeare. It is the play's Arabic biography, and it stands complete on its own documents.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who gave the tragedy its first Arabic stage life, and under what title?

Najib al-Haddad gave the play its first documented Arabic stage life, in Cairo in 1891, under the title Martyrs of Love. The staging preceded the printed text by a full decade, since al-Haddad's version appeared in print only in 1901, and Tanyus 'Abdu's rival version had appeared in 1898. The sequence is the reception in miniature: the Arab world met the tragedy first as a performance, in a commercial theatre, under a title that announced romance rather than civic tragedy, and only afterward as a book. Shetawi's catalogue preserves the order of events, and the order matters because it reverses the assumption that translation precedes staging. In the Arabic case the stage came first, and the printed versions were transcripts of a success the box office had already certified. Every later landmark of the reception, from Bakathir's verse to Daood's Baghdad, inherited that stage-first character.

### Q: Why did al-Haddad call his version Martyrs of Love?

The title Martyrs of Love reframed the tragedy as a martyrology, presenting the lovers as witnesses who die for love rather than as casualties of a civic war between two houses. The reframing was a commercial Arabization in miniature. A Cairo audience of 1891 was being offered the story in the idiom of popular romance, in which suffering for love carries religious resonance and the lovers' deaths testify to the feeling's truth. The Shakespearean machinery of the feud, the Prince, and the city's accounting receded behind that frame, and the play became portable as a love story first and a tragedy of Verona second. The title's success can be measured by the play's regular performance in Egypt from that staging onward. Later translators restored the lovers' names to the title, but the martyrdom frame survived in the culture's memory of the play, and it shaped the borrowed-name register of Arabic journalism, where doomed lovers are still mourned more often than their cities are indicted.

### Q: Through which language did the earliest Arabic versions of the play characteristically pass?

The earliest Arabic Shakespeare characteristically passed through French, because French was the European language of the Levantine and Egyptian commercial theatre and because French adaptations had already performed part of the work of domestication. The pattern persisted into the literary period: Khalil Mutran's celebrated Othello of 1912, the most famous Arabic Shakespeare translation of its generation, rested on Georges Duval's French text rather than on the English original. A French intermediary meant that a play could arrive with its verse tamed, its edges smoothed, and its situations already legible to an audience formed by French melodrama. The consequence for the reception's history is methodological. Accounts that imagine a direct line from Shakespeare's English to the Arabic stage misdescribe a chain of mediations, and the chain explains both the freedom of the early versions and the anxiety of the later translators, who defined their fidelity against predecessors that had never faced the English text at all.

### Q: What did the word Arabized on an early title page promise its reader?

The word Arabized, ta'rib, promised a text made Arabic in idiom, setting, and sensibility rather than a text carried across languages with its joints intact, and the promise was printed as a selling point. The second edition of 'Abdu's Hamlet in 1902 announced on its title page that the play had been Arabized by the skilled writer Tanyus Effendi 'Abdou, proprietor of the well-reputed al-Sharq newspaper, a line that advertises the adapter's commercial standing in the same breath as his method. A Palestinian student, Khalil Baydas, writing to the journal al-Hilal in 1895, had already defined the ideal behind the term: a version carried into eloquent Arabic that took liberties as its maker saw fit while leaving no impression of having been Arabized at all. The definition is candid about the method and proud of the result. Arabization was not failed translation by its own lights. It was a rival theory of what a foreign play owed its new audience, and the tragedy entered Arabic under its banner.

### Q: Why did Ali Ahmed Bakathir translate the play into blank verse?

Bakathir translated the tragedy into blank verse, in a version made in 1936 and published in 1942, to demonstrate that the Arabic language could sustain Shakespeare's unrhymed iambic line without breaking. The experiment was a claim about Arabic's capacities as much as about the play. Arabic poetry possessed its own metres and its own prestige system, and the assertion that a European verse form could be carried across intact was a nationalist argument conducted by prosodic means, aimed at a generation testing whether Arabic literature could absorb foreign forms without surrendering its own. The wager succeeded institutionally. Bakathir's version entered the canon that the Egyptian stage and schools carried into the 1950s, where the tragedy stood in verse while its neighbours in the standard repertoire, Hamlet, Othello, and Macbeth, stood in prose. The version's survival as the mid-century standard, in a field that produced many prose renderings, is the measure of the demonstration's force.

### Q: How many Arabic versions of the play does Shetawi's catalogue record?

Shetawi's catalogue records at least nine Arabic versions of the tragedy between 1898 and 1990: six under named translators and three anonymous. The named sequence runs from 'Abdu in 1898 and al-Haddad in 1901, the latter staged a decade earlier, through Bakathir's blank verse of 1936, published 1942, Mu'nis Taha Husayn in 1960, Mohamed Enani in 1965, and Ghazi Gamal in 1978. The three anonymous versions, dated between 1959 and 1990, are as significant as the named ones, because they show the play circulating in texts whose makers went unrecorded, copied, staged, and taught without an author for readers to venerate. The catalogue's shape therefore corrects two errors at once. It refutes the lazy summary that hands the early field to Mutran, who never translated the play, and it refutes the romance of the named translator by documenting a wide anonymous circulation beneath the landmarks, the waterline under the reception's visible peaks.

### Q: Why did Mohamed Enani translate the play twice?

Enani translated the tragedy twice because the first attempt, in prose, failed to satisfy its maker on the question of idiom, and the failure was principled rather than accidental. His first version is dated 1965 in Shetawi's catalogue. In his testimonial on translating Shakespeare, Enani describes the central obstacle as one of language and tone: the inherited tradition had matched Shakespeare's English to an elevated classical Arabic register, while the prose renderings of Taha Hussein's mid-century project had pursued fidelity at the price of theatrical life. Dissatisfied with his prose versions of the tragedy and of A Midsummer Night's Dream, Enani retranslated the tragedy in a mixture of prose and verse, letting verse carry the poetry and prose carry the plain speech. The scholarship on his work judges that mixed idiom the contemporary language of Arabic Shakespeare translation, the solution later translators actually used. The double translation is the reception's method in one career: a standard is not found but negotiated, across attempts, against predecessors.

### Q: What does the name Majnun mean, and who bestowed it?

Majnun means the madman, in the Arabic idiom a man possessed by jinn, and the name was bestowed on Qays ibn al-Mulawwah by his own community when his open proclamation of love for Layla passed from devotion into obsession. The naming is the legend's diagnostic moment. Qays composed verses naming Layla in public, and the publicity itself destroyed his marriage suit, since a love announced to the tribe became a scandal no father could bless; her family refused him and married her to another man. The community's verdict on the lover who would not stop speaking was madness, and the name replaced his own so completely that the legend is known by it, Majnun Layla, Layla's madman. The detail matters for the comparison with the play. Verona's lovers are destroyed by a feud between houses while remaining lucid to the end; Qays is destroyed by a diagnosis, a community's word for a love it could not accommodate, and madness, not enmity, is his story's engine.

### Q: Why does Layla speak so much less than Juliet?

Layla speaks less because the legend's design requires her to remain an icon rather than an agent, so that Majnun's madness can keep its purity as a solo performance of devotion. In the anecdotes and largely in Nizami's poem, her love is reported, her suffering described, and her death narrated, while her own words are few and her inner life reaches the audience through the lover's verses. Juliet, by contrast, argues the balcony scene, proposes the marriage, rebukes delay, faces the potion in a soliloquy that inventories her fears, and chooses her death. The asymmetry is not a defect in the legend but its premise: the Udhri tradition of chaste, fatal love measures the lover by the beloved's unattainability, and an articulate, scheming Layla would dissolve the measure. The consequence for the Arabic reception was practical. A stage that wanted a heroine who argued had to import one, and the imported play supplied what the native masterpiece withheld, a young woman whose speech changes the plot.

### Q: How does the ending of Layla and Majnun differ from the ending in Verona?

The legend ends without reconciliation, accounting, or civic consequence: Layla dies in Iraq, where her marriage had taken her, and Majnun is found dead at her grave in the wilderness, with verses attributed to him cut into a rock nearby. No elder is fined, no house is shamed into peace, no prince totals the cost. The community that named him madman grieves and continues, unindicted. Verona's ending is a tribunal by contrast. The Prince fines every party in the closing speech, the fathers compete in penitential statues, and the city is left with a glooming peace and a public ledger of loss. The difference decides what each story can be used to argue. A story ending in a tribunal can arraign a society, and Arab adapters borrowed the play for exactly that purpose in Jerusalem, Beirut, and Baghdad. A story ending at a desert grave can testify only about love itself, which is why the legend's adapters, from Shawqi onward, staged it as poetry rather than as politics.

### Q: What part does Layla's husband play in the legend's machinery?

Layla's husband is the legend's quiet proof that its obstacle is not a feud but a settlement: her family marries her to another man, named Ibn Salam in Nizami's telling, and the marriage simply stands, lawful and unchallenged, while Majnun withdraws to the wilderness. No rival house defends the match, no duel tests it, and the husband himself remains a shadow in the anecdotes, less a character than a social fact. The contrast with the play is structural. Shakespeare gives Juliet a suitor, Paris, who is a preference of her father's and a casualty of the plot, killed by Romeo in the tomb; the marriage to Paris is planned but never performed, and the performed marriage is the secret one to Romeo. The legend performs the unwanted marriage and forbids the wanted one a ceremony of any kind. That inversion explains the two stories' different temperatures. The play's catastrophe is an accident inside a race against time. The legend's catastrophe is a permanence, a life lived out beside the wrong marriage until death, first hers and then his, closes the account.

### Q: Why did Lebanese reviewers connect The Last Day with basketball?

Lebanese reviewers connected Akhir Yom with basketball because the production staged the country's sectarian geography through its most familiar public proxy, the rivalry between Beirut's two great basketball clubs, al-Hikmeh and al-Riyadi. A headline in al-Diyar reported that Oussama al-Rahbani's adaptation put the two clubs onstage, and the observation captured the localization's wit. The clubs' fixtures had carried, for decades, the charge of their support, one side historically anchored in Christian Beirut and the other in Muslim Beirut, so that a Lebanese audience needed no exposition to read the lovers' division in sporting allegiance. The feud, translated into a fixture list, became the form in which the country rehearsed its division safely and weekly, in stadiums, after a civil war fought over the same lines. Dima Sa'd, writing in al-Balad, supplied the reviewers' shared verdict in calling the piece a love story that exposes the fragility of the civil peace. The basketball was not decoration. It was the adaptation's thesis about how Lebanon stores its enmities, in games, until the games stop containing them.

### Q: What did the Rahbani family name bring to Akhir Yom?

The Rahbani name brought the production the authority of Lebanon's unofficial national theatre, the musical tradition built by Assi and Mansour Rahbani and sung by Fairuz, in which the country had imagined itself whole for decades. Oussama al-Rahbani, of the family's second generation, made in Akhir Yom the first Shakespeare adaptation by a member of the family, and the choice of material was itself legible to its audience: the tragedy of two houses, retold inside the form that had always promised one house, one village, one Lebanon. The family name therefore set the production's central tension before the curtain rose. A Rahbani evening conventionally moves toward communal harmony, and this plot ends in a tomb, and Yvette Khoury's study of the production reads it as questioning the civil peace rather than celebrating unity, an exposure staged from inside the genre of reassurance. The name also guaranteed the work a hearing that an unknown adapter could not have commanded. In Lebanon, a Rahbani premiere is a national occasion, and the tragedy of Verona received, in 2004, the closest equivalent the Arab stage offers to a state occasion, without any state being involved.

### Q: Who replaces the Prince at the end of the Baghdad production?

A history professor replaces the Prince at the end of Monadhil Daood's Romeo and Juliet in Baghdad, stepping into the closing scene to talk the two fathers into the reconciliation that Shakespeare assigns to public authority. The substitution is the adaptation's thesis in a single casting decision. In Verona, the lesson is pronounced by the ruler who failed to keep the peace, and its authority is the state's. In Daood's Baghdad, the state is absent or disgraced, an occupying power reduced to a soldier tending the old Montague's drip, and the only figure left with standing to address the fathers is the custodian of the past. A history professor reconciles where a prince judged. The change also reverses the direction of the original's ending. Shakespeare's Prince looks backward over the bodies and assigns blame in a shaken civic order. Daood's professor looks forward, arguing two exhausted men out of a feud whose costs the audience had spent the evening counting, in a city where the counting continued outside the theatre. Critics who found the ending schematic and critics who found it moving were responding to the same fact: the adaptation trusts education with the work the play gives to power.

### Q: How do the Baghdad lovers die, and what real event does the staging quote?

The Baghdad lovers die together in a suicide bombing, when Paris, recast as a foreign al-Qaeda militant, storms the church of Our Lady of Salvation, where Romeo and Juliet have taken refuge, and detonates a belt of explosives that kills both. The staging quotes a specific atrocity. On 31 October 2010, militants attacked that same Baghdad church during mass and killed forty-four worshippers and two priests, and Daood's climax restages the lovers' deaths inside the city's freshest wound. The departure from Shakespeare is total and deliberate. The play's deaths arise from private machinery, a letter gone astray, a potion's timing, a dagger and a cup of poison in a tomb, and their horror is the horror of accident. The adaptation abolishes accident. Its lovers are killed by the public violence that constitutes their world, on ground consecrated by a real massacre, and their deaths indict an order rather than a misunderstanding. Tim Arango's reporting from the Baghdad audience recorded spectators reading the scene as their own reality restaged, and the reading was available precisely because the address was real. A Verona tomb could hold two fictional bodies. The Baghdad church already held forty-six real ones.

### Q: Why did the 1994 Jerusalem production divide its households by language?

The 1994 production divided its households by language because the Khan Theatre of Jerusalem and the Al-Kasaba Theatre of Ramallah built the production as a joint act by two companies, and the division of tongues was the joint act made audible. The Montagues were played by Palestinian and Israeli-Arab actors speaking Arabic under Fuad Awad, the Capulets by Israeli actors speaking Hebrew under Eran Baniel, with the shared scenes directed by both men, so that Romeo, played by Khalifa Natour, and Juliet, played by Orna Katz, crossed a linguistic frontier every time they met. The design distributed understanding unevenly through the audience, and the unevenness was the meaning: each side of the house followed its own household in full and met the other across a barrier of partial comprehension, living inside the feud's structure. Freddie Rokem, the production's sharpest critic, objected that the casting announced its meaning too easily and privatized the tragedy into human interest. The production's interrupted rehearsals, halted after the Hebron massacre of February 1994 and resumed only after the full company met, stand as the record's answer: the language split was not a poster device but a working condition, sustained at cost.

### Q: What is forensic fascination, and why was the term applied to the Baghdad production?

Forensic fascination is the charge, framed by Margaret Litvin, Saffron Walkling, and Raphael Cormack in the journal Shakespeare, that a festival production of a war-zone classic can invite its audience to inspect another society's wounds as evidence, converting suffering into an exhibit. Surveying the Arabic productions of the 2012 Shakespeare Festival, the three scholars judged Romeo and Juliet in Baghdad the closest of the three to succumbing to that charge, and described the production as an attempt to use Shakespeare as a cultural bridge whose meanings had been engineered for two audiences at once, with nothing left open to misinterpretation. The production's performance notes, which addressed separate messages to British and to Iraqi spectators, gave the charge its documentary basis. The term is not a dismissal, and the scholars weigh the production's Baghdad life alongside its export life. It names a structural temptation of the festival economy, in which a company is rewarded for rendering its country's war legible to the countries watching it, and the legibility can flatten what it transmits. The Baghdad production remains the test case because it was received as testimony at home and as explanation abroad, and both receptions are documented.

### Q: How does an Arabic headline borrowing differ from an Arabic adaptation of the play?

An Arabic headline borrowing spends the play's prestige without risking anything the play contains, while an Arabic adaptation wagers a company's standing on a public argument built from the play's machinery. The borrowing is the journalistic formula that captions any two lovers divided by sect, village, family, or politics as an Arab Romeo and Juliet. It requires of its user no knowledge beyond the lovers' names and their deaths, and its information concerns the play's fame rather than the couple in the story. The adaptation, by contrast, produces evidence in proportion to its archive: Daood's Baghdad text, the Khan and Al-Kasaba companies' interrupted rehearsals, al-Rahbani's basketball coding in Beirut. An adaptation can be checked against the local reality it maps and against the play it rebuilds, and it can fail those checks in instructive ways. A borrowing can only be repeated. The Arabic case sharpens the distinction because Arabic journalism disposes of a second formula, Qays and Layla, drawn from the native legend, so that every borrowed caption is also a choice between a foreign passport and a domestic one, and the choice itself is data about the borrower's cultural politics.
