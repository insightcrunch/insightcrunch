Disability studies comes to Romeo and Juliet looking for a disabled hero and finds none, and that absence is the beginning of its argument rather than the end of it. No character in Verona carries a lasting impairment of the kind the criticism of Richard III is built on. What the play has instead is a city that processes bodies without pause: it diagnoses them, doses them, seals them indoors, prices their starvation, misreads their pulses, carries them wounded off the square, lays them out for inspection, and finally casts the two most famous of them in gold. The verdict of this article is that disability studies earns its place among the play's critical schools by shifting the object of study from the impaired body to the disabling arrangement. Verona disables. Its medicine names love a sickness and a daughter's refusal a disease; its law makes the sale of relief a capital crime and then starves the seller; its plague orders disable a healthy friar by locking a door; its households read a drugged girl as a corpse and act on the reading. The lovers die less from anything wrong inside their bodies than from the machinery Verona builds around bodies, and the play says so in its own vocabulary of sickness, physic, poison, and cure.

![The bodies of Verona under the disability lens, the drugged, wounded, and gilded figures of the tragedy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-05.webp)

The argument proceeds by the play's own sequence of managements, because Shakespeare arranged the bodies in an order that is itself instructive. Act 1 diagnoses: a son's melancholy observed and reported, a daughter's age computed by her nurse. Act 2 prescribes: the eye's infection treated by a rival infection, the blood's disorder treated by a wedding. Act 3 wounds and sentences: a fatal scratch in the square, a bridegroom's temper read in his tears, an exile imposed on the body that dealt the wound. Act 4 counterfeits: a corpse manufactured to specification and certified by the household that commissioned the reading. Act 5 inspects and disposes: a messenger disabled by suspicion, a poison purchased from a starved body, corpses laid out for the city's examination, monuments promised in gold. Diagnosis, prescription, wound, counterfeit, inquest: the disability reading follows that order because it is the order in which Verona itself processes flesh into meaning, and because each stage hands the next its instruments.

That claim needs discipline, because the approach arrives with a genuine anachronism problem and a thin shelf of work on this particular play, and the honest version of the reading states both before it spends either. Disability as an identity, a politics, and a name for a class of persons belongs to the modern world; the word's legal and medical senses were assembled centuries after the play. The reading offered here therefore applies the field's questions to the play's documented bodies and marks the joins where application replaces citation. Where a disability scholar has written on the play itself, the article says so. Where the field's instruments are carried to Verona from work on other plays and other centuries, the article says that too, because a reading that conceals its scaffolding deserves the objection it will meet below.

## What Disability Studies Is, and What It Finds in Verona

Disability studies began as an argument about where disability lives. The medical model, the default of clinics and of most older criticism, locates disability inside a body: a defect to be diagnosed, treated, cured, or pitied. The social model, stated by the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation in 1976 and named by Michael Oliver in the years that followed, relocates it: impairment may be a fact of a body, but disability is the disadvantage imposed on that body by stairs, statutes, stares, and arrangements that assume a standard shape. Tobin Siebers complicated the pair in Disability Theory (2008) with the idea of complex embodiment, holding that bodies and societies write each other in both directions, that a body's conditions have social causes and social effects at once. Rosemarie Garland-Thomson, in Extraordinary Bodies (1997), named the figure against whom difference is measured the normate, the unmarked body a culture imagines when it imagines nobody in particular, and she made the stare the central transaction between that figure and the body it finds strange. Ato Quayson, in Aesthetic Nervousness (2007), argued that disability's appearance in a text disturbs the text's own representational calm, forcing a crisis in the forms that try to contain it. David T. Mitchell and Sharon L. Snyder, in Narrative Prosthesis (2000), gave the school its most portable instrument for literary work: stories, they argued, lean on disability as a crutch for characterization and plot, using the impaired body as a device that signifies disorder, and then resolve the story by curing, killing, or expelling the body that carried the meaning.

David Houston Wood, surveying the field for Shakespeareans in Literature Compass in 2011, sorted the representations into three registers that map onto Verona with uncomfortable ease: demonstrable disability, the visible kind, a limp or a wound; covert disability, the hidden kind, chronic pain or an inner condition; and the performance history of disability, the record of what stages have done with bodies. Romeo and Juliet is poor in the first register and rich in the other two. Its demonstrable bodies are temporary ones, wounds and corpses. Its covert bodies are the play's engine: a lovesick young man whose sickness is invisible except as behaviour, a girl whose refusal is diagnosed as a disease of the blood, a messenger disabled by an order rather than an illness. And its performance record is where the field's most concrete work on this play exists, a point the next paragraphs make plain.

Candour about the shelf comes first, because the run of scholarship this article belongs to has made a habit of distinguishing landmarks from legends. The flagship volume of early modern disability studies, Recovering Disability in Early Modern England (2013), edited by Allison P. Hobgood and David Houston Wood, ranges across Spenser's dwarfs, Aphra Behn's medical satire, jest-book humour about impairment, madness in revenge tragedy, performed blindness, and Richard III recast in postcommunist Prague; it contains no essay on Romeo and Juliet. Katherine Schaap Williams, in Unfixable Forms (2021), reads Richard III, the lame soldier of the citizen plays, the counterfeit cripple of The Fair Maid of the Exchange, and the feigned sickness of the city comedies; the lovers of Verona are not among her cases. Lindsey Row-Heyveld, in Dissembling Disability in Early Modern English Drama (2018), counts more than forty plays in which able-bodied characters counterfeit disability and gives her chapters to Marston, to Fair Em, to Richard III, and to Jonson's city; this play is not one of her forty. Sonya Freeman Loftis, in Shakespeare and Disability Studies (2021), the Oxford Shakespeare Topics volume that argues disability theory is essential equipment for readers, teachers, and directors, builds its cases on Richard III, on accessible performance spaces, on Shakespeare therapy, and on a documentary about a retirement community staging A Midsummer Night's Dream; Romeo and Juliet receives no chapter. The pattern is consistent and worth stating flatly: the field's landmark books pass this play by, because the play offers them no Richard, no Falstaff of the body, no figure whose impairment organizes a plot from within.

What exists on the play itself is of two kinds, and the article keeps them separate. The first is a documented strand of attention to the play's disability language. Ria Cheyne's Disability, Literature, Genre (2019), a study of how disability representation interacts with literary kind, is the work teachers of the play cite for the pattern in its love language: the lovers' passion is figured, persistently, through metaphors of disability, blindness and madness laid on Romeo above all, and Cheyne's argument about affect explains what those metaphors purchase, an emotional pressure on audiences that the romance plot spends at the tomb. The second kind is practice. CRIPtic Arts, the company led by the artist Jamie Hale, built its Cripping Breath project with the University of Sheffield around this play, centring performers who use ventilators and taking breath as the production's governing question, because the text is saturated with it, from the fume of sighs in the first act to the Friar's specification for the potion, "No warmth, no breath, shall testify thou livest" (4.1). Hale's company seizes on the Nurse's line to Juliet, the question of a working body asked to keep performing, and reads the tragedy as a story about young people fighting for self-definition against families that manage their bodies for them. A disability-led Romeo and Juliet exists; a disability monograph on Romeo and Juliet does not. The reading that follows is therefore a labelled application of the field's verified instruments, Mitchell and Snyder's prosthesis, the social model, Garland-Thomson's stare, Siebers's complex embodiment, to the play's documented bodies, with the joins showing.

One more piece of the field's record belongs in the foundation, because it converts the article's thesis from an analogy into a history. Early modern England was building the legal category of disability during the play's own decades, and it was building the category out of suspicion. The poor relief statutes of the 1590s and the great act of 1601 sorted the poor by body: the impotent poor, the lame, the blind, the aged, those unable to work, were licensed to receive relief, while the sturdy beggar, whose body was judged capable, was whipped and set to labour. Row-Heyveld's research argues that the era's panic over counterfeit disability, the fear that the lame and blind at the church door were actors, drove the drafting of these distinctions and produced something like a first legal definition of disability in England, a definition written by men trying to catch frauds. Simone Chess, in the Hobgood and Wood volume, reconstructs the performed blindness of popular print and stage, the ballad blind men and counterfeit blind beggars, and shows a culture rehearsing its suspicion in entertainment before applying it at the parish door. The relevance to Verona is structural. The play's world, like the statutes' world, is one in which a body's classification decides its treatment, in which the classification is performed by interested readers, fathers, officers, princes, and in which the cost of a wrong classification falls on the classified. Juliet is sorted as sick when she is disobedient, as dead when she is asleep; Friar John is sorted as infectious when he is healthy; the Apothecary is sorted as a criminal tradesman when he is a starving one. The disability reading of the play is not importing a foreign machinery of classification. It is reading the play in the decades when that machinery was being assembled, in the play's own city of statutes as much as in its city of tombs.

The primer's rows share a shape, and the shape is the article's thesis in miniature. In every row a body in some condition meets a reading imposed by the surrounding order, and the gap between the condition and the reading is where the damage is done. The lovesick boy is not harmed by his sighs; he is harmed by being handed a disease and a cure that consists of a new object for the eye. The drugged girl is not harmed by the potion, which works exactly as specified; she is harmed by a household that reads stillness as death and buries what it reads. The healthy friar is not harmed by plague; he is harmed by the order that treats suspicion as infection. Disability studies, applied with its joins showing, is the school that teaches a reader to look for the gap.

## Lovesickness: The Diagnosis the Play Opens With

The first body the play presents for inspection is Romeo's, and it is presented as a case. Montague describes his son's behaviour the way a family reports symptoms to a physician: the young man shuns company, steals home at dawn, locks himself in a darkened room, and makes, in his father's phrase, an artificial night (1.1). Benvolio is dispatched to take a history. Romeo, examined, produces the period's textbook presentation of love melancholy and names it in an oxymoron that is also a diagnosis: "O heavy lightness, serious vanity, / Mis-shapen chaos of well-seeming forms, / Feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health, / Still-waking sleep, that is not what it is" (1.1). Sick health is the whole condition in two words, a body functioning as its own opposite, and the speech that contains it is a catalogue of a system at war with itself. The series' broader account of [disease and the body in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/06/21/disease-and-body-imagery-analysis/) inventories this imagery across the play; the disability reading asks a narrower question of the same material, which is what the diagnosis does to the person carrying it.

Early modern medicine took lovesickness seriously as a somatic disease, and the play's first audiences had access to a clinical literature that a disability reading needs on the table. Robert Burton devoted the Third Partition of The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) to love melancholy, cataloguing its symptoms, its progress from admiration through fixation to despair, and its cures, which ranged from distraction and hard work to the last remedy of obtaining the beloved. Jacques Ferrand's treatise on lovesickness, published in French and Englished in 1640 as Erotomania, treated erotic melancholy as a disorder of the imagination with bodily signs a physician could read: sunken eyes, a changed complexion, sighing, insomnia, wasting. The disease had a physiology. In the humoral scheme the play assumes, desire overheated the blood, the overheated blood disturbed the animal spirits, and the disturbance printed itself on the surface of the body where observers could diagnose it. Romeo's friends and family behave exactly like people with this literature behind them. They observe, they confer, they prescribe.

### What does the Friar see written on Romeo's cheeks?

The Friar sees a complexion, and he reads it as a chart: "What a deal of brine hath wash'd thy sallow cheeks for Rosaline!" (2.3). Sallow is a clinical word in the period, the drained colour of a body whose humours are out of order, and the exclamation is a diagnosis delivered at sight.

Before Romeo has said a word about his state, his face has testified. The brine of tears has washed the colour out; the skin reports what the patient has not. In the disability frame, the moment matters because it shows the play's bodies under continuous interpretation: Romeo does not have to report his condition, because his skin reports it for him, and every practitioner he meets, friend, friar, father, reads the same page and prescribes from it.

The prescriptions are the disability reading's real subject, because they show a culture managing an unwanted inner state by treating the body that displays it. Benvolio's cure is the period's doctrine of contrary infection, stated as pharmacy: "Take thou some new infection to thy eye, / And the rank poison of the old will die" (1.2). The eye is the organ of the disease, love entering by sight in the optical theory the period inherited, and the cure is a controlled second infection that displaces the first. Mercutio's medicine is ridicule, the friend's physic of mockery, an attempt to laugh the patient out of the fit. The Friar's cure is marriage, solemnized and sacramental, the one prescription that works, and it works by changing Romeo's legal and social state rather than his humours. Three practitioners, three theories, one patient who never asked to be cured of anything except exclusion from Rosaline. The disability lens names this pattern without importing it: a difference of feeling is medicalized, the medicalization licenses intervention, and the interventions escalate until one of them, the marriage, rearranges the patient's world. That Romeo's sickness is figurative, a way the period talked about desire, is the objection the trial section takes up in full; the point here is that inside the play the figure is treated as fact, with consequences as material as any in the tragedy.

Ferrand's version of the disease sharpens the play's logic in one further respect, because he locates lovesickness in the imagination, the inner faculty that receives and stores images. The lover's imagination, corrupted by fixation, holds the beloved's image before the mind without rest, and the body's disorders follow from the picture that will not leave. The doctrine explains the shape of Verona's cures. If the disease is a fixed image, the treatments are image treatments: Benvolio's new infection for the eye, a rival picture to displace the stored one; the Friar's solemnized marriage, which legitimizes the picture and moves it from fantasy into household fact; even Mercutio's mockery, an attempt to make the stored image ridiculous so the faculty releases it. Burton's catalogue of remedies runs in the same channel, company, occupation, travel, the sight of the beloved's defects, anything that loosens the image's grip. Romeo's tragedy, in the clinical frame the period supplies, is that every cure works on the image and none works on the world the image must live in. The fixation transfers from Rosaline to Juliet in a single evening, the disease's form intact with a new object installed, and the physicians of Verona congratulate themselves on a recovery that has only changed the patient's address. The disability reading takes the clinical frame seriously precisely here: a diagnosis that can be cured by substitution was never describing the patient's body. It was describing the observers' need for the patient's desire to point where they aimed it.

Cheyne's account of the love language belongs here, because the metaphors accumulate precisely where the diagnosis is applied. Romeo's desire is figured as blindness, the lover who cannot see the woman in front of him because an absent one fills his eye; as madness, the distraction his father reports and his friends tease; as wounds and scars, in his own proverb at the orchard wall, "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" (2.2). The metaphors do the work Garland-Thomson assigns to the stare: they mark the lover as a body out of true, an object of fascinated, corrective attention, and they invite the audience to share the correctors' viewpoint. A romance hero is being processed as a patient. The disability reading restores what the processing conceals, which is that the disease model is doing political work. To call Romeo sick is to make his disobedience, his withdrawal from the feud's economy of young men, a symptom rather than a choice, and symptoms can be treated without being answered.

## Queen Mab and the Dreaming Body

Between the diagnosis of Act 1 and the prescriptions of Act 2 stands the play's strangest piece of physiology, Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, and the disability reading claims it as the taxonomy of Verona's sleeping bodies. Mab, the fairies' midwife, drives her chariot over sleepers and assigns each a dream fitted to a station: courtiers dream on curtsies, lawyers on fees, ladies on kisses, soldiers on cutting foreign throats. The catalogue is a humoral census of the city at rest, every trade and rank receiving the dream its waking appetite has prepared, the body in sleep continuing the body's economy by other means. Two entries in the catalogue carry the disability charge. Mab plagues ladies' lips with blisters because their breaths are tainted with sweetmeats (1.4), disease visited on the organ of kissing as a moral hygiene of the mouth. And Mab herself is the hag who, when maids lie on their backs, presses them and learns them first to bear (1.4), the nightmare of folk medicine made literal, the crushing visitation in the night that the period knew as the mare, the pressure on the chest that modern sleep medicine files as paralysis and the early moderns filed as assault. Mercutio delivers all of it as a party trick, an attempt to laugh Romeo's dreaming into proportion, and Romeo stops him with the tragedy's thesis in miniature: "Thou talk'st of nothing" (1.4). The speech is the play's dream-life in one catalogue, and its physiology is exact about the thing the disability reading tracks. Even asleep, Verona's bodies are being processed, dreamed on, plagued, pressed, each assigned the night its day has earned. There is no state in the play, waking, sleeping, drugged, or dead, in which a body is left unread. The dreams are diagnoses the sleepers give themselves.

## Green Sickness: A Daughter's Refusal, Reclassified

If Romeo's sickness is the disease of a son who will not fight, Juliet's is the disease of a daughter who will not marry, and the play gives it a name with a clinical history of its own. Romeo introduces the term as a figure for virginity itself, urging the moon to abandon her service: "Her vestal livery is but sick and green, / And none but fools do wear it. Cast it off" (2.2). The livery of the unmarried state is already coloured as illness in the lover's mouth. Capulet completes the reclassification when his daughter refuses Paris: "Out, you green-sickness carrion! Out, you baggage! / You tallow-face!" (3.5). Green sickness, chlorosis in the medical books, was described by Johannes Lange in a letter of 1554 as the disease of virgins, a condition of unmarried girls marked by a pale greenish complexion, breathlessness, and disordered appetite, and the prescription the tradition attached to it was marriage. The diagnosis and the cure together form a closed instrument. A girl who declines the husband chosen for her displays the symptom the books assign to her unmarried state; the treatment for the symptom is the marriage she declined; refusal becomes evidence for the remedy refusal was resisting.

### Why does Capulet reach for a diagnosis when his daughter refuses?

Because a diagnosis ends an argument that a command was losing. In the same scene Capulet has threatened force, dragging her on a hurdle to the church, and disowned her in advance: "An you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5). A disease name does quieter and stronger work than either threat.

The threat expels the disobedient body into the street economy of beggary, the economy the period's poor laws policed by sorting bodies into the impotent, who could not work and deserved relief, and the sturdy, who could and deserved punishment. The disease name does its work indoors. It converts a political act, a daughter's no, into a medical fact, a daughter's sickness, and medical facts are not negotiated with. They are treated, by the father who holds the prescription pad.

The disability reading of green sickness is the strongest single exhibit for the approach in this play, because nothing here is metaphor borrowed from elsewhere; the term is a diagnosis inside the text, applied by a character, with a documented clinical meaning and a documented social function. Editors gloss the phrase for modern readers, and René Weis, in his Arden Third Series edition of 2012, annotates the play's medical vocabulary with the period's own senses, green sickness as the anaemia supposed to afflict virgins and marriage as its standing remedy. The gloss is the medical model in miniature: identify the condition, name the cure, move on. What the gloss cannot carry, and what the disability frame restores, is the diagnosis's convenience. Juliet in Act 3 is not pale, breathless, or disordered in appetite; she is a married woman concealing a marriage, refusing a bigamous second match on grounds any confessor would recognize. The disease is assigned to her body at the exact moment her body becomes disobedient, and it is assigned by the man whose authority the disobedience challenges. A condition that appears when power needs it and prescribes the outcome power wanted is not a discovery about the girl. It is an instrument of the household, and disability studies, which was founded to notice instruments of exactly this shape, notices it.

The diagnosis also has a symmetry the play builds deliberately, and the symmetry is the household's confession. Green sickness and lovesickness were sister conditions in the period's medicine, twin disorders of blockage: the bachelor's melancholy, in which desire with no lawful outlet corrupted the blood and the imagination, and the virgin's chlorosis, in which the same retention, in the female body, stopped the courses and greened the skin. Both were prescribed the same pharmacopoeia of release, company, occupation, distraction, and at the last, marriage. Verona applies both halves of the system in a single plot. Romeo's blockage is treated as a misfortune to be sympathized with and cured by substitution; Juliet's is treated as a crime to be cursed and cured by surrender. The medicine is identical. The moral charge is opposite, and the variable is not the body but the obedience. A son's disorder is a phase; a daughter's is a pollution, carrion, a thing already rotting. The disability reading draws the conclusion the symmetry forces: the play's pathology is a technology for grading identical conditions by the sufferer's compliance, and the diagnoses tell a reader less about the patients than about what the household needed each patient to become. Juliet, it should be recorded, is the healthiest body in the play by any functional measure, the quickest mind, the steadiest hand, the only person in Verona whose decisions are executed exactly as planned. The disease is assigned to the one body that displays no disorder, at the moment that body declines its assignment. A criticism that cannot see the joke is not reading the chart.

Lennard J. Davis's warning belongs in this section because green sickness is where the anachronism objection has its best case and its limit. Davis argued in Enforcing Normalcy (1995) that the concept of the normal body is a modern statistical invention, unknown to cultures that measured bodies against an ideal rather than an average. Verona has no average body and no statistics. What it has is complexion, the humoral balance that shows in a face, and a set of named deviations, lovesickness, green sickness, melancholy, madness, each with its signs and its prescribed management. Davis wins the vocabulary: a reading that speaks of Verona's normalcy as if the city kept charts of the normal misdescribes its world. The reading loses if it stops there, because the machinery Davis's history reserves for later centuries, the classification of bodies into the sound and the disordered, the treatment of classification as nature, the prescription of social arrangements as cures, is demonstrably running in the play under humoral names. The names differ. The work the names do is the same work, and the field's question survives its own vocabulary.

## Madness: The Condition the Play Fears but Never Houses

Madness is the disability the play talks about most and embodies least, and the discrepancy is itself a finding. No character in Romeo and Juliet is mad in the way Lear is mad or Ophelia is mad; the condition exists in Verona as an accusation, a fear, and a figure of speech, never as a settled state the audience is asked to witness. The Friar, finding Romeo prostrate in his cell, reaches for the vocabulary as a scold: the young man is a fond madman, his behaviour the unreasonable fury of a beast, his form crying out that he is a man while his tears declare him womanish (3.3). Madness here is a failure of self-government assigned by an authority figure to a body that will not compose itself, and it is assigned in the same scene where the Friar is managing the consequences of his own prescription. Romeo's reported distraction in Act 1, the withdrawal and the artificial night, is madness's milder cousin in the period's nosology, melancholy tipping toward the frenzy the physicians warned it could become. The lovers' enemies and friends use the words the way Verona uses all its diagnoses, to make an unwelcome state of feeling into a condition that licenses management.

Juliet's soliloquy before the potion gives madness its one sustained imaginative habitation in the play, and it is habitation as horror. What she fears in the vault is not death but waking wrong: "O, if I wake, shall I not be distraught, / Environed with all these hideous fears? / And madly play with my forefathers' joints? / And pluck the mangled Tybalt from his shroud? / And, in this rage, with some great kinsman's bone, / As with a club, dash out my desperate brains?" (4.3). The fantasy is precise about what madness means to her: the loss of the self's government, the body turned puppet of its terrors, violence done by her own hands to her own skull with a dead man's bone for an instrument. She drinks the potion anyway, which measures the fear's rank in her accounting: madness is the worst outcome she can imagine, worse than the poison being real, and she accepts the risk of it as the price of refusing the marriage. A disability reading marks the hierarchy without flinching from it. In the play's imagination, madness is not a condition a person lives with, negotiates, or survives; it is the absolute of lost personhood, the state in which Juliet would stop being Juliet while her body continued. The Friar's scolding of Romeo gives the period's other figure for the condition: madness as animality. The unreasonable fury of a beast is the charge, and it is delivered over a young man lying prostrate on the cell floor, a posture the Friar reads as the symptom of the demotion he names, the human form emptied of the reason that governs it, tears where argument should be. Early modern culture housed its mad in the register of the animal without embarrassment, chaining the furious, exhibiting the distracted, and the play's cell scene works in that register at the level of insult: to be unmanaged by reason is to be reclassified downward, out of the species whose bodies are answered with words and into the kind whose bodies are answered with restraint. Romeo's reply is the patient's refusal of the classification, an insistence that the Friar cannot speak of what he does not feel (3.3), feeling itself being precisely the faculty the diagnosis had written off. The period's terror of the condition, the chained and exhibited mad of the hospitals' visiting galleries, stands behind the speech, and the speech is honest about inheriting it.

The field's own record on madness clarifies what the play is and is not doing. Row-Heyveld's contribution to the Hobgood and Wood volume, on antic dispositions in revenge tragedy, studies feigned madness as the counterfeit tradition's mental branch, the sane revenger performing distraction as a licence to speak and act. Shakespeare's great feigners, Hamlet above all, belong to that tradition; Romeo and Juliet conspicuously does not use it. Nobody in Verona pretends to be mad, because madness in this play confers no licence at all. It confers management. The distinction exposes the tragedy's economy of conditions with unusual clarity: feigned death, the play's one great counterfeit, works because death is a state Verona honours with ceremony and gold; feigned madness would fail because madness is a state Verona answers with confinement and scolding. Loftis's volume gives the mental branch of the field its own chapter, on neurodiversity and mental disability in performance, arguing that Shakespeare's stage has become a site where minds that work otherwise are represented by the people who have them. Against that horizon the play's treatment reads as a limit case the field should record plainly: a tragedy whose heroine can imagine madness only as self-annihilation, and whose hero is called mad only as a rebuke, is a play in which mental disability exists purely as threat. The disability reading does not convict the play for the limit; the limit is the period's, and the speech in the vault is the period thinking at full stretch. What the reading restores is the recognition that the threat does work in the plot. Juliet drinks because the alternative the household offers, bigamy under her father's curse, ranks below the risk of madness in her scale of horrors. The fear of the condition is a load-bearing member of the catastrophe, and criticism that treats the speech as gothic decoration misses the weight it carries.

## Two Pharmacies: The Garden, the Shop, and the Body That Poverty Wrote

Verona's medicine is practised in two establishments, and the disability reading sets them side by side because the contrast between them is a contrast between two kinds of body. The Friar works in a garden at dawn, gathering herbs, and his opening speech is the play's pharmacological charter: "O, mickle is the powerful grace that lies / In herbs, plants, stones, and their true qualities" (2.3). The same plant holds poison and medicine: "Poison hath residence and medicine power" in a single flower, and the doubleness is a moral physiology, because "two such opposèd kings encamp them still / In man as well as herbs, grace and rude will" (2.3). The series' study of [the Friar's herbs, poison, and cure in Verona](/2022/10/28/friar-laurence-herbs-poison-cure/) follows that speech through the plot; the disability reading attends instead to the practitioner and his patient population. The Friar's pharmacy is charitable, learned, unpriced. He dispenses to the great houses without fee, his authority is spiritual as much as medical, and his great prescription, the potion, is given as pastoral care to a girl in despair. His is medicine as the medical model imagines itself, knowledge applied to suffering by a licensed, benevolent expert.

The Apothecary practises in Mantua, in exile's city, and Romeo finds him by reading his body before he reads his shop. The shop's inventory is a museum of the body trade: "And in his needy shop a tortoise hung, / An alligator stuff'd, and other skins / Of ill-shaped fishes" (5.1), dried specimens and empty boxes, the apparatus of healing displayed by a man healing has abandoned. Romeo's salutation is a diagnosis delivered with the Friar's confidence and none of his charity: the man's looks are meagre, and "sharp misery had worn him to the bones" (5.1). Here is the play's one body whose condition is neither figurative nor temporary. The Apothecary is starved. His impairment is written by hunger into his frame, visible at a glance, and Romeo reads it expertly and uses it as the lever of the transaction: "The world is not thy friend nor the world's law; / The world affords no law to make thee rich; / Then be not poor, but break it, and take this" (5.1). The law in question is Mantua's statute making the sale of such poison a capital offence. The state's medicine is a prohibition; the market's answer is a price set by the buyer's knowledge that the seller's body cannot afford refusal. The Apothecary states the whole political economy in one line: "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1). Romeo's echo seals it: "I pay thy poverty, and not thy will" (5.1).

### What does the Apothecary's shop sell besides poison?

It sells the spectacle of the practitioner's own ruined body, and the spectacle is part of the merchandise. The stuffed alligator, the tortoise, the ill-shaped fishes are advertisements, a claim that the shop commands nature's rarities; the practitioner's bones, showing through his misery, advertise the opposite truth, that the trade cannot feed its trader.

A customer reads both displays and prices his offer accordingly. Romeo's forty ducats are calibrated not to the poison's rarity but to the seller's hunger, which is why the transaction belongs in a disability reading: the impairment produced by poverty is the mechanism of the plot's decisive purchase, the hinge on which the tomb scene turns.

Mitchell and Snyder's narrative prosthesis explains the Apothecary's narrative function, and the materialist wing of the field explains what the prosthesis account leaves out. As prosthesis, the starved tradesman is a device: his body's disorder signifies the world's disorder, a city where law starves the healer and gold buys death, and the story discards him once the meaning is delivered. He has no name, no second scene, no fate the play records. As material fact, he is the play's most honest piece of social evidence, a working body disabled by the arrangements the social model studies, wages, prices, prohibitions, and his consent is manufactured by his condition exactly as the model's founders described. The series' reading of [poison and medicine as metaphor](/2024/03/29/poison-and-medicine-metaphor-analysis/) tracks the substances; the disability reading tracks the seller. Both are needed, and the trial section below weighs which account governs which stratum of the play. For the transaction in Mantua, the material account governs: there is no metaphor in a starved man, and a criticism that converts his hunger into a symbol before it has registered his hunger repeats Verona's own habit of reading bodies as signs instead of treating them as lives.

The two pharmacies also divide the play's cures by the bodies they are allowed to treat. The Friar's patient is a daughter of the great house, and his medicine is free, secret, and aimed at restoring her to a marriage the city can bless. The Apothecary's patient is an exile with gold, and his medicine is priced, capital, and aimed at ending a body the city has expelled. Between them stands the law, which licenses the cleric's pharmacology as care and criminalizes the tradesman's as murder, though the Friar's vial comes nearer to killing its patient than the Apothecary's poison, which merely does what it was sold to do. Disability studies has a term for this distribution: it is the medical model's institutional face, in which the same act, administering a powerful drug to a body, is therapy or crime depending on the credentials of the hand and the status of the body receiving it.

Banishment, the sentence that places Romeo in the Apothecary's city, deserves its reading in this section, because the play presents exile as a disablement of exactly the social model's kind. Romeo hears the sentence as an amputation from the world: "There is no world without Verona walls, / But purgatory, torture, hell itself" (3.3). The Friar's consolation treats the sentence as an administrative fact with administrative remedies, a wide world, a future recall, a friend's advocacy. Romeo's account treats it as the loss of the body's enabling arrangements, the streets it knows, the air of the orchard, the sight of Juliet, everything that made his body a citizen's body rather than a carcass's. The play adjudicates between the two accounts by event. In Mantua the banished body is precisely what Romeo feared, a body without standing, shopping for death in a shop the law has made lethal, dependent for its one decisive transaction on another disabled body, the starved tradesman whose own arrangements have failed him in the mirror image of Romeo's. Exile and starvation meet in the shop as the two products of the same civic machinery, the city disabling the bodies it expels and the bodies it starves with the same instruments, sentence and statute, and the poison changes hands between them as naturally as a greeting.

## Ill-Shaped Fishes: Monstrosity and the Culture of the Specimen

The Apothecary's shop window deserves a section of its own, because the specimens in it, the tortoise, the stuffed alligator, the skins of ill-shaped fishes, belong to a culture of bodily display that the disability reading needs to reconstruct before it can judge Verona's habits of looking. Early modern England consumed nonstandard bodies as signs. Monstrous births were reported in pamphlets that sold at fairs and markets, the child with conjoined limbs or the calf with two heads rendered in woodcut and glossed as a wonder, a warning, a text from heaven written in flesh. The period's word monster carried its Latin ancestry on its face, a showing, a thing exhibited to be read. Physicians collected anomalies; apothecaries and barber-surgeons displayed preserved rarities to advertise command over nature's extremes; the shop window and the pamphlet page were the era's two galleries of the nonstandard body, and both charged admission in attention. Schaap Williams's final chapter in Unfixable Forms, on making the monster, traces how the stage took up this culture of exhibition, putting deformed and prodigious bodies before audiences whose looking was itself part of the event. The theatre was the third gallery, and its exhibits breathed.

Romeo reads the Mantuan window as a connoisseur of that culture. His inventory is appreciative, exact about the specimens' shapeliness or its absence, and the ill-shaped fishes do the same work in his description that the wonder did in the pamphlets: they certify that this shop keeps company with nature's irregular productions, and they prepare the scene's deeper transaction, in which a body even more irregular, the seller's starved frame, will be read and priced by the same connoisseur's eye. The sequence matters to the disability reading because it shows interpretation flowing downhill along a gradient of display. The fishes are exhibited to be wondered at and mean nothing in themselves; the tradesman is exhibited by his poverty and means whatever his customer needs him to mean; Juliet will be exhibited in the vault and mean death to every reader who files past. Verona's looking is a technology with settings, wonder, pity, suspicion, certainty, and the settings are chosen by the lookers. Garland-Thomson's stare is usually discussed as a response to a person encountered; the play extends it into an economy, in which whole institutions, the pamphlet trade, the apothecary's window, the cathedral vault of the Capulets, exist to stage bodies for interpretation and to charge the interpreted body with the cost.

Romeo's own earliest self-description belongs in this gallery, and the disability reading claims it as the moment the hero hangs himself in the specimen case. "Mis-shapen chaos of well-seeming forms" (1.1) is a diagnosis in the monster register: a chaos, a formlessness, whose forms only seem well, shapes that promise order and deliver confusion. The lover imagines his inner state as a prodigy, a body wrongly assembled, and he exhibits the prodigy in oxymorons for Benvolio's inspection the way a pamphlet exhibits a woodcut. The period's audiences were trained by the wonder literature to read such assemblages as messages. What messages they read in Romeo they decided by his story's end, when the mis-shapen chaos had resolved, in the city's official account, into golden forms whose seeming is never again questioned. The trajectory from specimen to statue, from the wrongly shaped body exhibited for interpretation to the perfectly shaped body exhibited for reverence, is the play's deepest arc in the disability frame, and it is an arc about looking rather than about flesh. Nothing in the lovers' bodies changes as radically as the setting of the city's stare: wonder at the chaos, suspicion of the sleep, certainty at the corpse, reverence before the gold. The bodies stay bodies. The readings do the travelling, and the deaths happen where a reading arrives too early and is acted on as fact.

## The Counterfeit Corpse: A Body the City Agrees Not to Read

Juliet's drugged body in Acts 4 and 5 is the play's most sustained exercise in the social construction of a bodily state, and it is the section where the field's instruments fit the text with the least forcing. The Friar's instructions to Juliet are a specification sheet for a corpse, itemized by the signs a body must stop giving: "When presently through all thy veins shall run / A cold and drowsy humour, for no pulse / Shall keep his native progress, but surcease; / No warmth, no breath, shall testify thou livest; / The roses in thy lips and cheeks shall fade / To paly ashes; thy eyes' windows fall, / Like death, when he shuts up the day of life; / Each part, depriv'd of supple government, / Shall, stiff and stark and cold, appear like death; / And in this borrow'd likeness of shrunk death / Thou shalt continue two and forty hours, / And then awake as from a pleasant sleep" (4.1). Pulse, breath, colour, suppleness: the list is a checklist of the evidence by which the period certified death, and the potion is engineered to falsify every item on it. The plan the series examines in its scene study of [Act 4 Scene 1 and the Friar's desperate potion plan](/2023/10/20/act-4-scene-1-potion-plan/) depends on a profound assumption the disability reading isolates: that a body is what its readers certify it to be, and that certification can be gamed by controlling the signs.

### What makes Juliet's stillness a performance rather than an illness?

The direction of the deception. An illness overtakes a body from within and announces itself; Juliet's stillness is composed and rehearsed, adopted as an act of will by a girl who runs through every failure mode she can imagine in soliloquy before she drinks: the vial's fraudulence, the terror of waking early among the bones, the madness she fears (4.3).

The state is real, pharmacologically induced, and yet its meaning is entirely theatrical, because its purpose is to be read. In the soliloquy she rehearses the performance against its worst reviews, waking too soon, waking mad, playing with her forefathers' joints and dashing out her desperate brains with a kinsman's bone, and drinks anyway, with a toast to Romeo. Juliet performs death for an audience of readers, her parents, her nurse, the bridegroom, the whole household, and the performance works because the audience reads exactly as the script requires.

Row-Heyveld's Dissembling Disability supplies the tradition this performance belongs to, and the article marks the join plainly: her forty plays counterfeit blindness, lameness, madness, foolishness, and deformity, and Juliet's counterfeit is of death, the limiting case, not an item in Row-Heyveld's census. The tradition's logic nevertheless governs the scene. Row-Heyveld argues that the counterfeit-disability plays taught audiences a double lesson about nonstandard bodies, that they are pitiful and that they are probably fraudulent, and that the suspicion the stage cultivated spilled into the street, where genuinely impaired people were pressed to prove their impairments were real. Shakespeare inverts the lesson's direction. His counterfeit is undetectable, and the fraudulence belongs not to the body but to the readers, who are so certain of their competence that they stage an elaborate lament over a sleeping girl: Capulet's "Death lies on her like an untimely frost / Upon the sweetest flower of all the field" (4.5), his "Death is my son-in-law; Death is my heir" (4.5), are interpretations delivered with total confidence over a body that contradicts them in every particular the Friar listed. The household does not test. It does not wait two and forty hours, or one. It reads the signs it expects and proceeds to the funeral, which is to say it disables the living girl by certifying her dead, withdrawing her from marriage, from speech, from the household's future, and depositing her in the vault where bodies that no longer signify are stored.

Garland-Thomson's account of the stare explains the tomb scene's otherwise strange poetry. Romeo, entering the vault, stares at the body he believes dead and finds the stare's evidence refusing to settle: "Death, that hath suck'd the honey of thy breath, / Hath had no power yet upon thy beauty. / Thou art not conquer'd. Beauty's ensign yet / Is crimson in thy lips and in thy cheeks, / And death's pale flag is not advanced there" (5.3). The corpse will not read as a corpse. Quayson's aesthetic nervousness is the name for the disturbance that follows, the representational system faltering because the body on display declines its assigned meaning, and Romeo's solution to the disturbance is the tragedy: unable to read the body, he joins it, drinking the Apothecary's physic and converting his own living body into the genuine article beside the counterfeit one. His cry over the cup, "O true apothecary! / Thy drugs are quick" (5.3), is the counterfeit tradition's bitter punchline: the false death was doubted by no one, the true one is self-administered on the strength of the doubt that failed to occur. A disability reading does not soften this into allegory. It states the mechanism the play builds: in Verona, bodies are governed by their readings, readings are governed by expectation, and the two readers who matter, a household and a husband, both read a living girl as dead because dead was the condition their arrangements required her to be in.

## The Actor's Body: Stillness as Labour

Schaap Williams's Unfixable Forms adds a stratum to the counterfeit corpse that neither prosthesis nor the social model reaches, because her subject is not the fiction but the performance. Disability in her account is made in the encounter among three bodies: the character's body as written, the actor's body as present, and the audience's body as witness. Romeo and Juliet tests her account at its most demanding point, the potion scenes, because the fiction's central device is a body performing the absence of every sign an audience uses to confirm that a performer is alive. The actor playing Juliet must lie in the Capulet vault and on the Capulet bed producing stillness as an action: breath suppressed to invisibility, colour supplied by the fiction's assertion against the evidence of a living face, suppleness withdrawn by an act of muscular will sustained across the laments of Act 4 Scene 5 and the whole tomb sequence. The audience reads two bodies at once, the dead girl of the story and the working performer whose labour manufactures the deadness, and the play's pathos depends on the double reading never quite resolving. Williams's point, that theatrical form remakes disability and is remade by it, is visible in the dramaturgy itself: Shakespeare builds his climax out of a performed incapacity so complete that the fiction's other characters, and the fiction's hero with a poison cup in his hand, must decide what the still body in front of them is.

The labour extends across the play's wounded. Mercutio's death is a nightly performance of a body failing in stages the actor must calibrate, wit first, balance next, breath last, while the text insists the character is concealing the failure; the actor conceals nothing and displays everything, and the audience's knowledge runs ahead of the characters' precisely because the performer's body is legible in a way the fiction's body is not. Even the Apothecary is a small essay in the stratum: the text writes starvation onto a frame, sharp misery worn to the bones, and the production must supply a body that can carry the writing, padding refused, thinness cast or counterfeited, so that Romeo's diagnosis at sight becomes the audience's experience rather than its report. The disability reading that stops at the fiction misses this machinery. The play's bodies are not only represented; they are manufactured in real time by bodies subject to fatigue, breath, and failure, and the manufacture is part of what the play means by a body's truth. Verona's characters keep misreading bodies because they read them as signs. The theatre's audience is trained by the same scenes to read bodies as labour, and the training is the tragedy's quiet counter-education.

## The Sealed House and the Unsurgeoned Wound

The catastrophe of Romeo and Juliet is usually assigned to chance, to haste, or to the stars. The disability reading assigns its decisive hinge to an access failure, and the text supports the reassignment in so many words. Friar John, sent with the letter that would have told Romeo everything, reports what stopped him: "Suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Seal'd up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2). The searchers of the town, the plague officers, suspected infection in the house where the friars had paused to visit the sick, and under the plague orders they sealed it. Friar John was not ill. No one in the scene suggests he was ill. He was suspected, and suspicion, institutionalized, was enough: a healthy man's body was treated as a contaminated one, confined, immobilized, and stripped of its function, which was movement and delivery. His errand died of his disablement, and Romeo died of the errand's death. The social model of disability, which holds that people are disabled by arrangements rather than by diagnoses, finds in this scene its purest early modern illustration, purer than any the field's own books collect: a man with nothing wrong with him is disabled completely, for exactly as long as it takes to ruin everything, by a locked door and a public health order. The letter that does not arrive is the play's most consequential object, and it does not arrive because a body was reclassified.

The searchers themselves repay a glance, because their office shows who Verona deputizes to read bodies. In the London of the plague orders, the searchers were parish appointees, most often older women of small standing, charged with viewing the dead and the suspected and reporting what their bodies declared; the city's mortality bills, its nearest approach to a statistics of the body, were compiled from their viewings. The play's searchers inherit the office's logic. Certification is the city's most consequential act of reading, and it is delegated downward, to officers whose suspicion needs no evidence and whose word seals doors, stops letters, and files the living among the infected. Juliet's household will perform the same delegated certainty over her body in Act 4, parents and nurse reading a sleeper as a corpse with a searcher's confidence. The play's chain of misreading is staffed at every link by readers whose authority over bodies exceeds their knowledge of them, which is why the disability reading locates the catastrophe in the staffing rather than in the stars.

Quayson's nervousness and the social model meet in the plague material at a second point, Mercutio's curse. "A plague o' both your houses!" (3.1) is the dying man's verdict, disease language hurled as a sentence, and the play makes the sentence fact: the plague, in its administrative form, does fall on both houses' plans, sealing the friar in a stranger's house and the lovers in their tomb. The curse works not as magic but as administration. Verona's machinery for managing suspect bodies is the instrument by which the feud's catastrophe is delivered, and a criticism attentive to disability is the one that watches the machinery instead of the magic.

### Why does no cure arrive for any body in this play?

Because the play's medicine is distributed by status and suspicion rather than by need, and the scenes are arranged to prove it. Mercutio calls for a surgeon and is carried indoors to die without one appearing. The Apothecary can sell death but is forbidden by statute from selling his way out of hunger.

Friar John is quarantined by officers whose suspicion needs no evidence. Juliet is prescribed a husband, a potion, and a funeral, never an inquiry. In each case a system that claims to manage bodies, medicine, law, public health, the household, processes the body in front of it according to its category rather than its condition, and the category is always the one the system finds convenient.

Mercutio's wound deserves its own paragraph in this section because it is the play's one demonstration of a body performing its way out of its assigned reading until the body wins. Struck under Romeo's arm, Mercutio refuses the reading the wound demands. "Ay, ay, a scratch, a scratch; marry, 'tis enough. / Where is my page? Go, villain, fetch me a surgeon" (3.1). The joke and the request arrive in the same breath: the wit performs the unimpaired self, the self that measures injuries in metaphors, "No, 'tis not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church-door; but 'tis enough, 'twill serve" (3.1), while the patient underneath the performance orders professional help. The last joke is the diagnosis the performance was concealing: "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1). Siebers's account of disability as masquerade, developed for the strategies by which impaired people manage the stare, runs in reverse here: Mercutio masquerades as able, and the masquerade holds exactly as long as his blood does. Romeo, watching, draws the body's conclusion about himself, that love has altered his temper like a metal softened in the tempering: "Thy beauty hath made me effeminate, / And in my temper soften'd valour's steel" (3.1). The period heard effeminacy as a bodily condition, a humoural softening, and Romeo's self-diagnosis assigns his failure to intervene to a change in his physical constitution. The disability reading notes the pattern without endorsing the diagnosis: in this play, when men fail, they blame their bodies' composition, and when women refuse, their bodies are blamed for them. The body is Verona's universal explanation, the alibi and the accusation in one.

## Old Bodies, Working Bodies: The Nurse, the Fathers, and a Death by Grief

The play's young bodies are diagnosed, dosed, wounded, and gilded. Its old bodies are worked, and the disability reading restores the cost accounting. The Nurse is the tragedy's indispensable labourer: she carries the messages, climbs the stairs, negotiates the marriage, and her body files its reports in the middle of scenes that criticism usually reads for their comedy. In Act 2 Scene 5, returning with news of the wedding, she delays the announcement inside a litany of symptoms: her head aches as if it would fall in twenty pieces, her back aches, she is out of breath. Juliet's teasing reply, "How art thou out of breath, when thou hast breath / To say to me that thou art out of breath?" (2.5), is the line the Cripping Breath project takes as a keynote, and the disability ear hears why. The joke assumes breath is a simple possession, present because speech is present. A body that works for a living knows breath as a budget. The Nurse's breathlessness is the price of the errand, itemized in the only currency she has, and the play lets her itemize it in comic form because the household she serves has no other form in which a servant's pain is speakable. She is also the play's archive of bodies: her memory of Juliet's childhood is stored in physical detail, the weaning, the wormwood on the dug, the fall and the broken brow, the husband's joke about falling backward, a whole paediatrics of recollection carried in the body of the woman who did the carrying. Lady Capulet must ask her for Juliet's age. The knowledge of the daughter's body lives in the servant's body, and the play's class order means the archive is also the drudge. Her chronology is somatic throughout: she dates Juliet's age from an earthquake she felt and a weaning she performed, the wormwood she laid on her own breast to break the child's habit, the fall that broke the toddler's brow and the jest her husband made over the crying. Where the great house keeps its records in lineage and law, the Nurse keeps hers in milk, bitterness, scars, and quakes, a body's filing system for a body's history. The disability reading values the archive for what it preserves that no other record in the play can supply: evidence that the tragedy's central body was once a nursing infant with a broken brow, tended by a woman whose own dead child, Susan, is filed in the same memory a breath earlier. Verona's monuments will hold the lovers in gold. The Nurse's memory holds them in milk and wormwood, and it is the more humane archive, because it remembers the body before the city began to read it.

Age is the impairment Verona jokes about because it cannot cure it. At the feast, Capulet seats his cousin with the confession that opens the tragedy's generational account: "Nay, sit, nay sit, good cousin Capulet, / For you and I are past our dancing days" (1.5). The old men's bodies have retired from the floor where the young bodies are about to meet, and the play's machinery depends on the division: the old command, threaten, diagnose, and vow statues; the young run, climb, fight, and die; the serving old carry messages between them at the cost of their backs and breath. The play opens with the old bodies already failing the feud's demands, and the failure is played as the comedy of an assistive device. In the first brawl the patriarchs call for their long swords, and Lady Capulet delivers the tragedy's most famous disability joke at her husband's expense: "A crutch, a crutch! Why call you for a sword?" (1.1). The crutch is produced as a punchline because the old man's body, in his wife's reading, has crossed the line where weapon and support exchange places; the instrument of his honour is reclassified as the instrument of his incapacity, and the reclassification is funny because the audience is invited to read the body as she reads it. Montague, on the other side of the square, is held back bodily by his wife, two old bodies restrained by two old bodies' hands, while the young bodies do the fighting their fathers' frames can no longer perform. The feud is an inheritance of rage assigned to bodies in sequence, the old willing what their frames refuse, the young performing what their frames allow, and the play's first scene distributes the parts by bodily capacity with the clarity of a casting call. The disability reading notes the joke's direction because it governs the play's sympathy thereafter: incapacity in the old is comic, incapacity in the young is tragic, and the difference has nothing to do with the conditions and everything to do with the city's expectations of the bodies carrying them.

Lady Capulet's book of love speech treats Paris's face as a volume to be read in her daughter's chamber, while her own relation to age is a warning she issues in the tomb scene's neighbourhood, that Juliet's body is a bell tolling her mother toward a sepulchre. The mothers' bodies register the feud's costs without the dignity of scenes. Lady Montague's death is the extreme case, and the disability reading insists on its weight precisely because the text gives it none: "Alas, my liege, my wife is dead tonight; / Grief of my son's exile hath stopp'd her breath" (5.3). A woman dies of sorrow offstage, her breath stopped by an emotion, reported by her husband in a subordinate clause while the stage is covered with younger corpses. Early modern medicine would have filed the death without surprise; grief was a humoural event with somatic force, capable of stopping a heart. The play files it as a footnote. In the primer's terms, hers is the body whose reading the city performs most economically: a clause, a cause, no statue. The gradient of attention, gold for the young lovers, a clause for the old mother, nothing for the starved tradesman, is itself the evidence for the article's thesis. Verona does not distribute significance by suffering. It distributes significance by the body's place in the arrangements, and the bodies the arrangements use up are the ones it declines to read.

The neighbouring lens deserves its sentence here. Ecocriticism, treated earlier in this run, reads the Friar's garden as the play's nature, the cultivated world pressing on the city; the disability reading reads the same garden as a pharmacy and the same herbs as a technology for managing bodies. The two readings share the garden and divide its meaning, and the division is instructive: where the ecocritical eye sees the nonhuman world acting, the disability eye sees institutions acting through the nonhuman world, dosing, sealing, and curing. Both find the Friar at the centre, which is where the play put him.

## Breath: The Measure the Play Keeps Taking

Breath is the smallest unit in which the play measures a body's ability, and the disability reading gathers the measurements because the field's performance wing has shown they form a system. Love enters the play as breath disordered: "Love is a smoke made with the fume of sighs" (1.1), the lover's respiration converted to weather, visible, wasteful, and diagnostic, the sigh being the period's recognized somatic sign of the melancholy the physicians catalogued. Romeo's wooing is a management of breath, the whispered exchanges at the orchard wall, the vows exchanged in air stolen from the household's sleep. The potion's specification returns to breath as the decisive test, the one whose absence, with warmth, certifies the counterfeit: no breath shall testify that Juliet lives. The Friar's checklist makes respiration the body's testimony, the witness that cannot be bribed, and the plot is the story of that witness suppressed. In the tomb Romeo reads the body for the testimony and finds the evidence ambiguous, the breath's honey sucked by death yet the cheeks' crimson unadvanced by death's pale flag, and his error is precisely a breath error, a failure to wait for the witness to speak. Juliet wakes into the play's last breath economy, kissing the poison from Romeo's lips in the hope that some restorative residue hangs on them, breath and its poisons exchanged mouth to mouth at the exact boundary the Friar's checklist drew.

Around the lovers the play distributes breath as labour and as loss. The Nurse spends breath as the cost of service and is teased for the spending. Lady Montague's breath is stopped by grief in a subordinate clause, the economy at its most brutal: the body's essential function withdrawn by an emotion, reported without ceremony. Mercutio's breath is spent on jokes until the supply fails mid performance, the grave man promise issued on credit against a respiration already mortgaged. The Cripping Breath project seizes on this system because its performers live inside the fact the play keeps dramatizing, that breath is not a possession but a budget, managed, assisted, rationed, and read by others as the sign of how long a body may be expected to continue. Hale's company reports choosing the play partly for the independence fight at its centre, young people defining their lives against families that manage their bodies, and partly for the sheer density of its breath language, sighs, winds, smokes, stopped throats, last embraces. A disability-led production does not decorate the tragedy with these recognitions. It reveals that the tragedy's own instruments, the sigh, the held breath of the vault, the kiss that transfers poison, were calibrated to a measure the unimpaired audience is trained not to notice it is taking. The play notices. Its most famous love scene is two young people negotiating in whispers because breath carries, its catastrophe is a breath suppressed too convincingly, and its heroine's last act is an attempt to breathe in what remains on a dead man's lips. The measure was always the meaning.

## The Trial: Prosthesis, Metaphor, and the Anachronism Charge

The brief for this article requires the approach's critics to be heard at full strength, and the strongest objections come from inside the field itself, which gives the trial an unusual shape: disability studies is prosecuted by disability studies, and the play is the evidence both sides cite.

The first position is Mitchell and Snyder's. Narrative prosthesis holds that literature uses disability as a device, a crutch of characterization, and resolves the narrative by eliminating the deviance the device introduced. Romeo and Juliet fits the theory's machinery with embarrassing precision at the level of language. The play's passion is figured through disability from the first scene onward: love as sickness, blindness, madness, wound, a chaos of well-seeming forms whose shapes are mis-shapen. The metaphors mark the lovers as disordered, the disorder demands resolution, and the resolution the plot supplies is the standard prosthetic one, death for the disordered bodies and a restored civic surface over their graves. On this account the lovers function the way Richard functions in Mitchell and Snyder's home territory, as the deviance a story generates in order to spend it.

The second position is the field's materialist correction, stated programmatically by Hobgood and Wood in the introduction to Recovering Disability in Early Modern England, where the volume sets itself to undo the impulse to read early modern disability as predominantly metaphorical. The objection is ethical before it is methodological. To read every impaired body as a metaphor for something else, the soul's disorder, the state's disease, the lover's distraction, is to repeat the culture's own gesture, in which disabled people exist to mean things for the unimpaired. Row-Heyveld's work supplies the historical edge: early modern England already operated a machinery for converting impaired bodies into meanings and suspicions, sorting beggars into the deserving impotent and the fraudulent sturdy, and the stage's counterfeit tradition taught audiences to interrogate real bodies for fraud. A criticism that reads the Apothecary's starved frame as a symbol of the world's corruption, without first registering that the man is hungry and that hunger is a condition with causes, performs the period's own trick in modern dress. Schaap Williams sharpens the correction into a method: in Unfixable Forms, disability on the early modern stage is interactive and in flux, produced in the encounter between the actor's body, the fiction, and the audience, and representations of disability are not necessarily about disability at all. The category will not sit still long enough to serve as a stable metaphor, and readings that lean on it as one are leaning on a fiction of fixity.

The third position is the historicist's refusal, Davis's argument given its full force. Disability, on this view, is a modern identity assembled by statistics, clinics, and rights movements; the early modern world knew impairment, deformity, sickness, and monstrosity as theological and humoural events, not as a class of persons; and a criticism that arrives in Verona carrying the modern category will find only what it carried, mistaking its own luggage for the city's contents. The objection has particular force against the language of normalcy, and this article has conceded the vocabulary point in the green sickness section: Verona keeps no averages.

A fourth position, Loftis's, challenges the trial's own terms. Shakespeare and Disability Studies argues that the field's earlier work, for all its recovery of impaired characters, located disability inside fictional persons and thereby overlooked the disabled readers, performers, and audience members whose access to the plays is a present material question. On her account, a criticism that perfects its reading of the Apothecary's bones while the theatre's stairs go unexamined has mistaken the subject. The charge lands on this article as much as on the prosthesis school, since a textual reading of Verona's machinery is precisely the older kind of work she describes, and the concession should be made without varnish: the play's disability life is not exhausted by its representation, and its richest documented disability engagement, Cripping Breath, is a work of access and casting rather than of interpretation. The adjudication therefore adds her stratum to the division that follows, the stratum of use, and awards it to her position outright. What the textual strata yield, the reading of language, machinery, and categories, stands as interpretation; what the play does in rooms where disabled artists claim its central roles is the field's living front, and on this play it is further advanced than the commentary.

The adjudication divides the play into the strata the evidence itself divides into, and awards each stratum to the position that explains it. The stratum of language goes to Mitchell and Snyder, with Cheyne's evidence as the proof: the lovers' passion is figured through disability metaphor as a matter of textual fact, in speech after speech, and a reading forbidden to notice the pattern would be forbidden to read the play's most famous sentences. The prosthesis account of the metaphors stands. The stratum of machinery goes to the materialists, and it is not close. The plot of Romeo and Juliet is not driven by metaphors. It is driven by a pharmacy, a statute, a plague order, a priced transaction, a sealed door, and a household that certifies a sleeping girl dead; the bodies in the machinery are hungry, aching, suspected, and misread, and the materialist demand, that a reading register conditions before converting them into symbols, is the only demand that fits the scenes. The stratum of categories goes to Davis in part and against him in the decisive part. He is right that the play's world lacks the modern category and the modern norm. He is wrong if his history is taken to imply that the play's world therefore lacks the phenomena the category organizes, because Verona visibly classifies bodies, assigns conditions, prescribes social cures, confines the suspected, and prices the impaired, all without benefit of statistics. Schaap Williams's formulation is the settlement the article adopts: disability in the play is unfixed, a process enacted by arrangements and encounters rather than an essence residing in characters, and a criticism that tracks the process rather than hunting for disabled persons will neither invent a crip hero nor miss the disabling city. The editor's testimony closes the trial. Weis's Arden notes, glossing green sickness, sallow cheeks, and the apothecary's specimens with the period's senses, practise a quiet medical model at the foot of the page, identifying each condition and moving on; the glosses are indispensable as documentary floor and insufficient as reading, because they record what the words meant without asking what the meanings did. The disability reading stands on the editor's floor and asks the question the floor supports.

## The Gilded Verdict: Statues, or the Stare Made Permanent

Narrative prosthesis predicts that a story resolves its deviant bodies by cure or elimination. Romeo and Juliet does something the theory's menu lacks, and the difference is the article's final evidence. The lovers are not cured; there is no cure in the case. They are not expelled; the city that expelled Romeo takes him back as treasure. They are exhibited. In the tomb scene the bodies are produced as evidence before the assembled city, the Prince presiding over an inspection, the Friar narrating, the fathers confronted with the physical record of their arrangements; and the settlement the fathers reach is to convert the bodies into permanent exhibits: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold," Montague vows, and Capulet answers, "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie, / Poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3). Gold is the material in which Verona has transacted all along, the gold that bought the poison, worse poison to men's souls in Romeo's phrase, and the gold now buys the lovers' final form, bodies perfected by being made unbodiable, smooth, incorruptible, and silent. The Prince's own summary assigns the loss with a bookkeeper's fairness, "And I, for winking at your discords too, / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punish'd" (5.3), punishment distributed across every body in the city, the ruler's kin as dead as the tradesman is starved.

The exhibition begins before the statues are promised. The tomb scene is an inquest staged as a viewing. Paris's body lies beside the lovers, a third casualty of the night's arrangements, and the Watch arrives with the apparatus of a public examination, officers, lights, prisoners taken at the doors, and the city's chief magistrate to preside. The bodies are produced one by one as evidence: Romeo's, identified by the poison's work and the page's account; Juliet's, waking into a scene whose verdict has already been written on her own body; Paris's, killed by a man whose errand was already a death warrant. The Friar is made to narrate over the bodies, a deposition delivered standing among the corpses it explains, and the Prince assembles the testimony, the letter produced, the page's report, the servant's account, until the physical record has been converted into a settled story. The disability reading notes what the inquest does to the bodies it exhibits. Their meanings are assigned by the same class of readers who misassigned them alive: the Friar's narrative replaces the bodies' own testimony, the Prince's summary apportions blame across the households, and the corpses, which cannot correct the record, become the record. A body that in life could not be heard over its diagnosis is in death heard only through its deposition. The promise of gold then completes the conversion, replacing a contested reading with an uncontestable object.

Garland-Thomson named the stare as the transaction in which a culture processes the body that differs. The ending of Romeo and Juliet institutionalizes the stare. The lovers, whose bodies in life were diagnosed, dosed, suspected, and misread by every institution that touched them, are in death made safe for permanent looking by the removal of everything that made them bodies at all, appetite, breath, pulse, refusal. A statue cannot be lovesick. A statue cannot refuse a husband, stop breathing at the wrong time, or be suspected of infection. The city that could not accommodate the living bodies accommodates the gilded ones without effort, and the accommodation is the indictment the disability reading exists to pronounce: Verona's problem was never the lovers' bodies. It was a civic order that could process bodies only by classifying them, and could honour them only by replacing them. The reading also restores the play's most famous architecture to the argument, because the orchard wall is Verona's first disabling arrangement and the balcony scene is a negotiation of access. The Capulet orchard is walled against the feud's violence, a barrier built by a household to sort bodies into the admitted and the excluded, and Romeo's courtship begins as a defeat of the barrier by an able body at its most able: with love's light wings he o'erperches the walls, and the stony limits cannot hold love out (2.2). Juliet's answering anxiety is an access assessment conducted from above, how camest thou hither, the place death to a Montague body found there, and the scene's lovers spend their first exchange auditing the barrier's consequences before they exchange a vow. The vertical staging the era's theatres inherited, the girl aloft at the window, the boy below in the garden, is an architecture of partial access, sight and voice permitted, touch rationed, the wall's top as the border between the managed interior and the street. Disability studies was founded on the insight that built environments distribute ability, that stairs and thresholds decide which bodies may enter a life; the orchard wall is the insight in masonry, four centuries before the vocabulary. The lovers' whole plot can be read as a campaign against Verona's access arrangements, the wall scaled, the cell's door opened by the Friar, the bedchamber's door closed against the Nurse, the tomb's door forced by the crow, and at every door a body is admitted, refused, suspected, or buried according to the classification it carries. The final door is the statue's pedestal, which admits the lovers to the city's centre at last, on the one condition the city always intended to impose: that they enter it as objects, incapable of breath, refusal, or return.

The verdict on the approach follows from the verdict on the play. Disability studies reveals in Romeo and Juliet a tragedy whose machinery is the management of bodies, whose catastrophe is an access failure compounded by a misread pulse, and whose ending gilds what it could not house. The revelation survives its anachronism because the play's own world supplies the classifications, the prescriptions, and the sealed doors; the field supplies only the decision to read them as the plot. That decision, exercised with the joins showing, is what the approach is for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch disability primer, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch disability primer is the table in this article that sets out the play's significant bodies, the condition each body is in, the reading Verona imposes on it, and the reading the disability lens restores. The pattern it exposes is a consistent gap between condition and interpretation. A lovesick boy is read as a patient, a refusing daughter as a case of green sickness, a drugged girl as a corpse, a healthy friar as a plague carrier, a starved tradesman as a nobody. In every row the damage is done not by the condition but by the reading imposed on it, and by the treatment the reading licenses.

### Q: What is disability studies as a way of reading drama?

Disability studies reads drama by asking where disability is made: in bodies, or in the arrangements around bodies. Its founding distinction separates the medical model, which locates disability inside an impaired body as a defect to cure, from the social model, which locates it in the barriers, categories, and stares a society builds around bodily difference. Applied to a play, the approach tracks diagnoses and prescriptions, the institutions that manage bodies, the metaphors that mark some bodies as disordered, and the endings that dispose of them. It treats bodily variety as a frame the drama thinks with, and it insists that conditions be registered as material facts before they are read as symbols.

### Q: Why does Romeo and Juliet have no disabled hero, and why does the field read it anyway?

The play has no disabled hero because no character carries a lasting impairment that organizes identity and plot, in the manner of Richard III. The field reads it anyway because the play is saturated with the machinery disability studies examines: diagnoses of lovesickness and green sickness, a pharmacology of cure and poison, a quarantine that disables a healthy messenger, a starved body priced by hunger, a counterfeit corpse certified by confident readers, and an ending that converts problematic bodies into golden exhibits. The landmark books of early modern disability studies pass the play by for want of a crip protagonist. The reading offered here proceeds on the field's other premise, that the disabling arrangement matters more than the impaired character.

### Q: What is narrative prosthesis, and how does the play's ending test it?

Narrative prosthesis is David T. Mitchell and Sharon L. Snyder's argument, from their book of that title in 2000, that stories lean on disability as a device: the impaired or disordered body signifies a world out of joint, generates the plot's energy, and is then resolved away by cure, death, or expulsion. The play's ending tests the theory because its resolution is none of the three. The lovers are not cured, and though they die, they are not expelled from the city that killed them; they are retained at its centre as statues in pure gold. Elimination the theory predicts. Exhibition it does not, and the difference exposes the ending's logic, a city that could not accommodate living bodies honouring them once they are made of metal.

### Q: How does the social model of disability explain Friar John's failure?

The social model explains it exactly, because Friar John's failure has no medical content at all. Sent to Mantua with the letter that would have saved the lovers, he is sealed indoors by plague searchers who suspect the house he visited of infection: "Seal'd up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2). He is not ill, and nobody claims he is ill. A public health arrangement reclassifies a healthy body as a contaminated one, immobilizes it, and strips it of its function. The social model holds that disability is the disadvantage imposed by arrangements rather than the impairment residing in bodies. Friar John, disabled completely by a locked door for precisely the interval the tragedy needs, is the model's purest illustration in early modern drama.

### Q: What was lovesickness in early modern medicine?

Lovesickness was a recognized somatic disease, erotic melancholy, with a clinical literature and a physiology. Robert Burton gave it the Third Partition of The Anatomy of Melancholy in 1621, cataloguing its symptoms and cures; Jacques Ferrand's treatise on the subject, Englished in 1640, described a disorder of the imagination with readable bodily signs, sunken eyes, changed complexion, sighing, insomnia, wasting. In the humoral scheme, desire overheated the blood and disturbed the spirits, and the disturbance printed itself on the body's surface. Romeo's presentation in Act 1, the withdrawal, the sighs, the artificial night his father reports, the sallow cheeks the Friar reads at sight, is the textbook case, and Verona's responses, the contrary infection Benvolio prescribes, the solemnized marriage the Friar performs, are the period's standard treatments.

### Q: Why was marriage the prescribed cure for green sickness?

Marriage was the prescribed cure because the disease was defined as a disorder of the unmarried state. Green sickness, or chlorosis, was described by Johannes Lange in 1554 as the disease of virgins, a condition of girls marked by a pale greenish complexion, breathlessness, and disordered appetite, and the medical tradition held that it arose from retained and corrupted blood that marriage and childbearing would release. The prescription closed a perfect instrument around the patient. When Capulet hurls "green-sickness carrion" at Juliet (3.5), the diagnosis converts her refusal of Paris into a symptom, and the symptom's remedy is the marriage refused. A disability reading notes the convenience: the condition appears at the moment of disobedience, and its cure is obedience, administered as medicine.

### Q: What does the Friar's list of Juliet's symptoms reveal about how the period certified death?

The list reveals that death was certified by a checklist of absent signs. Preparing Juliet in Act 4 Scene 1, the Friar specifies what the potion will suspend: no pulse shall keep its progress, no warmth and no breath shall testify that she lives, the roses in her lips and cheeks shall fade, her eyes shall close, and each part, deprived of supple government, shall lie stiff and stark and cold for two and forty hours. Certification was a reading of surfaces, pulse, breath, colour, flexibility, performed by household observers rather than instruments. The plot is the demonstration of the checklist's weakness. Every sign is falsified on schedule, every reader accepts the forgery without a test, and a living girl is buried on the strength of an inspection the play has shown to be gameable.

### Q: Why does the disability reading linger on the Apothecary's starved body?

Because the Apothecary's body is the play's one impairment with a fully material cause and effect. Romeo finds him by reading his frame: "Sharp misery had worn him to the bones" (5.1). Hunger has written itself into the tradesman's body, and Romeo prices his offer to the writing, forty ducats calibrated to a starvation the law maintains by making the man's trade capital and his living impossible. The Apothecary's consent is manufactured by his condition, as he states it: "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1). A reading that hurries past the body to the poison's symbolism misses the transaction's mechanism. Poverty disables the seller, the disability sells the poison, and the poison fills the tomb. The chain is the social model stated as plot.

### Q: How does Lindsey Row-Heyveld's counterfeit-disability tradition change the reading of Juliet's feigned death?

Row-Heyveld's Dissembling Disability in Early Modern English Drama (2018) recovers a stage tradition of more than forty plays in which able-bodied characters counterfeit blindness, lameness, madness, or deformity, and argues that the tradition taught audiences to hold nonstandard bodies as pitiful and probably fraudulent at once. Juliet's feigned death is the tradition's limiting case, a counterfeit not of impairment but of death itself, and the article marks the join rather than claiming her census includes the play. The tradition changes the reading by reversing the suspicion's direction. In Row-Heyveld's plays the performing body is suspect. In this one the performing body is believed without a single test, and the fraudulence migrates to the readers, the household that certifies a living girl dead because death is the condition its arrangements require.

### Q: Why does Lennard Davis warn against calling any body in the play normal?

Davis warns against it because the normal body is a modern invention. In Enforcing Normalcy (1995) he argues that cultures before the statistical age measured bodies against an ideal, a divine or heroic template nobody embodied, whereas the norm, the average body computed from a population, arrives with nineteenth-century statistics and brings disability into being as its shadow category. Verona has ideals and complexions, balances and disorders, but it has no average and no charts. A reading that speaks of the play's normalcy as a statistical fact misdescribes its world. The warning governs vocabulary, not substance: the article concedes the word and keeps the question, since Verona plainly classifies bodies, assigns conditions, and prescribes cures, under humoral names rather than numerical ones.

### Q: What do Hobgood and Wood object to in reading disability as metaphor?

Allison P. Hobgood and David Houston Wood, introducing Recovering Disability in Early Modern England (2013), set their volume to undo the impulse to read early modern disability as predominantly metaphorical. The objection is that metaphor-reading repeats the culture's own appropriation, in which impaired bodies exist to mean things, corruption, disorder, inward states, for the unimpaired people doing the reading. A starved apothecary converted into a symbol of a corrupt world has been used the way Verona uses him. The editors' alternative is to register conditions in their historical and material contexts first, wages, statutes, humours, hunger, and to let meanings follow facts rather than replace them. The objection does not forbid attention to the play's disability metaphors, which are textual facts. It forbids stopping at them.

### Q: What does Tobin Siebers mean by complex embodiment?

Complex embodiment is Siebers's correction, in Disability Theory (2008), to the choice between the medical and social models. The medical model puts disability in the body; the social model puts it in the environment; Siebers holds that body and environment write each other continuously, that a body's conditions have social causes and social effects at the same time, and that embodied experience is evidence neither model may discard. Verona runs on the correction. The Apothecary's starved frame is a social product, poverty and statute, that becomes a bodily fact with plot consequences. Juliet's drugged stillness is a bodily fact, pharmacology, whose entire meaning is social, certification and burial. Neither body is intelligible from one side of the old divide, which is the theory's point.

### Q: How does the Cripping Breath project put disability at the centre of this play in performance?

Cripping Breath, the project of CRIPtic Arts under its artistic director Jamie Hale, built with the University of Sheffield, centres disabled performers, including performers who use ventilators, in an adaptation of Romeo and Juliet organized around breath. The choice of play is textual. Breath runs through the tragedy as love's medium and death's measure, from the fume of sighs in Act 1 to the Friar's specification that no warmth and no breath shall testify that Juliet lives. The project reads the play as a story of young people fighting for self-definition against families that manage their bodies, a fight its performers know from the inside. It stands as the play's most substantial disability engagement, and it is practice rather than commentary, the field working on the stage instead of the page.

### Q: What does the Nurse's breathlessness in Act 2 Scene 5 become in a disability-led production?

In a disability-led production the Nurse's breathlessness stops being only a joke. Returning from her errand, she itemizes the cost of the labour in symptoms, the aching head, the aching back, the lost breath, and Juliet teases her for the contradiction: "How art thou out of breath, when thou hast breath / To say to me that thou art out of breath?" (2.5). The tease assumes breath is a simple possession, proved by speech. Cripping Breath takes the exchange as a keynote because performers who use ventilators know breath as a budgeted resource, present for one sentence and spent by the next. Heard that way, the scene records a working body's invoice, delivered in the only register, comedy, in which the household permits a servant's pain to be presented.

### Q: What does Mercutio's call for a surgeon admit that his jokes deny?

The call admits that the wound is real. Mercutio's first response to being struck is performance, the unimpaired self maintained by wit: the injury is a scratch, it is not so deep as a well nor so wide as a church-door, it will serve (3.1). In the same breath he gives the order the jokes contradict: "Where is my page? Go, villain, fetch me a surgeon" (3.1). The jokes deny the body; the order consults it. Read together, the lines stage the collapse of a masquerade, the performance of ability sustained exactly as long as the blood holds and then set aside for the professional summary the wit had been avoiding. His last joke surrenders the performance entirely, promising that tomorrow he will be found a grave man, the diagnosis inside the pun.

### Q: Does the play punish disordered bodies, or the city that misreads them?

The play punishes the city, and it distributes the punishment with a bookkeeper's fairness. The Prince's closing audit assigns loss to every party: "And I, for winking at your discords too, / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punish'd" (5.3). The disordered bodies, the lovesick, the refusing, the drugged, are punished in the action, but the tragedy's verdict falls on the readers and arrangers: the fathers who diagnosed a daughter rather than hearing her, the officers who sealed a healthy man indoors, the husband who could not read the body in front of him, the ruler whose winking let the machinery run. Disability studies frames the distribution as the point. The conditions were manageable. The management was the catastrophe.

### Q: Where does the play side with the medical model, and where with the social model?

The play's characters side with the medical model and its plot sides with the social model. Inside the fiction, every authority treats bodily difference as a condition inside a body: Romeo's melancholy is in his blood and eyes, Juliet's refusal is in her green sickness, the Apothecary's ruin is in his bones, and cures are prescribed accordingly, an infection, a marriage, a potion. The plot, however, keeps demonstrating that outcomes are decided by arrangements: a statute that starves a tradesman into selling poison, a plague order that disables a healthy friar, a household that buries a girl its checklist misread. The lovers are not killed by their humours. They are killed by doors, prices, certificates, and laws, which is the social model's whole argument, delivered four centuries before the model was named.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch disability primer to argue that Verona disables its lovers?

An essay should build the argument row by row, taking each entry in the primer as a claim with its evidence attached. The strongest sequence runs from diagnosis to machinery to monument: open with the two diagnoses, lovesickness and green sickness, showing how Verona converts feeling and refusal into conditions; proceed to the machinery, the Friar's pharmacy, the Apothecary's priced poverty, Friar John's sealed house, and the household's certification of the drugged body, showing outcomes decided by arrangements; close with the statues, where the city honours in gold the bodies it could not house in life. The primer's discipline should govern the prose: register each condition as a material fact before interpreting it, and attribute every disabling effect to the arrangement that produced it.

### Q: What is the strongest objection to a disability reading of this play, and how much of it stands?

The strongest objection is anachronism, in Davis's form: disability is a modern category, assembled by statistics, clinics, and rights politics, and a play of the 1590s knows impairment, sickness, and deformity as humoral and theological events, not as an identity a criticism can organize around. Importing the category risks finding only what was carried in. The objection stands against vocabulary and against any claim that Verona contains disabled persons in the modern sense; this reading concedes both, and claims no crip hero. It fails against the machinery. Verona classifies bodies, assigns conditions, prescribes social cures, confines the suspected, and prices the impaired, and a criticism may track that machinery with modern instruments provided the joins are marked. So used, the objection disciplines the reading without dissolving it.
