## The Word Congress Chose to Define

A statute can regulate a substance, a practice, a place, or a transaction, and each choice carries a different legal meaning. The Organic Foods Production Act belongs to a less familiar category. Its central object was a word. The question Congress answered was what the term on a food label could lawfully signify in American commerce, what conditions had to stand behind that term before a seller could use it, and who could rely on the term once it appeared in the marketplace. Before November 1990, the answer depended on geography and on the private or public body whose standard happened to govern a particular sale. After enactment, the answer was to be supplied by federal law. That shift, from many local answers to one national answer, is the act in its simplest form, and every other feature of the statute follows from it.

![The Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 and the national standard behind the organic label - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-83.webp)

The formal identity of the measure can be stated completely in a single sentence, and precision about that identity matters because the statute is often described loosely as a general food law. The Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 is Title XXI of the Food, Agriculture, Conservation, and Trade Act of 1990, Public Law 101-624, signed on November 28, 1990 by President George H. W. Bush, comprising sections 2101 to 2123 of the Act and codified at 7 U.S.C. 6501 and following. The placement carried significance of its own. The definition did not arrive as a freestanding consumer measure detached from agricultural law. It arrived inside a farm act, as a title concerned with certification, and its home in the United States Code placed it among the agricultural statutes administered through the Department of Agriculture. Form, placement, and codification all pointed in the same direction. Congress had written a marketing standard for agricultural products, housed where agricultural marketing law belonged.

Section 2102 of the act, codified at 7 U.S.C. 6501, stated three purposes, and those purposes define the statute more reliably than any shorthand description of it. The first purpose was to establish national standards governing the marketing of certain agricultural products as organically produced products. The second was to assure consumers that organically produced products meet a consistent standard. The third was to facilitate interstate commerce in fresh and processed food that is organically produced. Read together, the three purposes describe a single problem approached from three sides. A standard had to exist. Buyers had to be able to trust that the standard meant the same thing from one transaction to the next. Goods bearing the claim had to move across state lines without the claim dissolving into a different meaning at each border. The statute was built to hold those three elements together, and a reading that separates them misses the design.

The first purpose deserves close attention because of a word that summaries often drop. Congress did not declare a purpose of governing production in the abstract. The stated object was national standards governing the marketing of products as organically produced. Production mattered to the statute, and later provisions dealt with production and handling in detail, but production entered the legal frame through the claim made when a product reached the market. A farmer could grow food by many methods without federal interference under the act. The federal question arose when a product was sold or labeled with a particular claim attached. That distinction gave the statute its shape. Regulation attached at the point of representation, and the conditions for making the representation reached back into how the product had been produced and handled. The word on the label thus became the legal hinge between field practice and market conduct.

The second purpose turned on consistency rather than on enthusiasm for the products themselves. To assure consumers that products bearing the claim meet a consistent standard is a promise about meaning, not a promise about merit. A consistent standard tells a buyer that the term encountered in one store, in one state, in one season, rests on the same requirements as the term encountered elsewhere. Without that assurance, the label functioned as an invitation to guess. One seller might apply a demanding standard, another a lax one, and the buyer at the shelf had no reliable way to tell the difference from the face of the package. The assurance Congress described was therefore epistemic before it was anything else. The statute would let a buyer know what a word meant. Whether the buyer valued what the word meant remained the buyer's own judgment, formed for reasons the statute neither supplied nor endorsed.

The third purpose, facilitating interstate commerce, explains why consistency could not be left as a purely local achievement. Food in the United States moved across state lines as a matter of routine, through handlers, processors, distributors, and retailers whose operations ignored the boundaries that defined legal jurisdictions. A claim that changed meaning at a state line burdened that movement at every step. A handler receiving goods from several states faced several definitions of the same term. A producer selling into several markets faced several sets of requirements for the same word. A national standard addressed the burden directly, not by forbidding states from caring about the subject, but by fixing one meaning for the claim in national commerce, so that a product could travel without its description becoming false along the way. Interstate commerce in the statute was not an abstraction about federal power. It described the physical path that labeled food took from production to sale.

The three purposes converge on a claim that should be stated plainly, because misunderstanding it distorts everything that follows in accounts of the statute. The object of regulation was a word, a marketing term, and not the safety or nutritional quality of a product. The act created no federal judgment that food produced under the standard was safer to eat, more nutritious, or preferable on health grounds to food produced by other lawful methods. A product could be entirely safe, fully compliant with every food safety law that applied to it, and entirely ineligible for the claim, because eligibility turned on conformity to a production and handling standard tied to a label. Conversely, use of the claim certified conformity to that standard and nothing beyond it. To read the statute as a health measure is to misstate what Congress did. Congress legislated about truth and consistency in a commercial representation. Health legislation asks whether a product may be sold at all. The 1990 act asked what a seller could call a product that was sold.

That difference shaped who could break the law and how. Under a safety statute, the violation lies in the condition of the goods. Under the 1990 act, the paradigmatic violation lay in a mismatch between a representation and the reality behind it, in a product presented under a claim its production history could not support. The wrong was a wrong of misdescription, closer in spirit to the law of false labeling than to the law of adulteration. That character also explains the institutional machinery the statute went on to build, machinery concerned with plans, certification, accreditation, and lists of permitted and prohibited substances. Each element existed to make a representation checkable. A claim that anyone could assert and no one could verify would have reproduced, under federal sponsorship, the very unreliability Congress set out to cure. The definition therefore had to come with a method for testing whether a given use of the word was earned, and the testing method had to be uniform enough that its results traveled with the goods.

One further consequence follows from locating the statute in the law of marketing rather than the law of health. The act did not displace state food law generally, and it did not set food safety standards for the products within its scope. States retained their general authority over food sold within their borders under other bodies of law. What the act claimed for federal law was narrower and, within its narrowness, firmer. The meaning of one commercial term, used on certain agricultural products in commerce, would be a federal question with a federal answer. Narrowness of that kind is easy to underestimate. A single word can carry an entire market on its back when buyers use the word to organize their choices and sellers use the word to present their goods. By defining that word, Congress defined the terms on which a whole segment of agricultural commerce would describe itself, without purporting to judge the segment's worth.

### Why did Congress write a federal definition of organic?

Congress wrote a federal definition because the word had acquired commercial value without a single legal meaning. State statutes and private programs applied differing standards to the same claim, leaving consumers unable to rely on the label and producers facing conflicting requirements when products crossed state lines. A national standard solved both problems at once.

The conditions that produced the statute had been building through the 1980s, and their essential feature was fragmentation. By the late 1980s, numerous states had written their own statutes governing the claim, and private organizations had established their own programs for certifying production under the same word. Each program rested on a standard, and the standards differed. The differences were not a technical footnote to an otherwise settled practice. A standard decides which substances may be used, which practices qualify, how records are kept, and how compliance is judged, so two standards bearing the same name could demand different conduct from producers and could certify different realities under an identical label. The word circulated nationally. The meanings attached to the word did not. That gap between a national market and local meanings is the condition the statute answered, and the gap widened as commerce in products bearing the claim grew along national rather than local lines.

California and Washington illustrate the state side of the patchwork, and the act's background included states of that kind, jurisdictions that had moved ahead of federal law by writing definitions of their own. A state that wrote such a statute acted rationally within its borders. Its legislature could settle what the term meant for sales in that state, could lend public authority to a definition, and could give buyers in that state a measure of confidence. The difficulty lay in the aggregate. Each state solution added one more definition to a field that commerce treated as one market. A definition that was complete and coherent inside California answered nothing about a sale in another state with a different statute or with no statute at all. The patchwork was not the product of carelessness by any single jurisdiction. It was the predictable result of many jurisdictions solving a shared problem separately, at different times, with different judgments about what the standard should require.

Private certification filled part of the space that public law had left open, and it deepened the fragmentation at the same time. Private organizations certified production under their own standards, inspected against their own criteria, and allowed producers who met those criteria to present goods under the claim with the organization's backing. Such programs gave the term more substance than it would have carried as a bare assertion by a seller, because an outside body stood behind the assertion. Private programs could not, by their nature, produce a single meaning. Each organization defined the standard for itself, answered to its own membership or mission, and had no authority over rival programs that defined the standard differently. A buyer confronting two certified products certified under two different private standards confronted the original problem in a new form. Certification had multiplied the guarantors without unifying the guarantee.

The scale of the fragmentation eventually received a number, and the number has a specific source that careful accounts preserve. The Department of Agriculture stated in its March 2000 proposed rule that 49 State and private organizations had established individual programs and standards for certifying products under the claim. The figure belongs to the Department and to the rulemaking record of March 2000, where the Department used the count to measure the landscape the national standard would replace. Forty-nine separate programs did not mean forty-nine wholly unrelated approaches, and the count should not be inflated into a claim that every program contradicted every other. The count meant something simpler and more damaging to reliance. No buyer, handler, or producer dealing across the whole market could treat the word as self defining, because dozens of definers stood behind it, each with a program of its own.

For producers and handlers, fragmentation translated into cost and uncertainty of a practical kind. A producer whose goods moved into several states could face several certification regimes for a single crop, with separate applications, separate inspections, and separate judgments about whether the same practices qualified. A handler combining ingredients from multiple sources had to track which standard each source had satisfied and whether the combination satisfied the standard of the market where the finished product would be sold. None of that burden arose because any single program was unreasonable. The burden arose from multiplicity itself, from the absence of a common reference point against which compliance could be established once and recognized broadly. A national standard promised to convert repeated, jurisdiction by jurisdiction proof into a single certification that the market as a whole could read. The promise explains the presence of interstate commerce among the stated purposes, placed in the text of section 2102 rather than left to implication.

For consumers, fragmentation translated into a quieter but equally serious problem, the erosion of the label as a source of information. A label works when a buyer can learn its meaning once and apply that learning repeatedly. A fragmented standard defeated learning. The term that signified one set of practices on one package could signify a materially different set on the next package, and the difference was invisible at the point of sale, where decisions were made. Buyers who cared about the practices behind the claim, for whatever personal reasons moved them, had no dependable way to act on that care through ordinary purchasing. Buyers who did not care were equally affected in one respect, because a word that can mean anything in commerce invites misuse against everyone. A seller willing to apply the term loosely gained an advantage over a seller who met a demanding standard, and the honest seller had no authoritative definition to appeal to in response. Fragmentation therefore harmed reliance in both directions, weakening the buyer's confidence and the careful producer's reward at the same time.

A federal definition addressed both harms through one act of definition, and that economy of means is a large part of why Congress acted when it did. A single national standard gave interstate commerce a stable term to carry, and gave consumers a stable meaning to learn. No private agreement among certifiers could have achieved the same result with the same authority, because private bodies bind only those who accept their standards voluntarily. No single state could have achieved the result either, because a state definition governs sales under that state's authority and reaches no further. Only a federal statute could fix the meaning of the claim for the national market as such, attach legal consequences to misuse of the claim in that market, and create the institutional apparatus, accreditation of certifiers and approval of state programs under federal oversight, through which one meaning could be administered in many places. The choice of a statutory definition, rather than guidance, exhortation, or a voluntary federal seal without legal backing, reflected the nature of the failure. The failure was a failure of coordination, and coordination on a national scale required law.

What Congress chose to define, then, was neither a farming method in the abstract nor a category of especially virtuous food. Congress chose to define a claim. The definition told sellers when the claim could be made, told buyers what the claim guaranteed, told certifiers what the claim required them to verify, and told states how their own programs would relate to the federal meaning. Each of those audiences had lived with a different answer before 1990, or with no authoritative answer at all, and the statute replaced that variety with a text that could be cited, applied, and enforced. The word remained an ordinary English word in ordinary speech, free for anyone to use in conversation. In commerce, on a label, for the agricultural products within the statute's scope, the word carried the meaning Congress gave it in the act. That is the sense in which the Organic Foods Production Act began as an act about language, and the rest of the statute, its standards, its board, its list, and its penalties, existed to keep the language honest.

## How the Act Passed and How Its Text Is Built

The Organic Foods Production Act did not reach the statute books as a freestanding measure debated and enacted on its own. The text arrived as Title XXI, headed Organic Certification, of the Food, Agriculture, Conservation, and Trade Act of 1990, the omnibus farm bill of the 101st Congress, carried in the Senate as S. 2830 and signed into law as Public Law 101-624. The organic title was championed in the Senate by Patrick Leahy of Vermont, the chairman of the Agriculture Committee, and the title moved under his sponsorship, with Leahy shepherding the provisions through the committee process that shaped the larger bill. That placement inside a farm bill determined almost everything about the manner of enactment. A labeling statute for a defined category of agricultural products took its legislative path through the vehicle that Congress returns to on a recurring cycle for the governance of farming, food programs, conservation, and rural policy, and the votes that carried the title into law were votes on that larger measure as a whole.

The setting for the choice of vehicle was a fragmented field of private and state certification. By the late 1980s, numerous states and private organizations certified production under differing standards, so a product accepted under one set of rules in one place faced different rules elsewhere. California and Washington were among the states with their own statutes on the subject. The Department of Agriculture later quantified the fragmentation in its March 2000 proposed rule, stating that 49 State and private organizations had established individual programs and standards for certifying products of the kind the statute governs. A grower or handler selling across state lines therefore confronted a patchwork in which the same word on a label could rest on different underlying requirements depending on the certifier and the jurisdiction. The statutory purposes recorded at the head of the text answer that condition directly.

A farm bill is a peculiar instrument for a labeling law, and the peculiarity explains the shape of the enactment record. Farm bills are omnibus measures in the strict sense. They gather many titles on many subjects into a single bill, they proceed under deadlines created by the expiration of the programs they reauthorize, and they assemble coalitions in which a member may support the whole while caring intensely about one title and only tolerantly about the rest. A provision placed inside such a measure gains the protection of the coalition and the deadline. It also loses a separate identity in the voting record, because no member casts a vote on the title alone at the decisive stages. The organic title rode a must-pass agricultural measure rather than standing alone. The record shows a Congress that enacted a farm bill containing a national standard for a marketing claim, not a Congress that voted, title by title, on the merits of that standard in isolation. [the farm bill vehicle that carried the organic title](/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/) is therefore the correct unit of analysis for the passage narrative, and every tally that follows belongs to the omnibus.

The sequence began in the Senate, which passed S. 2830 on July 27, 1990, by a vote of 70 to 21. The House took up the measure and passed it on August 4, 1990, by unanimous consent, a procedure that records agreement without a tallied division and that again reflects action on the farm bill as a package. Differences between the two chambers then went to conference, the stage at which an omnibus measure acquires its final negotiated form. The conference report came back to the House first, which agreed to it on October 23, 1990, by a vote of 318 to 102. The Senate agreed to the conference report two days later, on October 25, 1990, by a vote of 60 to 36. President George H. W. Bush signed the measure on November 28, 1990, and with that signature Title XXI became law as sections 2101 to 2123 of the public law, codified at 7 U.S.C. 6501 and following as chapter 94 of title 7.

Each element of that sequence deserves a precise reading. The House action by unanimous consent in August measured the absence of objection sufficient to force a recorded vote, a different kind of evidence from a counted majority. The October tallies measured acceptance of the conference agreement, the single negotiated product that members could accept or reject but could not amend on the floor. By the conference stage, the question before each chamber was whether the farm bill as reconciled deserved enactment, and the title stood or fell with the reconciliation. A reader who treats any of these numbers as a referendum on the labeling standard alone misreads the institutional form. The numbers instead establish that the standard entered federal law with the full authority of an enacted public law, carried by majorities assembled for a much broader agricultural settlement.

Enactment, however, settled only the existence of the statute. The work of the text lies in its internal architecture, and the architecture rewards attention because the act is built less as a set of commands issued directly by Congress to growers than as a system of delegated and supervised decisions. Congress fixed the governing conditions, created the bodies that would elaborate them, assigned the certification function to accredited intermediaries, reserved a federal approval role over state programs, and attached penalties to misuse of the label. The sections that follow the opening provisions distribute those functions in a deliberate order, moving from purposes and definitions to the substantive standard, from the standard to the plan, from the plan to the state programs and the production and handling rules, and from those rules to accreditation, the two governing machines for substances and advice, and enforcement.

The text opens with its purposes. Section 2102, codified at 7 U.S.C. 6501, states three purposes, and each purpose maps onto a feature of the design that follows. The first purpose is to establish national standards governing the marketing of certain agricultural products as organically produced products. The emphasis falls on marketing and on the claim made to buyers, which confirms that the statute regulates a label and a representation rather than farming as such. The second purpose is to assure consumers that products sold under the claim meet a consistent standard. Consistency is the operative word, because the patchwork of state and private programs was objectionable under the statutory logic not for existing but for differing. The third purpose is to facilitate interstate commerce in fresh and processed food produced under the standard. The commerce purpose explains why a national rule mattered to producers as much as to consumers, since a claim that changed meaning at a state line burdened the very trade the label was meant to support. Read together, the three purposes describe a statute about the integrity of a word in commerce.

Definitions and operative language then do the quiet work of the chapter. In a statute of this kind, the defined terms carry the regulatory weight, because the standard applies through terms that distinguish what counts as production, what counts as handling, which substances fall on which side of the line the chapter draws, and which operations fall inside the certification system. A prohibition stated before its terms are fixed would invite dispute at every application, whereas a command stated through defined terms channels dispute toward the definitions and toward the listing process the chapter creates. Congress legislated at the level of conditions, categories, and institutional assignments, and left the granular specification of practices and substances to the administrative process the text constructs, bounded by the criteria and procedures the text itself supplies.

At the center of the architecture stands the national standard for production stated at 7 U.S.C. 6504, enacted as section 2105 of the act. The provision performs a gatekeeping function for the entire chapter. It identifies the conditions that must hold before the marketing claim may attach to a product, in three linked requirements that address inputs, land history, and process documentation together. No single requirement carries the standard alone. A product grown without a prohibited input on suitable land could fall outside the standard for want of the agreed plan, and a product grown under a meticulous plan could fall outside the standard because the land history or the input record failed a substantive condition. The tripartite structure is the statute in miniature, because it joins a rule about substances, a rule about place and time, and a rule about supervised agreement into one test for the use of a word.

### What must be true before a product can be sold as organic?

Three conditions must coincide under 7 U.S.C. 6504. The product must have been produced and handled without synthetic chemicals except as the chapter provides, must not come from land treated with prohibited substances during the three years before harvest, and must have been produced and handled under an agreed organic plan.

The first condition governs substances across both production and handling. The exception clause matters as much as the prohibition, because the chapter does provide, through the listing mechanism described later in the text, for specified exemptions. The condition therefore states a default rather than an absolute. Synthetic chemicals are outside the standard unless the chapter affirmatively brings a particular substance inside it through the prescribed process, and the burden of the design rests on that process rather than on ad hoc judgment in an individual case. By extending the condition to handling as well as production, the provision also closes the gap between the field and the label. A product could satisfy every field requirement and lose its standing through what happened in processing, storage, or transport, and the text forecloses that result by making the condition run the full length of the chain from production to sale.

The second condition governs land and time. A product within the standard must not be produced on land to which prohibited substances were applied during the three years immediately preceding the harvest of the product. The clause converts eligibility into a question of documented history. Land carries a record under the statute, and the record runs for a fixed period measured backward from harvest, so a field treated with a prohibited substance in the relevant window yields a crop that cannot bear the claim even if the crop itself was raised without any prohibited application. The three year period gives certifiers and inspectors a concrete interval to verify rather than an impression to assess. The text excludes livestock from that clause, a carve-out that reflects the different relationship between an animal and a parcel of land. Livestock eligibility rests instead on the production requirements addressed to animals directly, including feed, health care, and living conditions.

The third condition governs agreement and supervision. The product must be produced and handled in compliance with an organic plan agreed to by the producer or handler and the certifying agent. The plan requirement transforms the standard from a set of prohibitions into a supervised undertaking. Instead of merely forbidding specified conduct and punishing departures after the fact, the chapter requires the operation to state in advance, in a form the certifying agent accepts, how production and handling will conform to the standard. Compliance then becomes measurable against a document specific to the operation, and inspection becomes a comparison between the agreed plan and the observed practice. The agreement element also distributes responsibility. The producer and the handler commit to a course of conduct, the certifying agent accepts the commitment as adequate to the standard, and the certification system holds both sides to the document they share.

The provisions on the plan, at 7 U.S.C. 6505 and 6506, give the third condition its statutory elaboration. Placed immediately after the national standard, the plan provisions confirm that the plan is not an administrative afterthought added during implementation but a structural element enacted as part of the standard itself. Certification under the chapter rests on an approved plan of this kind, together with on-site inspection of the operation. The plan looks forward, describing how an operation will conduct production and handling, while inspection looks at the operation as found, and the certifying agent judges conformity by holding the two against each other and against the chapter. The arrangement also explains why the standard could govern a wide range of operations through a comparatively short statutory text. Congress did not attempt to write a separate rulebook for every crop, animal, and processed product inside the statute, and instead required each operation to translate the general standard into an agreed, examinable plan.

State programs enter the architecture next, at 7 U.S.C. 6507, and the provision states a mechanism rather than a slogan about federal and state authority. A governing State official may submit a plan for a State certification program to the Secretary of Agriculture for approval, and a State program must meet the requirements of the chapter to be approved. The provision then defines the one direction in which a State program may depart from the federal baseline. A State program may contain more restrictive requirements only when the added requirements further the purposes of the chapter, are not inconsistent with the chapter, are not discriminatory toward products produced in other States, and take effect only after approval by the Secretary. Substantive changes to an approved program must likewise be submitted for approval before implementation, the Secretary reviews approved programs at least once every five years, and the Secretary determines applications within six months. The consequence deserves plain statement. For the federal label, the national standard operates as a floor. A State cannot authorize a weaker version of the label, because a program that fell below the chapter could not be approved, and a State may add stricter requirements only through the federal approval channel the provision creates.

The production and handling requirements follow, giving the general standard its subject specific content. At 7 U.S.C. 6509, the chapter addresses livestock production through requirements on feed, health care, and living conditions. Feed requirements reach what the animal consumes, health care requirements reach the treatments and interventions an operation may use, and living conditions requirements reach the environment in which the animal is kept. At 7 U.S.C. 6510, the chapter addresses handling, the stage at which raw production becomes a marketed product through processing and postharvest treatment. The handling provision governs what a handling operation may add or apply during that stage, holding processed products to the substance discipline of the standard after harvest just as the production provisions hold growers to that discipline before harvest. Together, the two provisions complete the chain that the condition at 6504 announced, in which production and handling stand under one standard from land and animal to finished product.

Accreditation and certifying agents occupy the next position in the sequence, at 7 U.S.C. 6515. The Secretary of Agriculture accredits certifying agents, and both state officials and private entities may serve as agents once accredited. The federal government therefore does not certify individual farms and handlers directly under the ordinary operation of the chapter. Accredited intermediaries perform certification, judging plans, conducting inspections, and granting or withholding the certification on which lawful use of the label depends, while the Secretary supervises the intermediaries themselves through accreditation. The structure multiplies the reach of the program, since private and state certifiers distributed across producing regions can examine operations that a single federal office could not examine with equal frequency, and the structure simultaneously creates the accountability problem that accreditation exists to solve, since a certifier too lax in judgment could debase the claim for every honest participant in the market.

Near the end of the operative sequence, the text installs its two governing machines, the National List at 7 U.S.C. 6517 and the National Organic Standards Board at 7 U.S.C. 6518, and the architectural point is their pairing. The exception clause in the production standard, under which synthetic chemicals are barred except as the chapter provides, requires a lawful method for deciding which substances the chapter provides for, and the National List is that method given documentary form, a list maintained under statutory criteria through which exemptions and prohibitions acquire legal force. A list of that kind requires a body to develop and recommend its contents, and the Board is that body given institutional form, an advisory board established under the statute to develop proposed list contents and to advise the Secretary on implementation. Neither machine operates intelligibly without the other. The List without the Board would be a departmental register detached from the representative and expert judgment the chapter prescribes, and the Board without the List would be advice without a legal instrument through which advice takes effect. A fuller account of each machine, covering composition, voting, criteria, and the periodic review of listings, belongs to the discussion that follows this architectural survey. Within the architecture itself, the essential observation is positional, because the machinery exists to serve all of the provisions placed before it.

Enforcement closes the sequence at 7 U.S.C. 6519, with import and related provisions in the sections that follow. The enforcement provision attaches consequences to the misuse of the claim, providing that knowingly selling or labeling a product as within the standard, except in accordance with the act, carries a civil penalty of not more than 10,000 dollars for each violation, as enacted. The knowledge element confines the penalty to deliberate misuse rather than inadvertent error, while the per violation measure allows the sanction to scale with the conduct. The chapter also supports the certification sanctions on which the system depends in practice, since certifications may be suspended or revoked, and a person whose certification is revoked for a willful violation is ineligible to receive certification for five years. Read as a whole, the architecture moves in one direction throughout, from a word used in commerce, through the conditions that give the word a fixed meaning, to the plans, programs, certifiers, and lists that administer the meaning, and finally to the penalties that defend it. Passage through a farm bill explains how such a structure entered the law without a standalone vote. The structure itself, title by title and section by section, explains what kind of law Congress made, a supervised system for governing a claim, in which the statute fixes the test and builds the institutions that apply the test case by case and substance by substance.

## A Citizen Board Written Into the Statute

The Organic Foods Production Act could have left the federal label to the Secretary of Agriculture alone. Departmental rulemaking follows a familiar pattern of proposal, public comment, revision, and final publication. The 1990 act inserted a different institution into that pattern and wrote the institution into the statute itself. Under 7 U.S.C. 6518, the National Organic Standards Board stood as a body of appointed outsiders whose seats Congress allocated among named constituencies. Its proposals formed the required foundation for the National List, the part of the standard that allowed or prohibited particular substances. Composition, proposal function, and voting rule were operative law, not reassurance offered to growers or consumers. A secretary would set the standard in any event, but the statute required the secretary to act on a record built first by fifteen citizens who had to persuade one another under a supermajority rule.

The Board consisted of fifteen members appointed by the Secretary of Agriculture, each serving a five year term, and the Board was established under the Federal Advisory Committee Act. Fifteen was large enough to hold every named constituency without giving any constituency a majority, and small enough for deliberation as a single body. Appointment by the Secretary tied the Board to the administering department, while allocation of the seats remained a congressional decision. A secretary could choose which farmer filled a farming seat, but could not convert that seat into a second scientist seat or add a seat for a trade association. The five year term let a member learn the technical record, sit through petition cycles and sunset reviews, and judge substances under the statutory criteria rather than under pressure surrounding a single meeting. The advisory committee framework fit an institution whose formal product was advice and proposals rather than rules issued in its own name.

Congress filled the seats by category, and the categories contain the theory of the institution. Four members owned or operated an organic farming operation, and two members owned or operated an organic handling operation. One member owned or operated a retail establishment with significant trade in organic products. Three members held expertise in environmental protection and resource conservation, and three members represented public interest or consumer interest groups. One member held expertise in toxicology, ecology, or biochemistry, and one member was a certifying agent. The trade categories therefore held seven seats, the environmental and consumer categories held six, and the scientist and the certifier held one each. Congress distributed seats by function in the system the act created rather than by market size or by capacity to generate comments during rulemaking. Each category corresponded to a distinct relationship to the label, and each relationship carried a different exposure when the standard changed.

A farmer lived under the production requirements and faced questions of land, feed, and inputs at the point of origin. A handler lived under the handling requirements and faced the practical question of which substances a processing operation could use. A retailer met the purchaser at the point of sale and carried the commercial risk when confidence in the label weakened. The environmental members represented effects extending beyond a single operation, since evaluation of a substance included harm to human health or the environment. The consumer members represented purchasers who paid for the label without operating under it and who could verify a claim only through the credibility of the system behind the seal. The scientist supplied training in what a substance did in a body and in an ecosystem over time. The certifying agent inspected operations, reviewed organic plans, and applied the standard to concrete facts. That seat carried knowledge of whether a requirement could be verified during inspection or would dissolve into ambiguity against a record book, a feed purchase, or a cleaning log.

The allocation assigned custody of the standard among five kinds of holder at once. The regulated parties held seats because a standard detached from practice could demand the impossible or forbid the workable, and because those bearing the cost of a listing decision had a claim to participate in making it. Consumers of the label held seats because a promise defined only by sellers would carry a structural conflict of interest that good faith could not remove. The environmental interest held seats because the criteria reached beyond the boundary of a certified operation. The scientist held a seat because harm, necessity, and consistency could not be resolved by preference alone. The certifier held a seat because a standard that could not be administered was not a standard at all. No constituency held more than four seats, so no single constituency could write the standard alone. The farming bloc at four could anchor a coalition but could not carry a motion or supply a quorum. The environmental and consumer members together numbered six, enough to deny victory to a trade only proposal, yet short of decision. Persuasion had to cross constituency lines as a matter of arithmetic.

That arithmetic imposed a discipline of translation on every member who wanted a listing changed. A concern framed only in the language of farm economics lacked the votes, and a concern framed only in the language of consumer preference lacked the votes as well. The proponent of a change had to restate the concern within the statutory criteria, in terms a toxicologist, a certifier, a retailer, and a grower could each evaluate from a different seat. A handler seeking an exemption had to speak about necessity and about the absence of nonsynthetic alternatives, not merely about cost or convenience. An environmental member seeking a prohibition had to connect the objection to harm or to inconsistency with certified practice, in a form the production seats could test against field experience. The scientist and the certifier held one seat each, yet each held influence beyond that count when the Board divided closely, because contested evaluations often turned on evidence and enforceability. Deliberation inside the Board therefore resembled the standard itself, which had to be at once scientifically defensible, commercially workable, and verifiable during inspection.

Under 7 U.S.C. 6517(d), the National List established by the Secretary had to be based on a proposed National List or proposed amendments developed by the Board. The sequence ran from the Board to the Secretary, not from departmental preference to Board reaction. The Secretary could not add exemptions for synthetic substances beyond those the Board had proposed. That limit addressed the central sensitivity of the program, because synthetic substances were prohibited unless specifically exempted on the list, while nonsynthetic substances were allowed unless specifically prohibited. Every synthetic exemption was an exception carved from a prohibition, and Congress assigned initiation of those exceptions to the Board. An official who favored an exemption had to obtain a Board proposal first. The Board also advised the Secretary on implementation of the chapter generally. The listing function carried teeth, because the statute converted advice on the list into a precondition for valid secretarial action on synthetic exemptions.

Evaluation applied the criteria stated in 7 U.S.C. 6518(m). The Board considered whether a substance would be harmful to human health or the environment. The Board considered whether the substance was necessary because wholly nonsynthetic alternatives were unavailable. The Board considered whether use of the substance was consistent with organic farming and handling. The criteria pulled in different directions, and the categories mapped onto them. Harm invited the scientist and the environmental members. Necessity invited farmers and handlers, who could judge whether an alternative existed in practice and performed the same function at operational scale. Consistency invited the whole Board into judgment about the character of the certified system, a judgment neither a laboratory result nor a production record could settle alone. A substance could pass one test and fail another, presenting little harm yet failing necessity, or proving necessary yet sitting uneasily with certified practice. Petitions, which any person could file to add, remove, or amend a listing, fed individual substances into that evaluation.

Under 7 U.S.C. 6517(e), no exemption or prohibition was valid unless the Board had reviewed it within five years of adoption or previous review and the Secretary had renewed it. Listings expired unless affirmatively renewed. Each generation of members therefore inherited listings approved by predecessors and examined them again under the same criteria. A substance admitted when nonsynthetic alternatives were scarce could face a different necessity analysis at renewal. A substance whose effects had been uncertain at adoption could face a fuller record at renewal, built from petitions, inspection experience, and the evaluations of intervening cycles. The five year cycle also made the seated membership at each renewal consequential, because the members conducting a review applied the criteria to the record as that record stood at the review. The statute built reconsideration into the life of the list, and the Board performed the reconsideration on which renewal depended.

Section 6518(i) provided that two thirds of the votes cast at a meeting where a quorum was present were decisive of any motion. A majority of members was a quorum. Fifteen members made that majority eight. At full attendance, ten votes carried a motion. With nine voting, six were required, and with eight voting, five fell short and six were required. The threshold moved with attendance, but the proportion never fell below two thirds, and a bare majority of those present could never decide a motion. A faction could assemble a quorum and open a meeting, yet a faction needed support well beyond its own numbers to act. The quorum rule protected against action by a fragment of the Board. The supermajority rule protected against action by a temporary majority coalition rather than a settled judgment across the statutory categories. Decisive meant that the motion carried the Board and spoke as a Board proposal or recommendation, so the threshold governed the point at which divided deliberation became institutional action.

The Agricultural Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, specified that a motion proposing to amend the National List required the two thirds vote to prevail. An amendment adding an exemption, removing an exemption, or altering a prohibition had to command that margin at a meeting with a quorum present. The 2018 specification treated the list as the place where the supermajority mattered most. That treatment matched the 1990 design, because the list was where general prohibitions and permissions became concrete for every certified operation. A single substance on the list could determine whether a practice remained available to growers and handlers across the country, and a single removal could withdraw a material on which operations had built formulations and rotations. Changing the operative content of the standard therefore demanded broader agreement than changing a recommendation on general administration.

A supermajority rule in a gatekeeping body produces stability. An adopted listing reflected a coalition of at least two thirds assembled across divergent categories, and removal required an opposing coalition of the same breadth under the same criteria. Listings tended to persist through membership changes short of a fundamental realignment. Growers planning rotations and input purchases, handlers formulating products, and certifiers advising clients could order affairs around a list that did not change with each appointment cycle. The same stability protected a decision to keep a substance off the list, because a failed exemption remained prohibited under the general rule. Addition required breadth, removal required breadth, and prohibition of a nonsynthetic substance required breadth. The status quo for any substance enjoyed a procedural advantage. The statute accepted that advantage as the price of content that would not swing with narrow majorities, and consistency was among the purposes declared in 7 U.S.C. 6501.

The burden fell on change in both directions, and the cost deserves equal weight. A minority larger than one third of votes cast held a blocking position, and denying two thirds was easier than assembling a majority for an affirmative program. A listing could persist after its factual basis weakened until sunset review forced the question. An emerging nonsynthetic alternative supplied a necessity argument for removal, but the argument had to attract two thirds. In September 2013, the Department published a revised sunset procedure at 78 Federal Register 56811, under which a listing continued unless the Board voted by the decisive two thirds to remove it and the Secretary concurred. Producer and consumer organizations criticized the change as reversing the statutory renewal burden. The Department defended the procedure as consistent with the decisive vote requirement and as providing stability. The dispute concerned which side of a listing decision the supermajority should burden, continuation or expiration, and its intensity confirmed that procedure determined outcomes for substances whose merits members contested.

### How much power does the National Organic Standards Board actually have?

The Board holds proposal power rather than final decision power. The Secretary establishes the National List, but the list must rest on the Board's proposals, and no synthetic exemption may be added without a Board proposal. Its supermajority rule makes that gatekeeping power consequential.

The Board did not issue the National List, certify operations, inspect farms, or revoke certificates. The Secretary established the list, conducted rulemaking, accredited certifying agents, approved state programs, and enforced the chapter. General advice on implementation carried no compulsion, and a secretary could proceed on a different course within statutory limits. Decisive authority operated at one chokepoint, the origin of the list and its amendments. That chokepoint governed the content certifying agents applied and the Department enforced. Control over origin was control over the range of outcomes available to every later decision maker. A secretary who wanted an exemption the Board had refused to propose could not create the exemption by rule. The Board could foreclose options without dictating every particular of a rulemaking, and foreclosure at the proposal stage was power that later stages could not easily undo. Sunset review extended that power across time, because a listing the Board declined to examine favorably at renewal faced expiration rather than indefinite continuation by inertia.

Appointment imposed a further boundary. The Secretary appointed every member, and categories constrained only the kind of person each seat required. Within a category, the Secretary chose the individual. Over successive cycles, an administration could seat sympathetic members in every category, and five year terms spread that turnover over time rather than concentrating it at one moment. The supermajority blunted that influence, because an expected outcome needed two thirds across categories filled by different experience. Members brought the perspective of a category into deliberation, and the design made constituency judgment insufficient by itself. Appointment gave the Secretary influence over who deliberated, while the statute reserved control over how deliberation became a proposal. In that reservation the Board kept a measure of independence from the official who appointed its members and received its advice.

The Board was first appointed in 1992 and developed National List recommendations during the middle years of that decade. The first proposed rule, published December 16, 1997, would have permitted genetic engineering, ionizing radiation, and sewage sludge, and departed from Board recommendations on those points. The Department could therefore propose a rule at odds with Board judgment, at least at the proposal stage. The Department received 275,603 comments, a volume the Department described in the March 2000 proposed rule as the largest public response to a proposed rule in Department of Agriculture history. On May 8, 1998, Secretary of Agriculture Dan Glickman announced that genetic engineering, sewage sludge, and irradiation would not be allowed in the national standards. The final rule, published December 21, 2000, prohibited all three practices. The Board held no veto over the proposal, but its recommendations supplied the benchmark against which the departure was measured in the comment record. The constituencies seated on the Board were among the constituencies whose acceptance the label required, and their opposition was opposition the Department had to answer in public.

Power lay in the combination of composition, proposal requirement, and supermajority. Composition placed relevant knowledge and interests in one room, from the grower who knew whether a practice worked to the certifier who knew whether a requirement could be verified. The proposal requirement made presence legally consequential, because the list had to rest on Board proposals and synthetic exemptions could originate nowhere else. The two thirds rule made the product represent more than the largest faction. A representative board without the proposal requirement would have been a forum a secretary could acknowledge and disregard. A supermajority in a homogeneous body would have entrenched one perspective instead of forcing agreement among divergent ones. Together, the features required the hardest questions to be answered first, among the holders of custody, by a margin signifying settlement rather than victory. The resulting power was narrower than governing the program and stronger than being heard, and the fifteen seats were its foundation.

## The National List and the Exceptions That Expire

The National List is the place where the Organic Foods Production Act reveals its actual design. Section 6504 states the baseline for a product sold or labeled as organically produced: production and handling without synthetic chemicals except as the chapter provides, no prohibited substances applied to the land during the three years immediately preceding harvest, and production and handling under an organic plan agreed to by the producer, the handler, and the certifying agent. The exception clause in that first element does much of the work. Congress did not ban every synthetic input in every circumstance, and Congress did not admit every substance found in nature. Instead, the statute built a list at 7 U.S.C. 6517 and made compliance turn on what the list permits, what the list forbids, and whether each entry has been reviewed and renewed within the period the statute allows.

For the substances the list covers, the list is the standard, expressed in named exemptions and named prohibitions rather than in general language about what certified production should resemble. A single listing can decide whether a production practice fits the federal standard, whether a handler may use a particular ingredient or aid, and whether a certifying agent can approve a plan that depends on that input. General language matters, because the evaluation criteria control how entries reach the list and how entries remain there, but practical guidance requires a move from the general rule to the specific entry.

The general rule runs in two directions, and the symmetry is deliberate. Synthetic substances are prohibited in organic production and handling unless specifically exempted on the National List. Nonsynthetic substances are allowed unless specifically prohibited on the list. Neither category receives automatic treatment based on origin alone. A synthetic substance starts from prohibition and moves toward permission only through a specific exemption, supported by the statutory evaluation and adopted through the process the act prescribes. A nonsynthetic substance starts from permission and moves toward prohibition only through a specific listing that removes it from the substances a certified operation may use. The list therefore functions as a set of exceptions to two opposite defaults, and every entry has to be read against the default it displaces.

The consequence deserves careful statement because popular shorthand about the label often gets the point backward in both directions. The word organic, as the act uses the term, means compliance with a defined standard, not the absence of all synthetic inputs. A certified operation may use a synthetic substance if that substance has been specifically exempted, if the exemption remains valid, and if the use fits the conditions under which the operation has been certified. The same word also does not mean that every natural substance is permitted. A nonsynthetic substance that has been specifically prohibited is outside the standard even though the substance occurs in nature, and a plan that relies on such a substance does not become compliant because the input can be described as natural. Origin matters, since origin determines which default applies, but origin alone does not settle the legal question. The listing settles it, subject to the criteria and the sunset that govern the listing itself.

This two way structure also clarifies what kind of law the act is. The statute regulates a label and a marketing claim, establishing national standards governing the marketing of certain agricultural products as organically produced products and assuring consumers that products sold under the claim meet a consistent standard. The National List serves that consistency purpose by replacing individual judgment with a common reference point. Before the national standard took shape, numerous states and private organizations certified production under differing standards, and the Department stated in its March 2000 proposed rule that 49 State and private organizations had established individual programs and standards for certifying organic products. A single federal list did not end disagreement about which substances belonged within the standard, but the list changed the form of that disagreement, turning a contest among competing local definitions into a dispute about a specific federal entry, evaluated under statutory criteria and decided through a process that produced a public record.

### How does a synthetic substance get onto the National List?

A synthetic substance reaches the National List only through exemption. The Secretary establishes the listing based on a proposal developed by the National Organic Standards Board, after evaluation against the statutory criteria for health and environmental harm, necessity, and consistency with organic farming and handling.

The division of labor in that answer comes from the text. The National List established by the Secretary must be based on a proposed National List or proposed amendments to the list developed by the National Organic Standards Board, under 7 U.S.C. 6517(d). The Secretary may not add exemptions for synthetic substances beyond those the Board has proposed. That limit matters because a synthetic exemption is the point at which the statute relaxes its own default prohibition, and Congress placed the initiative for that relaxation with a board composed to bring several kinds of knowledge and interest into the same proceeding. The Board also advises the Secretary on implementation of the chapter generally, but the listing function has a sharper edge. For synthetic substances, the Board proposal marks the outer boundary of the exemptions the Secretary may establish.

The Board is a body of fifteen members appointed by the Secretary of Agriculture for five year terms, established under the Federal Advisory Committee Act: four who own or operate an organic farming operation, two who own or operate an organic handling operation, one who owns or operates a retail establishment with significant trade in organic products, three with expertise in environmental protection and resource conservation, three who represent public interest or consumer interest groups, one with expertise in toxicology, ecology, or biochemistry, and one who is a certifying agent. Decisions inside the Board carry their own threshold. Under 7 U.S.C. 6518(i), two thirds of the votes cast at a meeting at which a quorum is present are decisive of any motion, and a majority of members is a quorum. The Agricultural Improvement Act of 2018 amended section 6518(i) to specify that a motion proposing to amend the National List requires the two thirds vote to prevail.

Evaluation turns on three criteria stated at 7 U.S.C. 6517(c) and 6518(m). The first asks whether the substance would be harmful to human health or the environment. The second asks whether the substance is necessary because wholly nonsynthetic alternatives are unavailable. The third asks whether use of the substance is consistent with organic farming and handling. Each criterion does different work, and none can be reduced to the others. A substance might present little evidence of harm and yet fail the necessity test if a wholly nonsynthetic alternative is available for the same function. A substance might be necessary in a narrow practical sense and yet raise a consistency problem if the use pulls production away from the practices the standard is built to recognize. The criteria force the record to address all three dimensions before permission follows.

Necessity carries a particular burden in this scheme because the default for synthetic substances is prohibition. The question is not whether a synthetic input would be convenient, less expensive, or familiar to producers who have relied on the input outside certified production. The statutory question is whether wholly nonsynthetic alternatives are unavailable. That phrasing pushes the inquiry toward function and toward evidence. What task does the substance perform in production or handling, and can that task be performed without the synthetic input at all? If an alternative exists but performs less well, the debate moves into harder territory, because the statute asks about availability rather than about equivalence of cost or ease. The exemption, under the words Congress chose, is a response to a gap in nonsynthetic means rather than a general license to prefer a synthetic tool.

Consistency performs a different kind of discipline. Harm and necessity can be framed as inquiries into effects and alternatives, but consistency asks whether the proposed use belongs within the system the statute creates. That system includes the organic plan, the production and handling requirements, the role of certifying agents, and the land history rule that precedes harvest. A use that is difficult to confine, difficult to document in a plan, or difficult for a certifying agent to verify places pressure on the consistency criterion even when the substance itself raises no separate alarm. Conversely, a use that fits within a documented plan and that a certifying agent can inspect against clear conditions has a stronger claim under the third criterion. The evaluation reaches beyond the substance in isolation and into the operation that would use it.

The petition channel keeps the list open to correction from outside the government. Any person may petition to add a substance to the list, to amend a listing, or to remove a listing. A producer who believes that a needed synthetic substance meets the criteria can place the request into the process rather than waiting for the Department or the Board to raise the question independently. A person who believes that a nonsynthetic substance should be prohibited, or that an existing exemption no longer satisfies the criteria, can use the same channel to press the opposite result. The petition does not decide the question. A petition frames a question for evaluation under the criteria, through the Board proposal structure and the Secretary action that the statute requires. The channel guarantees access to the process, not success within it, and that distinction preserves the criteria as the deciding test rather than the identity or persistence of the petitioner.

### Why does every National List exemption expire after five years?

An exemption or prohibition lasts only within a five year review cycle because section 6517(e) makes validity depend on timely Board review and renewal by the Secretary. A listing that is not reviewed and renewed within that period ceases to be valid, so no exception becomes permanent by default.

The sunset provision at 7 U.S.C. 6517(e) states the rule without qualification. No exemption or prohibition on the National List is valid unless the Board has reviewed the exemption or prohibition within five years of its adoption or review and the Secretary has renewed it. The provision applies to both kinds of entry, the exemption that permits a synthetic substance against the default prohibition and the prohibition that bars a nonsynthetic substance against the default permission. Listings expire unless affirmatively renewed. Expiration is not a penalty for a listing that has failed, and renewal is not a formality that follows automatically from the passage of time. Continued validity depends on two affirmative steps, review by the Board and renewal by the Secretary, completed within the five year period that runs from adoption or from the most recent review.

That design answers a problem built into any system of exceptions. An exemption granted on a particular record can outlive the conditions that justified the grant. Alternatives that were unavailable when a synthetic substance was first exempted may become available through changes in production practice, supply, or technique, and the necessity finding that supported the exemption may weaken as those alternatives develop. Evidence about health or environmental effects may deepen as a substance is used at greater scale or studied over a longer period. A use that appeared consistent with certified farming and handling at adoption may sit less comfortably within the system after experience shows how the use operates across many operations and many inspections. None of those developments requires a conclusion that the original listing was mistaken. The sunset assumes only that a finding made on one record should not govern indefinitely without a later record against which the finding can be tested again.

The sunset is therefore the statute's answer to exception creep, the gradual process by which narrowly justified permissions accumulate into a broader standard than any single decision would have approved. A list grows one entry at a time, each entry defended on its own facts, each entry modest beside the whole, until the combined effect of the entries alters what the label permits in practice. A prohibition list can drift in the opposite direction as well, as prohibitions adopted for particular reasons remain in force after the reasons have faded from attention. Sunset review interrupts both forms of drift by forcing each entry back into the open. Every exception must periodically re earn its place on the public record, under the same criteria that governed its adoption, with the Board reviewing and the Secretary renewing or allowing the entry to expire.

The five year period also creates a rhythm that participants can plan around without converting the list into a permanent settlement. Producers and handlers who build a certified operation around an exempted substance receive a defined period of validity rather than a permission that can vanish without a structured review, and consumer and public interest participants receive a guaranteed occasion to contest continuation. Renewal within that period is a substantive act because the criteria remain the measure of the listing. A review asks whether the substance would be harmful to human health or the environment, whether the substance remains necessary because wholly nonsynthetic alternatives are unavailable, and whether the use remains consistent with organic farming and handling. For a synthetic exemption, necessity is often the criterion most exposed to change, since availability of alternatives can shift as markets and methods develop.

The same sunset mechanism sits at the center of criticism from opposite directions, and the statute leaves room for both criticisms to be stated without either one displacing the text. Some producers have contended that the list is too restrictive for practical farming, a position that rests on the experience of operating within a default prohibition where a needed input cannot be used unless an exemption has been proposed, evaluated, adopted, and kept valid through renewal. From that vantage point, the combination of the decisive vote, the necessity criterion, and the sunset can appear to make permission difficult to obtain and uncertain to retain, even for a substance that a producer regards as essential to a viable certified operation. Other participants, including advocates for a narrower reading of the exemptions, have contended that exceptions have accumulated over the life of the program, a position that rests on the length and combined effect of the exemptions that the list contains. From that vantage point, each renewal that continues an exemption adds to a total that the default prohibition was meant to keep small.

Both criticisms run through the same sunset and renewal mechanism because the sunset is where continuation is decided. A producer who finds the list too restrictive confronts the sunset as another point at which a needed exemption might lapse, and the argument from that side presses toward renewal as the means of preserving practical continuity. An advocate who finds the exemptions too numerous confronts the same sunset as the regular opportunity to end entries that no longer satisfy the criteria, and the argument from that side presses toward expiration as the means of restoring the default. The mechanism does not favor either account in the abstract. Sunset review supplies the occasion on which the competing accounts meet the record for a particular substance, and the criteria supply the questions that the record has to answer.

The dispute that followed the Department action in September 2013 shows how much can turn on the procedure by which sunset review is conducted. In September 2013 the Department published a revised sunset procedure at 78 FR 56811, under which a listing continues unless the Board votes by the decisive two thirds to remove the listing and the Secretary concurs. The revised procedure addressed the practical question that the statutory text leaves close to the surface: when a listing reaches its review point, which outcome follows from Board action, and which outcome follows from the absence of a decisive Board vote to change the entry. The answer given in 2013 treated continuation as the result unless removal commanded the decisive vote and the Secretary agreed with removal. That answer gave the sunset a particular burden structure, and the burden structure became the subject of the dispute.

Producer and consumer organizations criticized the 2013 change as reversing the statute renewal burden. On that account, section 6517(e) places the burden on continuation, since a listing is not valid unless the Board has reviewed the listing and the Secretary has renewed it, and a procedure under which a listing continues unless the Board votes decisively to remove it shifts that burden toward the opponents of continuation. The criticism drew its force from the statutory phrasing of validity, which conditions the life of an entry on review and renewal rather than on the failure of a removal effort. Participants who pressed that reading treated the 2013 procedure as more than an administrative adjustment, since the procedure determined which side of a sunset dispute had to assemble a decisive vote in order to prevail.

The Department defended the revised procedure as consistent with the decisive vote requirement and as providing stability. On that account, the two thirds rule at section 6518(i) governs Board motions, and a removal that cannot command the decisive vote has not carried the motion that the statute requires for Board action of that character. Stability entered the defense as a practical consideration for producers, handlers, and certifying agents who organize operations around listed substances and who face disruption when a listing turns on a vote short of the threshold the statute sets for decisive Board action. The Department position treated the 2013 procedure as an application of the voting rule Congress wrote, rather than as a departure from the sunset rule, and the two positions therefore rested on different elements of the same statutory scheme, one on the validity language of section 6517(e) and the other on the decisive vote language of section 6518(i).

A static catalogue would ask only whether a substance had once been listed. The act asks a further question: whether the listing has been reviewed within five years of adoption or review and renewed by the Secretary. The Board cannot treat its earlier proposals as settled beyond recall, the Secretary cannot treat an earlier renewal as a permanent answer, a producer cannot treat an exemption as an entitlement detached from the review cycle, and an opponent of an exemption cannot be told that the question was closed at adoption. The list stays open by operation of the statute, on a schedule the statute sets, under criteria the statute states. The result is a standard whose exceptions are real but conditional: permission for an exempted synthetic substance, and a bar on a prohibited nonsynthetic substance, are each the statute working as written, through the same listing structure and the same sunset.

## Certification, Labels, and the Federal Floor

Certification was the mechanism that turned a statutory definition into a claim a buyer could rely on at the point of sale. The Organic Foods Production Act did not leave the word to individual judgment by a seller, and it did not assign verification to the Department of Agriculture acting alone in every case. Instead, the statute built a chain of responsibility in which the Secretary accredited certifying agents, those agents certified producers and handlers against a national standard, and an approved plan connected the daily practice of an operation to the requirements that the label represented. Each link served a distinct purpose, and the absence of any one link weakened the others.

Under 7 U.S.C. 6515, the Secretary accredits certifying agents. State officials and private entities may serve in that role. The structure mattered because the market the act addressed was national in reach and varied in local organization. Before the national standard reached full implementation on October 21, 2002, certification had developed through numerous State and private programs, and the Department stated in its March 2000 proposed rule that 49 State and private organizations had established individual programs and standards for certifying products under the label. Accreditation allowed that existing capacity to continue within a federal framework, while placing the authority to recognize a certifier in the Secretary rather than in the certifier itself.

Accreditation also separated two functions that a less careful design might have merged. A certifying agent evaluated an operation and granted or withheld certification, but the agent operated under recognition granted by the Secretary and under the requirements of the chapter and the rule. A private entity did not create its own definition of the standard by certifying under it. The definition came from the statute and the National Organic Program, housed within the Agricultural Marketing Service.

That application began with the organic system plan. Under the production standard stated in 7 U.S.C. 6504, a product sold or labeled as organically produced had to be produced and handled under a plan agreed to by the producer, the handler, and the certifying agent. Sections 6505 and 6506 carried the plan and related compliance requirements into the certification structure. The agreement element deserves emphasis. The plan was not a unilateral declaration filed for form, and it was not a set of instructions imposed without reference to the operation that had to follow it. Agreement among the parties placed the operation and the certifier on a shared statement of how the standard would be met in that setting.

A shared statement had practical value because the underlying requirements reached conduct over time. The statute required production and handling without synthetic chemicals except as the chapter provided, and it required that a covered product not be produced on land to which prohibited substances had been applied during the three years immediately preceding harvest, with livestock excluded from that land clause. A plan allowed the certifier to address compliance before a product reached a shelf, and it gave inspection a benchmark against which to measure practice.

Inspection was the verification practice that kept the plan from becoming paper alone. Certification rested on an approved plan and on site inspection, and annual inspections were the program operating practice under the rule. The plan described the agreed route to compliance, inspection tested whether the operation followed that route, and certification communicated the result to the market through a regulated claim.

The act regulated that claim rather than food quality in a broader sense. The statute made no representation that certified production was safer or more nutritious, and it set no food safety standard for products carrying the label. That limit separated the program from [the department's food safety rulemaking](/2021/02/01/usda-food-safety-rulemaking/), which addressed a different kind of risk through a different regulatory logic. A food safety rule asks whether a product presents a hazard that regulation must control. The certification system under this act asked a narrower question, whether the product had been produced and handled in the manner that the federal definition required for use of the claim. Confusing those questions would misstate both regimes.

Congress stated the consumer purpose of the narrower question in section 2102, codified at 7 U.S.C. 6501. The purposes were to establish national standards governing the marketing of certain agricultural products as organically produced products, to assure consumers that such products met a consistent standard, and to facilitate interstate commerce in fresh and processed food produced under that standard. Consistency was the promise. A buyer who encountered the claim in one State was to encounter the same federal meaning that the claim carried in another State, even though a State official or a private entity might have performed the certification in a given case. The machinery of accreditation and the plan existed to make that consistency credible across many certifiers and many operations.

That promise placed the program within a wider concern about truthful commercial claims, and the comparison with [the consumer protection framework against deceptive labeling](/2019/01/15/ftc-consumer-protection-rulemaking/) clarifies what the label did and did not convey. The label conveyed that a defined production and handling standard had been met through a certified process. It did not convey a general endorsement of the product, a judgment about health, or a guarantee extending beyond the standard itself.

Labeling tiers under the final rule translated the certification system into graduated language for packaged products. Regulation 7 CFR 205.301 supplied percentage categories that determined what claim a product could carry and where that claim could appear. The percentage structure mattered because many processed products combined ingredients from different sources, and a single unqualified claim for every mixture would have erased distinctions that the standard required the label to preserve. The tiers allowed a product to communicate the proportion of its ingredients that met the standard, while reserving the strongest claim and the federal seal for products at the top of the scale.

The highest tier was 100 percent organic. In that tier, all ingredients were organic, excluding water and salt. The exclusion of water and salt from the calculation reflected the way the rule measured the ingredient proportion for a processed product, and the tier itself left no allowance for a nonorganic ingredient within the measured total. A product in this tier communicated completeness under the rule definition. The claim did not mean that every substance involved in commerce in the abstract had been certified, because the rule defined the calculation and excluded water and salt from it. Within that defined calculation, however, the proportion was complete, and the label stated the strongest form of the claim that the rule recognized.

The second tier carried the claim organic, without the 100 percent qualifier. A product in this tier contained at least 95 percent organic ingredients. The remaining portion was subject to limits rather than open to any ingredient a handler preferred. The remainder had to consist of allowed substances or substances not commercially available in organic form. That structure preserved a high threshold while acknowledging a narrow category of ingredients for which the rule permitted an exception. The five percent margin was therefore not a general license to dilute a product and retain the same claim. It was a bounded allowance, defined by the character and availability of the substances that filled it.

The boundary between the first two tiers and the third tier carried the greatest labeling consequence. A product with at least 70 percent organic ingredients could be described as made with organic ingredients, with the specified ingredients identified in that claim. The made with formulation communicated a substantial presence without presenting the product as organic without qualification. The difference in wording was the information. A buyer reading the principal display panel encountered a qualified statement that pointed to particular ingredients rather than an unqualified characterization of the whole product. The tier recognized the contribution of certified ingredients to a mixed product, while withholding the claim that the product as a whole had crossed the higher thresholds.

Below 70 percent, the rule narrowed the permitted communication further. Ingredients that met the standard could be identified only in the ingredient panel, and no claim under the label could appear on the principal display panel. The ingredient panel served a different function from the display panel. It listed components for a reader who examined the composition of the product, while the display panel presented the product identity to a buyer at a glance. By confining identification to the ingredient panel for products below the 70 percent threshold, the rule prevented a minority presence from becoming the headline characterization of the product. The proportion governed the prominence that the claim could receive.

The USDA Organic seal followed the same graduated logic. Products in the first two tiers, the 100 percent tier and the tier at 95 percent or more, could display the seal. Products in the made with tier could not display the seal. The seal was therefore not a generic decoration for any product containing a certified ingredient. It was a federal mark reserved for products that had reached the thresholds for the two strongest claims. Reserving the seal in that way gave the mark a meaning that a buyer could learn once and apply across brands and certifiers. If the seal had appeared on products at the 70 percent level as well, the visual signal would have blurred the very distinction that the percentage tiers existed to communicate.

Each tier communicated a different relationship between the product and the standard. The rule matched language to proportion, prominence to language, and the seal to the highest proportions. The percentage rules also connected labeling back to handling, because a handler had to know the status of ingredients and the character of any substance within an allowed remainder. An error in those judgments placed the product in a tier that its composition did not support.

A small operation exemption qualified the certification requirement without removing the substantive standard. Under 7 CFR 205.101(a), a production or handling operation whose gross agricultural income from sales under the label totaled 5,000 dollars or less annually was exempt from certification and from the system plan requirement. The exemption addressed scale. It recognized that the full certification process, built around an accredited certifier and an approved plan, could bear heavily on an operation with a very small volume of covered sales, and it drew the line by income from those sales rather than by the total size of a farm or business in every line of activity.

The limits of the exemption were as important as the relief it granted. An exempt operation had to comply with the production and handling standards, and it had to comply with the labeling rules for exempt operations. Exemption removed the obligation to obtain certification and to maintain the required plan through a certifier. It did not authorize production by prohibited methods, and it did not authorize a claim that the labeling rules withheld. The distinction preserved the meaning of the claim at every scale. A small seller received relief from a process requirement, not permission to sell a product under the label on terms that a larger seller could not use.

Two further restrictions marked the boundary between exempt sales and the certified supply chain. Products from an exempt operation could not be used as organic ingredients in another handler processed products. That restriction prevented an uncertified source from entering, through a handler, the percentage calculations and tier claims that depended on certified ingredient status. An exempt operation also could not display the USDA seal. The seal signaled certification under the program as well as composition at a qualifying threshold, and an operation that had not obtained certification could not use the federal mark that represented the certified system. The exemption therefore created a narrow and carefully bounded category. It lowered a procedural barrier for the smallest sellers, defined by a precise income amount, while retaining the substantive requirements that gave the claim meaning.

State programs presented a different question, not whether an operation had to meet the national standard, but whether a State could maintain a program of its own around that standard and, if so, on what terms. Section 6507, codified at 7 U.S.C. 6507, supplied the mechanism. A governing State official could submit a plan for a State organic certification program to the Secretary for approval. A State program had to meet the requirements of the chapter to be approved. Approval was not a formality that followed automatically from submission. It was the federal decision that allowed a State program to operate within the framework for the federal label.

There is no bare preemption clause in this arrangement. The statute did not state in a single sentence that State law on the subject was displaced, and it did not leave States free to define the federal claim for themselves. Instead, it channeled State participation through submission, approval, and continuing federal review. For the federal label, the national standard operates as a floor beneath that label. A State could not authorize a weaker version of the label through an approved program, because a program that failed to meet the requirements of the chapter could not receive the approval on which its place in the system depended. The floor formulation captures the direction of the relationship, provided the mechanism that created the floor remains in view.

A State program could contain more restrictive requirements than the federal requirements, but only under conditions that the statute specified. The added requirements had to further the purposes of the chapter. They could not be inconsistent with the chapter. They could not discriminate against products organically produced in other States. They could not take effect until the Secretary approved them. Each condition addressed a distinct risk. The purpose condition tied added strictness to the statutory aims of national standards, consumer assurance, and interstate commerce. The consistency condition prevented an addition that contradicted the federal framework while claiming to build on it. The nondiscrimination condition prevented a State from using added strictness as a barrier against products from other States. The prior approval condition prevented a State from presenting the Secretary and the market with an addition already in force.

Continuing oversight accompanied initial approval. The Secretary reviewed approved State programs at least once every five years and made determinations on applications within six months. Substantive changes to an approved program had to be submitted for approval before implementation. A State could not implement a substantive change first and seek ratification afterward.

The federal floor had consequences for interstate commerce, one of the three purposes Congress stated for the act. A handler shipping a certified product across State lines faced a national definition of the claim, even though State programs could add approved restrictions. The possibility of approved stricter requirements meant that uniformity under the act was uniformity of a floor and of a process, not a promise that every approved program would be identical. The act did not displace State food law generally.

### What happens when a product is falsely labeled organic?

Knowingly selling or labeling a product as organic outside the requirements of the act exposed the seller to a civil penalty of not more than 10,000 dollars for each violation under 7 U.S.C. 6519. A certified operation also faced suspension or revocation of its certification for noncompliance.

The penalty provision in 7 U.S.C. 6519 turned on knowledge. It applied to a person who knowingly sold or labeled a product as organic except in accordance with the act. The amount was not more than 10,000 dollars for each violation. The per violation structure mattered because a course of conduct involving multiple products or multiple acts of labeling could present more than a single violation, and the statute measured the exposure by violation rather than by a single global sum for an entire course of dealing. The provision addressed the commercial act of selling or labeling, which was the point at which a false or unsupported claim reached a buyer and affected a transaction.

Knowledge distinguished the penalty provision from a regime that punished every error in the same way. The certification system contained many points at which a mistake could arise, in a plan, in ingredient status, in a percentage calculation, or in the placement of a claim on a package. Section 6519 singled out knowing conduct for the stated civil penalty. That choice did not make other departures harmless. A certified operation that failed to comply with the requirements faced consequences within the certification system itself, including suspension or revocation of certification. Loss of certification removed the foundation for selling products under the claim through the certified channel, a consequence that could affect an operation as seriously as a monetary penalty.

Suspension and revocation operated on the status that certification conferred. Suspension interrupted that status, while revocation withdrew it. The act added a further consequence for the most serious category identified in the fact of revocation. A person whose certification was revoked for a willful violation was ineligible to receive certification for five years. The five year period extended the effect of revocation beyond the immediate loss of a certificate. It barred a return to certified status during the period, even if the person sought certification through the ordinary application process after the revocation. Willfulness, as the trigger for that extended ineligibility, marked a distinction between a revocation arising from conduct the statute treated as willful and other grounds that did not carry the same stated period of exclusion.

The civil penalty addressed the act of mislabeling itself. Suspension, revocation, and the five year ineligibility addressed the standing of an operation within the system that made the label credible. Enforcement also completed the logic of the exemption and the tiers, because an exempt operation remained bound by the standards and the labeling rules, and a handler that displayed the seal for a product in the made with tier used a claim that the rule had withheld.

Read together, certification, labeling, State programs, and enforcement formed a single design. Accredited agents certified operations under an agreed plan verified by inspection. Percentage tiers matched the language and prominence of a claim to the composition of the product, and the seal marked the two highest tiers. A narrow exemption relieved the smallest operations of certification and the plan while retaining the substantive standards, barring their products from use as certified ingredients in another handler processed products, and withholding the seal. State programs operated through federal approval, with the national standard as a floor and stricter State requirements allowed only under the statutory conditions and prior approval. Penalties and certification sanctions protected the resulting claim. The design rested on a premise stated in the purposes of the act, that a national marketing claim required a national meaning, verified before sale and enforceable after it.

## Twelve Years From Statute to Working Standard

The Organic Foods Production Act became law on November 28, 1990, as Title XXI of the Food, Agriculture, Conservation, and Trade Act of 1990. Its stated purposes reached toward a national marketing standard, assurance to consumers that products sold under the label met a consistent standard, and interstate commerce in food produced under that standard. The enactment of those purposes did not create an enforceable definition by itself. The statute supplied the architecture, the prohibitions in outline, the certification structure, and the institutional process through which a detailed standard had to be built. Until the Department of Agriculture completed a regulation under the act, producers, handlers, certifiers, and state programs had statutory direction without a single operative rule that could govern the label in commerce. The distance between the 1990 enactment and full implementation on October 21, 2002 therefore measured more than administrative delay. The interval showed what happened when Congress assigned the content of a contested standard to a process that seated the affected constituencies inside the advisory institution, tied the Secretary's authority to recommendations developed through that institution, and then required the resulting regulation to survive notice and comment rulemaking on a scale that turned procedure into a second legislative arena.

The statute made negotiation part of the design from the beginning. The National Organic Standards Board consisted of fifteen members appointed by the Secretary of Agriculture for five year terms. Four members owned or operated an organic farming operation. Two owned or operated an organic handling operation. One owned or operated a retail establishment with significant trade in products sold under the label. Three had expertise in environmental protection and resource conservation. Three represented public interest or consumer interest groups. One had expertise in toxicology, ecology, or biochemistry. One served as a certifying agent. The composition mattered because the Board did not merely comment on a standard drafted elsewhere. Under 7 U.S.C. 6517(d), the National List established by the Secretary had to be based on a proposed National List or proposed amendments developed by the Board, and the Secretary lacked authority to add exemptions for synthetic substances beyond those the Board had proposed. The Board also advised the Secretary on implementation generally. A regulation that broke with Board recommendations on central questions therefore did more than disappoint an advisory body. The departure exposed a fault in the legitimating structure the statute had created.

The Board seated constituencies that did not enter the process with the same definition of fidelity to the label. Farmers who had built operations around certified production had investments in practices and in the commercial meaning those practices carried. Handlers faced questions about ingredients, processing aids, storage, and commingling that differed from the questions raised by field production. Retailers had an interest in a label consumers could understand at the point of sale and in supply sufficient to stock shelves. Environmental and resource conservation experts evaluated substances and methods against effects that extended beyond a single farm or product. Consumer and public interest representatives guarded the expectation attached to the word on the package. The scientist seat brought toxicology, ecology, or biochemistry to judgments that also involved necessity and consistency with certified farming and handling. A certifying agent confronted the practical question whether a requirement could be inspected and applied across operations of different size and type. Agreement among those seats had a different character from agreement inside a single trade association or a single agency bureau. The Board's recommendations carried weight because the statute forced unlike perspectives into one formal recommendation process.

The statutory test for the National List reinforced the negotiated character of the enterprise. Synthetic substances faced prohibition unless specifically exempted on the National List, while nonsynthetic substances faced allowance unless specifically prohibited. Exceptions ran in both directions, so every listing decision could move a substance across a consequential line. Evaluation turned on whether a substance would harm human health or the environment, whether the substance had become necessary because wholly nonsynthetic alternatives remained unavailable, and whether use accorded with certified farming and handling. Those criteria did not answer themselves. A substance might present limited evidence of harm and also sit uneasily with the practices consumers associated with the label. A material might solve a genuine production problem and also invite objection if the problem could be addressed through a different rotation, input, or handling method. The Board had to translate such judgments into proposed listings, and the Secretary then had to translate Board proposals into regulatory text that regulated parties could follow and certifying agents could enforce. Each translation created room for disagreement about whether fidelity meant fidelity to the statutory words, to the Board's negotiated balance, to established certifier practice, or to consumer expectation as expressed in the marketplace.

Time built into the process before any public controversy over a proposed rule. The Board received its first appointments in 1992, two years after enactment, and developed National List recommendations during the mid 1990s. Those dates alone consumed a substantial part of the eventual twelve year span. A board with fifteen appointed members had to organize, consider substances and practices across crop production, livestock production, and handling, and produce recommendations that could support a comprehensive national regulation. The Department then had to draft a proposal covering production and handling requirements, certification, accreditation, labeling, state programs, and enforcement within one regulatory structure. The breadth of the undertaking helps explain a portion of the elapsed time, although breadth alone does not explain the sharp break that followed the first proposal. The rulemaking record turned contentious because the Department's first comprehensive attempt departed from the Board on three practices that many participants treated as defining boundaries for the label.

### What were the big three in the first proposed organic rule?

The big three were genetic engineering, ionizing radiation, and sewage sludge. The December 16, 1997 proposed rule would have permitted all three in certified production, departing from the recommendations developed by the National Organic Standards Board during the mid-1990s and treating practices many commenters regarded as incompatible with the label.

The first proposed rule appeared on December 16, 1997, more than seven years after the statute became law. Its treatment of genetic engineering, ionizing radiation, and sewage sludge became the center of the dispute. The core of the controversy can be stated without embellishment. The Board had developed recommendations during the mid 1990s. The proposal departed from the Board's recommendations on those points. The proposed text would have allowed the three practices within production governed by the national standard. Critics attached the labels big three and dirty three to the disputed permissions, and the shorter phrase entered the public record of the rulemaking with unusual durability. The terminology mattered because it compressed a complicated regulatory disagreement into a test of institutional trust. Commenters did not have to master every provision on accreditation or labeling to understand the claim that the Department had proposed a standard at odds with the body Congress had placed between the Secretary and the National List.

Each disputed practice raised a distinct problem, and the Department's decision to permit all three in one proposal magnified the reaction to each. Genetic engineering concerned the methods by which an organism or production input came into being, and the final rule later addressed the subject through the category of excluded methods. Ionizing radiation, also called irradiation, concerned treatment applied to a product, and the final rule later prohibited ionizing radiation in production and handling governed by the standard. Sewage sludge concerned a material applied within production, and the final rule later prohibited sewage sludge as well. Those later categories give precision to the endpoint. The final regulation did not rest on a general statement of philosophy. The final regulation prohibited genetic engineering through the excluded methods prohibition, prohibited ionizing radiation through the radiation prohibition, and prohibited sewage sludge through the sludge prohibition. In 1997, however, the proposal moved toward permission on each front. The combined effect transformed three technical questions into a public judgment about whether the national standard would ratify practices that the Board had resisted and that a large segment of the label's existing community regarded as outside the standard's meaning.

The institutional cost of the departure became clear in the comment process. The Department extended the comment period twice. The public received an initial ninety days, then forty five additional days, and then a further extension into the spring of 1998. Extensions of that kind serve a formal purpose in administrative law by enlarging the opportunity to comment on a proposal of broad effect. Extensions also signal that participation has exceeded the agency's first estimate and that the record under construction may alter the outcome. In the organic rulemaking, the extensions gave farms, handlers, certifiers, consumer organizations, and individual purchasers time to frame objections not as isolated preferences but as evidence about the viability of the proposed standard. A comment could address the statutory role of the Board, the consistency of a practice with certified production, the expectations attached to the label, or the practical effect on operations that had produced food under state and private standards before a national rule existed. By the late 1980s, numerous states and private organizations had certified production under differing standards, and the Department stated in the March 2000 proposed rule that forty nine state and private organizations had established individual programs and standards. The 1997 proposal therefore landed in a field with established practices and organized certifiers, not in an empty regulatory space waiting for any definition the Department chose to supply.

The Department received 275,603 comments on the first proposal. The number requires attribution because the superlative attached to the number came from the Department itself. In the March 2000 proposed rule, the Department described the 275,603 comments as the largest public response to a proposed rule in USDA history. The figure documented scale rather than unanimity, and a careful reading of the episode should preserve that distinction. A comment total does not reveal the content of every submission or prove that every commenter addressed the big three. The figure does establish that the proposal activated participation far beyond the regulated firms that ordinarily dominate a technical rulemaking docket. The response also changed the political and administrative cost of proceeding. An agency may defend a contested provision against a trade group or a professional association and complete a rule on roughly the original schedule. A docket of 275,603 comments creates a record that the agency must digest, categorize, and answer in the reasoning for any final action. The volume also gives members of Congress, journalists, and organized constituencies a visible measure of public engagement. Notice and comment rulemaking, designed as a channel for information and objection, became a referendum like contest over the boundaries of the label.


Secretary of Agriculture Dan Glickman resolved the immediate impasse on May 8, 1998. Secretary Glickman announced that genetic engineering, sewage sludge, and irradiation would not be allowed in the national standards. The Department withdrew the proposal to rewrite the regulation. The sequence deserves emphasis because the announcement preceded a new proposed text. The Secretary first restored the boundary that the Board's recommendations had supported on the three disputed practices, and only after that restoration did the Department rebuild the proposal through further comment and a second rulemaking round. The decision acknowledged that the Department could not obtain a durable standard by treating Board disagreement as one comment among many. The Board's authority rested in part on statute and in part on confidence. The statute required the National List to rest on Board proposals, and the broader implementation role gave the Board a continuing place in the program. Confidence came from the Board's representative composition. A proposal that discarded the Board's judgment on highly visible practices weakened the claim that the standard had emerged from the process Congress designed.

Withdrawal carried a price in years. The Department discarded the first comprehensive proposal after an extended comment fight and returned to the drafting process with a public commitment that narrowed one set of choices while leaving many other questions unresolved. Livestock production illustrates the unfinished business. On October 24, 1998, the Department published a further request for comments on livestock and related issues. That request drew 10,817 comments. The smaller total relative to the first proposal should not be mistaken for a minor episode. Livestock provisions govern feed, health care, and living conditions under 7 U.S.C. 6509, and each subject joins animal management to the integrity of meat, milk, and other livestock products sold under the label. Questions about feed determine whether the production chain behind a livestock product satisfies the standard from the ground up. Questions about health care determine how an operation responds to illness without moving the animal or the product outside the standard. Questions about living conditions determine the physical circumstances under which certified livestock production occurs. A general endorsement of the label cannot settle those subjects. The Department needed a record specific enough to draft provisions that producers could meet and certifiers could inspect.

The October 1998 livestock request also showed how the rulemaking had fragmented into successive arenas. The first arena concerned the legitimacy of the overall standard after the big three dispute. The second arena concerned the detailed conditions for one major sector. Later proceedings would test the revised text as a whole. Each arena added information, and each added time. Participants who had mobilized against genetic engineering, radiation, and sludge did not disband after May 1998, because the remaining provisions defined the standard under which certified operations would function. Producers and handlers who supported a workable national label also had reason to remain engaged, because delay left the prior patchwork in place and postponed the uniform standard that Congress had authorized in 1990 to facilitate interstate commerce. The same process that protected the standard against a legitimacy failure also prolonged uncertainty for the market the statute meant to organize. That tension sits at the center of the twelve year story. Participation improved the correspondence between the final text and the constituencies expected to use the label, while the mechanisms of participation repeatedly reset the calendar.

The Department released the second proposed rule on March 7, 2000, and the proposal appeared in the Federal Register on March 8, 2000. The revised proposal excluded the big three. Exclusion represented more than a change in drafting preference after Secretary Glickman's May 8, 1998 announcement. The Department had publicly committed the rewritten rule to a boundary, had taken a separate round of comments on livestock and related issues, and had returned with a comprehensive proposal that participants could test against the earlier failure. The second proposal drew 40,774 comments. The Department's figure shows sustained engagement at a level far below the extraordinary response to the first proposal and far above the routine response to a narrow technical amendment. The drop from 275,603 comments to 40,774 comments fits the changed posture of the rule. The most explosive permissions had been removed, and commenters could concentrate on provisions governing production, handling, certification, and labeling rather than on the threshold question whether the Department had departed from the Board on defining practices. The record does not justify treating the lower number as apathy. A second comprehensive proposal, arriving almost a decade after enactment, required the Department to read and answer tens of thousands of submissions before a final rule could issue.

Kathleen Merrigan, Administrator of the Agricultural Marketing Service, oversaw completion of the rule. The institutional location mattered. The National Organic Program sat within the Agricultural Marketing Service, the part of the Department charged with the administrative completion of a marketing standard rather than with a food safety regime. The act regulated a label and a marketing claim. The statute did not certify a nutritional claim, and the rulemaking did not convert the label into a general judgment about food safety. Administrative completion therefore meant building a defensible system for determining which products could enter commerce under a defined claim. That system had to coordinate accredited certifying agents, approved production and handling plans, inspections in program practice, state program approval, labeling tiers, and enforcement. A final rule that settled the big three while leaving certification machinery incoherent would not have satisfied the statute. Conversely, elegant certification machinery attached to a standard that key constituencies rejected would not have produced a stable national label. Merrigan's tenure at the Agricultural Marketing Service during completion of the rule placed responsibility for both dimensions in the office that had to bring the long proceeding to a close.

The final rule appeared on December 21, 2000, at 65 FR 80548. Publication came a decade after enactment, yet publication did not complete the chronology. The legal effective date received correction to April 21, 2001 under the Congressional Review Act. Full implementation arrived on October 21, 2002, the date by which all products sold under the label had to comply. The three dates answer different legal and practical questions. December 21, 2000 marks the moment the Department placed the completed regulatory text in the Federal Register after two proposed rules, a withdrawal, a separate livestock comment request, and hundreds of thousands of comments across the proceedings. April 21, 2001 marks the corrected legal effective date that governed when the rule took effect under the Congressional Review Act. October 21, 2002 marks the compliance horizon for products in commerce, the point at which the national standard moved from published law and phased compliance into full operation. Precision about the sequence prevents a common compression in accounts of the act. The standard took ten years to reach publication in final form and nearly twelve years to reach full implementation. The governing date for a farmer planning production, a handler revising labels, a certifier preparing inspections, or a retailer stocking products was the implementation date, not the anniversary of enactment and not the day the final text appeared in print.


The twelve year path resulted from the interaction of those features rather than from a single failure. Congress created a standard whose detailed content depended on a representative Board, the Department tested the limits of deference to that Board in December 1997, and the comment record forced a public reversal in May 1998. Withdrawal protected the eventual rule from a disputed foundation, but withdrawal also required a livestock inquiry, a second proposal, another comment round, a corrected effective date, and a transition to full compliance. Notice and comment rulemaking supplied the arena in which those corrections occurred. The arena also imposed the calendar. By October 21, 2002, the standard that reached full implementation prohibited the big three through operative regulatory categories and rested on a record shaped by the constituencies seated under the statute itself. The delay and the legitimacy of the endpoint came from the same source.

## The Court Decision Congress Reversed Within the Year

Full implementation of the national standard arrived on October 21, 2002, the date by which all products sold as organic had to comply. In that same month, Arthur Harvey sued the Secretary of Agriculture in the federal district court in Maine. Harvey was an organic blueberry farmer from Maine, and his complaint ran to nine counts against the final rule. The theory was narrow and textual. The Department of Agriculture, acting through the National Organic Program in the Agricultural Marketing Service, had issued a rule governing production, handling, certification, and labeling. Harvey argued that in several places the rule allowed what the Organic Foods Production Act forbade, or allowed it on terms the act had not supplied. He lost in the district court. The case then moved to the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, where the court examined three regulatory provisions, compared each provision to the words Congress had enacted in 1990, and concluded that each provision crossed the line the text had drawn. A statute that regulates a marketing claim lives on definitions, exemptions, and the precise placement of qualifying phrases.

### What did Harvey v. Veneman actually hold?

The First Circuit held that three parts of the final rule exceeded the statute. Synthetic substances in processing and handling lacked a textual basis, the dairy conversion allowance conflicted with the requirement for organically produced feed, and the commercial availability treatment for nonorganic agricultural ingredients reached beyond the act.

The judgment was Harvey v. Veneman, 396 F.3d 28, decided on January 26, 2005. The chronology gave the holding unusual weight. The National Organic Standards Board had first been appointed in 1992 and had developed its National List recommendations in the mid 1990s. A first proposed rule had appeared on December 16, 1997, had drawn 275,603 comments, a figure the Department described in the March 2000 proposed rule as the largest public response to a proposed rule in the history of the Department of Agriculture, and had been withdrawn after Secretary Dan Glickman announced on May 8, 1998 that genetic engineering, sewage sludge, and irradiation would not be allowed. A second proposed rule followed in March 2000, the final rule was published on December 21, 2000, its effective date was corrected to April 21, 2001 under the Congressional Review Act, and full implementation was deferred to October 21, 2002.

The first count concerned synthetic substances in processing and handling. The rule provisions at 7 CFR 205.600(b) and 205.605(b) permitted synthetic substances in the processing and handling of products labeled organic. The court held those provisions contrary to the act. Section 6510(a)(1) of Title 7, the handling provision, barred a handling operation from adding any synthetic ingredient during processing or postharvest handling, and the bar, as the court read the text enacted in 1990, was not qualified by a general reference to the National List. The court then turned to section 6517(c)(1)(B)(iii), a clause within the National List provisions, which contemplated exempted handling substances that are nonsynthetic but not organically produced. The act created a general structure in which synthetic substances were prohibited unless specifically exempted on the National List, and nonsynthetic substances were allowed unless specifically prohibited on the list. Handling, however, had its own operative sentence in section 6510, and a regulation that treated a listed synthetic as permissible in a processed product had to find its authority in that sentence. The court concluded that the authority was absent.

The practical stakes reached beyond a single ingredient list. A handler operating under the rule had reason to consult the regulatory lists and to treat a listed substance as settled. The court unsettled that reliance by locating the governing command one level higher, in the statute. Certification could not cure the defect, because certification attests compliance with the applicable standard, and the court had declared part of that standard unlawful. The label was national, the rule was national, and the alleged defect sat in the text every handler selling a processed product under the label had to use.

The second count concerned the conversion of a dairy herd. The rule allowed a converting dairy operation, during the twelve month conversion period, to be fed up to 20 percent nonorganic feed. The shorthand for the provision was the 80/20 provision, reflecting a ration built predominantly from organic feed while the operation completed conversion. The court held the provision inconsistent with the act, because the act required that livestock feed be organically produced. Section 6509 set requirements for livestock production covering feed, health care, and living conditions, and the feed requirement contained no percentage qualification of the kind the rule had inserted. The court nevertheless asked whether the act had authorized the Department to soften the feed requirement for the conversion year, and on the text the answer was no. The difference between 100 percent and 80 percent became legally decisive precisely because Congress had written the standard as a condition of the claim rather than as an aspiration for the sector.

The third count concerned commercial availability and nonorganic agricultural ingredients. Under the labeling structure at 7 CFR 205.301, a product in the tier labeled organic had to contain at least 95 percent organic ingredients, and the remaining ingredients had to be allowed substances or ingredients not commercially available in organic form. Commercial availability functioned as a narrow release valve. Where an ingredient could not be obtained in organic form, the rule allowed a nonorganic version within limits, so that a product was not disqualified by a supply failure outside handler control. The First Circuit held that the rule treatment of nonorganic agricultural ingredients permitted on commercial availability grounds exceeded the act as the court read it. The distinction mattered because an agricultural ingredient is itself a farm product, capable in principle of certified production, and its nonorganic version competes directly with the certified supply the statute was meant to foster.

Taken together, the three holdings shared a single logic. Listed synthetics in processing could serve functions for which handlers lacked a wholly nonsynthetic alternative. A conversion ration could ease a dairy operation into compliance. A commercial availability allowance could keep a processed product viable when an organic ingredient could not be sourced. The court in each instance measured purpose against text and found the text controlling. The court did not hold that the practices were unsafe, and the act set no food safety standard against which safety could have been judged. Consistency, for the court, had to be the consistency Congress enacted, under the purposes declared at section 6501: to establish national standards governing the marketing of certain agricultural products as organically produced products, to assure consumers that organically produced products meet a consistent standard, and to facilitate interstate commerce in fresh and processed food that is organically produced.

The remainder of the complaint did not succeed, and that boundary deserves equal attention. Harvey brought nine counts and prevailed on three, after the whole case had failed in the district court. The program survived. The apparatus of accredited certifying agents, organic plans agreed to by the producer, the handler, and the certifying agent, inspection under the rule, labeling tiers, and the National List procedure all remained in place. The decision did not return the country to the patchwork that had preceded the national standard, under which, by the Department account in its March 2000 proposed rule, 49 State and private organizations had established individual programs and standards for certifying organic products. That restraint shaped the legislative response, because Congress was asked to repair three defined provisions rather than to rebuild a dismantled program.

The remedy arrived first as a judgment about timing. On remand, a consent judgment on June 9, 2005 gave the Secretary one year to publish conforming rules, bringing the regulatory text into line with the First Circuit construction. The judgment also allowed nonconforming products to remain in commerce for up to two years. The one year period imposed a deadline on the Department, ensuring that the judicial construction did not linger as an unenforced declaration while the rule on the books contradicted it. The two year allowance recognized the physical reality of processed food, which moves through warehouses, distribution chains, and retail shelves on schedules a court order cannot instantly reverse. The settlement held both concerns in negotiated form: the agency received a defined interval to rewrite, the market received a defined interval to clear, and the statute as construed became the destination toward which both intervals ran.

Congress intervened before the Department acted under that judgment. The vehicle was section 797 of the Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies Appropriations Act, 2006, Public Law 109-97, enacted on November 10, 2005. Section 797 was an appropriations rider, carried on a measure whose principal business was funding the Department and related agencies, and the rider amended the organic statute directly. The Organic Trade Association promoted the rider. Consumer and grower organizations opposed it. Those attributed positions capture the division the decision had opened. The trade association position treated the invalidated provisions as workable elements of a functioning national program, displaced by the court on textual grounds and worth restoring by supplying the missing statutory language. The mechanism of [how riders on omnibus measures change substantive law](/2022/04/15/omnibus-bills-and-riders/) sits inside that dispute, because the rider did not merely fund the program, direct a study, or delay a deadline. The rider rewrote the underlying statute, and the rewrite determined what the Department could lawfully do when the consent judgment deadline arrived.

The textual surgery was exact, and its exactness confirms that Congress legislated with the opinion in view. First, section 797 amended section 6510(a)(1) by inserting the phrase "not appearing on the National List" after the words "synthetic ingredient." The sentence the First Circuit had read as an unqualified bar on adding a synthetic ingredient during processing or postharvest handling became, after amendment, a bar on adding a synthetic ingredient that did not appear on the National List. Second, section 797 struck section 6517(c)(1)(B)(iii) altogether, removing the clause the court had read as confirming the absence of synthetic exemptions in handling. Third, section 797 added a new provision, section 6517(d)(6), on commercial availability, supplying a statutory basis for the treatment of nonorganic agricultural ingredients when organic forms were not commercially available. The amendment also restored the dairy conversion feeding practice on a statutory footing, returning the authority to permit the conversion ration the court had held inconsistent with the feed requirement.

The sequence deserves to be stated plainly. A court construed the statute and invalidated three provisions of a rule. The agency accepted a judgment requiring conforming rules within one year. Before that year ran, and before the Department published conforming rules, Congress amended the statute so that the rules the court had invalidated would conform to the statute as amended. The judgment was not defied. The statute underneath the judgment changed. That distinction preserves the formal authority of the court while recording the practical result, which was reversal in substance within months of the January 2005 decision. Judicial construction fixes the meaning of a text only for as long as the text stands. A legislature that disagrees with a construction, and that can assemble a majority on a measure the executive will sign, may rewrite the text and displace the construction without disturbing the judgment as an order binding the parties. An appropriations measure offers a particularly efficient vehicle for such a rewrite, because funding legislation moves on a calendar driven by the fiscal year, attracts votes for reasons unrelated to any single rider, and presents the President with a package in which a discrete amendment is one provision among many. The cost of that speed was compression.

Before the national standard became enforceable, the label rested on a patchwork. By the Department account in the March 2000 proposed rule, 49 State and private organizations had established individual programs and standards, and California and Washington were among the States with organic statutes of their own. Full implementation in 2002 gave the claim one enforceable national meaning. From that date, a product sold or labeled as organically produced had to be produced and handled without synthetic chemicals except as the chapter provided, had to satisfy the condition that prohibited substances not have been applied to the land during the three years immediately preceding harvest, and had to be produced and handled under an organic plan agreed to by the producer, the handler, and the certifying agent. Certification became the gate through which that definition reached the market. Producers and handlers were certified by certifying agents accredited by the Secretary, with state officials and private entities both eligible to serve, and certification rested on an approved organic system plan and on site inspection, with annual inspections forming the operating practice under the rule.

Enforcement gave the gate a sanction. Knowingly selling or labeling a product as organic except in accordance with the act carried a civil penalty of not more than 10,000 dollars for each violation under section 6519 as enacted, certifications could be suspended or revoked, and a person whose certification was revoked for a willful violation was ineligible to receive certification for five years. The labeling tiers translated the standard into information a buyer could read. Under 7 CFR 205.301, a product labeled 100 percent organic contained all organic ingredients, excluding water and salt. A product labeled organic contained at least 95 percent organic ingredients, with the remaining ingredients limited to allowed substances or ingredients not commercially available in organic form. A product labeled as made with specified organic ingredients contained at least 70 percent organic ingredients, and below 70 percent organic ingredients could be identified only in the ingredient panel, with no organic claim on the principal display panel. Products in the first two tiers could display the USDA Organic seal, and the made with tier could not. The seal marked the tiers in which certified content crossed the highest thresholds, depended on certification by an accredited agent, and was withheld from exempt operations and from the 70 percent tier alike. Neither the court nor Congress disturbed the tier structure in 2005, because the litigation concerned the permissible content of the certified stream at three points, not the architecture by which content percentages were disclosed.

The commercial setting was a market already large enough to sustain national contention over definitions. Organic food sales were reported at more than 3 billion dollars annually in 1998, a figure attributed to contemporary reporting and floor debate from that period. The figure belongs to the years of the rule fight, when the first proposed rule had been withdrawn and the second was in preparation, and the figure measures the stakes as participants perceived them during that period rather than any later market. Scale also helps explain the speed of the 2005 response. Formulations, supply contracts, conversion plans, and retail relationships across a national market rested on the three provisions the court had invalidated, and the consent judgment had set a date by which the regulatory basis for those arrangements would change.

An honest account of the statute must then record, with equal clarity, what the act did not do. The act did not set food safety standards. A product could satisfy every condition of certified production and handling and remain subject to the body of food safety law that governed food generally, because the organic statute supplied no safety criteria of its own and certified no safety outcome. The act did not certify any claim about nutrition or health. The standard did not measure nutrient content and did not warrant that a certified product differed nutritionally from a product sold without the claim. Readings of the label that treat certification as a safety grade or a nutrition grade import into the statute a function Congress did not assign to the Department, to the Board, or to certifying agents, and the persistence of such readings belongs to the territory surveyed by [the myths that gather around the farm bill](/2021/03/15/farm-bill-myths/), where measures built for one defined purpose acquire reputations for purposes their texts never contained.

The act also did not displace State food law generally. The State role under the act was specific and channeled. Under section 6507, a governing State official could submit a plan for a State organic certification program to the Secretary for approval, an approved program had to meet the requirements of the chapter, and a State program could contain more restrictive requirements only if those requirements furthered the purposes of the chapter, were not inconsistent with the chapter, were not discriminatory toward products organically produced in other States, and did not take effect until approved by the Secretary. For the federal label, the national standard operated as a floor, a State could not authorize a weaker version of the label, and a State could add stricter requirements only through the federal approval channel.

The final limit is chronological, and the litigation cannot be understood without it. The act was signed on November 28, 1990, yet the act by itself made no operative standard that a producer could be held to in the market. Nothing was enforceable until the rule. The Board was first appointed in 1992, the first proposal arrived in December 1997 and was withdrawn, the second proposal arrived in March 2000, the final rule was published on December 21, 2000, the effective date was corrected to April 21, 2001, and full compliance was required only on October 21, 2002. Harvey filed in October 2002 because October 2002 was the first moment at which the complete standard bound every product sold under the claim and a challenger could test the finished rule against the finished statute in a live market. The act promised a national meaning for a label, delivered the machinery to define and police the meaning a decade later, and left safety, nutrition, and the general law of food to other instruments, while Congress retained, and in 2005 exercised, the authority to rewrite the definition whenever a court reading of the enacted text diverged from the definition Congress was prepared to defend.

## What the Definition Settled and What It Left Open

The statute settled the prior question of authority over a word. After November 28, 1990, the claim on a label for covered agricultural products carried a federal definition, administered through certification, and a state program that addressed the same label had to meet the federal chapter and pass through federal approval. The definition settled what the claim promised, a consistent standard behind a marketing representation, and settled what the claim withheld, any federal judgment about safety or nutrition. What enactment left open was the content inside the definition, because Congress wrote conditions, criteria, and institutions rather than a finished catalogue of permitted and prohibited substances, and the catalogue had to be built, defended, and rebuilt inside the institutions the text created.

Two machines did that building. The National Organic Standards Board, fifteen appointed members seated by constituency, developed the proposals on which the National List had to rest, and the list gave the standard its operative content through exemptions and prohibitions that expired unless the Board reviewed each listing and the Secretary renewed it within five years. The authority of the resulting standard rested on that process, and the process faced two tests of different kinds. The first proposed rule of December 16, 1997 departed from Board recommendations on genetic engineering, ionizing radiation, and sewage sludge, drew 275,603 comments, and ended in withdrawal after the announcement of May 8, 1998. The second test arrived after full implementation, when the First Circuit in Harvey v. Veneman measured the final rule against the enacted text, and Congress answered within the year through section 797 of Public Law 109-97, amending the statute so the contested provisions rested on statutory language.

The record of the act therefore points outward in several directions at once, toward the history and structure of the farm bill vehicle that carried the title into law, toward the separate food safety rulemaking that governs hazards the label never addressed, toward the consumer protection framework that polices deceptive labeling in commerce generally, toward the practice of riders on omnibus appropriations measures, and toward the myths that gather around farm legislation when a narrow instrument acquires a broad reputation. Each direction explains one part of the design, and none replaces the text at 7 U.S.C. 6501 and following. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Organic Foods Production Act create?

The act created one national standard governing the marketing of certain agricultural products as organically produced products, codified at 7 U.S.C. 6501 and following in chapter 94 of title 7. The act created a certification system in which the Secretary of Agriculture accredits certifying agents and producers and handlers operate under an organic plan agreed among the producer, the handler, and the certifying agent. The act created the National Organic Standards Board to develop proposals for the National List, the list that exempts specified synthetic substances from a general prohibition and bars specified nonsynthetic substances against a general permission. The act also supplied labeling tiers, approval of state programs, and penalties for misuse of the claim.

### Q: Who sits on the board under the Organic Foods Production Act?

The National Organic Standards Board holds fifteen members appointed by the Secretary of Agriculture, each serving a five year term, and the Board stands established under the Federal Advisory Committee Act. Four members own or operate an organic farming operation. Two members own or operate an organic handling operation. One member owns or operates a retail establishment with significant trade in organic products. Three members hold expertise in environmental protection and resource conservation. Three members represent public interest or consumer interest groups. One member holds expertise in toxicology, ecology, or biochemistry. One member serves as a certifying agent. The allocation gives no constituency a majority of the fifteen seats.

### Q: What is the National List under the Organic Foods Production Act?

The National List is the list established by the Secretary under 7 U.S.C. 6517 through which the general substance rules of the act acquire specific content. Synthetic substances face prohibition in production and handling unless the list grants a specific exemption, and nonsynthetic substances face permission unless the list imposes a specific prohibition. The Secretary establishes the list based on a proposed list or proposed amendments developed by the National Organic Standards Board, and the Secretary may not add exemptions for synthetic substances beyond those the Board proposed. Evaluation applies criteria covering harm to human health or the environment, necessity where wholly nonsynthetic alternatives remain unavailable, and consistency with organic farming and handling.

### Q: Does organic mean no synthetic substances under the Organic Foods Production Act?

The label does not mean the absence of every synthetic substance. Under the general rule at 7 U.S.C. 6517, synthetic substances face prohibition in production and handling unless the National List grants a specific exemption, so a certified operation may use an exempted synthetic substance within the terms of the listing and the operation plan. The reverse rule governs natural substances. Nonsynthetic substances face permission unless the list specifically prohibits a substance, so a natural substance can sit outside the standard despite a natural origin. The word on the label therefore certifies compliance with the defined standard, including valid exemptions and prohibitions, rather than a claim that no synthetic input played any part in production or handling.

### Q: Why did the Organic Foods Production Act rule take twelve years?

The interval ran from enactment on November 28, 1990 to full implementation on October 21, 2002. The Board received first appointments in 1992 and developed National List recommendations during the middle years of the decade. The first proposed rule appeared on December 16, 1997 and would have permitted genetic engineering, ionizing radiation, and sewage sludge against Board recommendations. A record comment response followed, and Secretary Dan Glickman announced on May 8, 1998 that the three practices would not be allowed, with the proposal withdrawn for rewriting. A livestock comment round followed in October 1998, a second proposal arrived in March 2000, and the final rule appeared on December 21, 2000 with an effective date corrected to April 21, 2001.

### Q: How many comments did the first Organic Foods Production Act proposal draw?

The first proposed rule, published on December 16, 1997, drew 275,603 comments. The Department of Agriculture stated that figure in the March 2000 proposed rule and described the response in that document as the largest public response to a proposed rule in the history of the Department. The comment period had begun at ninety days, gained forty five additional days, and extended further into the spring of 1998. The volume matched the substance of the dispute, because the proposal would have permitted genetic engineering, ionizing radiation, and sewage sludge in production under the label. On May 8, 1998, Secretary Dan Glickman announced that the three practices would not be allowed, and the proposal was withdrawn.

### Q: Does the Organic Foods Production Act preempt state organic rules?

The act contains no bare preemption clause, and the relationship runs through a mechanism at 7 U.S.C. 6507. A governing state official may submit a plan for a state organic certification program to the Secretary for approval, and a state program must meet the requirements of the chapter to gain approval. For the federal label, the national standard operates as a floor, and a state may not authorize a weaker version of the label. A state program may contain more restrictive requirements only where the additions further the purposes of the chapter, remain consistent with the chapter, avoid discrimination against products produced in other states, and take effect after approval by the Secretary.

### Q: Did Congress overturn a court ruling on the Organic Foods Production Act?

Congress reversed the practical effect of a court construction by amending the statute itself. In Harvey v. Veneman, 396 F.3d 28, decided January 26, 2005, the First Circuit held three parts of the final rule inconsistent with the act, covering synthetic substances in processing, the dairy conversion feeding allowance, and the treatment of nonorganic ingredients on commercial availability grounds. A consent judgment on June 9, 2005 gave the Secretary one year to publish conforming rules. Before the Department acted, Congress enacted section 797 of the Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies Appropriations Act, 2006, Public Law 109-97, on November 10, 2005, amending the underlying provisions and restoring the contested positions on a statutory footing.

### Q: What is the National Organic Program?

The National Organic Program is the program office within the Agricultural Marketing Service of the Department of Agriculture that administers the national standard created by the act. Through the program, the Secretary accredits certifying agents, approves state organic certification programs, maintains the National List on proposals developed by the National Organic Standards Board, and enforces the chapter against misuse of the label. Kathleen Merrigan served as Administrator of the Agricultural Marketing Service during completion of the final rule, published on December 21, 2000. The program administers a marketing standard for a label claim. The program sets no food safety standard and certifies no claim about nutrition or health for products sold under the label.

### Q: Who may use the USDA Organic seal?

The USDA Organic seal may appear only on products in the two highest labeling tiers under 7 CFR 205.301. A product in the tier for 100 percent organic, in which every ingredient counts as organic apart from water and salt, may display the seal. A product in the tier labeled organic, with at least 95 percent organic ingredients and a remainder limited to listed substances or substances unavailable in organic form, may also display the seal. A product in the tier for made with organic ingredients, at 70 percent or more, may not display the seal. An exempt operation with annual organic sales of 5,000 dollars or less may not display the seal either.

### Q: How does organic certification work?

Certification runs through accredited certifying agents rather than through direct federal certification of each operation. Under 7 U.S.C. 6515, the Secretary accredits certifying agents, and state officials and private entities may serve in that role. A producer or handler gains certification from an accredited agent on the basis of an approved organic system plan and on site inspection. The plan states how the operation will meet the production and handling requirements, including the rule against synthetic chemicals except as the chapter provides and the three year land history rule for crops. Annual inspections formed the operating practice under the rule, testing the operation against the agreed plan and the standard the plan implements.

### Q: How can a substance be added to or removed from the National List?

A substance reaches the National List, leaves the list, or gains an amended listing through a sequence the statute assigns to named actors. Any person may petition to add, remove, or amend a listing. The National Organic Standards Board develops proposed listings and amendments after evaluation under the statutory criteria, covering harm to human health or the environment, necessity where wholly nonsynthetic alternatives remain unavailable, and consistency with organic farming and handling. Board action requires two thirds of the votes cast at a meeting with a quorum present. The Secretary establishes the list based on Board proposals and may not add exemptions for synthetic substances beyond those the Board proposed.

### Q: What is sunset review under the Organic Foods Production Act?

Sunset review is the periodic reexamination that section 6517(e) imposes on every exemption and prohibition on the National List. No exemption or prohibition remains valid unless the National Organic Standards Board has reviewed the listing within five years of adoption or of the prior review and the Secretary has renewed the listing. A listing lapses unless review and renewal occur within the period, so an exception granted on one record must face a later record under the same criteria of harm, necessity, and consistency. In September 2013, the Department published a revised sunset procedure under which a listing continued unless the Board voted by two thirds to remove the listing and the Secretary concurred, a procedure producer and consumer organizations criticized as reversing the renewal burden and the Department defended as consistent with the decisive vote requirement.

### Q: What is the difference between 100 percent organic and organic on a label?

The two tiers state different proportions under 7 CFR 205.301, and the wording on the label tracks the proportion. A product labeled 100 percent organic contains organic ingredients throughout the measured total, with water and salt excluded from the calculation. A product labeled organic, without the 100 percent wording, contains at least 95 percent organic ingredients. The remainder in the second tier faces limits and must consist of substances allowed under the rule or substances not commercially available in organic form. Both tiers may display the USDA Organic seal. The distinction gives a buyer a complete claim in the first tier and a high proportion claim with a bounded remainder in the second tier.

### Q: Are imported foods held to the same organic standard?

The label carries the same condition wherever a product originates. A product sold or labeled as organically produced must rest on certification under the program, and origin outside the country does not substitute a different condition for that requirement. The statute assigns accreditation of certifying agents to the Secretary, and the accreditation channel carries no confinement to domestic agents in the text. Certification can therefore stand behind a product through an accredited agent without the act treating origin as a separate test. The governing question for the label remains constant across sources: certification under the program, judged against the same national standard that governs the claim for any product offered under the label in commerce.

### Q: What penalties apply for misusing the organic label?

A person who knowingly sells or labels a product as organic except in accordance with the act faces a civil penalty of not more than 10,000 dollars for each violation under 7 U.S.C. 6519. The per violation measure lets exposure grow with the number of violations in a course of conduct. The certification system adds sanctions of a different kind. A certification may face suspension or revocation for noncompliance, and loss of certification removes the standing on which lawful use of the label depends for a certified operation. A person whose certification faced revocation for a willful violation remains ineligible to receive certification for five years after the revocation.

### Q: Does the act set standards for organic livestock?

The act addresses livestock directly at 7 U.S.C. 6509 through requirements on feed, health care, and living conditions. Feed requirements govern what an animal in certified production consumes, health care requirements govern treatments and interventions during production, and living conditions requirements govern the environment in which the animal is kept. The three year land rule at 7 U.S.C. 6504 excludes livestock from that clause, so animal eligibility rests on the livestock provisions rather than on the history of a parcel. The feed requirement later stood at the center of the dairy conversion dispute in Harvey v. Veneman, where the First Circuit measured a rule allowance for nonorganic feed during conversion against the statutory requirement for organically produced feed.

### Q: Can a farm produce both organic and conventional crops?

Nothing in the act requires an entire farm to convert before any part of the operation can sell under the label. The structure governs a mixed operation piece by piece instead. The organic plan, agreed among the producer, the handler, and the certifying agent, defines how the certified portion of the operation will meet the standard. The three year land rule applies field by field, so a parcel gains eligibility through a documented history without prohibited applications during the three years before harvest, while a parcel without that history stays outside the claim. The prohibition on commingling certified and uncertified product polices the boundary between the two streams during handling and sale.

### Q: When did the USDA Organic seal first appear on labels?

The seal belongs to the labeling framework of the final rule, and the date that anchors the seal on labels in commerce is October 21, 2002, the date of full implementation of the national standard. The final rule appeared on December 21, 2000, and the legal effective date stood corrected to April 21, 2001 under the Congressional Review Act. Full implementation on October 21, 2002 marked the point by which every product sold as organic had to comply with the standard. From that compliance date, products in the two highest tiers under 7 CFR 205.301, the 100 percent tier and the tier at 95 percent or more, could display the USDA Organic seal as the federal mark of the certified claim.

### Q: What should a student remember about the Organic Foods Production Act for an exam?

A student should remember the act as a federal definition of a marketing word rather than as a food safety or nutrition law. The purposes at 7 U.S.C. 6501 join national standards, consumer assurance of a consistent standard, and interstate commerce. The production test at 7 U.S.C. 6504 joins three elements: no synthetic chemicals except as the chapter provides, three years without prohibited substances on the land before harvest, and an agreed organic plan. The National List runs in two directions, exempting synthetics against prohibition and prohibiting nonsynthetics against permission, with five year sunset review of every listing. The timeline matters for an examination answer: enactment in 1990, final rule publication in 2000, and full implementation on October 21, 2002.
