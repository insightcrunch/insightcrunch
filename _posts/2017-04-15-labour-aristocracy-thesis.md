The labour aristocracy, as a named thesis, is an explanation of a political fact that has puzzled revolutionaries for well over a century: workers in the richest countries, who on a straightforward reading of Marxist theory should have been the most revolutionary class in history, have mostly not been revolutionary at all. The thesis answers the puzzle with an economic mechanism. It holds that profits drawn from colonies and dependent economies were so large that employers and states in the imperialist countries could afford to pay a layer of their own workers more than the value of what those workers produced, and that this material privilege bought political loyalty. The explanation is elegant, it is morally charged, and its empirical standing is contested at every joint. A reader who has met the phrase in an argument, usually as a verdict rather than as a claim, deserves to know what exactly is being asserted, what evidence would settle the matter, and why the argument generates so much heat. That is the work of the pages that follow.

Precision matters here more than in most glossary entries, because the phrase does double duty. In loose usage it names any group of relatively privileged workers, and the argument ends there, in description. In its strict theoretical usage it names a causal claim with three separable parts: a source of extra profit located outside the imperialist economy, a transfer of part of that profit into the wages and conditions of a domestic stratum, and a political effect, the conversion of that stratum into a support for reformist rather than revolutionary politics. Each part can be examined separately, and the thesis can survive the failure of one part only by becoming a different thesis. Much of the confusion surrounding the term comes from sliding between the loose and strict usages mid-argument, so that evidence for the existence of privileged workers is treated as evidence for the transfer, and evidence for the transfer is treated as proof of the political effect. Keeping the three parts apart is the first discipline this article imposes, and it is the discipline on which the final assessment depends.

A second preliminary point concerns what kind of claim this is within the wider theory of [imperialism](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/). The thesis is not the theory of imperialism itself, and it does not stand or fall with that theory. A reader could accept the whole Leninist account of monopoly, finance capital, and the export of capital, and still reject the claim that colonial superprofits funded a domestic bribe. Conversely, a reader could reject the classical theory of imperialism and still find something in the observation that privileged strata of workers have often defended the order that privileges them. The thesis is a detachable component, bolted onto the theory of imperialism at a specific historical moment to solve a specific problem, the collapse of working class internationalism in August 1914. Understanding why it was bolted on, and what load it was asked to carry, is essential to judging whether it can carry that load still.

## What the labour aristocracy thesis claims

The canonical definition can be stated in one sentence. The labour aristocracy thesis holds that superprofits extracted from colonies and dependent economies allow employers and states in imperialist countries to grant a stratum of workers wages and conditions above the value of their labour power, producing reformist rather than revolutionary politics among them. Every word of that sentence is doing work. Superprofits are profits above the average rate, obtained, on the theory, because capital invested in colonies and dependent territories can pay labour far below the metropolitan level while selling into protected or monopolised markets. A stratum is not the whole class: the claim, in its classical form, concerns an upper layer, skilled, organised, and relatively secure. Above the value of their labour power is the technical heart of the assertion, because it distinguishes the bribe from an ordinary high wage. A worker whose high wage merely reflects the high productivity of the industry employing her is, on this theory, not bribed at all. She is paid what her labour power is worth in an advanced economy. The bribe begins only where payment exceeds what the domestic economy alone would sustain, with the difference made good from outside.

The mechanism, spelled out as a chain, runs as follows. Colonial and dependent territories yield returns to metropolitan capital well above what the same capital could earn at home, because land, raw materials, and above all labour are cheap there, and because political control suppresses the resistance that would raise their price. Those returns flow home as dividends, interest, and trading margins. Their existence enlarges the total surplus available to the metropolitan ruling class beyond what domestic exploitation alone would provide. Out of that enlarged surplus, concessions can be made to a section of the domestic working class without squeezing the general rate of profit to a dangerous degree: higher wages for the skilled trades, toleration of strong unions in strategic industries, social reforms funded from a buoyant revenue. The workers so favoured acquire a material stake in the arrangement's survival, and with the stake comes a politics: defence of the empire that funds the privilege, hostility to movements that threaten it, and a preference for gradual improvement within the system over the risks of overthrowing it. Reformism, on this account, is not a mistake in workers' heads. It is the rational ideology of a bribed stratum, and its leaders are, in the polemical phrase the theory made famous, labour lieutenants of the capitalist class.

### Are colonial superprofits large enough to fund a bribe?

The evidence does not support it. Profits extracted from colonies and dependent economies were small relative to the total wage bill of imperialist countries, and no study has traced a transfer of the magnitude the thesis requires. That is the principal objection to the argument.

That answer, placed early because it is the question on which everything else turns, needs the rest of the article to be more than an assertion. The claim that the transfer was too small is itself a finding of a literature, assembled by scholars who went looking for the money and reported what they found, and it will be examined in its own section below, under the name this article gives the governing test: the magnitude test. Before the objections, however, fairness requires two things. The origins of the thesis must be set out, because the phrase meant something narrower and more observational in Engels's hands than it came to mean in Lenin's. And the strongest available defence of the thesis must be stated at full strength, in the form its most serious contemporary proponent has given it, before any verdict is reached. A glossary article that refuted a weak version of its subject would be worthless. The version refuted here, insofar as it is refuted, is the strongest one on offer.

One more distinction belongs in this opening section, because examiners and debaters alike stumble over it. The thesis explains reformism by privilege, but it is not the only theory that connects the two, and it is not a theory of why the working class as a whole has been reformist. Classical Marxism expected revolutionary consciousness to arise from the experience of exploitation itself. When, in the advanced countries, large majorities of workers instead supported parties committed to managing capitalism, several explanations competed. Some pointed to the organisations workers had built, which acquired interests of their own. Some pointed to the franchise, which offered a plausible road to reform that did not pass through insurrection. Some pointed to rising living standards delivered by productivity growth inside the national economy, which required no external transfer at all. It entered this competition with a distinctive advantage and a distinctive burden. Its advantage was that it preserved the theory of imperialism as the master key: the same superprofits that explained the rivalry of the great powers also explained the quietude of their workers. Its burden was that it needed money to move, in identifiable quantities, from the periphery to a stratum in the core, and money, unlike ideas, leaves records that can be checked.

## The phrase before the theory: Engels and England

The term begins as an observation about one country at one moment. In a letter to Marx of October 1858, Engels remarked that the English proletariat was becoming increasingly bourgeois in its outlook, and that a nation holding a monopoly of the world market might be expected to develop a bourgeois aristocracy and a bourgeois proletariat side by side with its bourgeoisie proper. The remark was private, almost an aside, and it rested on a specific economic fact: for roughly the middle decades of the nineteenth century, British industry faced no serious foreign competition. British workshops supplied manufactured goods to the entire world, British ships carried them, and British finance discounted the bills. That temporary monopoly generated returns above what competitive conditions would later allow, and Engels's suggestion was that some of those returns reached the upper layer of the English working class, the skilled trades organised in the amalgamated unions, whose members enjoyed high wages, benefit funds, short hours by the standards of the day, and a social standing that set them apart from the mass of unskilled labourers.

Two features of Engels's usage deserve emphasis, because both were altered when the idea was generalised. First, the source of the privilege in his account was England's industrial monopoly, a position in the world market, rather than colonial extraction considered on its own. Colonies mattered to that monopoly, as markets and as sources of raw material, but the surplus Engels pointed to was the surplus of the workshop of the world, not a separately itemised colonial tribute. Second, his observation was resolutely specific. He was describing the England of the Great Exhibition decades, a country whose skilled engineers and cotton spinners stood at the top of a global hierarchy of production. He did not claim that every advanced country possessed such a stratum, or that the phenomenon would persist once the monopoly was broken. Indeed, the later Engels, writing prefaces in the eighteen eighties and nineties, treated the privileged position of the English unions as a wasting asset, eroded as German and American competition ate into the monopoly that had sustained it.

### What did Engels actually observe in the English unions?

He observed that the organised skilled trades of mid nineteenth century England, the engineers, carpenters, and cotton operatives of the amalgamated societies, enjoyed wages, security, and respectability far above the unskilled mass, and that their politics were correspondingly moderate, defensive of their craft position, and largely indifferent to revolutionary aims.

The historical literature on the English case is rich, and it complicates Engels's aside in instructive ways. Historians of the Victorian labour movement have documented the world of the skilled trades in detail: the apprenticeship controls that restricted entry, the tramping systems and friendly benefits that made the unions resemble insurance societies, the elaborate rules about overtime and piece rates, the pride in craft that could shade into contempt for the labourers below. Eric Hobsbawm, in essays that did much to define the scholarly debate, treated the labour aristocracy as a real stratum with a distinctive culture and politics in the middle decades of the century, while questioning how sharply it could be separated from the rest of the class and how long its distinctiveness lasted. Other historians pushed the scepticism further, noting that skilled wages reflected genuine scarcity and productivity, that the boundary between skilled and unskilled was porous, and that supposedly conservative craftsmen could turn militant with startling speed when their position was threatened. The point for the present purpose is that even in its home case, the descriptive part of the thesis, the existence of a privileged layer, is easier to establish than the causal part, the claim that the privilege was paid for out of a surplus extracted abroad rather than generated by the productivity and bargaining strength of the trades themselves.

There is also the question of what Engels thought the phenomenon meant politically, and here his writings are more ambivalent than later usage suggests. He criticised the narrowness of the great unions, their exclusion of the unskilled, their indifference to the Irish and to the unorganised, and he connected that narrowness to the material comfort the monopoly had bought. But he also treated the stratum as a product of a passing phase. Once rival industrial powers arose, the English worker's cushion would thin, competition would do what exhortation could not, and the working class would be compelled toward a more general politics. That expectation matters because it shows the original form of the idea carrying its own expiry date. The aristocracy of labour, for Engels, was an episode produced by a temporary configuration of the world market. It took a later writer, confronting a catastrophe Engels did not live to see, to convert the episode into a structural feature of an entire epoch.

Where classical sources of this period discuss the peoples of the colonised world, their vocabulary belongs to their century and is not reproduced in these pages. The characterisation matters more than the wording: the economic writings of the period routinely treated colonised populations as objects of policy rather than as political subjects, and that habit of mind is part of the record the thesis itself must answer for, since a theory about the proceeds of empire was developed by men whose movement often shared the empire's assumptions about who counted as an agent. The observation is not decorative. It will return when the political conclusion of the thesis is examined, because a theory that divides the world's workers into the bribed and the betrayed inherits, whether it wants to or not, some of the categories of the age that produced it.

## Lenin's generalisation: from an English episode to a law of the epoch

The transformation of Engels's remark into a general theory was Lenin's work, and it was driven by an event. In August 1914 the socialist parties of the Second International, pledged for decades to refuse support for an imperialist war, voted in their several parliaments for war credits and rallied to their own governments. For Lenin, writing in exile, this was not merely a betrayal by leaders. Betrayals on that scale required a material explanation: entire strata of the working class movement had to have acquired an interest in the imperialist order, or their leaders could not have carried them so easily into its camp. The theory of imperialism he was constructing in the same years supplied the mechanism. In [the classical statement of that theory](/2014/04/15/lenin-imperialism-readers-guide/), monopoly capitalism, having divided the world among the great powers, extracted superprofits from the colonies and dependent countries on a scale Engels's England could not have matched. Out of such superprofits, Lenin argued in the prefaces he added to his pamphlet after the war, it was plainly possible to bribe an upper stratum of the working class, and the capitalists of the advanced countries were doing so in a thousand ways, direct and indirect.

The generalisation changed the thesis in three respects, and the changes should be registered precisely. The source of the bribe shifted from a single country's temporary industrial monopoly to the systematic superprofits of imperialism as a stage of capitalism, available in principle to every imperialist power. The stratum widened in function if not always in definition: alongside the skilled workers stood the officials of the unions and parties, a bureaucracy whose salaries and careers depended on the reformist machinery, and whom Lenin treated as the aristocracy's political general staff. And the political conclusion hardened. For Engels, the aristocracy's conservatism was a phase that competition would dissolve. For Lenin, writing after 1914, opportunism had a durable economic base in the epoch itself, the split in socialism was correspondingly deep, and no reunification with the bribed parties was possible or desirable. The thesis now carried the weight of a strategy: it explained why the revolutionary wing had to break organisationally from the reformist wing, and why that break was not sectarianism but a recognition of a material division inside the class.

### How did the outbreak of war change what the thesis had to explain?

Before the war, the privileged position of skilled workers was a curiosity of the English case, an exception to be noted. After the socialist parties voted for war credits, the phenomenon demanding explanation was the collapse of internationalism itself, and Lenin's version of the thesis was built to explain that collapse by giving reformism an economic foundation in imperialist superprofits.

The intellectual honesty of the construction deserves a word, because it is often missed by both defenders and detractors. Lenin was not inventing a insult to throw at opponents. He was applying, to an unwelcome fact, the method he applied everywhere: ask what material interest sustains a political position. If the leaders of mass parties had led their members into support for an imperialist war, and the members had largely followed, then something in the members' situation, and not only in the leaders' characters, had to account for it. His answer was the generalised thesis, and it had the virtue, rare in explanations of political defeat, of being falsifiable in principle. It asserted that identifiable quantities of value moved from the colonial world to the metropolitan economies, that a portion of that value reached a definable stratum of workers in the form of wages and conditions above what domestic production would have supported, and that the politics of that stratum tracked its privilege. Each assertion invites investigation. The investigations were eventually undertaken, many of them by Marxists who began as sympathisers, and the following sections report what they found. But the order of presentation set by the governing brief must be honoured first: the defence, in its strongest contemporary form, comes before the objections.

One further element of Lenin's version must be isolated, because it is the element most often quietly dropped by later users of the phrase. Lenin insisted on the smallness of the stratum. The aristocracy was an upper layer, in the imperialist countries a minority of the working class, and its importance lay not in its numbers but in its position: it staffed the unions, edited the newspapers, and supplied the parliamentary parties with their cadres. The mass of workers, on his account, remained exploited and potentially revolutionary, misled by a leadership whose material base he had identified. Later versions of the thesis, including the contemporary defence examined next, expand the stratum dramatically, in the boldest case to encompass the working class of the rich countries nearly entire. That expansion is not a minor variation. A thesis about a bribed minority staffing a movement's apparatus and a thesis about a bribed majority constituting the movement's social base are different theories with different evidence requirements and opposite strategic implications, and they will be kept apart in what follows.

## The defence at full strength: the case as its contemporary proponent makes it

The most substantial contemporary defence of the thesis is the work of Zak Cope, set out in his book Divided World Divided Class, published in 2012, which reconstructs the argument from its nineteenth century origins and restates it for a globalised economy. The defence must be given as its author makes it, at full strength, because the brief governing this article requires it and because intellectual fairness does. Cope's case has four pillars, and each deserves to be stated in the form that makes it hardest to answer.

The first pillar is the sheer scale of global wage differentials. Workers performing comparable tasks, sometimes within the same transnational firm and the same production chain, receive wages that differ not by percentages but by multiples, and the multiple tracks citizenship and location rather than skill or effort. On Cope's reading, a differential of that size, maintained across the whole structure of the world economy, cannot be explained by productivity differences alone, because productivity is itself partly a function of the capital, infrastructure, and market access that the wealthy economies monopolise. The wage hierarchy of the world is, in his phrase, a stratification of labour under capitalism, and it corresponds to a division of the world's workers into a protected minority and an exposed majority as stark as anything Engels described within England.

The second pillar is the mechanism of transfer in its modern form. Where Lenin pointed to colonial superprofits in the age of formal empire, Cope points to unequal exchange and to the operation of global production chains: goods consumed in the wealthy countries embody more labour time than the labour time their consumers expend to purchase them, because the wages of the workers who made them are held far below the wages of the workers who buy them. The difference, on this account, is a flow of value from low wage to high wage economies, and it subsidises the living standards of the entire consuming population of the rich countries, workers included. The subsidy does not arrive as a wage packet marked colonial tribute. It arrives as cheap goods, as the fiscal room that lets states fund welfare without confiscatory taxation of capital, and as the profitability that lets employers concede wage increases without existential resistance. The bribe, in other words, has been generalised and rendered invisible by being dissolved into the price structure.

### What is the strongest form of the claim that the whole northern working class benefits?

It is the claim that cheap imports, buoyant state revenues, and high domestic profitability all rest partly on value transferred from low wage economies, so that even an unorganised, low paid worker in a rich country consumes and is protected at a level the transfer helps to fund, sharing in the proceeds of the global hierarchy.

The third pillar is political, and it is the one Cope presses hardest. The labour movements of the wealthy countries, across more than a century, have in their majority accommodated themselves to the global hierarchy rather than challenging it. Their parties supported colonial wars or administered colonial systems; their unions organised nationally and bargained within national frames; their response to migration from the poorer world has frequently been restrictionist; and no mass movement in any rich country has made the equalisation of global wages, or the dismantling of the mechanisms that hold southern wages down, a central demand. A theory of working class politics, Cope argues, has to explain that record, and the explanation that workers in the rich countries are simply deceived, generation after generation, by leaders and newspapers, attributes to deception a durability that deception rarely possesses. Material interest explains it more economically: the arrangement delivers real benefits, and movements defend arrangements that deliver real benefits to their members.

The fourth pillar turns the critics' favourite evidence around. It is often said against the thesis that the best paid workers have frequently been the most militant, the miners and engineers and transport workers who led the great strikes of the twentieth century. Cope's reply is that militancy in defence of a privileged position is not merely compatible with the thesis but predicted by it. A stratum defending its differential, its craft controls, its national wage floor against dilution, will fight hard, and its fighting will look, from the inside, like class struggle. What the thesis predicts is not passivity. It predicts the direction of the struggle: sectional, national, and defensive of the hierarchy that sustains the privilege, rather than internationalist and directed at the hierarchy itself. Judged by that standard, the defence concludes, the record of the northern labour movements is not an embarrassment for the thesis but its confirmation.

Set out in this form, the defence is a serious piece of work, and it changes the character of the dispute. The question is no longer whether a handful of Victorian craftsmen were comfortable. The question is whether the living standards and the political behaviour of the working classes of the wealthy countries are materially underwritten by the low wages of the rest of the world, and whether that underwriting explains why those working classes have not acted as the classical theory of revolution expected. Any answer worthy of the question must engage the defence at this strength. The sections that follow therefore do not waste time on the weak version. They apply, to the strong version and to the classical version alike, the three empirical tests around which the critical literature has organised itself: magnitude, identity, and comparison. But a preliminary clarification is owed to the reader, concerning what the defence has quietly done to the thesis in the course of strengthening it. Cope's stratum is not Lenin's. Where Lenin identified an upper layer within a working class whose majority remained, in his view, exploited and available for revolution, the contemporary defence extends the benefit, in some degree, to nearly the whole working population of the rich countries. That extension rescues the thesis from one objection, the smallness of the classical aristocracy relative to the reformism it was asked to explain, only by exposing it to another, which the verdict will weigh: a benefit shared by nearly everyone in the core explains the politics of the core only in the way that gravity explains a particular fall, as a background condition rather than as the mechanism that picked out this stratum, this party, this moment for reformist victory.

## The magnitude test

This article's namable claim can now be stated with the precision the subject demands. The magnitude test holds that the thesis stands or falls on whether the transfer from periphery to core was large enough to fund a material bribe to a significant stratum, and that every serious attempt to trace that transfer has found it too small, which means the political conclusion rests on an economic claim that has not been substantiated. The test is not a rhetorical device. It follows from the structure of the thesis itself. A bribe is a payment, and payments have sizes. If the total flow of colonial profit into an imperialist economy amounted to a small fraction of that economy's wage bill, then it could not, as a matter of arithmetic rather than interpretation, have raised the wages of a substantial stratum above the value of their labour power by an amount sufficient to purchase their politics. The money has to be somewhere. Either it can be found, in quantities commensurate with the effect attributed to it, or the mechanism fails and some other explanation of reformism must do the work.

Stating the test this way clarifies what would count as success for the thesis, and that clarification is itself a service, because much writing on the subject proceeds as though any evidence of colonial exploitation, however small in aggregate, confirmed the bribery claim. It does not. Colonial exploitation can be established beyond argument, in episode after episode of conquest, forced labour, and confiscation, while the magnitude test still fails, because the test concerns the ratio between the extracted surplus and the metropolitan wage bill it supposedly supplemented. A fortune can be enormous to the family that receives it and trivial to the economy that contains it. The dividends of colonial companies enriched identifiable investors, funded country houses and endowments, and underwrote the fortunes of particular firms and ports. The question the test asks is colder: spread across the whole wage bill of an industrial economy, and concentrated, as the thesis requires, on one stratum within it, could those flows have purchased the difference between the wages that stratum received and the wages its own economy would have paid? The critical literature's answer, arrived at by scholars who examined the surviving accounts and trade statistics with exactly this question in mind, is that the flows were an order of magnitude too small.

### Why does the argument turn on a ratio rather than on the existence of colonial profit?

Because the thesis is a claim about wages, and wages are paid from the whole surplus an economy generates. Colonial profit existed and enriched its recipients; the test asks whether it was large relative to the total wage bill of the imperialist countries, since only a flow large in that comparison could lift a stratum's pay above domestic levels.

The contested figures protocol governing this cluster of articles must be honoured here, and it shapes the way the test is argued. No wage differential figure, no colonial revenue figure, and no transfer estimate is stated as fact anywhere in these pages. That abstinence is not evasion. The figures in this literature are genuinely contested, assembled from colonial accounts drawn up for purposes other than answering this question, and a glossary article that brandished a precise percentage would be laundering an estimate into a datum. The magnitude objection is therefore argued in the only form that is both honest and sufficient: as a finding of the literature about the direction and rough scale of the flows, and as an observation about the absence of the traced transfer. In study after study, scholars who set out to follow the money from colony to metropolis to wage packet have reported the same structural difficulty. The money is easy to find at the point of extraction and at the point of private enrichment. It disappears as a distinct stream precisely where the thesis needs it, in the wage settlements of the metropolitan industries, which track domestic productivity, domestic labour scarcity, and domestic bargaining strength with embarrassing fidelity, and which do not move as the colonial flows move. The magnitude objection, in its strongest form, is not a rival number. It is the absence of a traced transfer where the thesis requires one to exist.

That formulation also disciplines the reply available to defenders, and the discipline is useful to both sides. A defender who answers the magnitude test must do more than demonstrate that exploitation occurred, or that global wages differ enormously, or that the beneficiaries of empire included some workers. The defender must identify a channel and a scale: this much value, arriving by this route, raised the pay of this stratum by roughly this margin above the domestic counterfactual. Cope's defence, stated above, can be read as an attempt to meet exactly that demand by relocating the channel from the wage packet to the price structure and the state budget. Whether relocation is a legitimate development of the thesis or an abandonment of it is a question the assessment will take up directly. What can be said at this stage is that the classical thesis, the one Engels sketched and Lenin generalised, asserts a wage bribe, and it is the wage bribe that the magnitude test examines and finds unsubstantiated.

## The first objection: the money is not there in the quantities required

The critical literature on the thesis was assembled most fully in Marxist work of the two decades either side of the turn of the century, and its most systematic statement is associated with Charles Post, an American Marxist historian whose essays on the subject, beginning with a two part examination in the journal Against the Current in 2009 and continuing through an extended reply to Cope in Research in Political Economy in 2014, gathered the objections into a single case. Post's standing in the dispute is worth noting: he writes from inside the tradition the thesis belongs to, accepts much of the classical account of imperialism, and directs his fire not at the observation that privileged workers exist but at the mechanism said to privilege them. His work is used throughout these objection sections as the fullest assembly of the case, supplemented by the wider scholarship he engages.

The magnitude argument, as that literature states it, begins from the composition of metropolitan income. The wage bill of an advanced industrial economy is enormous, because it comprises the earnings of tens of millions of workers across every trade. Against that total, the profits remitted from colonies and dependent territories, even at the height of formal empire, constituted a modest stream, and the stream was not directed at wages. Colonial profits accrued to the firms and investors who owned the colonial assets. For those profits to become a wage bribe, they had to pass through further hands: taxed by the state and spent on the social wage, or conceded at the bargaining table by employers whose margins the profits had fattened. Each passage is a place where the alleged transfer can be looked for, and the literature reports that at each passage the sums attributable to the colonial source shrink toward insignificance beside the sums generated domestically. The fiscal capacity of the imperial states, the resource that funded their welfare settlements, rested overwhelmingly on domestic production and domestic taxation. The margins of the great employers rested overwhelmingly on the productivity of their home plants and the exploitation of their home workforces. Strip the colonial stream away in imagination, and the wage structure of the metropolis, on this evidence, stands essentially unchanged. A bribe that can be removed without altering the payment was never the payment's source.

### What would the defenders of the thesis have to show in order to answer the magnitude objection?

They would have to trace a flow of value from the colonial economies into the wage settlements of a definable metropolitan stratum, large enough to lift that stratum's pay above what its own economy would have provided, arriving by an identifiable channel rather than inferred from the coexistence of empire and high wages.

There is a corollary that sharpens the objection, concerning who within the metropolitan economy actually received the colonial surplus. The literature on the distribution of imperial gains, including work by historians with no polemical interest in the labour aristocracy question, consistently finds the gains concentrated: in the City and its investing classes, in particular ports and trading houses, in the officer corps and colonial service, in the shareholders of a limited set of companies. The social layer that empire visibly enriched was the layer that owned it. For the thesis, this concentration is an embarrassment of a specific kind. It does not merely fail to find the bribe in the workers' wages. It finds the surplus in the hands of the class the workers were supposedly bribed to support, which is where a simpler theory, in which capital keeps what it extracts and pays wages according to domestic conditions, would predict it to be. The thesis needs the ruling class to have shared its colonial winnings with a section of its own workforce on a scale large enough to redirect that section's politics. Ruling classes do share winnings, under pressure, at bargaining tables, and the history of concession is real. But the sharing documented in the record follows the pressure, rising where workers were organised and dangerous and falling where they were weak, in a pattern that points to domestic struggle as the cause of high wages and to colonial profit as, at most, background music.

A defender may respond, and Cope's book in effect does respond, that the accounting frame is too narrow: that the transfer works through the cheapening of the goods workers consume, so that a wage of a given size buys more because the periphery's labour is cheap, and the bribe is real even though no wage settlement records it. The response will be examined in full when the defence is assessed. For the purposes of the magnitude objection, one observation suffices. A subsidy delivered through prices is delivered to everyone who buys the goods, capitalist and worker, pensioner and child, in proportion to consumption rather than in proportion to stratum. It therefore cannot, by itself, explain the specific phenomenon the classical thesis was built to explain, which is the political differentiation within the working class, the conservatism of its upper layer against the radicalism expected of its mass. The more the mechanism is generalised to escape the magnitude test, the less it explains the particular thing it was invented to explain. That tension, between escaping the arithmetic and keeping the explanation, runs through the whole subsequent history of the thesis, and the remaining objections tighten it further.

## The second objection: the wrong workers were militant

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the transfer could be found. The thesis would still have to survive a test of identity: the workers who received the privilege should be the workers whose politics the privilege allegedly purchased. The prediction is clear. The best paid, most skilled, most secure stratum should be the least revolutionary, bought off and loyal, while radicalism should concentrate among the worst paid and least secure, who received no share of the bribe. The historical record, as the critical literature assembles it, points persistently the other way. Again and again, the vanguard of militancy in the advanced countries was drawn from precisely the trades the thesis casts as the aristocracy, while the most quiescent sections of the class were often those at its impoverished margins.

The exhibits are familiar to any student of labour history, and they are assembled here as the critical literature assembles them, as a pattern rather than as anecdotes. The miners, among the best paid manual workers in Britain for much of the twentieth century and possessed of the strongest unions, supplied the labour movement with its most formidable industrial battalions and its most sustained challenges to the state. The engineers, Engels's own archetype of the privileged craft, built organisations that employers and governments treated as a standing danger. The transport workers and dockers, whose strategic position gave them earnings and security above the unskilled mass, were repeatedly at the centre of general confrontations. Across the Atlantic, the industrial unions of steel and auto, whose members stood at the summit of the manual wage hierarchy by the middle of the twentieth century, were built in struggles of extraordinary violence and radicalism. Meanwhile the most exploited layers, the casual labourers, the sweated homeworkers, the unemployed, were more often politically inert, not because they were bribed but because exhaustion, insecurity, and disorganisation are enemies of collective action. Militancy, in short, tracks organisation and strategic leverage far better than it tracks deprivation, and it tracks privilege, where the two coincide, in the direction opposite to the one the thesis predicts.

Defenders of the thesis have a reply, and it was given its sharpest form in the defence stated earlier: the militancy of the privileged was sectional militancy, directed at defending and extending the differential, and a miners' strike for the miners' wage is no more a refutation of the bribe theory than a cartel's aggression refutes its privilege. The reply deserves its full weight, and the assessment below returns to it. But the critical literature has a counter that should be recorded here, because it concerns the evidence rather than the interpretation. The privileged trades did not confine their action to their own differentials. They built the general unions of the unskilled in the great organising waves, they funded and led the political parties of the whole class, they struck in sympathy and in general confrontations whose demands were not sectional at all, and in the revolutionary crises of the century's first decades, skilled workers in the imperialist countries repeatedly appear in the front ranks of movements that threatened the entire order, empire included. The pattern is too broad to be dissolved into sectionalism without remainder. At a minimum, the identity of the bribed stratum, which the theory requires to be identifiable by its politics, dissolves on inspection: the same trades are conservative in one decade and insurrectionary in another, defensive of privilege in one dispute and internationalist in the next, and a variable that predicts everything predicts nothing.

### Does the militancy of skilled workers refute the thesis on its own?

Not on its own, because a defender can reinterpret sectional militancy as defence of privilege, and some of the record supports that reading. The evidence accomplishes something narrower and still damaging: it removes the clean correlation between high pay and political loyalty the thesis needs, forcing the defence into interpretations the records cannot always carry.

The timing objection, named in the brief for this article and treated here as a companion to the identity objection, attacks the thesis from the chronological side. Reformism, as an organised politics within the working class, does not wait for the age of high imperialism. Revisionist currents, trade union legalism, and parliamentary gradualism are all visible in the European movements well before the scramble for colonies reached its height, in some cases before the colonial empires whose profits allegedly funded them had been assembled. Conversely, the great reformist settlements of the twentieth century, the welfare states and collective bargaining regimes of the decades after 1945, were constructed during the very period when the formal empires were being dismantled. If colonial superprofits purchased reformism, the purchase should falter as the colonies depart. Instead the most thoroughgoing reformism in the history of the metropolitan working classes coincides with decolonisation, funded, as the fiscal record shows, out of the rapid productivity growth of the metropolitan economies themselves. Chronology is a blunt instrument, and no single coincidence refutes a mechanism. But a mechanism whose effect grows as its alleged cause shrinks has, at the least, a great deal of explaining to do, and the explaining, in the literature, is generally done by quietly substituting domestic causes for the colonial one, which is the substitution the next sections make explicit.

## The third objection: countries without empires behaved the same way

The comparative test is the quietest of the three objections and, to many readers, the most persuasive. If reformism in the metropolitan working class was purchased out of colonial superprofits, then countries with negligible colonial holdings should show a different political pattern: weaker reformism, stronger revolutionary movements, a working class with nothing to lose but its chains and correspondingly readier to lose them. The European record refuses to cooperate. The Scandinavian countries, whose colonial possessions were trivial or nonexistent, developed by the middle of the twentieth century the most complete reformist labour movements in the world, social democratic parties governing for decades, unions integrated into national bargaining, and working classes whose living standards stood at the global summit. Switzerland, with no colonies at all, built a labour movement of exemplary moderation. Germany, whose colonial empire was acquired late, held briefly, and lost entirely in 1918, generated both the largest reformist party of the pre 1914 world and, after the empire's loss, a welfare settlement of the first rank. The correlation the thesis requires, between the size of a country's colonial extraction and the reformism of its workers, is not merely weak. Across the European cases it is close to absent, and in the Scandinavian cases it inverts.

Defenders have two responses available, and both should be stated. The first is that the relevant unit is not the individual country but the imperialist world system: a small country without colonies of its own still traded within, borrowed from, and consumed the products of a world economy structured by the great empires, and so shared indirectly in the general transfer. The second is that late developers such as Germany held their workers' reformism in place by other rents, from cartels, tariffs, and a protected home market, which performed the same function as colonial superprofits. Both responses have a common structure, and its cost should be noticed. Each widens the mechanism until it denotes any surplus, from any source, that gives employers and states room to concede. But a thesis that began by identifying a specific flow, colonial superprofit, as the funder of a specific stratum's privilege, and that now rests content with surplus in general, has conceded the comparative objection in the act of answering it. Surplus in general is available in every growing capitalist economy, including economies with no periphery of their own, and an explanation of reformism that requires only surplus in general is the domestic productivity explanation wearing imperial dress. It will be met again, in its honest form, among the alternatives.

### What does the Scandinavian case show, and what does it not show?

It shows that the fullest reformism and the highest working class living standards in Europe arose where colonial extraction was negligible, breaking the correlation between empire and reformism the thesis predicts. It does not show those economies stood outside the world market, which is why defenders retreat to the world system as the unit of analysis.

The three objections converge on a single point, and the convergence is the article's analytical spine. Magnitude says the money was too small. Identity says the recipients' politics were wrong for the theory. Comparison says the effect appears where the cause is absent. Any one objection might be parried by adjusting the thesis: narrowing the stratum, relocating the channel, widening the unit. The adjustments, however, cannot all be made at once without emptying the claim, because each adjustment surrenders the specificity that made the thesis an explanation rather than a mood. What survives the three objections intact is the observation with which Engels began, that strata of workers can be privileged and that privilege shapes politics. What does not survive is the mechanism that was supposed to distinguish this theory from every other account of privilege, the traced transfer of colonial superprofit into the wages of the stratum. The artifact that follows sets the components side by side so that the reader can see the assessment whole before the alternatives are developed.

The table below is the article's findable artifact, and it is designed to be used rather than merely read. A student revising from this page, through a study tool such as the VaultBook study notes for this series, should be able to reconstruct the entire argument from the five rows, because the rows are the argument, disassembled.

Reading the table vertically teaches the method as well as the result. Notice that no row is graded on the moral character of empire, which is not in dispute in these pages, and no row is graded on whether privilege exists, which nobody denies. Every row is graded on the connection the thesis asserts between a specific economic flow and a specific political effect. That is what it means to assess a theory rather than to cheer or boo it. Notice also that the verdicts are not uniform in kind. The mechanism row records an absence, the failure to trace the transfer. The magnitude row records a disproportion, between the flow and the effect. The identity row records a contradiction, evidence running against the prediction. The comparison row records a missing correlation. The conclusion row records a non sequitur, a political inference that depended on premises the earlier rows withdrew. A theory can survive one kind of failure by repair. The thesis, as the following sections argue, would have to be repaired at every row simultaneously, and the repaired theory, at each row, turns out to be one of the alternatives.

## The alternatives: reformism explained without a transfer

The strongest reason for doubting the thesis is not any single objection. It is that reformism can be explained, in its timing, its geography, and its social distribution, by causes that are documented in the places where the thesis's cause is not. Four such explanations dominate the literature, and they are complementary rather than rival: each accounts for a feature of the pattern that the others leave loose. They are set out here at the length their explanatory power earns, because a reader who possesses the alternatives possesses the real means of assessing the thesis, which is to ask, of any fact offered in its support, whether the alternatives explain that fact as well or better.

The first alternative is institutional integration. Trade unions, once they win legal recognition, acquire assets to protect: funds, buildings, full time officials, bargaining relationships, and a legal status that a revolutionary adventure would put at risk. Their officials acquire careers. Their procedures acquire the force of habit and then of virtue. None of this requires corruption, and none of it requires a bribe from any external source. It requires only that organisations, like organisms, develop an interest in their own survival, and that survival inside a functioning capitalist state counsels negotiation over insurrection in almost every concrete instance. The explanation has a distinguished pedigree inside Marxism itself. Robert Michels, studying the German Social Democratic Party before the First World War, formulated what he called the iron law of oligarchy from exactly this observation, and the generations of scholarship since have refined rather than refuted him. Institutional integration explains the conservatism of union leaderships directly, without any detour through colonial accounts, and it explains why that conservatism appears in countries with no colonies to fund it. It also explains a fact the thesis handles awkwardly, that the officials Lenin identified as the aristocracy's general staff were often paid modestly and lived austerely. Their privilege was positional, a matter of office and security within the movement, not a share of imperial rent.

The second alternative is the franchise and the electoral route. As the vote was extended to working class men, and later to all adults, a plausible mechanism for improving workers' lives came into existence that had not existed for Engels's generation: elect a government, legislate, and administer. Every success of that route, a factory act, a pension, a health service, strengthened the belief that the route was the practical one, and every failure could be attributed to insufficient votes rather than to the route itself. Electoral politics also reshaped the organisations that practised it, rewarding breadth of appeal over depth of conviction and punishing, at the ballot box, any party that frightened the marginal voter. A theory of reformism that starts here needs no transfer from the periphery. It needs only the observation that rational people use the levers that move, and that for most workers in most decades the electoral lever visibly moved things the insurrectionary lever had never touched.

### Why does the electoral explanation not need any premise about empire?

Because its mechanism is internal to each country's politics: once a mass franchise exists and workers' parties can win office or force concessions by threatening to, gradual reform becomes a rational strategy for voters and leaders alike, its successes reproducing the strategy. It therefore predicts reformism wherever the franchise arrives, with or without colonies.

The third alternative is the welfare settlement. From Bismarck's social insurance schemes of the eighteen eighties, designed explicitly to detach workers from socialism, through the comprehensive welfare states constructed after 1945, states learned to purchase social peace directly, with pensions, unemployment insurance, public housing, and health provision funded from general taxation. The purchase is real, and its political effect is the one the thesis attributes to the bribe: populations with much to lose are cautious about losing it. But the funding source is documented and domestic, drawn from the taxation of a rapidly growing home economy, and the timing fits the thesis badly, since the greatest extensions of welfare coincided with the dissolution of the colonial empires rather than with their zenith. The welfare explanation also handles the distribution of quiescence better than the bribe does. The groups most thoroughly pacified by the postwar settlements were not a skilled stratum but the broad mass of organised and unorganised workers alike, which is what a universal welfare state would predict and what a stratum bribe would not.

The fourth alternative is the most fundamental, because it denies the premise on which the magnitude test's counterfactual rests. The thesis assumes that wages above a bare subsistence, rising living standards, and material concessions to workers require an external subsidy, since the domestic economy, left to itself, would hold wages at the value of labour power while the value of labour power itself stagnated. The productivity explanation replies that a growing capitalist economy raises the value that workers can claim and the surplus from which claims can be met simultaneously, because output per worker grows. Real wages across the advanced economies rose, over the long run, roughly in step with productivity, financed by the growth of the product itself. No colonial input is required to explain why a steelworker in a country without colonies could live better than his grandfather. He lived better because the economy he worked in produced vastly more per hour, and because his unions and parties organised to capture a share of the increase. This explanation absorbs, without remainder, the phenomenon the thesis was invented to explain, the rising living standards of metropolitan workers, and it does so with a mechanism whose magnitudes can be checked in every national statistical record, where they are found, unlike the colonial transfer, to be amply sufficient for the effect.

Taken together, the four alternatives explain the geography of reformism, since union institutions, the franchise, welfare provision, and growth in productivity were present in the countries without empires as surely as in those with them. They explain the timing, since each alternative cause strengthens across exactly the period when reformism consolidated. They explain the distribution, since institutional office, electoral incentives, universal welfare, and productivity linked wages bear on the class broadly and on its officials particularly, in the pattern the record shows. And they explain the militancy of the privileged, since a worker whose high wage is the product of her own organisation's strength has every reason to defend that organisation militantly and no purchased loyalty to restrain her. An explanation set that accounts for the evidence at every point where its rival fails is not guaranteed to be true. But the burden of proof has moved, and it now rests on the defender of the transfer to produce the transfer.

## What separates a bribed stratum from a merely prosperous one

The glossary function of this article obliges it to leave the reader with a usable test for the term's application, since the phrase will go on being used in arguments the reader must judge. Three marks distinguish a bribed stratum, in the strict sense the thesis requires, from a merely prosperous section of workers. First, the source mark: the stratum's advantage must originate outside the economy that employs it, in a flow that the domestic product cannot account for. A prosperous stratum whose prosperity is explained by the productivity of its industry and the scarcity of its skills fails this mark, however comfortable it is. Second, the counterfactual mark: remove the external flow, and the advantage should collapse toward the domestic level. Prosperity that would survive the removal of empire, as the prosperity of Scandinavian workers survived the loss of empires they never had, is not a bribe in the thesis's sense. Third, the political mark: the stratum's politics must be traceable to the advantage, expressed in defence of the arrangement that funds it, above all in defence of the empire against those it exploits. Comfort that expresses itself in internationalist militancy, or in indifference to empire, fails the third mark whatever its source.

### Why insist on all three marks rather than any one of them?

Because each mark alone fits cases nobody would call a labour aristocracy: wealthy workers exist without external funding, externally funded enclaves exist without political loyalty, and imperialist enthusiasm exists among workers with no privilege at all. Only the conjunction, external source, dependence on it, and politics explained by it, states the thesis fairly.

Applying the three marks to the historical record is a sobering exercise for the thesis's users. The Victorian skilled trades, the founding case, pass the political mark partially, in their frequent indifference to colonial questions and their suspicion of the unskilled, and they fail the first two marks on the evidence available, since their wages tracked the productivity and scarcity that the workshop of the world conferred and persisted, transformed but recognisable, as the monopoly waned. The union bureaucracies of Lenin's account pass the political mark almost by definition and fail the source mark, their salaries being paid from members' dues. The northern working classes of the contemporary defence pass none of the marks cleanly: their advantage is documented but its external source is the very point in dispute, its dependence on that source is untested by any removal, and their politics, restrictionist in some decades and solidarity minded in others, tracks domestic institutions and domestic insecurities more faithfully than it tracks any transfer. The marks do not settle the argument by definition, because a defender can contest the evidence at each. They do settle what the argument is about, and a reader equipped with them will notice how much polemical use of the phrase proceeds by exhibiting one mark, usually comfort, and collecting assent for all three.

## Misreadings to correct

Five confusions recur wherever the thesis is discussed, and a precision article earns its keep by clearing them. The first is attribution. The thesis is not Marx's. Marx died in 1883, before the age of high imperialism the theory describes, and while his writings contain analyses of colonial plunder and of the English working class that later writers drew on, the labour aristocracy as a mechanism is Engels's observation generalised by Lenin. Attributing it to Marx lends it an authority its actual authors' more tentative formulations do not claim, Engels's being an aside in a letter and Lenin's a wartime construction he revised as he wrote.

The second confusion concerns scope. The classical thesis claims that a stratum was bribed, not that the working class of the rich countries as a whole was bribed, and the difference is not pedantry. Lenin's politics depended on the distinction: the majority remained, for him, exploited and winnable, and the revolutionary party's task was to break the aristocracy's hold over them. Users of the phrase who extend the bribe to entire national working classes are not applying Lenin's thesis but replacing it, usually without noticing that the replacement carries the opposite strategy, since a class bought entire is a class written off. The contemporary defence examined in this article makes the extension deliberately and accepts the consequence. Casual users make it inadvertently and accept nothing, which is worse.

The third confusion treats the thesis as established because its vocabulary is old and its conclusion is often repeated. Longevity is not evidence. The thesis has been disputed from inside Marxism for as long as it has existed, by writers who accepted its premises about imperialism and rejected its mechanism on the record, and the three objections set out above are not debating points invented for this article but the settled findings of that internal dispute. A reader who encounters the phrase deployed as though it named a fact of nature is entitled to ask which of the objections the speaker has answered, and to treat silence on all three as the answer.

### Does rejecting the thesis mean denying that wages differ enormously across the world?

It does not. Global wage differentials are a documented fact of the world economy, among its defining injustices. The thesis is not the fact but one explanation of a political consequence, and rejecting the explanation leaves the fact standing in full, demanding other explanations and remedies. Confusing fact with thesis protects the thesis from evidence.

The fourth confusion runs in the opposite direction and matters equally. Rejecting the thesis does not commit anyone to the view that workers in wealthy countries gain nothing from the global order, or that empire was a sideshow, or that the living standards of the metropolis were innocent achievements. The alternatives set out in this article are compatible with a severe account of colonial violence and with the observation that the world economy is hierarchically ordered to the advantage of its wealthy core. What they deny is a specific proposition: that a traced flow of colonial superprofit purchased the politics of a metropolitan stratum. A reader can hold that empire enriched the core, that its violence was constitutive of the modern world, and that northern workers consume goods made cheap by southern misery, and still judge, on the evidence, that the bribe mechanism fails its tests. Precision about which claim is being denied is the whole difference between assessment and atmosphere.

The fifth confusion concerns a neighbouring theory with which the thesis is constantly amalgamated, and it is important enough to take a section of its own.

## Unequal exchange is a different claim

The theory of unequal exchange, developed by Arghiri Emmanuel and given wide currency in the dependency literature, holds that when goods trade between economies with very different wage levels, value is transferred from the low wage economy to the high wage economy through the prices formed on the world market, even where the exchange is formally free and equal. Readers who want the theory itself should go to the article devoted to [dependency theory and its account of the periphery](/2016/07/01/dependency-theory-explained/), where it belongs. Here the point is strictly the relation between that theory and the labour aristocracy thesis, because the two are routinely treated as premises of each other, and they are not.

Unequal exchange is a claim about value transfer between economies. It is a claim about wages and politics within one economy. Neither entails the other. Value could be transferred from periphery to core on a large scale and accrue entirely to the core's capitalists, raising their profits without touching the wage relation, in which case unequal exchange would be true and the labour aristocracy thesis false. Equally, a metropolitan stratum could in principle be privileged out of domestic surplus while world trade transferred nothing, in which case the descriptive half of the thesis would hold and unequal exchange would fail. The contemporary defence of the thesis leans heavily on unequal exchange, treating the transfer between economies as though it settled the question of wages within them, and that slide is the precise point at which the assessment must press. What the defender needs is not transfer in the abstract but transfer into the wage fund, in amounts sufficient to lift a stratum above the value of its labour power, with the political consequences following. Emmanuel's mechanism, even granted in full, delivers the value to the importing economy, not to its workers' pay packets, and the further step, from the economy's gain to the stratum's bribe, is the step the original magnitude problem blocks.

### If value transfer between economies were proven tomorrow, would the thesis be proven with it?

No, and the gap between the two is the analytical heart of this article. Proof of transfer would establish that the core gains from the periphery. The thesis requires in addition that the gain reach a stratum of the core's workers as wages above the value of their labour power, their politics explained by the receipt.

A related reformulation deserves a paragraph, because readers will meet it in the literature and mistake it for the thesis restated. The concept of an imperial mode of living, developed by the German political scientists Ulrich Brand and Markus Wissen, describes the way everyday life in the wealthy countries, its mobility, diet, consumption, and expectations, is built upon the appropriation of labour and nature elsewhere, secured by the structures of the world market and the states that police it. The concept is illuminating about consumption and about ecological cost, and it deliberately generalises what the labour aristocracy thesis particularised: where the thesis picked out a stratum and a wage, the mode of living picks out a whole society and its material culture. That generality is the concept's strength for its own purposes and its weakness as a rescue for the thesis, since a condition shared by an entire population cannot explain differentiation within it, and the thesis, to be the thesis, must explain differentiation: why this layer led the movement into reformism while that layer, in the classical expectation, was to have led it out of capitalism altogether.

## Assessing the defence: transfer is not a bribe

The defence stated earlier must now be answered directly, and the answer begins with a concession the brief for this article requires to be made without evasion. The first element of the defence, the claim that value passes on a substantial scale from economies of low wages to economies of high wages, is not settled either. Serious economists dispute the size, the mechanisms, and in some formulations the coherence of the transfer claim, and this article takes no position on it beyond recording that it is arguable and argued by serious people. A reader who grants Cope the whole of the transfer claim has granted him a great deal. The assessment that follows grants it for the sake of argument, because the thesis fails, in this article's judgement, even on that generous assumption, and an assessment that wins only by refusing the opponent's premises wins cheaply.

Grant the transfer. Four difficulties remain, and they are the difficulties the three objections already exposed, now visible in their application to the strong version. The channel difficulty: transferred value reaches the core economy as profit, as cheapened inputs, and as fiscal room, and each of those destinations benefits the core's capitalists before and more certainly than its workers, so the specifically working class receipt that the thesis needs is the last and least documented leg of the journey. The distribution difficulty: a benefit diffused through prices and public services falls on the whole population of the core and cannot explain why the labour movement in particular, rather than every institution of the core, settled into the politics it did. The correlation difficulty: the countries whose workers gained most from whatever transfers the world economy performs include countries whose own extraction was negligible, so the gain cannot be assigned to the mechanism the thesis names without dissolving the mechanism into the world market as such. And the falsifiability difficulty, which the defence's handling of militancy creates: a version of the thesis under which quiescence proves bribery and militancy also proves bribery, as defence of the bribe, has arranged to be confirmed by every possible state of the evidence, and a claim that no evidence could embarrass has quietly left the company of empirical claims altogether.

### What is the difference between saying the core gains and saying its workers are bribed?

The first statement concerns an economy and is compatible with every gain accruing to its owners. The second concerns a class relation inside that economy and asserts a specific payment, from the gain, to a stratum of workers, made because their loyalty was worth purchasing. The defence argues the first and concludes the second.

Fairness requires the other side of the ledger to be entered before the verdict, because the defence is not without genuine explanatory victories. It is true, and the alternatives alone do not easily digest it, that the labour movements of the rich countries have a long record of indifference or hostility toward the workers of the poorer world: exclusionary migration politics, support for or silence about colonial wars, bargaining strategies that treated southern competition as a threat to be fenced out rather than as fellow workers to be organised. The institutional and productivity explanations account for reformism at home more readily than they account for that record abroad. The defence's deepest intuition, that the record abroad has a material root and is not a century long accident of prejudice, deserves to be honoured as an intuition even by readers who reject the mechanism offered to explain it. Nationalism and racism within working classes are themselves phenomena requiring material explanation, and a complete theory of reformism will have to supply one. The mechanism it supplies is the wrong one, on the evidence assembled here. It does not follow that the phenomenon it misexplains is unreal, and the verdict below is careful to convict the mechanism, not the observation that prompted it.

## The political stake: why the argument is heated

Every empirical dispute in this field carries a political charge, but this one carries an unusual voltage, and the brief for the article directs that the stake be stated plainly, as an implication of the thesis rather than as a motive attributed to any of its holders. It can be stated in one sentence. If the workers of the wealthy countries are materially bribed by imperialism, then they have an interest in the continuation of imperialism, and a class with an interest in the continuation of the existing world order is not a potential ally of the workers struggling against that order but an obstacle to them. Everything else is commentary.

Follow the implication through, because its reach explains the temperature of a century of argument. If the thesis is true in its strong contemporary form, then the classical strategy of the workers' movement in the rich countries, organising the majority class to take power in its own country, is addressed to a class that the theory itself describes as bought. Strategy must then relocate: to the workers of the periphery, whose exploitation funds the arrangement and who alone, on this view, have nothing to lose; or to minorities and margins within the core who are excluded from the bribe; or to a long work of exposure aimed at a metropolitan class whose material interest resists it. If the thesis is true only in Lenin's narrower form, the strategy differs again but remains marked by it: the bribed apparatus must be broken and the unbribed majority won from under it, which was the founding logic of the communist parties that split from social democracy after 1917. And if the thesis is false, then the reformism of the northern working classes has domestic causes, those classes remain what the classical theory said they were, the potential allies of workers everywhere, and strategies premised on their corruption are not merely mistaken but corrosive, dividing the class in imagination before capital divides it in fact. No wonder the argument is heated. The dispute about a ratio in the colonial accounts is, at one remove, a dispute about who in the world can be trusted to make a revolution, and there is no question in this tradition with higher stakes.

### Why state the political implication inside a glossary article at all?

Because the heat of the dispute is itself evidence a reader needs. When an empirical question is argued as though livelihoods depended on it, the reader who knows what conclusion the answer is used to carry can discount in both directions, scrutinising the defence for wishful arithmetic and the criticism for wishful innocence.

Stating the stake also imposes a discipline on this article's own verdict, which should be declared. The verdict that follows is a verdict on evidence, reached by the tests the article set out before the stake was described: magnitude, identity, comparison. It would be a poor return on the reader's attention if, having insisted that defenders must not argue backward from their strategy to their arithmetic, the article argued backward from its own strategic sympathies to its verdict. The tests were stated in advance of their results, the alternatives were required to explain the same facts, and the concession owed to the defence, that the transfer claim between economies is arguable and unsettled, was made before the answer. A reader who disputes the verdict is invited to dispute it at the same joints: produce the traced transfer, repair the correlation, or show that the alternatives fail where the thesis succeeds. That invitation, and not any political preference, is the article's closing position on method.

One more consideration belongs in this section, because it explains a feature of the debate that puzzles newcomers, the way the thesis functions as a loyalty test in some political milieux, asserted as a shibboleth rather than argued as a claim. A proposition that carries a strategy inside it will always attract that treatment: to question the arithmetic is heard as questioning the allegiance. The phenomenon is not confined to one side. The thesis's critics have their own shibboleth available, the unity of the working class, which can be invoked to forbid the inquiry rather than to conduct it. The scholarly literature at its best, Cope's assembly of the defence and Post's assembly of the objections alike, refuses both shortcuts and argues the evidence. That is the company this article has tried to keep.

## The verdict

The assessment can now be gathered. The thesis explains a political fact, the reformism of the metropolitan working classes, by an economic mechanism, the transfer of colonial superprofits into the wages of a stratum. The mechanism fails the magnitude test: the transfer, in the form the classical thesis requires, has not been traced, and the flows that can be traced are too small beside the metropolitan wage bill to fund the effect. Its prediction about identity fails: the strata it casts as bought were repeatedly the most militant in the class, in causes that exceeded their own differentials. Its cross country prediction fails: reformism flourished where empires were absent and deepened as empires dissolved. The alternatives, institutional integration, the electoral route, the welfare settlement, and productivity growth within national economies, explain the timing, geography, and distribution of reformism without requiring any transfer, and they explain them with magnitudes that can be verified in the domestic record. The contemporary defence, granted its strongest premise, the reality of value transfer between economies, still does not reach the thesis, because a gain to an economy is not a bribe to a stratum, and because a benefit shared by an entire population cannot explain the differentiation the thesis exists to explain. The political conclusion that gave the dispute its heat, that the workers of the rich countries are not potential allies, therefore does not follow, resting as it does on a mechanism that has not been substantiated.

What remains standing, after the assessment, is not nothing, and a verdict that pretended otherwise would fail the reader. Engels's observation remains: privilege within a working class is real, it is unevenly distributed, and it shapes political behaviour in ways any serious theory must reckon with. The question the thesis posed remains: the labour movements of the wealthy world have a record toward the workers of the poorer world that demands a material explanation and has not yet received a complete one. And the method the best work on both sides exemplifies remains: state the mechanism, follow the money, compare the cases, and let the verdict fall where the evidence puts it. As an account of why the metropolitan working class was not revolutionary, the thesis is best understood as a brilliant conjecture of the war years that organised a century of inquiry and did not survive it. The inquiry was worth the century. Readers deciding whether the thesis is still credible, the question with which the FAQ section below is partly concerned, now have the apparatus to answer: credible as a question, not as a conclusion.

The question of [whether the working class still exists](/2011/11/01/does-the-working-class-still-exist/), in the form the classical theory imagined it, is taken up in its own article, and the older debate over [reform or revolution](/2013/02/01/reform-or-revolution-debate/) supplies the strategic background against which Lenin's intervention made sense. The contemporary form of the underlying issue, the organisation of production across a world of radically unequal wages, belongs to the article on [imperialism and global supply chains](/2017/06/01/imperialism-and-global-supply-chains/). This article's territory is narrower and, it is hoped, now precisely surveyed: one phrase, one mechanism, three tests, and a verdict.

## The stratum up close: the world of the skilled trades

Abstract talk of a stratum can mislead, and a precision article should at some point describe the human material the theory was built to explain. The skilled trades of Victorian and Edwardian Britain, the population from which Engels drew his observation, inhabited a world with its own dense institutional fabric. The amalgamated unions, of which the engineers' society founded in 1851 was the model, were as much benefit societies as fighting organisations. Members paid high weekly contributions, steep by the standards of unskilled wages, and drew in return sick pay, unemployment benefit, funeral money, and a superannuation allowance that amounted to a private pension. The union card was a certificate of respectability, presented to employers as evidence of skill and steadiness, and the societies policed their members' conduct, fining drunkenness and disorder, because the collective reputation of the trade was a collective asset. Apprenticeship, served over years and guarded jealously, restricted the supply of recognised skill and thereby defended its price. A craftsman who had served his time possessed something like a property in his trade, and he behaved, in many observable respects, as small proprietors behave: cautiously, exclusively, and with a strong preference for order.

Beneath and beside this world stood the mass from which it distinguished itself: the labourers, the casual dock and building workers hired by the day or the hour, the sweated trades of the East End workshops, the domestic servants, and the Irish immigrants who competed at the bottom of every urban labour market. The distance between the two worlds was not only a wage distance. It was a distance of housing, of dress, of accent, of chapel and club, of the ability to plan a year ahead or to survive a month's sickness without the parish. Contemporaries saw the line clearly and had a vocabulary for it, the respectable and the residuum, and the skilled trades themselves drew it with enthusiasm, organising processions, building institutes, and campaigning for the vote as men whose character had earned it. When Engels described an aristocracy within labour, he was not theorising in a void. He was naming a social fact that any visitor to a northern industrial town could verify in an afternoon.

The political expression of that world is where description ends and dispute begins, because the same fabric can be read in two directions. Read one way, the benefit society is the embryo of working class self government, a school of administration and solidarity from which the whole movement grew, and its caution is the caution of men protecting something real that they had built with their own contributions. Read the other way, it is a machine for separating the fortunate from the unfortunate, its benefits financed by exclusion, its respectability purchased by distance from the mass, and its caution the caution of a group with a recognised place to lose. Labour historians have argued both readings for a century, often about the same unions in the same decades, and the disagreement is not resolvable by adding facts, because the facts are largely agreed. What divides the readings is the question of where the craftsman's advantage came from, and that is, once again, the question of this article. If his high contribution and high wage rested on the scarcity of his skill and the productivity of the industry that employed him, the benefit society is a domestic achievement, an early conquest of the working class from its own economy. If they rested even in part on a national monopoly underwritten by empire, then the separation of the trades from the mass was subsidised from abroad, and a moral shadow falls across the achievement that no amount of solidarity within the craft can lift. The description cannot decide between the readings. Only the accounting can, and the accounting, as the magnitude sections have shown, keeps failing to find the subsidy where the shadow requires it to be.

One further feature of the stratum's world deserves recording, because it recurs in every later application of the thesis and is often missed. The line between the aristocracy and the mass was never a line between different kinds of family. It ran through streets, through households, and through individual lives. A craftsman could fall out of the aristocracy through sickness, through a trade depression, through the deskilling of his craft by machinery, and his children could fail to enter it; a labourer's son could serve an apprenticeship and rise into it. The boundary was real and consequential, and it was also permeable and patrolled rather than hereditary, which distinguishes an aristocracy of labour from an aristocracy proper in a way the theory's rhetoric sometimes obscures. Permeability matters politically. A stratum that families enter and leave across a generation does not develop the sealed corporate consciousness of a caste, and its politics, as the identity objection showed, oscillate accordingly.

### What did a union card certify in the world of the amalgamated societies?

It certified that its holder had served an apprenticeship, paid substantial weekly contributions, and submitted to the society's discipline, drawing sick, unemployment, funeral, and pension benefits in return. The card functioned as a passport of respectability with employers and as the boundary marker between the organised trades and the unorganised mass.

## Codification: from argument to orthodoxy

Between Lenin's wartime writings and the century's midpoint, the thesis changed its epistemic status. What had been a conjecture, advanced to explain a catastrophe and open to investigation, hardened into a component of orthodoxy within the communist movement, and the hardening is itself a chapter in the assessment, because claims that become articles of allegiance stop being examined by the people who repeat them. Grigory Zinoviev, the president of the Communist International, gave the thesis its canonical organisational form in the years after 1919, arguing that the victory of opportunism in the old parties was bound up with the narrow corporate interests of the labour aristocracy and drawing the strategic moral that the split in the working class movement was not a misfortune to be repaired but a reality to be organised. The Comintern's early congresses absorbed the claim into their founding analysis. To belong to the new International was, among other things, to accept that the reformist parties rested on a bribed stratum, and that acceptance did argumentative work long after anyone had looked at a colonial account book.

The codification had consequences that a fair assessment must count on both sides. On one side, it preserved the question. Generations of activists and scholars inside and around the communist movement took seriously the problem of why the metropolitan working class was not revolutionary, and the thesis directed their attention to material interests rather than to the comforting explanation that the masses had merely been fooled. On the other side, codification inverted the burden of proof. A claim that began as a hypothesis to be tested became a premise to be applied, and its applications multiplied far beyond its evidence: every reformist leadership, every sectional dispute, every defeat could be processed through the category, and the processing had the form of explanation without the risk of it. When scholars inside the tradition, in the later twentieth century and after, returned to the accounts and the strike records and reported that the transfer could not be found and the correlations did not hold, their findings were received in some quarters not as contributions to a shared inquiry but as defections, which is the reception a claim gets when it has become a badge. The pattern is worth stating in a glossary article because readers will meet the thesis most often in its codified form, as a settled possession of a tradition, and they are entitled to know that its settlement was organisational before it was ever evidential.

There is an irony in the codification that the historians of the idea have not failed to notice. Lenin's own usage retained the investigative temper: he pointed to a phenomenon, proposed a mechanism, and revised his formulations as the argument developed, distinguishing the aristocracy from the broader strata of better paid workers and from the petty bourgeois layers the war had created. The codified version flattened those distinctions into a single denunciatory category. The thesis as orthodoxy is therefore cruder than the thesis as its author left it, and an assessment aimed at the crude version, as much contemporary polemic is, shoots under the original. This article has aimed at the strongest versions available, the classical one in Lenin's distinctions and the contemporary one in Cope's reconstruction, and the codified middle version, the one that merely repeats, can be left to the reader's newly equipped judgement.

## The historians' debate: a stratum in search of its evidence

Because the thesis began as a remark about English history, English historians were the first to test it as history, and their debate, running across the second half of the twentieth century, is the closest thing the subject has to a laboratory. Eric Hobsbawm's essays, collected and argued over for decades, gave the concept its most influential scholarly form. Hobsbawm accepted that a labour aristocracy existed in mid Victorian Britain, identified it with the upper layer of skilled workers whose position rested on the craft organisation and the world market monopoly of those decades, and traced its cultural and political distinctiveness: its respectability, its Liberalism, its distance from the unskilled. Crucially for the present assessment, Hobsbawm's mechanism was Engels's rather than Lenin's. The aristocracy's cushion, in his telling, was the product of British industrial supremacy and of the craftsmen's own organisation, a conjuncture of the world market and of domestic bargaining, and he treated the stratum as a formation of a phase, dissolving as the supremacy waned and the mass unions rose.

The critics of the concept, within the same historiographical generation, pressed on exactly the joints where the thesis is weakest in all its forms. John Foster's study of Oldham, a cotton town that ought to have been a heartland of the aristocracy, argued that the supposed aristocrats were central to the radicalism of the earlier nineteenth century and that the mid century quiescence had other causes, in employer strategy and state repression, than a purchased stratum. Robert Gray, examining Edinburgh, found a labour aristocracy of a kind but insisted on its heterogeneity and on the limits of separating it from the class around it. Geoffrey Crossick's work on the artisans of Kentish London dissolved the category further into a spectrum of statuses and cultures. The cumulative effect of the debate was not a verdict of guilty or innocent. It was a shrinkage. The aristocracy of labour survived the historians as a description of a real layer in a particular place and period, with its explanatory ambitions cut down to size, its mechanism reassigned from imperial transfer to industrial conjuncture and craft organisation, and its period firmly bounded. What the historians did to Engels's version, the economists and political scientists of the critical literature later did to Lenin's, and the parallel is instructive: in both cases the descriptive kernel survived and the causal shell cracked.

### Why did the historians keep the stratum while discarding the mechanism?

Because the stratum is visible in the sources, in wage books, union rules, and the testimony of contemporaries, while the mechanism requires a flow of funds that the sources do not record. A layer of privileged workers can be photographed, so to speak, in the archive; a transfer from colony to wage packet has to be reconstructed from accounts, and the reconstruction, attempted repeatedly, does not yield the required sums.

For the researcher persona of a later section the moral is plain, but it is worth drawing here as well. Debates of this kind are rarely settled by a single decisive document. They are settled, insofar as they settle at all, by the slow redistribution of plausibility as each component claim is tested in its home discipline. It has now been tested in three homes: in history, where its stratum survived and its mechanism shrank; in economics, where its transfer could not be traced at the required scale; and in comparative politics, where its predicted correlation failed to appear. A claim can survive failure in one home by migration to another, and the contemporary defence can be understood as such a migration, from the wage packet to the price structure, from the stratum to the whole population, from testable mechanism to background condition. Migrations of that kind are sometimes how theories grow. They are also how theories retreat, and the reader is now equipped to judge which of the two the migration represents.

## The war and the split: what the thesis was for

To understand why a conjecture about wages carried the weight it did, the reader has to recover the scene of its generalisation, because the thesis was never only about wages. The socialist movement of the early twentieth century was built on an expectation so deep it was rarely stated: that the working classes of the rival powers, organised in their millions, would refuse to slaughter one another for their rulers' empires. The Second International had said as much in congress resolutions of increasing solemnity. When August 1914 falsified the expectation in a fortnight, the movement faced a choice of explanations with enormous strategic consequences. One explanation was moral and personal: the leaders had betrayed, the members had been stampeded by nationalist frenzy, and the remedy was better leaders and firmer resolutions. Another was sociological in the manner of Michels: the organisations had become ends in themselves, and their officials had led them to the side of the states that licensed their existence. Lenin's explanation was pitched beneath both, at the level of material stratification inside the class, and its appeal to revolutionaries who had watched the collapse is not hard to reconstruct. It preserved the doctrine. If the majority of workers remained unpurchased and only an upper layer, with its officialdom, had been bought, then the theory of revolution survived the catastrophe intact, damaged in its timetable but not in its sociology, and the catastrophe itself became evidence for the theory of imperialism that had predicted the war.

That preservative function is the key to the thesis's longevity, and recognising it is not the same as dismissing the thesis, since explanations that preserve a research programme are sometimes true. But it does identify the pressure under which the claim was formed, and pressure of that kind should raise a reader's evidential standards rather than lower them. A conjecture adopted because a movement needed it will be retained past its evidence unless the movement cultivates the habit of checking, and the history traced in these pages, codification followed by a century of repetition followed by the slow audit of the accounts, is the record of a checking that arrived late and from the margins. The lesson generalises beyond this thesis, and it is one of the reasons the series gives the thesis a full article: in political argument, the claims that do the most consoling work deserve the most exacting tests, and the magnitude test is simply the exacting test appropriate to a claim about money.

The split that followed 1914 gave the thesis its second career as a founding warrant. The communist parties that broke from social democracy after the war understood themselves as the parties of the unbribed, and the labour aristocracy thesis supplied their self understanding with an economics. Reformism was not a rival interpretation of socialism; it was the ideology of a purchased layer, and organisational separation from it was therefore not schism but hygiene. Historians of the communist movement have shown how thoroughly the category structured its holders' reading of every subsequent event, from the general strikes of the nineteen twenties to the popular fronts of the thirties, and how poorly it prepared them for the episodes that did not fit, above all the militancy of the very strata it had written off. A theory that functions as a warrant accumulates this kind of cost invisibly, in the events it renders illegible, and the cost belongs in the assessment alongside the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was always in service of the warrant.

## Following the money: the methods behind the magnitude finding

The magnitude finding is sometimes misheard as a shrug, an absence dressed as a result, and a precision article should show the work. Scholars who set out to test the transfer approached it from three directions, and the convergence of the three is what gives the finding its weight. The first approach worked from the colonial side: assembling, from the published accounts of colonial administrations and chartered companies and from the trade statistics of the imperial powers, the best available picture of what the colonies actually yielded to their metropoles, in dividends, interest, salaries remitted home, and the profits of the carrying and insuring trades. The picture that emerged was of real wealth, unequally shared and often brutally extracted, concentrated in particular sectors and cities, and modest when laid beside the national income of the extracting power. The second approach worked from the metropolitan side: examining the wage series of the industries where the aristocracy was supposed to sit, and asking whether those wages behaved as a bribe would behave. A bribe funded from colonial returns should rise and fall with those returns, should appear in the trades closest to the imperial economy, and should exceed what comparable skill commanded in economies without empires. The series instead behaved as wages behave in every well documented labour market, tracking the productivity of the industry, the scarcity of the skill, the cost of living, and the strength of the organisation bargaining over them. The third approach worked comparatively, setting the wage levels and wage structures of imperial and non imperial economies side by side and looking for the premium the thesis predicts in the imperial cases. The premium, adjusted for productivity, was not there to be found in any systematic form.

Each approach has its weaknesses, and the defenders of the thesis have identified them fairly. Colonial accounts were kept by the extractors for their own purposes and understate what was taken in kind, in land, and in the destruction of competing industries whose absence never appears as a receipt. Wage series are patchy before the twentieth century and treacherous across occupational boundaries. Comparisons between countries import every confounding difference of endowment and institution. A single approach, challenged on these grounds, might wobble. The finding rests on the fact that all three wobble toward the same conclusion, and that the defenders, challenged in turn to produce the transfer by any method they prefer, have produced instead a relocation of the claim, to channels, through prices and budgets, where measurement is harder and assertions are correspondingly safer. There is a name for that pattern in the methodology of the sciences, and this article will not spare the thesis its application: a claim that retreats, under testing, from the measurable to the diffuse has the profile of a claim in protective decline, however sincere its holders.

The honest summary of the quantitative literature is therefore a double one, and both halves belong to the reader. No transfer of the kind and scale the classical thesis requires has been demonstrated, and the attempts to demonstrate the components have repeatedly returned sums too small for the effect. At the same time, the deep structure of the world economy, the hierarchy of wages and the flows of value within global production, remains incompletely measured, and a defender is entitled to say that absence of demonstration is not demonstration of absence. The entitlement has a limit, and the limit is the burden of proof. The thesis asserts the transfer. A century of assertion, against the background of an archive that has been open to its defenders throughout, leaves the burden where it started. The verdict of this article is a verdict about that burden, not a claim to have measured the unmeasurable: the transfer is unsubstantiated, and a political conclusion of the first magnitude has been rested on it.

### What is the difference between finding no transfer and proving there was none?

Proof of a negative, across a whole economy over a whole century, is not available to any method history possesses. What the literature offers is the repeated failure of well designed searches, conducted by scholars of several allegiances, to find flows of the required kind and size, together with positive evidence that the wages in question are explained by other, measurable causes. That combination shifts a rational reader's confidence without pretending to logical certainty.

## The relocation examined: prices, budgets, and the cheap loaf

Because the contemporary defence rests so much of its weight on the relocated channels, each channel deserves examination on its own merits rather than dismissal by association with the failed wage channel. The price channel runs as follows. The goods consumed by metropolitan workers, food, fibre, and in the era of global production chains nearly every manufacture, are cheap because the labour embodied in them is cheap, and the labour is cheap because the workers who perform it are held in low wage economies by the structures of the world market. A metropolitan wage therefore commands more goods than the same wage's domestic product would supply, and the difference is a real income transferred from the world's poor to the world's comparatively comfortable. The budget channel runs in parallel. States in the wealthy countries fund welfare, infrastructure, and public employment from revenues that depend on the profitability of their economies, and that profitability is enhanced, on the defence's account, by the same global hierarchy of wages, so that the social wage of the northern worker is also partly a dividend of the arrangement.

Both channels describe something real, and the assessment that follows is not a denial of them but an audit of what they can explain. The price channel's benefits are distributed by consumption, and consumption in a wealthy economy is distributed across every class and every age, to the shareholder and the pensioner and the unemployed in proportion to their spending, with the largest absolute gains accruing to those who spend most, the wealthy themselves. A mechanism whose benefits pool at the top of the consuming society and thin toward its bottom is a strange instrument for purchasing the loyalty of a stratum of its workers specifically, and it is silent on the question the thesis was invented to answer, why one part of the working class led it into reformism while another part was expected to lead it out. The budget channel suffers the same diffusion with an additional embarrassment: the fiscal capacity of the wealthy states grew most rapidly, and their welfare settlements most generously, during the decades when the formal colonial systems were being dismantled and when the growth of domestic productivity was at its historical peak, so the channel's flow swells as its alleged source contracts.

There is a deeper difficulty, and it is the one the verdict has already used, which should now be made fully explicit. A theory earns its standing by the discriminations it makes: it tells the investigator where to look for differences, between strata, between countries, between periods. The relocated thesis discriminates almost nowhere. If the whole population of the core benefits through prices and budgets, then the theory predicts the politics of the core as a fog predicts the weather, uniformly and without contrast. The phenomena that need explaining are contrasts: reformism here and insurgency there, loyalty in this trade and rebellion in that one, quiescence in this decade and explosion in the next. For contrasts, the investigator needs causes that vary as the effects vary, and the domestic causes, organisation, institutions, electoral incentives, and the fortunes of particular industries, vary exactly as required. The fog may be real. It may even matter, for other questions, above all for the question of what the core's comfort costs the periphery. But a fog is not a mechanism, and the labour aristocracy thesis, which began as a mechanism of admirable sharpness, does not survive its conversion into one.

## Two national cases

Generalisations in this field live or die in the national records, and two cases, chosen because the literature on them is deep, show the tests at work. The German case is the thesis's hardest, because Germany was the homeland of organised reformism and the laggard of European empire. The Social Democratic Party of Germany, the largest socialist party in the world before 1914, perfected the politics the thesis attributes to bribery: electoral growth, trade union legalism, a revisionist wing arguing openly for gradualism, and, in 1914, the vote for war credits that Lenin's theory was built to explain. Yet the German colonial empire, acquired in the eighteen eighties, was small, late, unprofitable in aggregate, and a net charge on the imperial budget, subsidised by the taxpayer rather than subsidising him. The superprofits that were to have purchased the SPD's millions of voters cannot be located in an empire that consumed revenue instead of remitting it. What can be located, in abundance, are the domestic causes: a franchise at the imperial level that made electoral growth rational, a state that combined repression with social insurance in Bismarck's founding settlement, unions whose funds and legal standing grew with every year of peace, and an industrial economy whose productivity growth delivered rising real wages to organised and unorganised alike. The German record reads, from end to end, like a demonstration of the alternatives.

The American case tests the identity objection in its purest available form. The United States built, in the late nineteenth century, the most famously conservative craft union movement in the industrial world, the American Federation of Labor, whose skilled affiliates practised an exclusiveness toward the unskilled, toward immigrants, and toward Black workers that the theorists of the labour aristocracy could have taken as their type specimen. The federation's conservatism is not in dispute. Its funding is. The American republic held no significant colonial empire during the federation's formative decades, and the craft unions' advantages are documented to have rested on the scarcity of their skills in a labour hungry economy, on their control of apprenticeship, and on the racial and ethnic segmentation of the labour market, which allowed them to defend their scarcity against cheaper competition. Then, in the nineteen thirties, the sequel inverted the thesis's prediction a second time. The great organising wave that transformed American labour was led through the new industrial unions of steel, motor manufacture, and rubber, whose members stood at the top of the manual wage structure, and it was conducted with a militancy, sit down strikes, pitched battles with company police, and open defiance of courts, that the bought stratum of theory should have been incapable of. The privileged, in the American record, are first the most conservative and then the most militant workers in the country, within two generations, with no colonial transfer appearing at either turn to explain the change. What changed was organisation and law: a new form of union and a new legal framework for bargaining. The variables that move are the alternatives' variables.

### Why does the German case weigh so heavily against the thesis?

Because it combines the maximum of the effect with the minimum of the alleged cause. The largest, most successful reformist workers' movement of the classical period grew up in the imperial power whose colonies were belated, smallest, and loss making, while its domestic institutions, franchise, social insurance, and industrial growth supply a complete alternative explanation visible in the same record.

A third case can be treated more briefly because it has already done duty in the comparison section, and it is the case of the settler economies, which the literature on the thesis in Australia and elsewhere has made unavoidable. In the settler societies, high wages for white workers were real, were defended with ferocious sectional politics, and rested visibly on the exclusion and dispossession of indigenous peoples and on immigration restriction against cheaper labour. Defenders of the thesis cite these societies as its clearest confirmation: here, surely, is a stratum whose privilege was founded on the colonial relation itself. The assessment should concede the description and dispute the mechanism. The settler premium was produced by political exclusion within a labour market, by land taken and labour fenced out, not by a transfer of superprofits from a periphery into wages; the colonies in question were the place where the privileged workers themselves lived, and the flows ran from the dispossessed on the spot to the settlers on the spot, through the state they controlled. That is a real and damning political economy, and it generated exactly the racial sectionalism the thesis predicts. But it is a different mechanism from Lenin's, requiring no imperial metropolis and no remitted superprofit, and its existence shows something the thesis's users rarely notice: privilege within labour can be manufactured domestically, by exclusion and by law, wherever a group of workers captures the state that polices its labour market. The general phenomenon is the segmentation of workers by politically enforced advantage. Empire is one of its historical theatres, not its necessary condition, and a theory that mistakes the theatre for the mechanism will misread every case where the play is staged elsewhere.

## The family wage: a privilege the thesis saw and misdescribed

One component of the historical stratum's privilege deserves separate treatment because the thesis noticed it, in passing, and then failed to use it, and the failure is diagnostic. The skilled trades of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries campaigned, as a central demand, for a family wage: a male wage sufficient to keep a wife out of the labour market and children at school. The demand was partly protective and partly exclusionary, and its achievement, where it was achieved, constituted a genuine privilege of the organised trades over the mass, whose families survived only by sending every member to work. Here is stratification inside the working class, with clear political consequences, conservatism about the social order that guaranteed the arrangement among them. Yet the family wage was not funded from colonial superprofits. It was funded from the product of the industries the men worked in, extracted at the bargaining table, and its other face was the cheapness of the female labour excluded from the bargain, a transfer within the domestic working class from women's wages to men's. The episode matters because it shows the thesis's characteristic error in miniature. Confronted with a real privilege and a real conservatism, the theory reached past the visible, domestic, measurable transfers, from the unorganised to the organised, from women to men, from the casual to the permanent, toward an invisible external transfer that flattered the larger theory of imperialism. The internal transfers are where the evidence lives. A research programme that began from them, from the segmentation of labour markets by craft, race, sex, and citizenship as a general phenomenon with domestic mechanisms, would have explained more of the record with less conjecture, and it is in that direction, as the researcher persona notes, that the serious literature has in fact moved.

## The explanandum examined: what reformism actually is

An explanation can fail in a quieter way than falsehood: it can explain a phenomenon that was never quite there, in the shape the explanation requires. Before the verdict on the thesis is allowed to rest, the thing it purports to explain deserves the same scrutiny as the explanation, because reformism, inspected closely, is a less uniform substance than either the thesis or its critics habitually assume. The word covers at least three distinct phenomena that a careful analysis must separate. There is reformism as a doctrine, the explicit gradualism of a Bernstein, arguing that the movement is everything and the final goal nothing. There is reformism as a practice, the daily conduct of unions and parties that bargain, legislate, and administer without any doctrine of gradualism, often while professing revolutionary aims on feast days. And there is reformism as an outcome, the long run pattern in which no revolutionary situation in an advanced country issued in a revolution, whatever the actors intended. Lenin's version was designed for the second and third phenomena and is weakest exactly where it is applied to the first, since the doctrinal gradualists argued their case in the open, on grounds of democracy and economic evolution, and their writings show no trace of the colonial connection the thesis would lead one to expect at the centre of their self understanding.

The separation matters for assessment because the three phenomena have different distributions. Doctrinal reformism flourished in some movements and was marginal in others with similar wage structures. Reformist practice was nearly universal wherever unions existed legally, in colonial and non colonial countries alike. The outcome, the absence of revolution in the advanced West, was universal across the entire set, including in countries whose movements were doctrinally revolutionary and practically insurrectionary at intervals. A mechanism located in a stratum's wage packet might plausibly account for patches of the second phenomenon. It strains visibly when asked to account for the third, the universal outcome, since the outcome obtains in cases where the mechanism is absent, weak, or inverted, and a universal effect with a parochial cause is the standing signature of a misidentified explanation. The alternatives earn their advantage here as well: institutions, franchises, welfare states, and productivity growth were present across the whole set of advanced countries during the period of the outcome, varying in degree as the phenomena varied, which is the signature of causes in the right place.

There is also the baseline problem, and an honest article should expose it, because it disciplines both sides. The thesis measures reformism against an expectation, that the metropolitan working class would otherwise have been revolutionary, and treats every shortfall from that expectation as an effect requiring a purchasable cause. But the expectation itself was a theoretical construction, drawn from a reading of the tendencies of capitalist development, and its failure across a whole century might indicate not that the class was bought off course but that the course was mischarted. If workers in advanced economies were never, in their majority, on a trajectory toward insurrection, because their grievances were real but remediable and their organisations rationally pursued the remedies, then there is no residue left for a bribe to explain, and the thesis dissolves from the other side: not refuted in its mechanism but unemployed, an answer to a question that better theory would not have asked. The critics of the thesis divide on this point. Some hold the classical expectation and seek better mechanisms for its frustration. Others abandon the expectation and treat reformism as the default politics of a class improving its position inside a growing economy. The reader should know that the second position exists and that it renders the whole dispute, defence and objections alike, a dispute about the decoration on a conclusion already reached by other means. This article has conducted the assessment on the first position's ground, granting the thesis its explanandum, because a claim deserves to be beaten on its own field if it can be. It can be, and it has been. But the second position waits behind the result, and a complete education in the subject includes knowing that it is there.

## The officialdom: a stratum the thesis found and mismeasured

Within the broad failure of the mechanism, one of the thesis's identifications retains a stubborn plausibility, and precision requires isolating it. Lenin's aristocracy always included, alongside the skilled workers, the stratum of full time officials: the union secretaries, party organisers, newspaper editors, insurance administrators, and parliamentarians whose livelihoods were supplied by the workers' movement itself. That stratum existed, in every mass movement, by the early twentieth century. Its material interest in the movement's institutions, in their funds, premises, legal toleration, and electoral prospects, is not conjectural but definitional: a general secretary's salary, status, and career are extinguished by the illegality that a revolutionary adventure invites. The officials' consequent caution, their hostility to unofficial strikes, their preference for negotiation and for the parliamentary road, are among the best documented regularities in the history of labour movements, and the thesis deserves credit for fastening, earlier than most sociology, on the material basis of that caution.

The mismeasurement lies in the source assigned to the officials' comfort. Their salaries were paid from members' dues and from the movement's own enterprises, out of the wages of the workers they represented, in a domestic circuit that can be followed in the published accounts of any large union. No colonial subvention was required to pay a general secretary, and none has been shown to have paid one. The officials' privilege, where it existed, was the privilege of office within an organisation, of the kind Michels anatomised in the same years without reference to empire, and its political effect, caution, follows from the office and not from the alleged external fund. The episode is a paradigm of the thesis's general procedure, worth a section because it recurs everywhere: a real stratum is identified, a real political effect is correctly associated with it, and then the material basis of the stratum is assigned, without measurement, to the theoretically convenient source rather than to the visible one. A reader who learns to spot that procedure in this instance will spot it in a hundred others, in this literature and beyond it, which is part of the educational value this series assigns to the article.

It is worth adding, in fairness to the officials' memory, that the caution the thesis attributes to purchase had honourable sources as well, and a complete account should register them. The men and women who administered the unions had usually built them out of nothing, against employers' associations, blacklists, and hostile courts, and their reluctance to hazard the accumulated funds and legal standings on insurrectionary gambles was the reluctance of trustees, not only of placemen. Reformism at the top of the movement was overdetermined: office, institution, memory of defeat, and sincere gradualist conviction all pushed the same way, and a theory that selects one strand, and the least documented strand at that, as the explanation has mistaken the richness of the record for a receipt.

## Why wages differ: the productivity account in its own right

Since so much of the dispute turns on the explanation of high metropolitan wages, the competing account deserves a statement in its own right rather than as a residue left when the transfer fails. The productivity account begins from the observation that a worker's wage, in a capitalist economy, is bounded over any long period by the value the worker's labour adds, and that this added value differs enormously between economies because the worker in an advanced economy works with more and better capital, more reliable energy and transport, denser networks of suppliers and skills, and institutions that make complex cooperation routine. A machinist tending automated equipment in a plant supplied by a continent of specialised firms produces, in an hour, a multiple of what the same machinist, transplanted to an economy without that supporting structure, could produce, and the multiple shows up in the wage each economy can sustain. On this account the global wage hierarchy is, in the first instance, a hierarchy of productive systems, and the metropolitan worker's advantage over the peripheral worker is the advantage of the system she works in, captured in part through her organisations' bargaining.

The account must immediately be complicated in two directions, and the complications are the points where the defence of the thesis digs in. First, productivity is not a natural endowment. The capital stock, infrastructure, and skill base of the advanced economies accumulated historically, and part of that accumulation, in its early centuries, drew on colonial plunder, slave produced commodities, and captive markets. A defender is therefore entitled to argue that the productivity explanation, pushed back in time, re-encounters empire at the origin, so that the high wage is colonial in its ancestry even if no transfer can be found in its present accounts. The argument is serious and this article grants its force for the early period of industrialisation, while noting what it concedes in the granting: the mechanism has migrated again, from a flow that pays a stratum in each generation to a founding contribution capitalised centuries ago, and a contribution to the capital stock of 1800 cannot, without a great deal of further argument, explain the politics of a stratum in 1900 or 2000, mediated as it is by everything that happened between. Second, productivity does not distribute itself. The share of the advanced economy's product that reaches its workers as wages is settled by bargaining, law, and politics, and varies between countries of similar productivity, which is why the institutional alternatives are complements to the productivity account rather than rivals. The composite picture, productivity setting the feasible range and institutions settling the point within it, explains the level of metropolitan wages, their variation between countries, and their growth over time, in quantitative agreement with the national records. Against that composite, the transfer hypothesis offers a flow that cannot be found in the accounts, distributed to a stratum whose politics do not match, in countries whose empires do not correlate. The comparison is not close, and the article's verdict does no more than register it.

### Does the colonial origin of early industrial capital rescue the thesis?

It rescues a different claim, that empire contributed to the making of the advanced economies, which few critics deny and which historians argue about in terms of degree. The thesis needs a continuing payment to a continuing stratum whose politics the payment explains, and an ancestral contribution to a capital stock, shared by every institution in the society, cannot pick out a stratum or a politics in the way the thesis requires.

## The defence replies: three answers and their costs

Completeness requires that the defence be allowed its replies to the three objections in their most developed form, since a verdict that ignored them would be advocacy wearing the dress of assessment. To the magnitude objection, the reply is that the accounting frame is rigged: value transferred through trade does not appear as a flow of colonial profit at all, because it is realised in the prices of goods and in the profits of firms that may have no colonial address, so that searching the colonial accounts for the bribe is searching the one place the modern form of the transfer never visits. To the identity objection, the reply is the one already recorded, that the militancy of the privileged is the defence of privilege, and that the test of the stratum's politics is not the noise of its strikes but their object, which the record shows to have been, again and again, the protection of differentials, of national labour markets, and of the imperial order that underwrote them. To the comparison objection, the reply is the world system move: the unit of exploitation is the global economy, the Scandinavian worker consumed the products of the same low wage periphery as the British worker, and the absence of a flag over a colony is a legal detail that the material relation ignores.

Each reply has now been priced, in the sections above, and the prices can be collected. The reply to magnitude purchases immunity from the accounts at the cost of the claim's testability: a transfer that leaves no trace in any account, realising itself invisibly in prices, may exist, but a theory resting on it has exchanged evidence for insulation. The reply to identity purchases consistency at the cost of falsifiability, since a reading under which loyalty confirms the thesis and rebellion confirms it too cannot be embarrassed by any record whatsoever. The reply to comparison purchases scope at the cost of discrimination, since a mechanism that operates identically in imperial and non imperial countries cannot explain anything that differs between them, and everything interesting in the subject differs between them. Three replies, three surrenders of the qualities that made the classical thesis a serious empirical conjecture: its specificity, its exposure to evidence, its power to discriminate. The defence, at full strength, is the strongest available shelter for the thesis, and the shelter is built from the thesis's own timbers, removed one by one. That is the deepest reason the verdict goes the way it does, deeper than any single objection: the thesis can be saved, but only by being dismantled, and what remains standing in the shelter, a stratified world economy whose comforts are unevenly shared and politically consequential, is a truth that no one in the dispute, on the evidence of their writings, ever actually denied.

## Among the neighbours: opportunism, embourgeoisement, and the uses of a category

The thesis sits in a crowded neighbourhood of concepts, and a glossary article should walk the reader past the adjacent doors, because polemic constantly substitutes one for another. Opportunism, in Lenin's vocabulary, is the political phenomenon itself, the sacrifice of the movement's long term aims to immediate advantage, and the labour aristocracy is its alleged social base: the concept pair divides the labour of explanation between a politics and a stratum said to generate it. Embourgeoisement, the thesis that rising living standards were making workers bourgeois in outlook and aspiration, circulated in the same decades and was debated in the sociology of the postwar years, where careful field studies of affluent workers found instrumental attitudes to work and unionism rather than conversion to middle class values, a finding that cut against the simple version of every bribery and embourgeoisement claim at once. Social imperialism, the charge that a workers' party had become an agency of its own state's imperial policy, is the thesis weaponised for a later polemic and inherits all its evidential debts. Labour segmentation, the neighbour with the strongest claim on the future, studies the division of workers into protected and exposed tiers by law, credential, race, sex, and citizenship, explaining the tiers by the institutions that police them, and it can be read as the labour aristocracy thesis with the imperial mechanism removed and the observational core retained and generalised. A reader who can move among these concepts without confusing them owns the territory this cluster of the series exists to survey, and will notice that the direction of serious travel has been constant for fifty years: away from the single external transfer, toward the multiple internal mechanisms by which advantages among workers are made and defended.

### What work does the phrase still do in argument, given the state of its evidence?

Chiefly political work: it assigns a motive, closes a question, and marks an allegiance. As an analytical instrument it has been superseded by finer tools, the study of segmentation, of institutions, and of productivity, but as a verdict pronounced on whole working classes it retains a polemical life that its evidential standing cannot justify, which is why a precision article treats it at this length.

## A reader's protocol

The article can close its substantive work by handing the reader a short protocol for the next encounter with the phrase, in a speech, a pamphlet, a seminar, or a feed. Ask, first, which version is in play: the observation that privileged workers exist, the classical mechanism of a colonial transfer into a stratum's wages, or the strong contemporary claim that the core's whole working class is maintained by global transfers. The three are argued as one and evidenced as none. Ask, second, for the transfer: by what channel, in what rough proportion to the wage bill, does the alleged bribe travel, and what would the world look like if the answer were different. Ask, third, for the contrast: which country, trade, or decade behaves as the claim predicts and which behaves as its rivals predict, and whether the speaker's reading of militancy could ever, in principle, count against them. Ask, finally, what conclusion is being carried: whether the sentence about wages is doing duty for a sentence about allies, and whether that sentence has been argued or merely imported. Four questions, none of them hostile, all of them answerable by a speaker who has done the work. The thesis has had a century of assertion. What it has earned, and what this article has tried to supply on both sides, is a century overdue examination, after which the reader may keep the phrase, as a question and a warning, and decline it, as a verdict and a receipt.

## The New Unionism as a test case

History occasionally performs an experiment on a theory's behalf, and the explosion of organisation among the unskilled in Britain at the end of the eighteen eighties is as close as this subject comes to one. For decades the aristocracy of labour had stood apart, its unions closed to the labourers beneath them, its model of organisation, high contributions for high benefits among the skilled few, treated as the only viable one. Then, in a few years around 1889, the unskilled organised themselves on a different model entirely: gasworkers, dockers, match workers, and general labourers built unions with low dues and fighting funds, struck with a solidarity that astonished contemporaries, and won. The episode bears on the thesis in two directions at once, and both should be followed honestly. In the thesis's favour, the episode confirms the depth of the division it described: the new unions were necessary precisely because the old ones would not organise the mass, and their founders said so, in print, in the language of indictment. The aristocracy's exclusiveness was real, and it had real consequences for the class's unity. Against the thesis, the episode displays the political character of the unorganised mass in a way the theory's schema does not predict. The least privileged workers in the country, the supposed raw material of revolution whom the bribed stratum allegedly restrained, had been quiescent not from any satisfaction with their condition but from the absence of organisation, and the moment organisation arrived they moved, led in many cases by socialists the aristocracy's unions had kept at arm's length. Quiescence, in this exhibit, is a function of organisation's absence, and militancy a function of its presence, across the whole range of privilege, which is the alternatives' account of the matter stated in a single episode.

The sequel is equally instructive, because the new unions, victorious and legalised, underwent within a generation the same institutional settling as their predecessors: funds accumulated, officials multiplied, caution grew, and the grandchildren of the dockers of 1889 were administered by men whose outlook their grandfathers would have recognised from the amalgamated societies they despised. No colonial transfer arrived in the interval to purchase the second settling. The empire, in those decades, was at its territorial zenith and then in dissolution, while the unions' caution grew steadily through both phases, tracking their institutional age and their legal embedding with a fidelity no imperial variable can match. A theory of the stratum's politics that works for both generations is available, and it is the institutional one. A theory that works only by adjusting the alleged bribe's timing to each generation's behaviour is not a theory of the bribe. It is a biography of the unions with the bribe written in as a watermark.

## Reading strikes as evidence

Because the identity objection rests on the strike record, the reader deserves a note on how that record should and should not be read, since both camps in the dispute have read it tendentiously. A strike is an ambiguous datum. It records a willingness to fight, under specific leadership, for specific demands, at a specific moment, and it does not record its participants' views on empire, revolution, or the world order, except where those views are written into the demands themselves. The defence of the thesis exploits the ambiguity in one direction, reading every sectional strike as the defence of privilege and every political strike as an exception. The criticism exploits it in the other, reading every large strike as evidence of revolutionary potential and every sectional demand as a mere form. A disciplined reading attends to three features that resist both exploitations. The demands: whether a movement's stated aims were confined to its own differential or extended to other groups, to the unorganised, to the unemployed, to workers abroad. The conduct: whether, at moments when the existing order was genuinely in question, the stratum's organisations moved toward the confrontation or toward the settlement, and who led them in each direction. And the counterfactual texture of the sources: what the participants said they were doing, in the records they left, which are richer for this subject than for most, since the labour movement documented itself obsessively.

Read with that discipline, the record refuses both slogans. It shows privileged trades defending differentials with a selfishness that shames their traditions, and the same trades, sometimes in the same years, carrying general movements whose demands reached far beyond any differential, funding the organisation of workers who could not repay them and striking in sympathy at cost to themselves. It shows strata of the worst paid sunk in an apathy that no bribe explains and no agitator could shift, and the same strata erupting, when organisation and occasion met, with a radicalism that frightened their own leaders. The pattern that organises this variety is not the wage level. It is the presence or absence of organisation, the state of the law, the behaviour of employers and governments, and the memory of the last victory or defeat. Those are the alternatives' variables again, and the strike record, read whole, is their archive. The thesis's reading of the same archive requires it to sort the identical evidence into two piles by a principle, the defence of privilege, that the participants' own accounts mention rarely and the demands mention inconsistently. Evidence that must be sorted by the theory it is meant to test is not evidence for the theory.

### What single kind of episode would most strengthen the thesis in the strike record?

An episode in which a privileged stratum, its differential credibly threatened by an anti imperial measure rather than by an employer, mobilised to defend the imperial arrangement as such, in its own name and for that stated reason. Episodes of sectional defence are plentiful; episodes of that specific kind are what the theory needs and what the record, read with discipline, does not supply in the required form.

## Citizenship and the national frame

There is a form of privilege inside the world's working classes that the literature on the thesis circles constantly and names rarely in its classical texts, because the classical texts were written when it was being constructed: the privilege of citizenship in a wealthy state, with the legal right to work in its labour market, claim its welfare, and exclude others from both. Later scholarship, including the sociology of split labour markets associated with Edna Bonacich, analysing how higher priced labour defends itself against cheaper labour through exclusion, supplied a mechanism for this privilege that is everything the transfer mechanism is not: visible, legal, and enforced by identifiable institutions whose records can be read. On the split labour market account, the antagonism between a protected stratum and an exposed one arises from the price difference of their labour itself, and the protected stratum's politics, exclusionary, national, defensive of the border that maintains its price, follow from the market structure without any detour through superprofits. The account explains phenomena the thesis gestures at, above all the labour movements' long record of restrictionism, with a parsimony the thesis cannot match, and it explains them in economies with no colonial history as readily as in imperial ones, since a price differential and a border are all it requires.

The citizenship case also refines the article's central distinction one final time. A northern worker's citizenship is a real material advantage, held against the world's majority, and it is defended, sometimes disgracefully, by the movements that speak in her name. In that sense the intuition behind the contemporary defence, that the core's workers are implicated in the global hierarchy by interest and not only by ideology, survives every objection in this article and should be carried away intact. But an advantage is not a bribe. A bribe is paid by someone, to someone, for something, in a transaction that could in principle be invoiced. Citizenship is allocated by states according to their own logics of membership and exclusion, defended by its holders as a birthright rather than received as a payment, and bound up with the productivity and institutions of the economy it admits to rather than with a fund transferred from the excluded. The politics it generates, the defence of the border, is exactly the politics the thesis wants to explain, arrived at by a road the thesis did not build. An assessment that refused the intuition because it refused the mechanism would throw away the most valuable thing the tradition produced. This assessment keeps the intuition, returns the mechanism, and commends the distinction to the reader as the article's final act of precision.

## What a successful defence would have to look like

It is a requirement of fair assessment that the losing side be told what winning would have required, and the requirement is not ceremonial, since research programmes have revived on less. A successful defence of the thesis, in any of its forms, would need to produce three things that a century of advocacy has not produced. It would need a channel, specified tightly enough to be looked for: a route by which value originating in low wage production reaches, identifiably, the wage settlements, household incomes, or public services of a specified population in the wealthy economies, as distinct from the general prosperity of those economies. It would need a proportion: a demonstration, by methods its critics could in principle accept, that the flow through that channel is of an order capable of sustaining the political effect attributed to it, set against the total income of the population said to be bought. And it would need a contrast: a case, a country, a period, or a stratum, where the flow's presence and absence, or its rise and fall, track the political behaviour the thesis predicts, against the predictions of the institutional and productivity accounts, which would have to be shown failing on the same case. A defence that produced those three things would not merely rescue a phrase. It would transform the study of reformism, and scholars who believe the phenomenon is real should regard the specification as an invitation rather than a taunt, since it tells them exactly what to go and find.

Until then, the standing of the thesis can be summarised in a form a student can carry into an examination and a reader into an argument. The observation at its root is sound and permanently useful: workers are stratified, privilege among them is real, and privilege has politics. The mechanism at its centre is unsubstantiated where it is specific and untestable where it is safe. The political conclusion built on the mechanism does not follow and should be retired from use as a premise, whatever a reader's strategy, because strategies built on unexamined premises about who can be trusted have a record, in this tradition, that no one should wish to extend. And the questions the thesis asked, about the material roots of the northern movements' record toward the south, remain open, better served now by the study of segmentation, citizenship, institutions, and production chains than by the repetition of a conjecture from the years of the First World War. That is the assessment. The magnitude test, applied without fear or favour to the strongest versions available, is what produced it, and the reader who has followed the application owns the result rather than borrowing it.

## Engels in his letters: the observation in context

The founding texts of the thesis are few enough that a precision article can afford to walk around them slowly, because centuries of argument have loaded a handful of sentences with more weight than sentences usually bear. Engels's 1858 letter to Marx, written from Manchester, where he worked in a cotton firm while studying the city around him, belongs to a correspondence preoccupied with the doldrums of the English movement after the defeat of Chartism. The Chartists had been the first mass working class political movement in the world, and their collapse into quiescence was the standing puzzle of the two exiles' middle years. Engels's remark about a proletariat becoming bourgeois was offered, in that setting, as one strand of an explanation whose other strands were prosperity after the gold discoveries, the exhaustion of a defeated movement, and the absence of a crisis. Later quotation amputated the remark from its setting and promoted the strand into the whole cloth. Restoring the setting does not refute the thesis that was built on it, since theories are entitled to outgrow their occasions, but it changes the authority the founding text can lend. The letter is a shrewd man's aside about a conjuncture he expected to pass, offered among several explanations, hedged with the tentativeness of private correspondence between collaborators who disagreed as a method. Its elevation into a doctrine's charter was the work of the codification described earlier, and the elevation, not the letter, is what the assessment has been examining.

Engels returned to the theme in print, in the prefaces he wrote in the eighteen eighties and nineties for new editions of his early book on the English working class, and the printed versions show the idea in motion across his lifetime. Writing in 1885, he looked back at the era of the monopoly and treated the privileged position of the great unions as the key to their politics in that period, while insisting that the monopoly itself was passing away under American and German competition and that the ground was therefore shifting under the aristocracy's feet. Writing again in the eighteen nineties, he dwelt on the new unionism of the unskilled as the sign that the old stratification was breaking down. The trajectory across the prefaces is the trajectory this article has attributed to the original idea throughout: a phase concept, tied to a configuration of the world market, expected to dissolve with the configuration. There is no point in the published record at which Engels promotes the observation into a law of capitalist development as such, and the promotion, when it came, came from the emergency of 1914 and from a writer who, to his credit, knew he was innovating. Precision about the pedigree protects both sides of the later dispute: the defence may not claim Engels's authority for Lenin's structure, and the criticism may not pretend that Lenin invented the phenomenon he was straining to explain.

The second founding figure in the prehistory deserves a paragraph, because the codified tradition usually omits him and the omission conceals something about the idea's original range. Karl Kautsky, the leading theorist of the Second International, wrote about the aristocracy of labour around the turn of the century in terms that located its basis in the organisation and scarcity of skilled trades within each national economy, and treated its politics as a problem of the movement's internal education rather than of imperial finance. Only later, and partly under the pressure of the war's debates, did the superprofit mechanism become the obligatory version. The existence of a pre Leninist Marxist discussion in which the stratum is real and the mechanism is domestic is a standing reminder that the thesis's later form was a choice, made under identifiable pressures, among available formulations, and that returning to the junction and taking the other road is not a departure from the tradition but a recovery of one of its own paths.

## The British century: the stratum followed through time

A single national case followed across the whole period does more work than a survey of many, and Britain, the thesis's birthplace, rewards the following. In the middle decades of the nineteenth century the fit between the observation and the society was as close as social concepts ever achieve: a visible stratum, a visible monopoly, a visible politics of respectability, and a mass beneath whose exclusion from all three was equally visible. The last third of the century loosened every element of the fit at once. The monopoly eroded as rival industrial powers equipped themselves, the mass unions rose on the new unionist model, the franchise widened in successive reforms that the respectable had demanded and the mass then used, and the skilled trades themselves, their apprenticeships undermined by machinery and their scarcity diluted, began the long transformation that would dissolve the old aristocracy into a general stratum of organised labour. By the early twentieth century the population to which the term could be applied with a straight face had changed its composition entirely, and Lenin, writing about Britain from exile, was obliged to relocate the aristocracy's weight from the crafts to the officialdom, a relocation the codified tradition preserved without noticing that it had changed the theory's subject. A stratum that must be redefined every generation in order to remain the explanation of a constant phenomenon is not tracking the phenomenon. The phenomenon, reformism, persisted through every redefinition, which suggests, once more, that its causes lie in structures that persisted likewise: organisation, law, the franchise, and the growth of the product.

The decades after 1945 administered the case's severest test and the thesis's quietest defeat. Britain after 1945 possessed a working class whose living standards rose faster than in any previous generation, a comprehensive welfare state, unions encompassing the unskilled mass the Victorians had excluded, and a Labour government elected by that class to administer a programme of domestic reconstruction, all of it constructed during the two decades in which the empire, the alleged paymaster, was liquidated colony by colony. Defenders of the thesis, confronting the postwar settlement, have sometimes replied that the paymaster changed rather than vanished, that American hegemony and the new hierarchies of the world market replaced the formal empire as the source of the core's advantage. The reply may be entertained for the strong contemporary version of the claim and has been, in the sections on relocation. For the classical thesis it is an epitaph. The theory that set out to explain Victorian and Edwardian reformism by the superprofits of the British empire confronts, in the empire's own homeland, the spectacle of reformism's greatest triumphs financed, demonstrably and in the published budgets, out of the taxation of a domestic economy growing at unprecedented speed. The budgets are the thesis's home ground, the place where the money was supposed to be. It is not there.

### What happened to the stratum as the monopoly that supposedly fed it disappeared?

It did not disappear with the monopoly, which is the difficulty. The organised workers of Britain grew more prosperous, more secure, and more thoroughly integrated into the nation's institutions across the very decades in which the world market monopoly waned and the empire dissolved, so the correlation the thesis requires, between the fund and the stratum's fortune, runs against the theory in its founding case.

## The concept of a bribe: an anatomy

The word bribe does quiet rhetorical work throughout this literature, and unpacking it is a service a glossary article owes, because the metaphor smuggles conclusions that the mechanism has not earned. A bribe, in ordinary usage, has four features. It is paid deliberately, by an agent who calculates its effect. It is paid for a specific service, the recipient's loyalty or abstention in a specific matter. It is paid from funds the payer controls and can itemise. And it corrupts, in the sense that the recipient's conduct after payment is explained by the payment rather than by the recipient's independent judgement. Transferred to the history of labour movements, each feature generates a testable implication that the literature rarely states as plainly as the metaphor implies it. Deliberateness implies a payer who decided to purchase the stratum, and the historical record of employers and states shows instead a ruling class that resisted concessions bitterly, conceded them piecemeal under pressure, and repeatedly attempted to retract them when the pressure relaxed, which is the behaviour of a party to a bargain it dislikes, not of a purchaser distributing largesse. Specificity implies that the payment should track the service, rising when loyalty is most needed, in wartime and crisis, and the wage record shows no such pattern, tracking instead the tightness of labour markets and the strength of unions in booms and slumps alike. Itemisation implies the fund, whose absence the magnitude sections have already recorded. And corruption implies that the recipients' politics are insincere in a specific way, performed for pay, whereas the record of the reformist movements is full of leaders and members whose gradualism was argued, principled, and maintained at personal cost, including the cost of the revolutionaries' contempt.

None of this is to deny that material interest shapes politics, which is the founding insight of the whole tradition under examination and the premise this article shares. It is to insist that between the premise and the bribe there lies a distance that the metaphor conceals and that only mechanisms can cross. Interest can operate without a payer, through the unplanned consequences of a position: a worker with a secure job, a house being purchased, children in school, and a pension accumulating has interests that counsel caution, and those interests were assembled by her own efforts inside her own economy, at a bargaining table where the other side yielded ground. To describe the outcome of her struggles as a bribe paid to her is to misdescribe her agency and her opponents' alike, converting a history of conflict into a transaction between a corrupter and the corrupted. The misdescription is not innocent. It licenses the political conclusion, since the corrupted can be written off as the merely mistaken cannot, and it is therefore the metaphor, as much as the arithmetic, that the assessment has been concerned to retire. Readers who retain one sentence from this article should retain the distinction: an interest is a fact about a position, a bribe is a claim about a payment, and the thesis needs the second while the evidence supports the first.

## Skill, scarcity, and the price of labour: the stratum's economics from below

The alternatives have so far been stated at the level of institutions and aggregates, and one of them deserves a hearing at the level of the individual labour market, because the thesis's home territory, the wage of the skilled worker, is governed there by a logic its literature rarely engages. A craft wage is a price, and like every price it is set by the relation between what buyers will pay and what sellers must accept, disciplined on the sellers' side by the cost of producing the skill and on the buyers' side by the cost of doing without it. Serving an apprenticeship costs years of low earnings; maintaining a family through them costs more; the trades that organised successfully were those whose members could recoup the investment through a scarcity they collectively policed. Where the policing worked, the wage carried a scarcity premium that contemporaries envied and theorists later misread. The premium looks, from outside, like privilege, and it is privilege, in the plain sense of an advantage others lack. Its source, however, is legible in the labour market itself, in the apprentice counts, the entry rules, the employers' complaints about the shortage of hands, and the premium's behaviour when the scarcity broke, as it broke whenever machinery redesigned a trade or a new reservoir of trained labour opened. Premiums that collapsed with the scarcity that sustained them were scarcity rents. A bribe does not collapse when a machine is installed. The distinction is not a verbal one, and the wage record, which shows craft premiums rising and falling with the techniques and training pipelines of their industries across the whole period, displays the signature of rents rather than of payments from an external fund that knew nothing of lathes or looms.

From this level of analysis, the whole landscape of the dispute rearranges itself instructively. The stratification of the working class appears as the aggregate of a thousand local scarcities, credentials, and closures, each maintained by an organisation, a law, or a custom, each yielding an advantage to its holders, and each generating its characteristic politics of defence. Empire enters the picture as one maker of scarcities among others, since colonial administrations and imperial labour codes did manufacture protected positions for metropolitan workers abroad and for settlers on the spot, and the assessment has conceded those cases their weight. But the general theory of the phenomenon, the theory that explains why stratification is found in every capitalist labour market ever studied, in colonies and in countries innocent of colonies alike, is the theory of closure and scarcity, and it requires no transfer. Generously read, the thesis can be understood as a first draft of that general theory, written when the most visible closures were national and imperial in form, and mistaking the form for the cause. First drafts are honoured by being superseded accurately, and the accurate supersession is the work the segmentation literature has done.

## Periods and their politics: a stratigraphic view

The thesis treats reformism as a single substance secreted by a single mechanism across a century, and the stratigraphic view, which distinguishes the periods and asks what kind of politics each produced, is a useful solvent. In the first period, of craft organisation before mass democracy, the politics of the privileged stratum were defensive and exclusionary in the manner described, and the mass beneath it was largely unorganised rather than reconciled. In the second, of mass parties and the approach of war, the question that dominated was the parties' relation to their states, and the stratum that mattered was the officialdom, whose material basis was internal to the movement. In the third, of the postwar settlements, the relevant population was the whole organised class, its politics were those of full employment and the welfare state, and the funds were fiscal and domestic beyond serious argument. In the fourth, of deindustrialisation and the weakening of the unions in the old core, the protected and the exposed changed places in ways no version of the thesis anticipated, as secure industrial employment, the classical bribe's supposed seat, was dismantled by the very governments and employers who were supposed to be paying for loyalty, and the strata that retained security were those sheltered by the state and the professions. A mechanism constant across the four periods should leave a constant signature. The signature in the record is instead a series of distinct configurations, each overdetermined by its period's institutions, and the honest conclusion of the stratigraphic exercise is that reformism is not one phenomenon with one cause but a family of settlements between organised workers and the orders they inhabit, renewed period by period on terms the period supplies. The thesis survives the exercise as the name of the first period's configuration, generalised beyond its warrant. That is a more respectful fate than most political concepts achieve, and it is the fate this assessment assigns it.

### Why does periodising the argument weaken the thesis more than any single statistic?

Because the thesis's claim is precisely that one mechanism explains a constant phenomenon. Showing that the phenomenon changes its composition, its social seat, and its funding from period to period, while the mechanism's alleged source waxes and wanes independently, removes the constancy the claim was built on, and it does so from the record the thesis's own authors accepted.

## The argument from silence: what the reformists said about themselves

A neglected test of any theory that attributes a motive is the testimony of the attributed. The reformist movements of the advanced countries were among the most self documenting movements in history. They published their programmes, their congress debates, their theorists' treatises, and their leaders' memoirs in industrial quantities, and they argued about the sources of their own politics incessantly, since the accusation of betrayal was levelled at them from the beginning. If the material basis of reformism had been the colonial connection, one would expect the connection to surface somewhere in that ocean of self examination: in a gradualist's candid memoir, in an opponent's documented exposure, in the private correspondence that movements eventually publish. The gradualists who argued their doctrine in the open, Bernstein above all, rested it on the evolution of capitalism itself, on democracy, on the growth of the middle strata and the complexity of the economy, and they were answered by opponents, Luxemburg and Kautsky among them, who attacked those arguments as arguments. The colonial superprofit appears in the internal debate of the movement hardly at all before Lenin made it the centre of an alternative movement's founding polemic, and it appears in the reformists' own explanations of themselves essentially never. Arguments from silence are treacherous, and this one is offered with the caution it deserves, since movements rarely confess their material bases in the terms their enemies prefer. But the silence has a shape. The participants, explaining themselves, pointed unanimously at the causes the alternatives name: the franchise, the growth of the economy, the strength and habits of their organisations, the memory of defeats. A theory that overrides the testimony of every participant in favour of a fund none of them mentions must meet a burden proportionate to the override. The magnitude test is that burden, formalised, and it has not been met.

There is one partial exception, and precision requires its registration. Colonial questions did agitate the movement, at congresses where the colonial policy of socialism was debated before 1914, and a wing of the reformist parties defended colonialism openly as a civilising and economically progressive enterprise. The debates show that empire was present to the movement's mind and that some of its leaders were imperialists by conviction. What they do not show is the mechanism: the defenders of empire argued from destiny, from markets, and from the alleged benefits to the colonised, not from a share in superprofits reaching their voters, and the opponents of empire inside the movement attacked the policy on moral and strategic grounds without alleging that their comrades' wages were its price. Empire as an ideology of the movement is documented. Empire as its paymaster is not, and the distance between those two findings is the distance this whole article has been measuring.

## The Second International debates its own soul: opportunism before the bribe

The category of opportunism, which the thesis exists to explain, had a history inside the movement before anyone attached it to a stratum's income, and recovering that history shows the bribe mechanism to have been one contender in an older argument rather than the argument's origin. Through the eighteen nineties and the first years of the new century, the parties of the Second International fought their internal battles over revisionism, ministerialism, and the colonial question in terms that were doctrinal and tactical: whether the final goal could be approached by increments, whether socialist ministers in bourgeois governments corrupted the movement or educated the electorate, whether the party's press and apparatus were becoming a world unto themselves. The participants in those fights explained one another's positions by conviction, by temperament, by the logic of parliamentary institutions, and by the sociology of organisation that Michels was codifying in the same years from inside the German party. Nobody of consequence, accuser or accused, explained reformism by a transfer of colonial funds into working class pockets, for the sufficient reason that the fund, had it existed at the required scale, would have been the worst kept secret in Europe, visible in every budget and balance sheet the movement's economists pored over. The bribe entered the movement's vocabulary as an explanation only when the catastrophe of 1914 created a demand for an explanation of matching enormity, and it entered, significantly, from the movement's revolutionary margin, as a weapon in the struggle to found a new International rather than as a finding of the movement's accumulated research.

The point is not that explanations adopted in emergencies are false. It is that the sociology of the explanation belongs in its assessment, and the sociology here is unfavourable in a specific way. A claim adopted because it performs organisational work, founding a split, warranting a separation, marking the boundary between the saved and the damned, will thereafter be defended by everyone whose organisation rests on it and attacked by everyone whose organisation it damns, and the evidence will be curated by both. That is precisely the century the thesis has lived. The scholarly assessments that stand outside the organisational struggle, the historians of the stratum, the economists of the transfer, the comparativists of reformism, were produced mostly by writers whose institutions did not depend on the answer, and their convergence on the verdict this article reports is therefore the more significant. Where the answer mattered organisationally, the thesis flourished. Where it mattered only evidentially, it withered. A reader learning the subject from the polemical literature alone would invert that distribution and mistake the loudest century for the most examined one.

## Affluence studied: the postwar investigators

The postwar decades supplied a second laboratory, this time staffed by sociologists rather than historians, when the affluent worker of the postwar boom became an object of direct study. The embourgeoisement thesis, the popular claim that rising wages were turning workers into members of the middle class in outlook and allegiance, was the labour aristocracy argument in a new dress and with the empire quietly removed: prosperity, from whatever source, was said to be dissolving the working class's oppositional identity. Investigators who interviewed affluent workers and their families at length, in the most cited British study of the nineteen sixties, found a picture that embarrassed every simple version of the claim. The affluent workers were instrumental about their jobs, valuing them as means to a privatised family life; they were loyal to their unions as instruments for defending the wage that made the life possible; they voted and struck in patterns that tracked their interests as they perceived them; and they showed neither the deference of a bought stratum nor the aspirations of an aspiring bourgeoisie. Their politics were a calculus, conducted inside an economy whose growth they experienced directly, and their conservatism, where it existed, was the conservatism of people protecting gains they had visibly made themselves, in overtime and productivity deals on their own shop floors.

The study's relevance here is methodological as much as substantive. For once, the stratum of theory was interviewed rather than inferred, and what the interviews returned was neither corruption nor false consciousness but a intelligible relation between interest, organisation, and outlook that required no hidden fund. The investigators' workers knew where their prosperity came from, down to the shift premium and the piece rate, and their account of its source agreed with the productivity and bargaining account and with no other. Later studies in other countries complicated the picture without overturning it, adding the dimensions of housing, of public services, and of the second income that households increasingly commanded, and each addition made the explanation of the workers' politics more domestic, more institutional, and more legible, and the postulated external transfer correspondingly more ornamental. A theory is in poor health when the direct study of its subject keeps returning explanations that render it unnecessary, and that, across the historians' archive, the economists' accounts, and the sociologists' interviews alike, is the cumulative finding this article has been assembled to report.

## War credits: the afternoon that called the thesis into being

The event at the theory's foundation repays a slow narration, because its details resist the simplification the theory imposed on them. In the first days of August 1914, as the European powers mobilised, the parliamentary parties of the Second International faced, within hours, the choice their congresses had rehearsed for a generation: refuse the war, by voting against credits and calling the class to resistance, or rally to the national defence. The German Social Democrats, the International's pride, voted for the credits, publicly unanimous, after an internal division in which a substantial minority had opposed and submitted to discipline. The French socialists entered the union sacrée after the assassination of Jaurès, their greatest leader, on the eve of mobilisation. The British movement split, its parliamentary party largely supporting recruitment while its independent wing opposed. Everywhere the pattern repeated with variations: majorities for the war, minorities against, and a speed of collapse that suggested the positions had been prepared by something deeper than the week's panic. Lenin, reading the news in Switzerland, initially refused to believe the report of the German vote, and his subsequent theory building has to be understood as the response of a man whose world picture had been falsified at its centre and who required an account that restored its coherence.

Notice what the details do to the simple form of the thesis. The minorities who opposed the war were drawn from the same strata, the same unions, and the same wage levels as the majorities who supported it, often from the same locals and the same trades. Karl Liebknecht, who broke discipline to vote alone against the credits in December of that year, was a product of the movement's heartland, not of its unorganised margins. The syndicalist and shop steward currents that sustained anti war agitation through the conflict recruited heavily among the skilled engineers of the munitions districts, the very archetype of the aristocracy, whose wartime militancy over dilution and conscription built the shop stewards' movement into a force governments had to reckon with. If a bribe paid out of superprofits had sorted the class into the loyal and the revolutionary in advance, the sorting should be visible in the crisis, with the privileged trades rallying to the state and the unprivileged refusing. The crisis shows instead a class divided at every level, by conviction, by organisation, by nationality's pull and by the state's coercion, with its most privileged industrial workers supplying both the war's industrial backbone and some of its most dangerous internal opposition. The thesis was built to explain the afternoon, and the afternoon, examined closely, does not bear the explanation. What the afternoon does bear out is the organisational account: parties and unions built for legal, electoral, and bargaining purposes, integrated into their states by a thousand threads of licence and livelihood, behaved as such institutions behave under mobilisation, and the individuals who resisted did so against their institutions, at the price the institutions exacted.

### What does the composition of the anti war minorities show about the thesis?

It shows that loyalty and resistance in the crisis of the epoch were not sorted by wage level or skill, since both camps were recruited from every stratum of the organised class. A mechanism whose operation consists in sorting strata by their politics cannot survive a crisis in which the strata conspicuously fail to sort.

## The longer view: what the dispute has taught the study of labour

A glossary article may end its substantive work by asking what a century of dispute has deposited in the disciplines it passed through, because the deposits are considerable and they belong to the reader regardless of the verdict. To labour history, the dispute bequeathed the stratum itself as an object: the fine grained study of skill, of craft organisation, of respectability and its discontents, would not exist in its present richness had generations of historians not gone into the archive to test a revolutionary's conjecture. To economics, it bequeathed a discipline of proportion, the habit of asking of any claimed flow whether it is large enough for the effect assigned to it, a habit whose absence from political argument the reader will now notice everywhere. To political science, it bequeathed the comparative reflex, the insistence that a mechanism claiming to explain a national pattern must survive the journey to the neighbouring country where the mechanism is absent. To the study of social movements, it bequeathed the permanent question of the officials, the material sociology of the people who staff the organisations that claim to speak for classes. And to the tradition from which it sprang, it bequeathed a cautionary tale of the first order, about the difference between a conjecture that organises research and a dogma that organises allegiance, and about how easily the first becomes the second when the conjecture is found, in a moment of catastrophe, to be consoling.

It is fitting that the last word of the substantive assessment should return to the political stake with which the brief charged this article, now that the evidence is in. The conclusion that the workers of the wealthy countries are bought, and are therefore unavailable as allies, has been used for a century to licence strategies of contempt toward the largest organised working classes in the world. The assessment finds the premise of that conclusion unsubstantiated: no traced transfer, wrong correlations, better explanations at every joint. A reader is not thereby instructed in what strategy to adopt, which is no article's business. The reader is instructed in what the evidence will not carry. Conclusions about who can be trusted to change the world are among the gravest a political tradition draws, and they deserve foundations stronger than an untraced flow and a metaphor. It asked to be judged as an economic claim, and it has been, by the test its own structure prescribes. On the magnitude test, it fails, and the failure, properly understood, is not a defeat for the tradition that produced it but the tradition's method, applied at last to one of its own consolations, working exactly as it should.

## Reformism's other homelands: municipal socialism and the cooperative commonwealth

The alternatives gain further credit from a quarter of the record that neither side of the classical dispute visits often, the municipal and cooperative institutions through which much of the metropolitan working class's improvement was actually delivered. Across industrial Europe and its settler offshoots, the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw workers and their allies build gas and water socialism in the great cities, cooperative stores that returned dividends on purchases to millions of households, building societies, allotment movements, and friendly societies of a scale that dwarfed the unions themselves. The politics secreted by that world were reformist to their marrow, gradual, administrative, and preoccupied with the price of bread and the quality of housing, and they were produced by institutions whose accounts are open and whose funding was entirely domestic: the dividend came from the shop's margin on its members' own purchases, the municipal surplus from the city's own ratepayers, the friendly society's benefits from its members' contributions. Here is a vast machinery of working class betterment and working class moderation, operating at the scale of whole populations, with no colonial input at any point, and its existence reframes the question the thesis was invented to answer. The puzzle was never why the metropolitan working class failed to be revolutionary in the midst of a vacuum. The puzzle, if there is one, is why anyone expected a class that was busy building the cooperative commonwealth, and drawing real dividends from it, to abandon the construction for insurrection. The expectation came from theory. The construction came from the class's own assessment of its opportunities, and the construction's funding requires no explanation beyond itself.

The municipal record also supplies a quiet control case for the comparison test, at the level of the city rather than the country. The most ambitious municipal socialism of the period flourished in cities of the landlocked and the late industrialising regions as readily as in the ports of empire, and its strongholds were as often inland manufacturing towns, whose connection to colonial trade was no closer than their railway timetables, as they were the great entrepôts. Vienna's celebrated interwar housing programme, the largest municipal achievement any workers' movement has to its name, was built by a landlocked successor state stripped of its empire entirely, financed from taxes on the city's own property and luxuries, in a country whose brief possession of colonies amounted to none at all. If the dividend that purchased reformism had been colonial, the city that built Red Vienna should have been the last place on earth able to afford it. The arithmetic of the programme is published, its taxes are itemised, and there is no imperial line in the budget. There rarely is, wherever the reader cares to look, and a theory can survive a certain number of absent lines, but not an absence that recurs at every scale, in every archive, across a century of looking.

## The language of the shop floor: how advantage was talked about

One more archive deserves a hearing before the assessment rests, the language in which the workers of the period described their own advantages, preserved in union journals, in the reports of trade congresses, in autobiographies, and in the evidence given to royal commissions. Language is not proof, and movements dissemble in prose as elsewhere, but a century's worth of self description has a grain, and the grain here runs consistently against the thesis's picture. The skilled trades talked about their advantages in the language of desert and defence: the apprenticeship served, the contribution paid, the standard rate held against the employer who would undercut it and the newcomer who would work beneath it. The employers talked about the same advantages in the language of cost and scarcity, complaining of the trades' restrictions exactly as buyers complain of any monopoly they face. Governments talked about them in the language of social peace purchased by concession, and said, when they conceded, that they conceded to strength. In none of the period's voluminous talk does the advantage appear in the language the thesis requires, as a share of a colonial dividend knowingly distributed and knowingly received. The colonial wealth of the age was talked about endlessly, by its defenders as glory and by its critics as plunder, and the audiences for both included the organised workers in their millions. The connection between that wealth and the standard rate of the engineering shops is a connection the period itself did not make, in public or, so far as the surviving correspondence shows, in private. It was made later, in exile, in wartime, by a theorist constructing an explanation for an event the period's own categories had failed to foresee. Genealogy is not refutation, and this article has been careful to test the claim on evidence rather than on origins. But when the evidence also fails, the genealogy explains the residue: why a claim that the record does not support has been believed so long and so warmly by people who never saw the record. It was never a finding. It was a verdict in search of its accounts, and the accounts, when they were finally audited, were not there.

## A note on the figures this article does not give

The reader will have noticed a discipline running through the preceding sections, the refusal to settle the argument by quoting numbers, and the discipline is a rule of the cluster to which this article belongs rather than a stylistic preference, so it should be explained rather than merely practised. The figures that circulate in this dispute, of wage differentials between countries, of the revenues of colonial administrations, of the value transferred between economies under this or that estimate, are among the most contested numbers in economic history. They are built from records kept for purposes alien to the question, converted across currencies and price regimes by methods that are themselves arguments, and cited by partisans with a selectivity that a glossary article would only amplify by joining. The protocol adopted here is therefore to state findings in the form the literature can honestly sustain: that remitted profits were small beside the wage bill, that differentials are large beyond argument, that transfers have not been traced at the required scale, and that each of those statements is a summary of scholarship rather than a measurement performed on this page. A reader who wants the measurements should go to the scholarship, where the methods are argued in the open, and should treat any single figure offered without its method, in this dispute above all, as a claim wearing a number's clothing. The magnitude test, it should be clear by now, does not depend on the missing precision. It depends on orders of scale, on the difference between a stream and a river, and orders of scale are exactly what survives the contestation of the figures. The bribe needed a river. The record shows, at most and on the most generous construction, a stream, and a stream whose course cannot be followed into the wage packets where the theory needs it to arrive.

## The thesis and the reader's world of work

A glossary article earns its length only if the reader leaves better equipped for the encounters that prompted the search, and the encounters, for this phrase, happen wherever the politics of labour are argued in earnest. The phrase will be met as an explanation of why colleagues in secure employment do not strike, of why unions in wealthy countries will not make common cause with unions in poor ones, of why the great expectations of the classical movement were disappointed. In each encounter the apparatus of this article is portable. Separate the observation from the mechanism. Ask for the channel and the proportion. Look for the contrast case, the neighbour without the alleged cause who behaves the same. Notice when a gain to an economy is silently converted into a bribe to a class. And hold the political conclusion up to the light separately, since it is the part of the argument doing the emotional work and the part with the weakest title to the evidence. The reader who practises the sequence a few times will find that it generalises, to other claims in which a fund is postulated to explain a politics, in this tradition and in every other, and that the sequence's power lies in its fairness: it grants the observation, honours the intuition, states the defence at its strongest, and then asks, with genuine openness, for the money. A claim that can produce the money will survive the sequence and deserve to. The labour aristocracy thesis, after a century, has not produced it, and this article's long examination resolves, in the end, into that short sentence, with the whole of the preceding argument standing behind it as the sentence's credentials.

## Three portraits in brief

Abstraction has been this article's method, and a corrective is owed before the personas take over, in the form of three compressed portraits drawn from the kinds of life the thesis was invented to explain. The first is the Victorian craftsman at the height of the supposed arrangement: a fitter in a great engineering town, apprenticed at fourteen, a member of the amalgamated society from his majority, his contributions paid through slump and boom, his union card his passport and his pride. His politics are Liberal, his chapel is Congregational, his ambitions are for his sons' schooling and his own allotment, and his attitude to the labourers in the yard below his shop is a compound of charity and contempt. Everything the thesis says about his stratum's outlook is true of him. Everything it says about the source of his position is, on his own account books, false: his wage was set in a market where his skill was scarce, defended by the society whose funds his own contributions built, and it survived, diminished but recognisable, into the decades when the monopoly that allegedly paid for it was gone. He is the thesis's best exhibit and its standing refutation, in one life.

The second portrait is the full time official of the mass party in the years before the Great War: a former compositor, salaried by the party he serves, editing its provincial paper, sitting on its executive, and spending his weeks in the management of funds, candidacies, and disputes. His caution is total and his radicalism is a memory he honours on anniversaries. The thesis reads him as the bribe's recruiting officer, and his comfort, a house, a salary, a pension in prospect, is real. But the money that pays him is counted in the party's published accounts and comes from the pennies of the members whose caution he is blamed for manufacturing, and his deepest motive, visible in his correspondence, is the trustee's terror of the illegality that would extinguish in a month the organisation he has spent a life assembling. He did not need to be bought. He needed only to be responsible, in the specific way that office makes men responsible, and the theory that required his corruption never reckoned with his accounts.

The third portrait belongs to the century's second half: a car worker in a great midland or midwestern plant in the decades of the postwar settlement, a member of an industrial union, a striker when the union calls and a sceptic when it settles, her household's standard of living rising on two wages, a mortgage, and a welfare state her taxes visibly fund. The strong version of the thesis classes her among the bought of the earth, a beneficiary of the global hierarchy whose politics the benefit explains. Her own explanation of her politics, when investigators recorded it, ran through the shift system, the speed of the line, the price of the house, and the memory of her father's unemployment, and the empire, by then dissolved, figures in it not at all. The hierarchy of the world economy is real, and her comfort is implicated in it in the diffuse way the relocation argument describes. But the woman in the portrait is not a corruption case. She is a worker in a rich economy, defending what she has, in organisations her class built, for reasons she can state, and a theory that cannot tell the difference between her and a placeman has lost the thread that the best of its tradition, the insistence on looking at how people actually live and work, once held firmly. The portraits are offered as the article's final evidence, of the kind that no table can carry: the thesis's people, examined closely, keep turning into the alternatives' people, and a theory whose exhibits defect has been weighed, in every sense that matters, and found wanting.

## The counterfactual workshop: how to think about what would have happened anyway

Every judgement in this dispute rests, at bottom, on a counterfactual, a picture of what wages and politics would have looked like in the absence of empire, and counterfactuals are the least disciplined instruments in the historian's workshop unless their construction is made explicit. The thesis's implicit counterfactual is dramatic: strip the colonies away, and the metropolitan stratum's privilege collapses, its politics radicalise, and the history of the twentieth century changes its course. The critics' implicit counterfactual is undramatic to the point of flatness: strip the colonies away, and the wage structure of the metropolis, driven by its productivity and its labour markets, stands broadly where it stood, with consequences for politics that are correspondingly slight. Between the two pictures the evidence adjudicates only if the investigator specifies, in advance, what traces the dramatic picture would leave that the flat one would not. The preceding sections have, in effect, been specifying such traces throughout: a premium in the imperial economies unmatched in the non imperial ones, wage movements that follow colonial returns rather than domestic conditions, a fiscal dependence of the welfare settlements on imperial revenue, a sorting of strata by politics that tracks their exposure to the alleged fund. Each trace was looked for, in the records appropriate to it, and each was absent or inverted. That is what it means to say the flat counterfactual wins. It does not win by being flat. It wins by leaving less of the record unexplained, which is the only contest counterfactuals can fairly enter.

The exercise has a general lesson worth stating for the student readers of this series, since the same structure recurs wherever the cluster's subjects are debated. Grand political conclusions in this field are usually counterfactuals in disguise, claims that without some named villain or hero the world would have run otherwise, and the discipline this article has practised, specify the trace, consult the record, price the reply, is the discipline that separates the claims that deserve their conclusions from those that merely desire them. The labour aristocracy thesis desired its conclusion with unusual intensity, for reasons the political stake section made plain, and its century of life is the measure of how long a desired conclusion can outlive its evidence when organisations are built upon it. The reader who has followed the counterfactual workshop to this point possesses not only a verdict on one thesis but a method for the next one, and the method, unlike the thesis, improves with every application.

## Envoi: the phrase retained, the verdict recorded

The series to which this article belongs proceeds on the principle that a term in constant political use deserves a permanent, precise, and examinable account of itself, neither a celebration nor a denunciation, and the account is now complete. It names a real stratum and a real problem, proposes a mechanism that the evidence does not sustain, and carries a political conclusion that the mechanism's failure withdraws. It will go on being used, because the work it does in argument, assigning a motive and closing a question, is work that arguers will always want done cheaply. The reader of this article is equipped to make them do it dearly: to ask for the channel, the proportion, and the contrast, and to distinguish, in the answer, between the observation that privilege exists, which is true, the intuition that privilege has material roots in the global order, which is arguable, and the claim that a traced transfer from colony to wage packet purchased the politics of a class, which has been examined for a century and found, on the magnitude test that is the claim's own, to be a receipt for money that was never shown to have moved. Precision of that kind is not pedantry. In a dispute where the conclusion decides who counts as an ally, it is the whole of the ethics of the matter, and it is the service this glossary exists to perform.

## The view from the colonised world

Every account so far has examined the thesis from the metropolis outward, asking what empire did to the politics of the imperial countries. The thesis was also read, from the beginning, at the other end of the relation, by the movements fighting the empires themselves, and its reception there forms a chapter of its history that the metropolitan literature understates. For the anti colonial movements of the twentieth century, the claim that the metropolitan working classes were compromised by their share in the proceeds was not an abstraction. It was an interpretation of bitter experience: of appeals for solidarity answered with indifference, of socialist parties that administered the colonies whose liberation the movements sought, of unions that organised against the employment of colonial seamen and labourers in metropolitan ports. The national liberation movements drew their own conclusions, and the conclusions shaped the century. Where the thesis was believed, strategy turned away from the metropolitan class and toward the peasantry of the colonised world, toward alliances among the colonised states themselves, and toward the construction of movements that owed the European tradition acknowledgement but not obedience. The great strategic innovations of the century's anti colonial revolutions, the protracted struggles based in the countryside, the united fronts that subordinated class to nation for the duration of the liberation war, were all, in part, answers to the question the thesis posed in its harshest form: if the workers of the empire will not move, who will.

An assessment of the thesis's evidence does not oblige the reader to mock the movements that believed it, and precision requires their reasoning to be stated with respect. They were not reading wage statistics. They were reading the conduct of the metropolitan movements at the moments when solidarity was requested and refused, and conduct is a kind of evidence, even if it is not the kind that can settle a question about the flow of funds. The error, where error there was, lay in the inference from conduct to mechanism: from the observed fact that the metropolitan movements would not act, to the explanation that they would not act because they were paid. The movements of the colonised world had no access to the metropolitan wage books and no reason to trust the distinction between an interest and a bribe, since from the receiving end the two feel identical. The distinction matters in the metropolis, where strategy depends on it. From the periphery, what mattered was the refusal itself, and the refusal is not in dispute in these pages. What the assessment withdraws is the receipt, not the record.

The traffic was not one way, and the closing of the circle is the most instructive part of the reception history. The thesis, exported to the colonised world as an explanation of metropolitan conduct, returned to the metropolis transformed, in the dependency literature and in the debates over unequal exchange, as a theory of the world economy's structure, and it was in that transformed shape that the contemporary defence reconstructed it. Anyone who has travelled the argument this far can see the whole itinerary at a glance: an observation about English craftsmen, generalised in wartime into a law of the epoch, codified as the warrant of a split, received in the colonies as a description of their betrayal, rebuilt by the theorists of the periphery into an account of global value flows, and reimported, in that rebuilt form, as the strongest available defence of the original claim. Ideas with itineraries of that richness are rarely simply true or simply false, and this article's verdict has been careful, throughout, to grade the claim's components separately rather than its biography. But the itinerary itself teaches the final lesson. At every stage of the journey, the thesis was believed because it explained something its believers had genuinely seen: the craftsman's exclusiveness, the parties' collapse, the movements' refusal, the world's obscene hierarchy of wages. The sightings were real. The mechanism that was supposed to bind them into one explanation was the invention, and inventions of that power, which organise real sightings into a false unity, are the most durable errors a tradition can make, because every new sighting seems to confirm them. The discipline this article recommends, separating the sighting from the mechanism and grading each on its own evidence, is the only known prophylactic, and it is offered, in closing, as the thesis's most valuable unintended bequest.

## The open file: questions the dispute leaves behind

An assessment that closes a claim should say plainly what it has not closed, and the file on this subject remains open at four points, which researchers entering the field deserve to have itemised without the varnish of a verdict. The first concerns measurement of the world economy's internal flows. The techniques for following value through global production chains, from the wage paid at the point of manufacture to the price charged at the point of sale, are younger than the dispute and cruder than its questions, and work that sharpens them will either give the defence the channel it has always needed or close its last line of retreat. Either outcome is progress, and the field should want the measurement for reasons beyond this quarrel, since no serious account of the world economy can be built without knowing how its gains are divided.

The second open point concerns the conversion of advantage into allegiance. Between a worker's material position and her vote, her union's policy, her movement's conduct toward distant strangers, there lie organisations, habits, stories, and fears, and the study of those mediations is where the explanation of reformism will be completed if it is completed anywhere. The bribery claim short circuited the mediations by positing a payment whose receipt explained everything downstream. Its failure returns the investigator to the harder and more interesting terrain, on which advantage sometimes radicalises and sometimes pacifies, depending on how it was won, how secure it feels, and who is asking it to be shared.

The third concerns the south's own labour movements, whose history the metropolitan dispute treats as a backdrop. The workers of the colonised and postcolonial world built organisations under conditions of repression and poverty that make the metropolitan record look sheltered, and their conduct, their internationalism and their nationalism alike, awaits an assessment as patient as the one attempted here for the northern stratum. A literature that asks only what empire did to the imperial working class has answered, at best, half of its own question.

The fourth concerns vocabulary. The phrase under examination will survive this article, because phrases that convict whole classes in two words answer a standing demand. What scholarship can do, and what this series exists to do, is to keep a precise account permanently attached to the phrase, so that any reader who meets it can discover, in one place, what it claims, what would prove it, what has been found, and what follows and does not follow from the finding. The file stays open. The verdict on the mechanism stands until the evidence changes, and the invitation to change it, by producing the channel, the proportion, and the contrast, stands with the verdict, addressed to any defender willing to do the work the claim has always required and never yet received.

## A coda on method

Writers who inherit a quarrel of this age inherit also a temptation, to settle it by weight of tone, awarding the palm to whichever side sounds more like history's plaintiff. The pages above have resisted that temptation by a deliberate routine, and the routine is worth naming once, at the end, for readers who will meet other venerable quarrels in this series and beyond it. Begin by writing down the claim so exactly that its owner would sign the formulation. Honour the ablest statement of the side you expect to reject, with its author's name attached and its hardest points unsoftened. Extract from the claim the observations that could, in principle, embarrass it, and go looking for those observations in the records least likely to have been arranged for your convenience. Count whatever can be counted, in the rough units the surviving evidence allows, and refuse to be shamed out of counting by the objection that the subject is too human for arithmetic, since it was the claim, not the examiner, that first spoke of money. When a rejoinder arrives, ask what it costs: which part of the original sharpness had to be surrendered to purchase safety. Finally, separate the verdict on the machinery from the honour due to the witnesses, because a claim can collapse while the experiences that made it plausible remain standing, demanding better explanations rather than ridicule. Applied here, the routine yielded an unfashionable result with an unfashionable symmetry: a famous mechanism failed, and the phenomena that inspired it survived, intact and still awaiting their full account. That symmetry is the signature of an honest audit, in this field above all, where the claims under review were forged by people who believed, not without cause, that the fate of nations turned on getting them right.

One practical habit separates readers who profit from a long entry of this kind and readers who merely finish it. Keep a pencil nearby. Mark each passage where a writer asks you to accept a payment nobody itemises, a motive nobody avows, a pattern nobody compares against a neighbouring example. Disagreement, pursued at that level of granularity, becomes cumulative rather than theatrical: page by page, the entries in your margin start to converse, and a private index of unanswered demands accumulates alongside the printed argument. Weeks later, encountering the slogan afresh in a meeting or a pamphlet, you will find the margin speaking first, asking its small, exact, unglamorous questions before indignation or loyalty can organise your reply.

## For students and exam candidates

Students meet the labour aristocracy thesis most often inside questions about imperialism and reformism, and the marking reality should be stated first: an answer that states the thesis accurately, states the magnitude test accurately, and reaches either conclusion on the evidence will outscore an answer that merely repeats the phrase with approval or contempt. Begin any answer with the definition in its strict form, source, transfer, and political effect distinguished, because examiners reward the distinction and its absence is the commonest reason answers on this topic drift. Then deploy the assessment table as a structure: one paragraph per row, claim, evidence, alternative, verdict, gives an essay a spine that weaker candidates, who narrate Lenin and stop, do not have. The worked answers and marking guidance for the whole cluster are gathered in the [exam guide to Marxism and imperialism](/2017/06/15/marxism-imperialism-exam-answers/), which should be read alongside this article rather than instead of it.

Two traps deserve specific warning. The first is the attribution trap: the thesis is Engels's observation and Lenin's generalisation, not a doctrine of Marx, and an answer that assigns it to Marx announces carelessness in its first paragraph. The second is the amalgamation trap: unequal exchange and the labour aristocracy are different claims about different relations, and an answer that uses the first as proof of the second will be marked down by any examiner who knows the literature, which on this topic is most of them. A card on the assessment table, kept in a study system such as the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) for this series, is the most efficient revision artefact this article produces: five rows, each a paragraph in embryo.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The thesis is a gift to a seminar leader precisely because it is wrong in instructive ways. A class can be asked to state the mechanism before being shown any evidence, and the exercise teaches what a falsifiable claim looks like from inside a tradition not famous for producing them. The three objections then organise discussion naturally: set one group to defend the magnitude of the transfer from the literature, one to prosecute the identity of the stratum from the strike record, and one to argue the Scandinavian comparison, and the seminar will discover for itself the pattern this article reports, that each defence of the thesis is purchased by surrendering part of it. The alternatives section supplies the constructive second half of the session, since students who have dismantled one explanation of reformism should be required to build with the materials that remain, and the four alternatives give them institutional, electoral, fiscal, and productivity based components to combine.

Handle the political stake explicitly rather than letting it govern the room from under the table. Students arrive with the conclusion already loaded, in one direction or the other, and a seminar that pretends the question is only about nineteenth century accounts will convince nobody. Naming the implication, that the thesis decides who counts as a potential ally, and then returning the class to the evidence with the stake acknowledged, models the discipline the article itself practises. The strongest seminar exercise available on this material is the rewrite: ask students to repair the thesis so that it survives one objection, then to price the repair by stating what the repaired version no longer explains. Few exercises teach the difference between a theory and a vocabulary as quickly.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the live questions sit where this article's verdict leaves them. The transfer between economies remains genuinely unsettled as a matter of measurement, and work that specifies channels rather than aggregates is where progress will come from: firm level studies of global production chains that follow value from the low wage plant to the final price, fiscal studies that ask how much of the metropolitan state's room for manoeuvre depended on the profitability of internationally exposed capital, and wage studies that test whether internationally exposed sectors paid premiums unexplained by their productivity. The identity problem also remains open in a refined form: not whether privilege exists, but how specific advantages, security, housing, citizenship itself, are converted into political behaviour, and by what organisational mediations. Historians have largely moved the debate from the aristocracy as a stratum to the segmentation of labour markets as a process, and that reframing, which owes something to the thesis's failure, is among its more productive legacies.

Journalists meet the phrase as a term of art inside movements and as an insult outside them, and the service this article can perform for a deadline is definitional hygiene. When a source asserts that western workers are bought off, the follow up questions write themselves from the assessment table: bought with what flow, in what amount, relative to what wage bill, and why then did the countries without empires behave identically. When a source dismisses the phrase as a slur, the symmetrical questions apply: what explains the record of the northern movements toward the south, if not material interest of some kind. Reporting that carries the questions rather than the slogan will serve readers better than either camp's vocabulary, and the distinction between a gain to an economy and a bribe to a stratum, which this article has made its centre, is the single most useful sentence a journalist can take from it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the labour aristocracy thesis?

It is the claim that profits drawn from the colonial world allowed the employers and states of the imperialist countries to pay a favoured upper layer of workers beyond what their labour power was worth, and that the payment purchased that layer's loyalty, converting its politics from revolutionary to reformist. The claim has three parts that can be tested separately: an external source of profit, a transfer of part of that profit into a stratum's wages and conditions, and a political effect following from the receipt. Privilege alone does not state the thesis; the mechanism connecting privilege to empire is the thesis.

### Q: Where does the term labour aristocracy come from?

Engels coined the usage in correspondence about England, remarking in 1858 that a nation monopolising the world market might grow a bourgeois aristocracy alongside a bourgeois proletariat of its own, and he applied the idea to the skilled, organised trades of mid Victorian England, whose wages and security rested, he suggested, on that temporary monopoly. The words named an observation about one country in one period. Lenin later took the observation up, generalised it into a theory of the imperialist epoch, and gave the phrase the meaning it carries in argument now, which is why histories of the term must keep the English episode and the general theory distinct.

### Q: How does the thesis explain reformism?

It explains reformism as the rational politics of a bought stratum rather than as error or betrayal. On the theory, the layer of workers favoured by the imperial bribe acquires a material interest in the order that favours it: empire abroad funds privilege at home, so the stratum defends empire, prefers gradual improvement to the hazards of revolution, and staffs the unions and parties through which the whole class is led. Reformist leadership is therefore not an accident of personnel. It is the political expression of an economic position, and it will persist, on this account, for exactly as long as the superprofits that fund the position persist.

### Q: Are colonial superprofits large enough to fund a bribe?

On the evidence assembled by the critical literature, no. The profits remitted from the colonies and dependent territories, while real and while enriching the investors who received them, were small when set against the aggregate wage bill of the imperialist economies, and the wage settlements of metropolitan industry track domestic productivity, labour scarcity, and bargaining strength rather than the movement of colonial returns. No study has followed a flow of the necessary size from colonial extraction into the pay of a definable stratum. The objection is one of proportion: a bribe must be paid out of something, and the something named by the thesis is not large enough for the purchase attributed to it.

### Q: Were the best-paid workers the least militant?

Frequently the opposite. The record assembled by historians of the labour movement shows the strongest, best paid trades, miners, engineers, transport workers, and later the industrial unions of steel and motor manufacture, repeatedly in the front rank of militancy, including general strikes and confrontations with the state, while the worst paid and least secure layers were often the hardest to organise at all. Defenders reply that such militancy was sectional, fought in defence of the differential itself, and some episodes fit that reading. But the privileged trades also built unions for the unskilled and led movements of the whole class, which the simple form of the thesis cannot accommodate.

### Q: Do countries without empires have reformist labour movements?

Yes, and the fact is one of the standing difficulties for the thesis. The Scandinavian countries, with negligible colonial possessions, produced reformist labour movements of unmatched completeness in Europe, governing social democratic parties, centralised bargaining, and working class living standards at the continent's summit. Switzerland had no colonies and a model moderate movement. Germany's reformism both preceded its brief colonial empire and survived the empire's confiscation. If reformism were purchased from colonial revenue, its distribution across countries should follow the distribution of empire. It follows instead the distribution of institutions, franchises, welfare states, and productivity growth, none of which requires a colonial source.

### Q: What are the alternative explanations of reformism?

Four explanations, complementary rather than competing, do the work in the critical literature. Trade unions and parties become institutionally integrated, acquiring funds, officials, and legal standing that give them an interest in negotiation over insurrection. The extension of the franchise opens an electoral route to reform whose visible successes reproduce gradualism as a strategy. Welfare settlements allow states to purchase social peace directly from domestic taxation. And productivity growth inside national economies raises real wages and funds concessions without any external subsidy, in step with the observed long run relation between output per worker and pay. Together they explain reformism's timing, geography, and social spread, including in countries with no empires.

### Q: Who defends the thesis today?

The fullest defence in book form is Zak Cope's Divided World Divided Class, published in 2012, which restates the argument for a globalised economy. Cope argues that wage differentials between the wealthy core and the poorer periphery are far too large to be explained by productivity, that value moves from economies where wages are low to economies where wages are high, through unequal exchange and global production chains, and that the transfer underwrites the living standards and the conservative politics of the core's working classes nearly entire. The defence is discussed at full strength in the body of this article, before the objections, because it is the version any serious assessment must answer.

### Q: What political conclusion follows from the thesis?

If workers in the wealthy countries are materially bought by imperialism, they hold an interest in imperialism's continuation and cannot be counted as potential allies by the workers it exploits; strategy must then be built elsewhere, among the exploited of the periphery or among the unbought margins of the core. In Lenin's narrower version the conclusion was organisational: the bribed apparatus had to be broken so the unbribed majority could be won. Either way, the conclusion is what gives an argument about colonial accounts its heat, because it adjudicates, in advance, the question of who in the world can be trusted to change it, and it is exactly as strong as the mechanism it rests on, which the evidence leaves unsubstantiated.

### Q: Is the labour aristocracy thesis still credible?

As a conclusion, on the assessment given here, no; as a question, permanently. The mechanism it asserts fails on magnitude, on the identity of the stratum, and on cross country comparison, and reformism is better explained by institutions, the franchise, welfare, and productivity. But the phenomena that made the thesis plausible remain in place: stratified privilege inside working classes, enormous global wage differentials, and a record of northern labour movements toward the south that no adequate theory has fully explained. A reader who rejects the thesis owes an account of those phenomena, and the alternatives offered in this article are the beginning of one rather than the end of the matter.

### Q: How does the magnitude test grade the thesis?

The magnitude test grades the thesis on the proportion between the alleged bribe fund and the effect it supposedly purchased. It asks whether the value transferred from periphery to core was large enough, once it reached a stratum of metropolitan workers, to raise their pay beyond what their labour power was worth, by a margin capable of redirecting their politics. The critical literature's finding is that remitted colonial profits were a modest stream beside the total wage bill, were received by investors rather than traced into wages, and therefore fail the test. Grading by magnitude converts a rhetorical dispute into an accounting question, which is why the test, rather than any slogan, organises this article's assessment.

### Q: What should a student separate between the thesis's mechanism and its evidence?

A student should separate three things that polemical writing habitually fuses: the observation, which is that privileged strata of workers exist and that privilege shapes politics; the mechanism, which is that colonial superprofits were transferred into that stratum's wages; and the conclusion, which is that the stratum's reformism and the wider class's quietude are explained by the transfer. The observation is well evidenced. The mechanism is the thesis proper, and it is precisely where the evidence fails, since the transfer has never been traced at the required scale. The conclusion inherits the mechanism's weakness. An answer that grades each element separately demonstrates the discrimination examiners reward and avoids both credulity and dismissal.

### Q: Why did a small transfer carry such a large explanatory burden?

Because it was framed to solve the largest problem the revolutionary movement faced in its classical period: the collapse of working class internationalism in 1914, when the socialist parties supported their own governments' war. A defeat on that scale seemed to require a material cause of matching scale, and the theory of imperialism, then being constructed, offered superprofits as the one fund large enough and discreditable enough to bear the weight. The burden was fixed by the size of the event to be explained rather than by the size of the fund, and that mismatch, between an enormous explanandum and a modest explanans, is the original form of the difficulty the magnitude test later formalised.

### Q: How does the thesis assessed table order the objections?

The table orders the assessment by component rather than by chronology, taking the mechanism first, then its magnitude, then the identity of the stratum, then the cross country pattern, and finally the political conclusion. The order is deliberate. Each row withdraws a premise the later rows would need: if no transfer is traced, its size cannot save it; if the size fails, the stratum's politics cannot be its receipt; if the pattern appears without the cause, the conclusion loses its ground. Verdicts differ in kind down the rows, absence, disproportion, contradiction, missing correlation, and non sequitur, and seeing the kinds distinguished teaches more about assessing theories than any single verdict does.

### Q: What separates a bribed stratum from a merely prosperous one?

Three marks, all required together. The advantage must originate outside the domestic economy, in a flow the home product cannot explain. It must depend on that flow, so that removing the flow would remove the advantage rather than leaving prosperity intact, as Scandinavian prosperity survived the absence of empire. And the stratum's politics must be explained by the advantage, showing itself in defence of the arrangement that funds it. Prosperous workers whose pay reflects their industry's productivity and their unions' strength meet none of the marks, however comfortable they are, and comfortable workers who meet only the first mark are a subsidised enclave, not the political phenomenon the thesis was invented to explain.
