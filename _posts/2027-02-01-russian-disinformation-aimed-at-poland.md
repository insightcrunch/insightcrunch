Russian disinformation aimed at Poland is usually described as an attempt to make Poles believe things that are not true. That description is not wrong, but it is a poor guide to what the campaign is for, and following it leads defenders to fight the wrong battle. A campaign built to persuade would look different: it would be internally consistent, it would push a single coherent line, and it would be measurable by counting converts. The activity actually observed against Poland and its neighbors is none of those things. It is often self-contradictory, it frequently pushes several incompatible claims at once, and it seems indifferent to whether any individual claim survives contact with evidence. That is not a failed persuasion campaign. It is a different kind of operation with a different objective, and the objective is closer to exhaustion than to belief.

The analytical move this article asks the reader to make is to shift attention from content to technique. The specific false claims circulating at any moment are the least durable and least useful thing to study. They change constantly, they are tailored to whatever grievance is live, and cataloging them tends to spread them. The technique underneath is stable, and it is what a staffer, an official, an editor, or an attentive citizen can actually learn to see. A reader who finishes this article should be able to look at a piece of manufactured content and identify what it is trying to do, which seam it is trying to widen, and which of the three standing targets it is aimed at, without needing to know whether the particular claim is true.

What this article owns is the information instrument itself: how it works, what it aims at, why it is hard to attribute and harder to answer, and what resilience against it actually consists of.

One further constraint shapes everything below. This article does not restate the false claims it analyzes. Reproducing a manufactured narrative in order to condemn it is the oldest way of doing an information operation's distribution work for it, and it is a mistake that well-intentioned analysis makes constantly. Where a theme is discussed, it is discussed at the level of the grievance being exploited rather than the fabricated specifics, and where an example is needed, it is described structurally. This is a real analytical cost, because concrete examples are persuasive, and it is accepted deliberately.

## What Russian Disinformation Aimed at Poland Is Built to Do

Start with the objective, because everything else follows from it. A persuasion campaign tries to move a population from one belief to another. A corrosion campaign tries to reduce a population's capacity to hold any shared belief at all, and to make the effort of sorting truth from noise so tiring that large numbers of people stop trying. The second objective is far cheaper than the first, far more robust to being caught, and much better suited to an actor with limited standing in the target society.

Consider why. To persuade Poles of a specific proposition about Russian intentions, an operation would need credibility inside Poland, which is precisely what Russia lacks there. Polish public attitudes toward Russia are shaped by a long historical record that no messaging campaign will overwrite, and the series treats that record in depth in its own place. An operation aimed at persuasion in that environment is close to hopeless. An operation aimed at making Polish public debate angrier, more distrustful, and more internally divided does not require any credibility at all. It only requires that the material find its way into arguments Poles are already having and make those arguments worse. Existing division is the raw material; the operation supplies accelerant, not ideas.

This is the reasoning behind the rule this article advances, which can be called the corrosion-not-conversion rule: a hostile information campaign against a society like Poland's is not trying to produce believers, it is trying to produce a tired, suspicious, and fragmented public, and therefore its success should be measured in the condition of the society rather than in the spread of any particular claim. The rule has three practical consequences that run through the rest of this analysis. First, a campaign can fail completely at persuasion and still succeed, so counting debunked claims tells a defender almost nothing about whether they are winning. Second, mutually contradictory outputs are not sloppiness but a feature, because contradiction is itself corrosive; a public that has heard four incompatible accounts of the same event and cannot adjudicate between them has been moved toward the intended condition even if it rejects all four. Third, the correct defensive metrics are social rather than informational: the level of trust between citizens and institutions, the willingness of political opponents to accept each other as legitimate, and the durability of public support for costly commitments.

### What does Russian disinformation aimed at Poland actually target?

It targets three things: internal cohesion, meaning the willingness of Poles to regard each other as fellow citizens rather than enemies; national resolve, meaning the durability of public support for costly security commitments; and ally trust, meaning Polish confidence in partners and partner confidence in Poland. Content is the delivery mechanism, not the goal.

Each of those three targets deserves separate treatment, and they get it below, because they are attacked with different material, respond to different defenses, and fail in different ways. But it is worth noting immediately that they are not independent. A society whose internal cohesion has degraded finds it harder to sustain resolve, because sustaining a costly commitment requires a level of mutual trust that lets citizens accept a burden they did not personally choose. A society with weakened resolve becomes a less reliable partner, which degrades ally trust. And a society that doubts its allies has a much harder political argument to make at home for continued expenditure, which loops back into resolve. The three targets form a cycle, and an operation that gains ground on any one of them gains a little on the others for free. This coupling is the single most important structural fact about the campaign and the reason its designers can afford to be patient.
### Why this instrument, against this target

The choice of instrument is not arbitrary and understanding the selection logic explains a great deal about how the campaign behaves. Four properties make information work unusually well suited to pressure on Poland specifically, as distinct from pressure on any target generally.

The first is cost structure. Every other instrument in the below-threshold toolkit requires physical presence, physical risk, or specialized access. Interference with infrastructure requires someone or something on the ground. Interference with navigation requires emitters in range. Pressure at a frontier requires the cooperation of a neighboring government and the movement of actual people. Information work requires none of these. It can be conducted entirely from outside the target's territory, at a cost that is trivial relative to any defense budget, with no exposure of personnel and no logistics. For an actor whose resources are heavily committed elsewhere, an instrument with this cost profile has an obvious appeal, and the appeal grows rather than shrinks as the other commitments deepen.

The second is the absence of a response threshold. Every other instrument generates at least a theoretical decision point. A physical incident might be treated as an armed attack under sufficiently severe circumstances. Interference with civil aviation raises questions under international conventions. Frontier pressure produces diplomatic and legal consequences. Information work produces none of these, and the reason is principled rather than accidental: democracies cannot construct a threshold for hostile speech without constructing machinery they would not want to own. The absence of a threshold is therefore permanent, structural, and not fixable, which means the instrument can be run continuously at any intensity without ever generating a moment at which someone must decide something. In a competition where the defender's advantage lies in collective response, an instrument that never triggers collective response is worth a great deal.

The third is the target's own openness, which is a genuine and unavoidable asymmetry. Poland is an open society with a free press, competitive politics, and no capacity or desire to control what circulates within it. The adversary's own information space is far more controlled. Effort therefore flows in one direction far more easily than the other, and the asymmetry cannot be corrected without the open society becoming less open, which would be a defeat of a different kind. Every serious analysis of this problem eventually arrives at the same conclusion: the vulnerability is a direct consequence of the thing being defended, and the defense must therefore be built from resilience rather than from control.

The fourth is the specific structure of Poland's security position. Poland's defense rests on a combination of national capability and allied commitment, and the allied component involves political decisions taken in other countries by other electorates. That structure creates a target that a purely military instrument cannot reach: the political willingness of distant parliaments. Information work can reach it, cheaply, continuously, and without ever crossing a line. No other instrument in the toolkit can touch the willingness of a legislature in another country to approve a deployment, and that willingness is a load-bearing element of Polish security. The strategic value of the instrument, in other words, lies less in what it does inside Poland than in what it does to the political conditions surrounding Poland, which is why the ally-trust target discussed below is where the serious return lies.

Taken together these four properties describe an instrument that is cheap, unbounded, structurally unstoppable, and aimed at the one part of the defender's position that force cannot reach. It would be strange if such an instrument were not used heavily, and the correct expectation is that its use will continue at roughly current intensity regardless of what happens in any negotiation or on any battlefield, because nothing about the cost-benefit calculation depends on those outcomes. A reader planning on any horizon should treat this as a permanent condition rather than a phase.
## How the Campaign Works at the Level of Technique

Describing method without providing method is the balance this section has to strike. What follows is the general architecture of contemporary influence work as it appears in the open record and in published research from academic and think-tank communities that study the field. It is deliberately pitched at the level a defender needs in order to recognize the pattern, and it omits anything that would function as instruction.

### Narrative laundering: the movement from origin to legitimacy

The central problem for any hostile information operation is that material carrying a visible hostile origin is discounted immediately. Nobody in Poland is moved by a claim that arrives with an obvious Russian state stamp on it. The solution, and the technique that most repays study, is what researchers call narrative laundering: the process by which a claim is separated from its origin and progressively re-presented in more credible packaging until it arrives in mainstream discussion carrying no visible provenance at all.

The structure of laundering is a chain of intermediate stops, each of which strips a little more of the original fingerprint. A claim may first appear somewhere obscure and low-stakes, a venue nobody checks and nobody cites. It is then referenced by a slightly more visible outlet, which cites the obscure venue rather than the origin. That reference is itself referenced further up the chain. At each step the claim gains an apparent source and loses its actual one, and by the time it reaches a venue with real reach, it can be honestly described by everyone handling it as something that was already circulating. The final step is the most valuable and the one that requires no participation from the operation at all: a domestic figure with genuine standing, arguing in good faith about a genuine domestic dispute, picks the claim up because it happens to support a point they sincerely want to make. At that moment the laundering is complete. The claim is now a domestic claim, made by a domestic voice, inside a domestic argument, and any attempt to trace it back sounds like an accusation against a fellow citizen.

That last property is what makes laundering so difficult to counter and so dangerous to counter badly. The people carrying a laundered claim at the point where it does the most damage are usually not agents of anything. They are participants in a real argument who found a piece of ammunition lying on the ground. Treating them as instruments of a foreign state is both usually false and precisely the response the operation would choose if it could choose, because it converts an information problem into a domestic political grievance and hands the campaign a fresh seam to work. A defender who cannot distinguish between the laundering chain and the citizen at the end of it will reliably make the situation worse.

### Inauthentic amplification: manufacturing the appearance of consensus

The second technique is amplification, and its purpose is misunderstood almost as often as the campaign's overall objective. Amplification is not primarily about reaching more people. It is about manufacturing a false impression of how many people already hold a view, which exploits a well-documented feature of how humans calibrate their own opinions.

People are strongly influenced by their perception of what others think, particularly on questions where they hold no strong prior view and lack the expertise to adjudicate directly. Most citizens, on most security questions, are in exactly that position, and reasonably so. Faced with a technical dispute they cannot resolve, a rational person looks at the apparent distribution of opinion and treats it as evidence. If a view appears to be held by many people, that is weak evidence it has something going for it. If a view appears marginal, that is weak evidence against it. This heuristic is sensible in an environment where the apparent distribution of opinion is real. Inauthentic amplification attacks the heuristic directly by making the apparent distribution unreal.

The effect a defender should watch for is therefore not volume but the shape of a conversation. A genuine surge of public opinion has characteristic texture: it arrives unevenly, it is expressed in wildly varying language, it comes from accounts with long and unrelated histories, it includes internal disagreement about details, and it decays at an irregular rate. Manufactured activity tends to be smoother in every one of those dimensions. It is more uniform in phrasing, more synchronized in timing, more monotonic in sentiment, and it originates disproportionately from participants with thin or recently constructed histories. None of these tells is decisive alone, and every one of them can be produced innocently, which is why recognition is a matter of accumulated weak signals rather than a single test.

There is a second and more sophisticated use of amplification worth naming, because it is invisible to anyone watching only for the promotion of falsehoods. An operation can achieve a great deal simply by amplifying true material selectively. A society always contains genuine failures, real injustices, and legitimate grievances, and reporting on them is the ordinary work of a free press. An operation that does nothing but ensure the true bad news travels far and fast, while true good news does not, produces a distorted picture of national life without ever making a false statement. This is the hardest variant to counter, since there is nothing to debunk and any attempt to suppress it is straightforwardly illegitimate, and it is the clearest illustration of why fact-checking is a necessary but badly incomplete defense.
### Tempo, saturation, and the exploitation of scarce attention

A third element of technique is temporal rather than structural. Influence work exploits the fact that attention is the scarcest resource in any public sphere and that the cost of producing a claim is a tiny fraction of the cost of examining one. A single fabricated assertion can be generated in seconds; establishing that it is false may require days of work by people with relevant expertise, and the correction will reach a fraction of the audience the original reached. The asymmetry is structural and cannot be engineered away.

What this asymmetry buys an operation is the ability to set the agenda of a national conversation without winning any argument within it. If enough manufactured material is in circulation, the serious institutions of a society, its journalists, its analysts, its officials, spend a growing share of their finite capacity responding rather than doing their own work. Every hour spent establishing that something did not happen is an hour not spent on something that did. Over a long enough period this constitutes a genuine tax on a society's capacity to think about anything, and the tax falls hardest on exactly the institutions a country needs functioning well during a crisis.

Saturation also produces a second-order effect that matters more than the first. A public repeatedly exposed to claims that turn out to be manufactured does not become more discerning in any reliable way. A substantial fraction becomes generally distrustful instead, applying to legitimate reporting the same suspicion earned by fabrication. That outcome is better for a hostile operation than any specific belief it could install, because a public that trusts nothing cannot be rallied, cannot be warned credibly, and cannot easily distinguish an actual emergency from another round of noise. The warning implications of that condition are serious enough that they connect directly to the problem of reading a genuine crisis signal, which the series treats in its warning cluster.

## The Three Targets in Detail

### Target one: internal cohesion

Cohesion is the first and most heavily worked target, and the reason is straightforward. Every society contains real internal divisions, and Poland is no exception. It has active disagreements about the proper relationship between the state and the church, about the direction and pace of European integration, about the balance between central authority and judicial independence, about regional economic disparity, about the terms on which large numbers of Ukrainian refugees have been hosted, and about the distributional consequences of a defense buildup. These are legitimate arguments among citizens with different values and different interests. They are the ordinary substance of democratic politics, and a healthy polity has them.

An operation working the cohesion target does not create these disputes and does not need to. It identifies the existing fault lines and works to make each argument nastier, less resolvable, and more identity-defining. The specific mechanism is worth naming because it is what a reader can learn to see: the operation pushes each dispute away from the question of what should be done and toward the question of who the other side is. An argument about the right level of defense spending is tractable, because it concerns tradeoffs, evidence, and priorities, and people who disagree about it can still cooperate on other things. An argument about whether people who favor a different level of defense spending are traitors, dupes, or agents is not tractable at all, and it contaminates every other question the two groups must resolve together. Converting the first kind of argument into the second is the core cohesion operation, and it can be accomplished with material that contains no factual claims whatsoever.

The consequence a defender should care about is not what any Pole believes about any policy question but whether Poles who disagree about policy still regard each other as legitimate participants in a shared enterprise. That is a measurable social condition, and it is the correct dependent variable for judging whether the cohesion attack is working.

### Target two: national resolve

Resolve is the willingness of a population to keep paying a cost that has no visible endpoint. It is a distinct target from cohesion, and it is attacked with distinct material.

Poland has taken on genuine and substantial burdens since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine: a large and sustained increase in defense expenditure, a major reorganization and expansion of its armed forces, the hosting of a very large displaced population, and the role of the principal logistical corridor for support flowing eastward. The series examines the fiscal dimension of that commitment in its treatment of whether Poland can afford its defense spending, and the logistical dimension in its work on Poland as Ukraine's lifeline. Each of those burdens is real, each has real costs borne by real people, and each generates entirely legitimate domestic debate about whether the burden is proportionate and sustainable.

The resolve operation works on the gap between cost and visible return. Its material tends to cluster around a small number of enduring themes: that the sacrifice is unappreciated by those who benefit from it, that the burden is distributed unfairly within Poland, that partners are not carrying a comparable share, that the effort is futile because the outcome is already determined, and that the country's own interests are being subordinated to someone else's. What makes these themes effective is that each contains a legitimate question that reasonable Poles genuinely ask. Burden distribution is a real issue. Allied burden-sharing is a real issue, and a contested one within NATO. The sustainability of open-ended commitments is a real issue. The operation does not invent these questions; it works to ensure they are always in the air, always framed in the most demoralizing available terms, and never allowed to reach a settled answer.

Fatigue is the intended product, and fatigue is a slow variable. It does not show up in an opinion poll as a sudden collapse of support. It shows up as a gradual increase in the political cost of maintaining a commitment, a growing preference for candidates who promise relief from the burden, and a shortening of the time horizon over which the public is willing to plan. Those changes are slow enough to be invisible month to month, which is exactly why a campaign built around them can afford to be patient and why measuring its progress requires looking at trends over years rather than reactions to events.

### Target three: ally trust

The third target is the most strategically valuable and the least discussed. It runs in both directions, and a defender who watches only one direction will miss half the operation.

Outbound, the effort works to reduce Polish confidence in partners. The argument it seeks to keep alive is that Poland will ultimately be left alone, that commitments made in peacetime will not be honored under fire, that the alliance's political will is thinner than its treaty text implies, and that historical experience of abandonment is the better guide to the future than any current declaration. Every one of those propositions touches something real. Alliance commitments are political as well as legal, the question of whether they would hold under pressure is genuinely contested among serious analysts, and Poland's historical experience gives the anxiety a foundation no amount of reassurance fully erases. The series addresses the substance of that question directly in its analysis of [the weak links in NATO cohesion](/2026/01/01/the-weak-links-in-nato-cohesion/), which is where the reader should go for the actual assessment rather than the manipulation of it.

Inbound, and less noticed, the effort works to reduce partner confidence in Poland. Material aimed at audiences in Western Europe and North America tends to characterize Poland as excessively hard-line, as a source of escalation risk rather than a bulwark against it, as domestically unstable, or as a difficult partner whose commitments cannot be relied upon. The purpose is to make the political argument for reinforcing Poland marginally harder to win in capitals whose parliaments must approve it. This inbound stream is easy for Polish observers to miss entirely, because it is not addressed to them and does not appear in their information space, and it is why serious monitoring of the campaign has to look at what is being said about Poland elsewhere and not only at what is being said inside Poland.

The reason this target is the most valuable is arithmetic. Poland's security rests on a combination of national capability and allied commitment, and the allied component is the larger of the two in any serious contingency. Degrading Polish confidence in the alliance while simultaneously degrading allied confidence in Poland attacks the linkage on which the whole structure depends, and it does so at a cost of essentially nothing. No other line of effort available below the threshold of armed force offers a comparable return.

## The Disinformation Recognition Grid

The framework below is the article's findable artifact. It is built for recognition rather than rebuttal, which means it is organized around what an operation is doing rather than what it is claiming, and it is designed to remain usable when the specific content has changed completely. A reader who has internalized the grid should be able to place an unfamiliar piece of manufactured material within it in under a minute, without knowing anything about whether its factual assertions are true.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Internal cohesion | Willingness of Poles to treat each other as legitimate fellow citizens | Identity conversion: recasting a policy dispute as a question of who the other side really is | Material that supplies no argument but assigns motive; sudden identity framing on a previously technical question; pushing several incompatible grievances at once | Cross-partisan trust levels; whether opponents accept each other as legitimate; institutional confidence |

It pairs each target with a metric that is social rather than informational, which is the operational form of the corrosion-not-conversion rule. And its rightmost column is the part a defender should actually track, because it is the only column that answers the question of whether the campaign is succeeding.

## The Attribution Problem

Attribution is where information operations become genuinely difficult rather than merely annoying, and it is the reason this instrument sits so comfortably in the gray zone. A state can be reasonably confident that an operation is hostile in origin and still be unable to say so in a way that survives adversarial scrutiny, which is precisely the position the technique is designed to create.

### How hard is it to attribute an information operation?

Harder than technical attribution of a cyber intrusion and much harder than attribution of a physical act. Content leaves fewer forensic traces than code, intermediaries can be genuine volunteers rather than assets, and the final carriers are often sincere domestic participants. Confident attribution usually rests on accumulated behavioral patterns rather than any single piece of evidence.

Attribution in this domain rests on three distinct kinds of evidence, and the differences between them matter for what a government can responsibly claim in public.

Technical evidence concerns infrastructure: registration patterns, shared hosting, account creation behavior, timing signatures, and the operational overlaps that occur when the same people run multiple efforts. It is the most concrete category and the most rapidly degraded, because the tradecraft that produces these overlaps is exactly what a competent operator learns to avoid. Technical evidence is also the category most likely to be classified, which means the strongest case a government holds is frequently the case it cannot show.

Behavioral evidence concerns pattern: whether a set of accounts posts in synchrony, whether a narrative appears simultaneously across venues with no plausible common source, whether activity tracks the working hours of a particular time zone, whether the emphasis of a campaign shifts in step with a state's diplomatic positioning. Behavioral evidence is more durable than technical evidence but weaker per unit, and its interpretation requires judgment that reasonable analysts can dispute.

Contextual evidence concerns benefit and coherence: whether the material consistently advances one state's interests, whether it aligns with that state's declared positions, whether it appears at moments that serve that state's diplomacy. Contextual evidence is the weakest kind and the easiest to abuse, because a great many things happen to align with a great many interests. An analyst who reasons only from benefit will find hostile operations everywhere, including in ordinary domestic politics, which is a failure mode with serious costs of its own.

Responsible attribution combines all three and states its confidence honestly. What it must not do is treat contextual alignment as sufficient. The claim that a piece of content serves Russian interests is often true and almost never enough, because a domestic political argument that happens to serve a foreign interest is still a domestic political argument and its participants are still citizens exercising a right.

### The deniability dividend

The structural asymmetry here is what makes the instrument attractive, and it is worth stating plainly. An operation's sponsor gains from ambiguity in two separate ways, and only one of them is obvious.

The obvious gain is escape from consequence. If attribution cannot be established to a standard that would justify a response, no response follows, and the activity continues at no cost. This is the ordinary logic of below-threshold action and it applies to sabotage and interference as much as to information work.

The less obvious gain is that the defender's uncertainty is itself corrosive. A society that knows it is being targeted but cannot reliably identify which specific arguments are manufactured develops a suspicion that contaminates its own internal debate. Citizens begin to wonder whether opponents are dupes. Officials begin to wonder whether a critical press story has been shaped. Journalists begin to wonder whether a source is authentic. Every one of those doubts is individually reasonable and collectively destructive, and the operation gets all of it for free. This is why the tactic of loudly announcing that one is under information attack, without the specificity to make the announcement actionable, is so frequently counterproductive: it generalizes suspicion without directing it, which is the outcome the operation would choose.

This second dividend explains something that otherwise looks like carelessness. Operations are frequently run in ways that make their origin fairly easy to guess. That is not always failure. An operation that is plausibly attributable but not provably so extracts maximum value from both dividends at once: it escapes consequence while ensuring the target knows it is being worked on. The gray zone rewards exactly this calibration, and the wider logic of that calibration across all the instruments in the toolkit is the subject of the cluster pillar.

## The Response Problem

Attribution difficulty creates the second problem, which is that every available response has a serious cost, and the cheapest-looking responses are frequently the most expensive.

### The amplification paradox

The central dilemma is easy to state and hard to escape. Most manufactured content reaches very few people on its own. The single most effective distribution mechanism available to it is a prominent debunking, which introduces the claim to an audience that would never otherwise have encountered it, attaches to it the interest that attends anything officially denied, and gives it the shape of a live controversy rather than a piece of noise.

The research literature on correction is genuinely mixed and should be represented as such rather than flattened into a slogan. Corrections do work in many settings, particularly when they are timely, when they come from a source the recipient already trusts, and when they supply a coherent alternative account rather than a bare negation. What is much less certain is whether correction is net positive when the corrected claim had negligible reach beforehand and the correction gives it substantial reach. The honest position is that the answer depends on distribution, and that a defender must estimate reach before responding rather than treating response as automatically virtuous.

The practical implication is a triage discipline rather than a rule. Material with negligible reach and no institutional carrier is usually best left alone; the response costs more than the material. Material that has crossed into mainstream circulation, or that touches directly on public safety, or that is being carried by figures with real standing, generally must be answered, and answered quickly, because the cost of leaving it unaddressed exceeds the amplification cost. Between those poles sits a large ambiguous middle where judgment is required and where reasonable officials will disagree. Anyone who claims this problem has a clean solution has not thought about it carefully.

### Rebut, expose, or build

Beyond triage, a state has three broad response postures, and the sensible approach uses all three for different purposes rather than choosing one.

Rebuttal addresses the claim. It is the fastest posture, the most intuitive, and the most limited, because it operates at the level of content and content is the layer the campaign treats as disposable. Rebuttal is essential for claims touching safety, where a false belief could get someone hurt, and for claims that have already achieved wide circulation. It is close to useless as a general strategy, because the supply of claims is effectively unlimited and rebuttal is always reactive.

Exposure addresses the technique. It consists of describing publicly how the operation works, what it is aimed at, and what its tells are, without dignifying any particular claim. Exposure is slower to take effect and much more durable, because a public that understands narrative laundering is somewhat inoculated against every future laundered claim rather than one. It is also the posture most compatible with a free society, since it adds information rather than contesting speech. The main risk of exposure is that it can shade into the generalized suspicion described above if it is done without specificity, and it can be politically abused if any government finds it convenient to characterize domestic criticism as foreign-shaped.

Building addresses the target. It consists of strengthening the social conditions the campaign is trying to degrade: institutional trustworthiness, the quality of domestic media, the health of cross-partisan relations, and the credibility of official communication in a crisis. It is the slowest posture by far, the least satisfying, and the only one that operates on the dependent variable that actually matters under the corrosion-not-conversion rule. A state that does only rebuttal is fighting the campaign at the layer the campaign least cares about. The resilience logic here runs closely parallel to the physical-security case, which the series develops in its treatment of [building resilience to sabotage](/2027/03/11/building-resilience-to-sabotage/), and the two problems share the same central insight: the defender's job is to reduce the payoff of the whole class of action rather than to intercept each instance of it.

### The credibility ledger

One further asymmetry governs every response decision, and it is the most easily squandered asset a state has in this contest.

A government's ability to be believed in an emergency is a finite stock built slowly and depleted quickly. Every official statement that is later shown to be wrong, overstated, or politically convenient draws down the stock. Every accurate, timely, and self-correcting statement adds to it slowly. The reason this matters more than it appears is that the stock is only really spent once, in a crisis, when a population must decide within hours whether to believe an official warning.

A hostile operation does not need to defeat official communication in ordinary times. It only needs the stock to be low enough at the moment it matters. This gives a defending state a counterintuitive obligation: the most important information-defense measure available is scrupulous accuracy in official communication about matters that have nothing to do with the campaign, because that is where the stock is accumulated. It also means that using the accusation of foreign manipulation as a domestic political instrument is not merely unfair but strategically self-defeating, since it converts the most valuable defensive asset into a partisan claim and spends it on nothing.
## Escalation Risk: Where Information Pressure Meets the Threshold

Information operations are usually treated as the least escalatory instrument in the gray-zone toolkit, and in the ordinary case that is correct. Nobody is injured, nothing is destroyed, and no state has ever treated hostile messaging as an armed attack. But the low-escalation reading is incomplete in three specific ways, each of which matters for how a defender should think about the instrument's place in a wider crisis.

The first is the enabling function. Information work is rarely the point of a gray-zone campaign; it is the medium in which the other instruments become more effective. A physical incident of ambiguous origin is far more disruptive if a prepared information environment already contains competing explanations for it. Interference with navigation or with critical services produces more political effect if the public is primed to blame domestic incompetence. The information layer does not escalate on its own, but it multiplies whatever else is happening, and any assessment that scores it in isolation will underrate it. This coupling is why the cluster pillar treats the instruments as a single campaign rather than a list.

The second is the crisis-window problem, which is the most serious escalation concern and the least appreciated. In ordinary times, the cost of a degraded information environment is a slow tax. In the compressed hours of an actual crisis, it becomes something else entirely. A crisis requires a population to receive, believe, and act on official instruction very quickly. It requires allied governments to converge on a shared factual account of what has occurred, quickly enough to decide together. It requires decision-makers to distinguish a genuine emergency from a manufactured one under time pressure and with incomplete information. Every one of those requirements is directly degraded by a successfully corroded information environment, and the degradation is not proportional but sharply nonlinear: a society that muddles through peacetime confusion adequately may fail badly when the same confusion arrives with a four-hour decision window attached. The consequences of that failure for warning and response run into the question of where accumulated pressure crosses into open conflict, which the series treats in its analysis of [when gray-zone activity becomes open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/).

The third is the threshold ambiguity that the instrument itself creates. Because information operations are not armed attacks and are not treated as such, there is no established point at which their intensity triggers a collective response. That is mostly a good thing, since a lower threshold would create enormous scope for abuse and would put alliances in the position of adjudicating speech. But it does mean the instrument can be run continuously and at any intensity without generating a decision point, which is the definition of an ideal gray-zone tool and a genuine structural problem for a defender who would prefer that sustained hostile pressure produce some consequence somewhere.

None of this justifies treating hostile messaging as equivalent to force, and the temptation to do so should be resisted for reasons that are practical as well as principled. A state that characterizes information activity as attack invites reciprocal characterization of its own diplomacy and public communication, sets a precedent that authoritarian governments find far more useful than democratic ones, and puts itself in the position of policing the boundary between manipulation and speech under political pressure. The correct position is that information pressure is a real instrument of coercion, that it is not force, that it should be answered with resilience and exposure rather than with the machinery reserved for armed attack, and that its most dangerous property is what it does to a society's capacity to handle the instruments that are force.

## Does It Actually Work? The Effect Debate

This is the genuinely contested question in the field, and it deserves an honest presentation of both serious positions rather than the assertion that usually stands in for one.

### Does Russian disinformation aimed at Poland actually work?

Serious analysts disagree. The corrosive school holds that cumulative effects on trust and cohesion are real and measurable in aggregate even when individual claims fail. The limited-effects school holds that measured persuasion effects are small, that campaigns mostly reach the already convinced, and that attributing social division to foreign action overstates external influence.

The limited-effects case is stronger than defenders usually acknowledge and rests on a substantial body of political-science research. Decades of work on media effects have consistently found that direct persuasion is hard, that people are considerably more resistant to messaging than intuition suggests, and that measured effects from exposure to political content are typically small. Studies of the reach of foreign influence content have repeatedly found that it constitutes a minute fraction of the total information diet even in heavily targeted populations, and that the fraction which does reach people lands disproportionately on those who already hold congenial views. On this reading, foreign operations are largely an expensive way of preaching to a small choir, and the tendency to credit them with major social effects reflects a comforting externalization: it is easier for a society to attribute its polarization to a foreign adversary than to acknowledge domestic causes.

That last point deserves particular weight because it is the strongest thing the limited-effects school has to say. Polarization, institutional distrust, and political fragmentation are occurring across many democracies, including ones that receive little targeted attention, and they have well-documented endogenous drivers: changes in media economics, the incentive structures of engagement-optimized platforms, economic dislocation, and the ordinary dynamics of partisan competition. A hostile operation that adds to these forces at the margin is a different thing from one that causes them, and conflating the two produces bad analysis and worse policy.

The corrosive school does not, in its serious form, dispute the persuasion research. It argues instead that persuasion is the wrong dependent variable, which is the same argument this article's central rule makes. If the objective is not to change beliefs but to degrade trust, raise the temperature of existing disputes, and exhaust the public's appetite for adjudication, then measuring persuasion effects and finding them small does not establish that the operation is failing. It establishes that the operation is not doing the thing it was not trying to do. The corrosive case points to effects that are real but diffuse: the marginal shifting of what counts as sayable, the incremental raising of the cost of maintaining a costly commitment, the slow conversion of policy disputes into identity disputes, and the erosion of the shared factual baseline that a crisis requires.

The honest position between these schools has three parts. First, the limited-effects school is almost certainly right that direct persuasion effects are small and that operations are frequently credited with more than they achieve. Second, the corrosive school is almost certainly right that persuasion is the wrong metric and that the cumulative, indirect, and enabling effects are underweighted by research designs built to measure persuasion. Third, and least comfortable, the effect size is genuinely not known with precision, because the relevant outcome variables are social and slow-moving, the counterfactual is unobservable, and the causal identification problem is severe. A defender should act on the assumption that the effect is real and modest, should resist both the claim that the campaign is decisive and the claim that it is negligible, and should be honest that the resulting posture is a hedge under uncertainty rather than a conclusion from evidence.

What would change the judgment is worth naming, since a judgment that nothing could change is not a judgment. Evidence of successful crisis-moment disruption, where manufactured content demonstrably degraded a population's response to a genuine emergency, would substantially strengthen the corrosive case. Evidence that measured social trust and cohesion indicators in heavily targeted societies diverge systematically from comparable untargeted societies, controlling for the domestic drivers, would do the same. Conversely, a sustained finding that targeted and untargeted democracies degrade at indistinguishable rates would strengthen the limited-effects case considerably.

## Keeping Genuine Debate Distinct From Manipulation

This is the hardest problem in the whole subject and the one where a defender is most likely to inflict serious self-harm. It deserves to be treated as a first-order analytical challenge rather than a caveat.

The difficulty is structural. An operation working the cohesion and resolve targets does so by amplifying grievances that are already present and often legitimate. It follows that a great deal of manufactured material is indistinguishable in content from sincere domestic argument, because the content is sincere domestic argument, borrowed and pushed. Any test that identifies manipulation by content will therefore catch enormous numbers of citizens making arguments they actually believe, and the political consequences of that error are severe: it delegitimizes real dissent, it hands the operation a genuine grievance to work, and it corrodes exactly the institutional trust the defense is supposed to protect.

The workable distinction is not content but conduct, and it rests on three questions that concern how material behaves rather than what it says. Is the apparent origin of the material what it claims to be, or is authorship, funding, or nationality concealed? Is the apparent scale of support real, or is it manufactured through coordinated inauthentic activity? Is the material presented as what it is, or is it disguised as something else, such as local reporting, organic commentary, or independent research? These are questions about deception in the mechanics of participation, and they can in principle be answered without any judgment about whether the underlying view is correct.

That distinction has an important implication that officials frequently resist. A citizen who sincerely holds a view that a hostile state also promotes is not participating in an operation, is not a dupe, and is entitled to argue their case without having their nationality of opinion questioned. This is true even when the view is, in the defender's judgment, badly mistaken. The correct response to a mistaken domestic argument is a better domestic argument, not an accusation of foreign origin. A society that loses this distinction converts an external problem into an internal one, which is the operation's stated objective, and it does so more efficiently than the operation could manage on its own.

There is also a self-interested reason for democracies to hold this line rigorously. The tools built to identify and suppress foreign manipulation are, in their mechanics, tools for identifying and suppressing speech. Whatever authority a government acquires for the first purpose is available for the second, under a future government with different intentions. The strongest safeguard is to keep the operative test firmly on deceptive conduct, where it can be applied without reference to viewpoint, and to accept the resulting under-inclusiveness as the price of a defense that does not become the thing it defends against.
## The Domestic Politics of the Response

There is a dimension of this problem that analysis frequently omits because it is politically awkward, and omitting it produces recommendations that will not survive contact with any real government. The machinery a state builds to defend against foreign manipulation is built by an incumbent administration, staffed under its authority, and operated in an environment where the incumbent has an interest in how it is used. That is not an accusation against any particular government; it is a structural feature of the problem that applies wherever the machinery is built, and it must be designed around rather than wished away.

The risk takes three forms, each with a different remedy. The first is scope creep, in which a body established to identify foreign-origin deception gradually extends its attention to domestic content that officials consider harmful. The extension is usually made in good faith, since the boundary is genuinely blurry and the harms on the domestic side are often real, and it is nonetheless the point at which a defensive institution becomes a domestic political one. The remedy is a scope defined by conduct, meaning concealed origin, manufactured support, and disguised presentation, written into the mandate rather than left to judgment, together with a standing prohibition on any assessment that turns on viewpoint.

The second is selective attention, in which the machinery works diligently on manipulation that happens to target the incumbent's positions and less diligently on manipulation that happens to assist them. This can occur without anyone intending it, simply because officials notice what damages them more readily than what helps them. The remedy is institutional distance: analysis conducted by bodies with tenure protection and publication independence, and a requirement that findings be published on the same terms regardless of whom they inconvenience.

The third is the accusation as instrument, in which the vocabulary of foreign manipulation enters ordinary political argument and is used to characterize domestic opponents. This is the most damaging of the three because it requires no institution at all, only the willingness of prominent voices to reach for it, and because it delivers the operation's objective directly. A political culture in which the charge of foreign direction is a normal move in domestic debate has arrived at the intended condition without any external assistance, and it will be difficult to reverse, since the charge is nearly impossible to disprove and therefore very attractive to whoever is losing an argument.

The reason this matters for the security assessment rather than only for the domestic politics is that the credibility ledger is shared. There is one national stock of official believability, not one per government, and it is spent by whoever depletes it. An administration that uses the manipulation vocabulary against domestic opponents reduces the credibility that a future administration will need in an actual emergency, and reduces it for everyone. Treating that stock as a national asset held in trust rather than a resource available to the government of the day is the single discipline that most determines whether the defense remains a defense.

The evenhanded statement of the problem is worth making explicitly, since this is territory where analysis easily becomes partisan. Governments of every political orientation face the same incentive and have yielded to it in various countries. The correct posture for an analyst is to specify the structural safeguards and to hold every administration to them identically, rather than to treat the risk as a property of one side of a political argument. A safeguard that applies only to opponents is not a safeguard.

## The Seams a Campaign Works, and How a Defender Audits Them

If the operation's raw material is existing division, then a defender's most useful analytical exercise is not to catalog hostile content but to audit its own society for exposed seams. This is an uncomfortable exercise, because every seam identified is a genuine internal problem rather than an external one, and the audit will produce a list of things the country ought to fix for its own reasons. That is the point. A seam that has been repaired is no longer available to be worked, and repairing it requires no cooperation from the adversary and no attribution certainty.

A seam becomes exploitable when four conditions coincide, and the four conditions form a usable test. The dispute must be real, because manufactured grievances have no traction. It must be unresolved, because a settled question offers no purchase. It must map onto identity rather than only onto interest, because identity disputes generalize and interest disputes stay local. And it must involve a plausible accusation of bad faith, because bad faith is what converts disagreement into contempt. A dispute that meets all four is a working seam. A dispute that meets two or three is a candidate that hostile effort will try to push over the line.

Applying that test to Poland produces a short list that any honest observer would produce independently. The generational and geographic divergence in political outlook is real, unresolved, and increasingly identity-coded. The distributional argument about who bears the cost of a large defense buildup is real and unresolved, though still mostly framed as interest rather than identity, which is why it is a candidate rather than a fully open seam. The questions surrounding the hosting of a very large displaced population involve genuine local pressures on housing, services, and labor markets, and they carry an identity dimension that makes them one of the most exposed areas in the whole set, which is precisely why they demand the most careful handling and the least inflammatory framing from everyone involved. Disagreements over the shape of the relationship with European institutions are real, unresolved, and heavily identity-coded. Arguments about the proper role of the state in matters of conscience are the most identity-coded of all, though their connection to security policy is indirect enough that they serve mainly as a general-purpose accelerant rather than a targeted line of effort.

The value of the audit is that it reorders the defensive priority list. A content-focused defender spends resources on monitoring and rebuttal, which addresses the accelerant. A seam-focused defender spends resources on the conditions that make the accelerant effective, which is a much smaller and more tractable list, and which happens to consist entirely of things worth doing anyway. The single highest-value defensive act available to Polish political leaders of any persuasion is to conduct their real disagreements in terms of interest and tradeoff rather than in terms of who the other side secretly is. That is not a communications strategy and it cannot be delivered by an agency. It is a norm, and norms are maintained by the people who benefit from them declining to defect first.

### How does the campaign change during a crisis?

It shifts from slow corrosion to fast disruption. Ambient themes give way to time-critical injections: contradictory accounts of a developing event, false claims about official instructions, and manufactured confusion about what has happened and who is responsible, all timed to the window in which decisions must be made.

That shift is the most operationally important thing to anticipate, because the defenses that suit the ambient campaign are almost useless against the crisis version. Slow institutional trust-building, media literacy programs, and technique exposure all work on a timescale of years. A crisis injection works on a timescale of hours and is aimed at a single decision point. The relevant defense is the credibility ledger described earlier, plus the specific institutional plumbing that lets a government reach its population fast and be believed, plus pre-established channels through which allied capitals can converge on a shared factual account without waiting for public reporting to settle.

There is a second crisis-mode shift worth naming. In ordinary times the campaign has an interest in staying deniable and diffuse. In a crisis, the incentive changes: sowing acute confusion may be worth a substantial loss of deniability, because the payoff is concentrated and immediate rather than spread over years. A defender should therefore expect that the crisis version of the campaign will be cruder, more obviously hostile, and easier to attribute than anything seen in peacetime, and should not be surprised when activity that was carefully laundered for years becomes suddenly and clumsily obvious. The change in tradecraft quality is itself a signal, and a fairly reliable one, that the actor has decided the situation has moved past the point where deniability is the priority.
## The Durable Themes and Why They Persist

Specific claims are disposable, but the thematic terrain is remarkably stable, and describing it at the level of theme rather than claim is both safe and useful. A theme is the grievance being exploited; a claim is the disposable fabrication attached to it. Naming the themes lets a reader anticipate where effort will be applied without spreading anything.

Five themes recur across essentially every documented influence effort directed at Poland and its neighbors, and each maps to one of the targets in the grid.

The abandonment theme works the outbound ally-trust target and holds that Poland will ultimately be left to face its position alone. Its durability comes from the fact that it engages a genuine historical experience and a genuinely contested analytical question, which means it can never be conclusively refuted and can be reactivated by any ambiguous allied statement.

The futility theme works the resolve target and holds that costs being borne now will produce no result, that the outcome is already determined, and that continuing is therefore waste. Its durability comes from the structure of long confrontations, in which the absence of visible progress is the normal condition rather than evidence of anything, and from the fact that the counterfactual, what would have happened without the effort, is unobservable by construction.

The unfairness theme works both cohesion and resolve and holds that the burden falls on some Poles and not others, or on Poland and not on partners. Its durability comes from the fact that burdens genuinely are distributed unevenly, as all burdens are, and that burden-sharing genuinely is a live and unresolved argument inside the alliance rather than a fabricated one.

The instability theme works the inbound ally-trust target and characterizes Poland to foreign audiences as unpredictable, internally chaotic, or escalatory. Its durability comes from the ordinary noisiness of democratic politics, which supplies a continuous stream of material that can be presented as dysfunction to an audience unfamiliar with the context.

The subordination theme works cohesion and resolve together and holds that Polish interests are being subordinated to someone else's, whether an ally's, an institution's, or a neighbor's. Its durability comes from the fact that alliance membership genuinely does involve accepting constraints, which is the price of the benefits, and that reasonable people genuinely do disagree about whether particular constraints are worth their price.

The pattern across all five is the same and it is the article's central lesson in compressed form. Each theme is anchored to something real, unresolved, and legitimately contested. None of them could be sustained by fabrication alone. This is why the campaign is durable, why it does not require creativity, and why it survives the debunking of every specific claim attached to it. It is also why the defense cannot be built at the level of claims: the themes will still be there afterward, because the underlying questions are real questions that a free society is entitled to keep arguing about.

The corollary is worth stating for anyone tempted by the opposite conclusion. The correct response to a theme anchored in a legitimate question is not to suppress the question. It is to answer it well, publicly, and repeatedly, and to accept that a question which cannot be conclusively answered will remain a seam and must simply be managed. A government that treats the abandonment question as illegitimate to raise, rather than as a serious question deserving a serious answer, converts an argument it could partly win into a grievance it cannot.
## Resilience: What Actually Defends a Society

The corrosion-not-conversion rule implies a specific and somewhat unwelcome answer to the resilience question. If the campaign's objective is a distrustful, fragmented, exhausted public, then resilience is the set of conditions that make a public trusting, cohesive, and durable, and almost none of those conditions are produced by anything that could reasonably be called counter-disinformation work.

### Why fact-checking is necessary and badly insufficient

Fact-checking deserves defense before it is criticized, because the criticism of it has become fashionable and is frequently overstated. Establishing what is true is the foundation of every other defensive measure. A society that stops doing it has no baseline from which to identify manipulation at all, and the institutions that do this work carefully are a genuine national asset. Nothing below argues for less of it.

The limitation is one of scope rather than quality. Verification operates on claims, and claims are the layer of the campaign that is cheapest to produce and least important to its objective. Four specific gaps follow. Verification cannot address selective amplification of true material, which contains nothing to correct. It cannot address the identity conversion of policy disputes, which involves no factual assertion. It cannot address fatigue cultivation, since the questions being kept open are often genuine questions. And it cannot outrun the production asymmetry, since the supply of claims is effectively unbounded while verification capacity is not.

The right way to think about the relationship is that verification is the defense's sensor and evidence base rather than its main effort. It tells a society what is circulating, it supplies the raw material for technique exposure, and it handles the subset of claims where a factual correction genuinely matters, which is the safety-critical and widely circulated subset. Treating it as the whole of the defense is what produces the familiar and demoralizing experience of a country that debunks diligently, accurately, and continuously while its public trust indicators decline anyway.

### The four components of a resilient information environment

Genuine resilience has four components, and they are ordered here by how much they contribute relative to how much attention they usually receive.

The first is institutional trustworthiness, which is not the same as institutional trust and cannot be produced by communications work. Institutions become believable by being accurate, by acknowledging error quickly, by being seen to act on the same rules regardless of who is affected, and by declining to shade communication for political advantage. Every departure from those standards is a deposit into the adversary's account, made by the defender, at no cost to the adversary. The single most consequential thing a government can do to reduce its vulnerability to hostile information pressure is to be scrupulously reliable about things that have nothing to do with hostile information pressure.

The second is a healthy domestic media ecology, which is largely an economic question rather than an editorial one. Manipulation flourishes in the space left by the collapse of local reporting, because a community with no independent account of its own affairs has no way to check a claim about itself and no habit of expecting one. The relevant policy levers here are unglamorous and mostly financial, and they sit uncomfortably close to questions of state involvement in media that democracies handle badly, which is why this component is chronically underinvested despite being one of the highest-return interventions available.

The third is population-level technique literacy, which is the exposure posture applied at scale and over time. The goal is not to make citizens into analysts but to make the mechanics familiar enough that recognition becomes ordinary: an understanding that a claim arriving with no traceable source has a characteristic shape, that apparent consensus can be manufactured, that contradictory claims from aligned sources are a signal rather than confusion. Evidence from societies that have invested seriously in this over long periods suggests it is genuinely effective, though it is slow, it requires sustained funding across changes of government, and it works far better when delivered through education systems than through public campaigns, which tend to reach the people who need them least.

The fourth is cross-partisan norm maintenance, which is the least amenable to policy and the most important. It consists of the ordinary, unremarkable practice of political opponents treating each other as legitimate, disputing policy without disputing patriotism, and declining to characterize domestic disagreement as foreign-directed. No agency can produce this. It is produced or destroyed by the daily conduct of politicians, editors, and prominent voices, and it is the component that most directly determines whether the cohesion attack finds anything to work with.

The ordering is deliberate and it is the opposite of typical resource allocation. Most national responses concentrate on monitoring and rebuttal, which sit outside this list entirely because they address content rather than condition. The four components above address condition, and they are what the rightmost column of the recognition grid is measuring.
## The Technology Layer and What It Changes

A question that runs underneath most current discussion of this subject is whether recent advances in content generation change the analysis fundamentally or merely at the margin. The answer matters for resource allocation, and the honest version has parts that cut in both directions.

What changes is the cost curve on the production side. Historically, the binding constraint on an influence operation was human labor: manufactured content had to be written, translated into idiomatic Polish, adapted to different audiences, and maintained across many personas without obvious repetition, and all of that required people with relevant language skill and cultural familiarity. That constraint was real and it limited both volume and quality. It is now substantially relaxed. Content can be produced in volume, in fluent target-language prose, in many stylistic registers, without the labor bottleneck that previously capped the effort. The tells that recognition traditionally relied on, particularly awkward translation and repetitive phrasing across supposedly independent voices, are correspondingly weaker than they were.

What does not change is anything on the distribution or effect side, and this is the part usually missed. Producing content was never the hard part of an influence operation; getting it carried was. Laundering still requires the chain of venues. Amplification still requires the infrastructure to manufacture apparent consensus, which is a platform-side problem rather than a generation-side one. The final and most valuable step, adoption by a domestic voice with genuine standing, is completely unaffected by how the material was produced, because that step depends on whether the claim serves an argument someone already wants to make. An operation with unlimited content production and no distribution advantage is not meaningfully more dangerous than one with a large writing staff.

The net assessment is that cheaper production raises volume and degrades some recognition tells, both of which are real costs to a defender, while leaving the campaign's fundamental structure and its ceiling unchanged. This reinforces rather than undermines the article's central argument. If content becomes effectively free and content-level tells become unreliable, then a defense organized around examining content becomes even less viable than it already was, and the shift toward conduct-based recognition and condition-based measurement becomes more urgent rather than less. A defender who was going to be overwhelmed by volume eventually is now going to be overwhelmed sooner, which is an argument for changing the approach rather than for scaling the existing one.

One genuine new concern deserves separate mention, since it belongs to a different category than volume. Convincing synthetic audio and video of identifiable people saying things they did not say introduces a specifically crisis-relevant risk that ordinary manufactured text does not. In peacetime such material is generally identified and discounted within a manageable period. In a compressed decision window, a plausible fabricated statement attributed to an official at the moment a population is deciding whether to believe an instruction is a materially different problem, and it lands precisely on the crisis-window vulnerability identified above. The defense is not detection technology, which will always be in a race it can lose, but pre-established authentication practice: channels a population already knows to check, habits of verification established before they are needed, and official communication disciplined enough that its absence from an expected channel is itself informative.

## Nuisance or Genuine Danger: A Triage Standard

A great deal of hostile information activity is genuinely trivial, and a defender who treats every instance as significant will exhaust their capacity on noise and will lose credibility when the significant instance arrives. The opposite error, treating the whole category as background irritation, misses the cases that matter. What is needed is a standard that separates the two, and the standard follows from the analysis above rather than from the volume or offensiveness of the material.

Four questions do most of the work. Does the activity touch a decision window, meaning a moment at which a population, a government, or an alliance must decide something quickly and where confusion has an irreversible cost? Does it have an institutional carrier, meaning has it been picked up by someone with genuine standing whose audience will treat it as credible? Does it work a seam that is currently open by the four-condition test, or one that is dormant? And does it enable another instrument, meaning is it preparing an information environment in which a physical or technical action would produce disproportionate effect?

Activity that answers yes to none of these is nuisance and should be logged rather than answered. Activity that answers yes to one deserves attention proportionate to which one. Activity that answers yes to two or more is a genuine problem and should receive a response designed for the specific combination rather than a generic rebuttal. The most dangerous combination, and the one worth naming as a standing priority, is decision window plus enabling function, because that pairing is the signature of information work being used to multiply something else at the moment it will hurt most.

There is a useful corollary. Because the dangerous combinations cluster around crisis conditions, the correct peacetime posture is not to fight the ambient campaign to a standstill but to build the four resilience components while conserving credibility for the moment it will be needed. A defender who spends the credibility ledger on peacetime skirmishing has optimized for the fight that does not matter and disarmed for the one that does. That is the strategic error most likely to be made by a competent, energetic, well-resourced national response, which is what makes it worth stating plainly.

### Is this instrument more dangerous than the physical ones?

No, and treating it as such distorts priorities. Information pressure cannot destroy infrastructure, cannot seize terrain, and cannot kill. Its significance is as a multiplier: it makes physical and technical actions harder to attribute, harder to answer, and more politically costly to absorb, which is a serious effect but a derivative one.

The comparison matters because resource allocation is genuinely contested. Poland's gray-zone exposure spans physical interference with infrastructure, interference with navigation and electronic services, engineered pressure at its frontier, and the information layer discussed here. Each competes for the same finite attention from the same officials. The honest ranking places the instruments that cause irreversible physical or economic harm above the one that shapes perception, while recognizing that the perception layer raises the cost of every other instrument and therefore cannot be neglected without raising the whole portfolio's effectiveness. The comparative judgment between hybrid pressure as a whole and the risk of open conflict is a separate question with its own treatment in the series, and readers weighing where to put finite resources should look there for the wider tradeoff.

## The Alliance Response Problem

Collective defense has an answer for armed attack, a developing answer for cyber operations, and essentially no answer for information pressure. Understanding why is useful, because the gap is structural rather than a failure of will and it is unlikely to close.

The obstacle is that any collective mechanism requires a shared definition of the thing being responded to, and allies cannot agree on one here. Member states differ substantially in their legal traditions regarding speech, in their constitutional tolerance for state involvement in the information environment, and in their domestic political appetite for treating messaging as a security matter. A state with strong constitutional protection for expression cannot sign up to a definition that a state with a different tradition would find natural. These are not petty differences to be negotiated away; they reflect genuine and defensible disagreements about the relationship between a government and public discourse, and the series treats the wider pattern of such divergences in its analysis of alliance cohesion.

The second obstacle is that the instrument is aimed partly at the alliance's own internal politics, which makes collective response recursive in an awkward way. A proposal to establish a shared mechanism for identifying foreign manipulation becomes itself a subject of domestic political contention in each member state, and that contention is exactly the kind of seam the campaign works. The effort to build the defense therefore generates material for the attack, which is not a reason to abandon the effort but is a reason to expect it to be slow and to be conducted carefully.

What the alliance can do, and does, sits below the level of collective response. Shared analysis and threat characterization work reasonably well, because pooling observation requires no common legal definition. Coordinated attribution, where several governments state a common assessment simultaneously, is meaningfully more powerful than any single statement and does not require a treaty mechanism. Support for the resilience components in more exposed members, particularly media ecology and technique literacy, is uncontroversial and effective. And exercise design that deliberately includes a degraded information environment is probably the highest-value alliance activity available in this domain, because it tests the crisis-window vulnerability directly and reveals the gaps in a setting where discovering them is cheap.

What the alliance should probably not attempt is a threshold, and the reasons are the ones already given. A stated line at which information activity triggers a collective response would be either so high as to be meaningless or so low as to require the alliance to adjudicate speech, and the precedent would be more useful to governments that wish to suppress domestic criticism than to those defending against foreign manipulation. Accepting that this instrument will not generate a collective decision point is uncomfortable, and it is the correct position.

## Watching the Campaign Over Time

Most monitoring of hostile information activity measures the wrong thing, which follows directly from measuring content. A monitoring program built on the analysis above looks different, and it is worth specifying because the difference is actionable.

A content-based program counts items: how many manufactured claims were identified, how many accounts were removed, how many corrections were issued, what the reach of a given narrative was. These numbers are easy to collect, they rise and fall for reasons that have little to do with the campaign's success, and they can all improve while the society they describe deteriorates. They are useful for tactical purposes and close to worthless as a measure of whether the defense is working.

A condition-based program measures the five variables in the recognition grid's rightmost column. Cross-partisan trust: do supporters of opposing parties describe each other as legitimate opponents or as enemies, and is that measure moving? Sustained-commitment support: is public willingness to bear defense costs stable, and is the political cost of maintaining it rising? Alliance confidence, in both directions: how much do Poles expect partners to honor commitments, and how do partner publics and parliaments characterize Poland? Ambient information trust: what share of the public reports trusting no source, which is the clearest single indicator of the corrosion objective succeeding? And crisis-response credibility, which can be assessed through the accuracy record of official communication and through exercises that test whether instructions are actually believed and acted upon.

These are slower, harder, and more expensive to measure than item counts. They also have the property that they answer the question a decision-maker actually asked. A minister who wants to know whether the country is more or less vulnerable than it was five years ago gets no answer from a count of removed accounts and a real answer from movement in these five variables. Constructing that baseline is the single most useful institutional investment available in this domain, and it should be built to be comparable over time and, where possible, comparable against societies that receive less attention, since the untargeted comparison is what separates the effect of the campaign from the ordinary drift affecting all democracies.

There is one further monitoring discipline worth adopting, which is to watch the inbound stream systematically. Because material about Poland aimed at foreign audiences never appears in the Polish information space, it will be missed entirely by any monitoring effort organized around domestic circulation. A serious program looks at how Poland is being characterized in the information environments of the countries whose parliaments would have to approve reinforcement, and it treats deterioration there as a security indicator rather than a public-relations problem.
## Six Recurring Analytical Mistakes

The literature on this subject and the public discussion of it contain a small number of errors that recur with enough regularity to be worth naming individually. Each has a cost, and the costs are not symmetric.

The first is amplification through condemnation. An organization identifies a manufactured claim, publicizes it in order to denounce it, and thereby delivers it to an audience that would never have seen it. This mistake is made almost exclusively by people acting in good faith, which is what makes it so persistent. The corrective is the reach estimate: before publicizing anything, establish what its circulation actually is, and accept that most material is best left to die unremarked.

The second is treating persuasion as the metric. An analysis measures whether people believed a claim, finds that few did, and concludes the operation failed. This inverts the actual objective and generates false reassurance. The corrective is to measure the social variables the campaign is aimed at rather than the informational variables it is indifferent to.

The third is the reverse error, attributing domestic dysfunction to foreign action. A society experiencing polarization, institutional distrust, or political fragmentation locates the cause outside itself. This is comforting, frequently wrong, and expensive, because it directs remedial effort away from the domestic drivers that actually dominate and toward an external actor whose contribution is marginal. The corrective is the untargeted comparison: societies receiving little attention are experiencing much of the same drift, which bounds how much of it can be externally caused.

The fourth is conflating sincere domestic argument with manipulation. This is the most damaging error available and it has been discussed at length above. Its cost is not merely unfairness to the citizens it mischaracterizes; it delivers the operation's objective directly, since a society in which political opponents accuse each other of foreign direction has already arrived at the intended condition without any further external help.

The fifth is symmetry blindness, which consists of monitoring only the domestic information space and missing the inbound stream aimed at partner audiences. The cost is that the highest-value target in the whole campaign goes unwatched, and deterioration in how Poland is characterized abroad registers, if at all, as a public-relations irritation rather than as movement on a security variable.

The sixth is threshold inflation, the tendency to characterize hostile messaging as attack or as warfare in a sense that would trigger the machinery reserved for armed force. The cost here is strategic and precedential rather than immediate: it invites reciprocal characterization, it hands authoritarian governments a framework they can use more comfortably than democracies can, and it puts a state in the position of adjudicating the boundary between manipulation and speech under political pressure, which is a position no democratic government should want to occupy.

A reader who avoids these six is doing better than most published commentary on the subject, and the six are more useful to carry away than any list of currently circulating narratives could be, because they will still be correct after the narratives have all been replaced.
## The Verdict

The campaign against Poland is real, it is sustained, it is one instrument among several in a coordinated below-threshold effort, and it is not what most descriptions of it suggest. It is not an attempt to convert Poles to a Russian view of the world, which would be a hopeless project and is not being attempted. It is an attempt to produce a society that is angrier with itself, more tired of its commitments, less confident in its partners, less trusted by them, and less able to establish a shared factual account of anything when it matters most. Measured against that objective, it does not need to win a single argument.

Three judgments follow, and each is offered with its confidence attached.

Held with high confidence: the correct unit of analysis is technique rather than content, and any defensive program organized around the claims in circulation is measuring a variable the campaign treats as disposable. This follows from the structure of the operation and does not depend on contested empirical questions about effect size.

Held with moderate confidence: the campaign produces real but modest effects, concentrated in cumulative degradation of trust and cohesion rather than in persuasion, and considerably smaller than alarmed accounts suggest while considerably larger than the persuasion literature alone would imply. The uncertainty here is genuine and the honest range is wide, because the outcome variables are slow and the counterfactual is unobservable. A defender should hedge rather than pretend to know.

Held with high confidence: the largest risk this instrument poses is not what it does to Polish opinion in ordinary times but what it does to Polish and allied decision-making in a compressed crisis, where a degraded information environment converts a manageable emergency into a badly handled one. That is where the accumulated peacetime tax is actually paid, and it is the reason to invest in the slow, dull work of institutional credibility rather than in the visible, satisfying work of rebuttal.

The practical takeaway for a reader is smaller and more usable than the analysis might suggest. When encountering material that looks manufactured, the useful questions are not whether it is true but which of the three targets it serves, which seam it is trying to widen, and whether it is arriving with a real origin, real support, and an honest presentation of what it is. A reader who asks those questions consistently is doing the recognition work the grid was built for, and is contributing to the only defensive variable that finally matters, which is whether a society facing sustained pressure remains able to argue with itself in good faith.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does gray-zone disinformation aimed at Poland target?

It targets three social conditions rather than any set of beliefs. The first is internal cohesion, meaning the willingness of Poles who disagree about policy to treat each other as legitimate fellow citizens. The second is national resolve, meaning the durability of public support for costly and open-ended security commitments. The third is ally trust, which runs in both directions: Polish confidence that partners will honor their obligations, and partner confidence that Poland is a reliable and non-escalatory ally. A fourth and more ambient target sits underneath all three, which is the society's capacity to establish any shared factual baseline at all. Specific claims are the delivery mechanism for reaching these targets and are treated as disposable by the people producing them, which is why cataloging claims tells a defender very little about whether the effort is succeeding.

### Q: How does the gray-zone disinformation campaign against Poland work?

It works through three linked techniques. Narrative laundering moves a claim through a chain of intermediate venues, each of which strips a little more of its origin, until it arrives in mainstream discussion carrying no visible provenance and is picked up by sincere domestic voices arguing in good faith. Inauthentic amplification manufactures the appearance of consensus, exploiting the reasonable human habit of treating apparent popularity as weak evidence of merit. Saturation exploits the cost asymmetry between producing a claim and refuting one, taxing the finite capacity of journalists, analysts, and officials and pushing part of the public toward general distrust rather than better discrimination. None of the three requires the adversary to have credibility inside Poland, which is precisely why they are used instead of direct persuasion.

### Q: Why does gray-zone disinformation seek to divide Poland rather than convince it?

Because conversion is close to impossible in Poland and division is cheap. Polish attitudes toward Russia rest on a long historical record that no messaging effort will overwrite, so an operation aimed at persuading Poles of a Russian view of the world would need credibility it does not have. An operation aimed at making Polish arguments angrier and less resolvable needs no credibility at all, because the arguments already exist and only require accelerant. This produces what can be called the corrosion-not-conversion rule: the objective is a tired, suspicious, fragmented public rather than a converted one. The rule explains behavior that otherwise looks like incompetence, particularly the pushing of mutually contradictory claims, since contradiction that no one can adjudicate is itself corrosive and therefore counts as progress toward the objective.

### Q: How can Poles recognize gray-zone disinformation without amplifying it?

Recognition works better at the level of conduct than content, and conduct can be assessed privately without repeating anything. Three questions do most of the work. Is the origin of the material what it claims to be, or is authorship, funding, or nationality concealed? Is the apparent support real, or does it show the smoothness of manufactured activity, meaning uniform phrasing, synchronized timing, monotonic sentiment, and participants with thin or recently constructed histories? Is the material presented as what it is, or is it disguised as local reporting or organic commentary? Amplification is avoided by declining to reproduce the claim while discussing the pattern. Most manufactured material has negligible reach on its own, and a prominent debunking is frequently its most effective distribution mechanism, so estimating reach before responding is a discipline worth adopting.

### Q: Which societal seams does gray-zone disinformation against Poland exploit?

It exploits seams that meet four conditions: the dispute is real, it is unresolved, it maps onto identity rather than only onto interest, and it admits a plausible accusation of bad faith. In Poland the most exposed seams by that test include generational and geographic divergence in political outlook, the pressures surrounding the hosting of a very large displaced population, disagreements over the relationship with European institutions, and arguments about the proper role of the state in matters of conscience. The distributional argument over who bears the cost of a defense buildup is a candidate rather than a fully open seam, because it is still framed mostly as interest. Every seam on the list is a genuine domestic issue rather than a manufactured one, which is the uncomfortable point of the exercise.

### Q: Is gray-zone disinformation against Poland all-powerful or limited?

Neither, and the honest answer requires holding two findings together. The political-science literature on media effects consistently finds that direct persuasion is hard and that measured effects from exposure to political content are small, and studies of foreign influence reach have found it constitutes a minute fraction of any population's information diet, landing mostly on people already inclined to agree. That case is stronger than defenders usually admit. But persuasion is the wrong dependent variable if the objective is corrosion rather than conversion, and cumulative effects on trust, cohesion, and fatigue are poorly captured by designs built to measure belief change. The defensible position is that effects are real, modest, concentrated in slow social degradation, larger than the persuasion research alone implies, and considerably smaller than alarmed accounts suggest.

### Q: How does gray-zone disinformation try to fracture Poland's ally trust?

It works both directions of the relationship. Outbound, aimed at Polish audiences, it keeps alive the proposition that Poland will ultimately be left alone, that peacetime commitments will not survive contact with a real crisis, and that historical experience of abandonment is a better guide than current declarations. Inbound, aimed at audiences in partner countries and rarely visible in Poland at all, it characterizes Poland as hard-line, escalatory, internally unstable, or unreliable, with the aim of making the political argument for reinforcement marginally harder to win in the parliaments that would have to approve it. The inbound stream is the higher-value half and the one most likely to go unmonitored, because a monitoring program organized around domestic circulation will never see it.

### Q: Why is resilience to gray-zone disinformation measured in cohesion and trust?

Because those are the variables the campaign is aimed at, and counting corrections measures something the campaign is indifferent to. A country can debunk diligently and accurately for years while its cross-partisan trust declines, its public appetite for costly commitments erodes, and its confidence in partners weakens, which would mean the defense was winning every engagement and losing the contest. The useful metrics are therefore social: whether political opponents accept each other as legitimate, whether support for sustained expenditure is stable, how much confidence flows in each direction of the alliance relationship, what share of the public reports trusting no source at all, and whether official communication would actually be believed in an emergency. That last one is the stock that gets spent, once, when it matters.

### Q: How does gray-zone narrative laundering target Poland?

Laundering solves the adversary's central problem, which is that material carrying a visible hostile origin is discounted immediately. A claim is moved through a chain of venues, beginning somewhere obscure that nobody checks, then referenced by a slightly more visible outlet that cites the obscure venue rather than the origin, then referenced again further up. Each step supplies an apparent source and removes the actual one. The final and most valuable step requires no participation from the operation at all: a domestic figure with genuine standing picks the claim up because it happens to support a point they sincerely want to make. At that moment the claim is domestic, made by a domestic voice, inside a real argument, and any attempt to trace it backward sounds like an accusation against a fellow citizen.

### Q: How is genuine Polish debate kept distinct from gray-zone disinformation?

By testing conduct rather than viewpoint. The operative questions concern deception in the mechanics of participation: whether origin is concealed, whether apparent support is manufactured, and whether material is disguised as something it is not. None of those questions requires any judgment about whether the underlying opinion is correct, which is what makes the test compatible with a free society. The distinction has a consequence officials often resist: a citizen who sincerely holds a view that a hostile state also promotes is not a participant in an operation and not a dupe, and is entitled to argue their case without having the nationality of their opinion questioned. The correct answer to a mistaken domestic argument is a better domestic argument, never an accusation of foreign origin.

### Q: How is gray-zone disinformation aimed at Poland attributed to its source?

Through three kinds of evidence of descending strength. Technical evidence concerns infrastructure, registration patterns, and operational overlaps, and it is the most concrete but the most rapidly degraded, since competent operators learn to avoid exactly those overlaps. Behavioral evidence concerns pattern, including synchrony, simultaneous appearance across unconnected venues, and shifts that track a state's diplomatic positioning; it is more durable but weaker per unit and requires disputable judgment. Contextual evidence concerns whose interests the material serves, and it is the weakest and most easily abused, because a great many things happen to align with a great many interests. Responsible attribution combines all three and states confidence honestly. The claim that content serves a foreign interest is frequently true and almost never sufficient on its own.

### Q: Should Poland publicly rebut gray-zone disinformation or ignore it?

It depends on reach, and treating response as automatically virtuous is a mistake. Most manufactured material reaches very few people on its own, and a prominent official rebuttal can introduce it to an audience that would never have encountered it while giving it the shape of a live controversy. The workable discipline is triage. Material with negligible circulation and no institutional carrier is usually best logged and left alone. Material that has crossed into mainstream circulation, touches public safety, or is being carried by figures with real standing generally must be answered quickly, because the cost of silence exceeds the amplification cost. Between those poles sits a wide ambiguous band where judgment is required and reasonable officials will disagree. Anyone claiming a clean rule here has not examined the problem closely.

### Q: How does gray-zone disinformation exploit Polish historical memory?

Historical experience is exploited as a source of durable anxiety rather than as a subject of argument. Poland's record includes partition, invasion from two directions, and the experience of guarantees that did not translate into effective help, and that record is a fact rather than a narrative anyone needs to construct. Hostile effort works by keeping the anxiety salient and by presenting a contested analytical question, whether current commitments would hold under pressure, as though history had already settled it. The technique to recognize is the deployment of historical analogy as prediction rather than as evidence. The appropriate answer is not to treat the anxiety as illegitimate, which would be both false and counterproductive, but to engage the underlying question seriously, since a question dismissed becomes a grievance that cannot be answered.

### Q: Do fact-checks defeat gray-zone disinformation aimed at Poland?

They are necessary and badly insufficient, and the fashionable dismissal of them overstates the case. Establishing what is true is the foundation of every other defensive measure, supplies the evidence base for technique exposure, and genuinely matters for the safety-critical and widely circulated subset of claims. The limitation is scope. Verification operates on claims, and claims are the cheapest and least important layer of the operation. It cannot address selective amplification of true material, which contains nothing to correct. It cannot address the recasting of policy disputes as identity disputes, which involves no factual assertion. It cannot address fatigue cultivation built on legitimate open questions. And it cannot outrun an unbounded supply. Verification is the defense's sensor, not its main effort.

### Q: How would analysts measure the effect of gray-zone disinformation on Poland?

Not by counting items, which is what most programs do and which can improve while the society being described deteriorates. A condition-based program tracks five social variables over years: cross-partisan trust, meaning whether opposing supporters describe each other as legitimate opponents or enemies; support for sustained defense expenditure and the political cost of maintaining it; alliance confidence in both directions; the share of the public reporting trust in no source at all; and crisis-response credibility, assessed through the accuracy record of official communication and through exercises testing whether instructions are actually believed. Comparison against societies receiving less attention is essential, since much of the observed drift in democracies has domestic causes and the untargeted baseline is what separates the two.
