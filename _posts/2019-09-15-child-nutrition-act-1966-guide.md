The operative question behind the Child Nutrition Act was deceptively simple: when a child arrived at school hungry, which level of government paid for the meal, under what conditions, and with what degree of legal obligation? Congress legislated against a record in which that question had no stable answer. School feeding had grown from charity and local initiative into a patchwork of surplus commodity shipments, relief labor, and state authorization, and the patchwork fed children well in districts with wealth, organization, and kitchen capacity while leaving poor districts with the weakest facilities and the least margin. War gave the gap a manpower measure, because Selective Service examinations linked poor childhood diets to rejected recruits, and the National School Lunch Act of 1946 answered with a permanent program that declared school lunch a measure of national security. Two decades later, the lunch framework reached lunch and little else. Breakfast had no federal assistance. Milk ran on separate and expiring authority. Equipment money lagged behind need in the schools serving the poorest children. The claim that organizes the entire statute is plain: entitlement or appropriation decides everything.

![Child Nutrition Act school meals and the entitlement line between programs - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-22.webp)

That claim explains why two programs inside the same statutory family could offer opposite levels of security to the families each program served. Under the lunch structure inherited from 1946, federal payment followed each qualifying meal that a participating school served. A school that served more eligible children generated more reimbursement, and the federal obligation arose from the act of service itself. Under the supplemental program added for women, infants, and children in 1972, federal money arrived as a grant fixed by an annual appropriation, divided among state agencies by formula, and translated by clinics into a funded caseload. Eligibility defined who could be served, and the appropriation defined how many would be served. The distinction between a reimbursement that follows service and a grant that precedes service runs through every later question in the field, from equipment aid for school kitchens to the survival of a program during a lapse in appropriations.

The 1966 statute worked inside the architecture that the 1946 act had built rather than replacing that architecture. Administration stayed with the Department of Agriculture. Funds moved through state educational agencies to local schools. Meals counted for federal payment only when meals met nutritional standards prescribed by the Secretary of Agriculture. Operation stayed nonprofit. Against that inherited frame, the new act added a pilot breakfast program aimed first at schools in areas of poor economic conditions and at schools whose pupils traveled long distances, consolidated the special milk authorization, funded food service equipment for schools with absent or grossly inadequate facilities, paid state administrative expenses, and strengthened free and reduced price service for children whom local authorities judged unable to pay. Each addition carried limits written into the text, and no provision created an individual entitlement for any child.

Later amendments sorted each experiment into permanent law or continued dependence on annual funding decisions. Breakfast reached permanence in 1975. A separate pilot authorized in 1968 divided in 1975 into a summer program and a child care food program, and the child care branch later widened to cover adults in day care. The supplemental program became permanent in 1975 as well, and permanence changed the duration of the authorization without changing the discretionary character of the funding. Permanence and entitlement therefore described different properties.

Eligibility supplied the second sorting mechanism, and the sorting happened at the level of the individual child. Federal law divided children in participating schools into free, reduced price, and paid categories, set the boundaries of the subsidized categories as percentages of the federal poverty guidelines, capped the charge for a reduced price meal, and paid a different reimbursement for each category. Direct certification and categorical eligibility later allowed a child to qualify through participation in another assistance program or through a status such as foster care or homelessness, without a separate household application. Community eligibility, added in 2010, allowed a high poverty school to set the application machinery aside altogether and serve every enrolled student without charge, with reimbursement calculated from a formula built on directly certified students. Each device answered the same administrative problem, the cost and error involved in proving need child by child.

Standards supplied the third sorting mechanism, at the level of the meal. Federal reimbursement had always depended on a meal pattern set by the Secretary, and the 2010 legislation directed a comprehensive revision of that pattern based on recommendations from the Institute of Medicine, paired with standards for foods sold outside the reimbursable meal. The revision raised measured dietary quality, coincided with a decline in paid participation, and then gave ground in part through a 2018 rule that restored flexibility on milk, whole grains, and sodium.

## A Meal Program Written as a Security Measure

The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 rested on a foundation that legislators had built two decades earlier, and that foundation rested in turn on an improvised Depression era system for moving food from farms that could not sell it to children who could not buy it. The sequence matters because the 1966 statute did not create federal concern with school meals from nothing. The statute expanded and reorganized programs that carried an older design, an older administrative home in the Department of Agriculture, and an older justification that joined the health of children to the economic health of agriculture. The National School Lunch Act of 1946 gave that design permanent statutory form. Section 2 of the 1946 act declared the policy of Congress, as a measure of national security, to safeguard the health and well being of the children of the United States and to encourage the domestic consumption of nutritious agricultural commodities. The wording placed a cafeteria program inside the language of defense, and the placement was deliberate. Between the early nineteen thirties and 1946, school lunch advocates, farm organizations, military manpower officials, and nutrition specialists had each found a separate reason to support the same program, and the statute succeeded because those reasons converged rather than because a single reason persuaded every supporter.

Before federal aid arrived, school feeding in the United States belonged mainly to voluntary effort and local initiative. The Congressional Research Service account records that lunch programs operated in many cities and localities before the nineteen thirties, that charitable organizations founded by women started many of those programs around the turn of the twentieth century, and that control passed over time to school boards and school districts. Financing came from a mixture of private, local, and state sources, and the mixture differed from place to place. A city with an organized charity, an interested school board, and a tax base able to support a lunchroom could feed children regularly. A poor rural district, or a district without kitchen facilities, had no comparable obligation to act and often no practical means to act. The patchwork therefore produced provision that followed local wealth and local organization more closely than child need.

The Depression turned that uneven local arrangement into a national problem visible from two directions at once. From the farm direction, collapsing demand and falling prices left producers with commodities that the market could not absorb at prices that sustained farm income. From the school direction, widespread unemployment left families without wages and children without money for lunch, and, in many households, without the resources to provide adequate meals at home. Federal histories of the program describe malnutrition among schoolchildren as a national concern under those conditions, and the concern had a material basis in the coexistence of surplus food and empty lunch pails. A surplus, however, did not move itself. Without a public mechanism to purchase commodities, transport them, store them, convert them into meals, and pay the labor involved, agricultural abundance and child hunger could persist side by side indefinitely. The federal role that developed during the nineteen thirties addressed separate parts of that chain through separate agencies, and the divided structure explains both the expansion of school lunch provision during the Depression and the limits that expansion reached before 1946.

### Why did Congress call school lunch a national security measure?

Congress used security language because Selective Service examinations during the Second World War linked poor childhood diets to rejected recruits, and because the lunch statute joined child health to farm demand. The declaration made feeding children a defense related public purpose rather than a local charitable activity alone.

That answer compresses an argument that took more than a decade to assemble, and the agricultural part of the argument entered federal law first. The Act of August 24, 1935, cited as Public Law 74-320, included Section 32, a provision that directed a share of customs receipts to the Department of Agriculture for purposes that included encouraging domestic consumption of agricultural commodities. The Congressional Research Service history states that Section 32 provided thirty percent of customs receipts to the department to purchase surplus commodities from farmers affected by the Depression, and that the commodities reached domestic outlets that included school lunch programs. The arrangement gave the Secretary of Agriculture money that did not depend entirely on a school lunch appropriation, a purchasing mission tied to farm markets, and a distribution problem for which schools offered a constructive answer. Schools gathered large numbers of children in one place on a predictable schedule. A school could receive food in quantity, prepare food on the premises, and serve food to children, some of whom would not otherwise have obtained the commodity through ordinary commercial purchase. Federal officials could therefore describe the same transaction as farm support and as child feeding without changing the transaction itself, and the dual description became a durable feature of the program rather than a temporary rhetorical device.

Distribution required an organization, and the purchase and distribution program was assigned in 1935 to the Federal Surplus Commodities Corporation. The corporation had been established in 1933 as the Federal Surplus Relief Corporation to distribute surplus pork, dairy products, and wheat to people in need. The archived history prepared by the Food and Nutrition Service gives the scale of the school lunch channel in concrete terms. In March 1937, a total of 3,839 schools received commodities for lunch programs serving 342,031 children daily. Two years after that date, participation had grown to 14,075 schools and 892,259 children. Those figures describe a program of real size well before Congress enacted a permanent school lunch statute, and the figures also describe a program whose size depended on commodity flows and administrative effort rather than on a permanent entitlement defined by statute.


Food alone did not make a lunch. Someone had to cook it, serve it, wash equipment, keep records, and supervise a room, and many districts lacked money for labor even when commodities arrived without charge. The Works Progress Administration, created in 1935 to provide work for unemployed people on public projects, supplied much of that labor and gave school lunch expansion a second federal engine quite distinct from commodity purchase. The Congressional Research Service history states that the Works Progress Administration began sponsoring employment for women in school lunchrooms in 1935, and the archived federal history explains that school lunch work fell under the Community Service Division of the agency. Because unemployed women in need of work lived in nearly every city, town, village, and rural community, the preparation and serving of school lunches offered a ready field of employment. State level supervisors directed the work, and menus, recipes, and manuals developed through that supervisory system improved meal quality and set standards for equipment, sanitation, and safety. With labor furnished at no direct wage cost to a district, lunch prices could be held low, participation could rise, and the program could expand into places that commodity donations alone could not have sustained.

By 1941, according to histories drawing on the work of Gordon Gunderson, Works Progress Administration school lunch projects operated in every state, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. Geographic reach, however, differed from uniform entitlement. A child gained access to a federally assisted lunch when a state agency participated, a local sponsor organized a project, a school had facilities, a commodity shipment contained usable food, and labor had been assigned. Each link depended on administrative decisions and local conditions. Federal guidance accompanied commodities, and the Congressional Research Service notes that recipient organizations, through agreements with state agencies, had to operate on a nonprofit basis, maintain existing local funding, keep records of food received, and serve meals free to poor children without singling out such children for adverse treatment. Those conditions introduced national expectations into local programs, yet the conditions attached to donated goods and sponsored labor rather than to a permanent statutory promise that federal assistance would follow every qualifying meal served by an eligible school. A program built from relief appropriations and surplus disposal could contract when relief policy changed, when wartime labor markets absorbed the workers, or when food supplies tightened, and contraction during the Second World War exposed exactly that vulnerability.

State action widened the base without removing the unevenness. Congressional debate on the history of the program recorded that many states and cities adopted legislation during the nineteen thirties authorizing or requiring school lunch provision, often with appropriations, and that by 1937 fifteen states had passed laws specifically authorizing local school boards to operate lunchrooms serving meals at cost or less. Fifteen authorizing states left the remainder of the states without an equivalent statutory framework, and authorization in any event permitted local operation rather than guaranteeing a meal in every school. Local wealth continued to shape kitchen construction, equipment purchase, staffing, and the price charged to children who paid. Federal encouragement could multiply local projects, and federal commodities could lower food costs, but neither device equalized resources among districts or converted a voluntary project into a right attached to enrollment in a participating school. The character of the food also reflected the origin of the supply, because a commodity program purchased what farmers had produced in surplus. Menus therefore began in part from availability, and nutrition advocates pressed for meals planned around child requirements rather than around warehouse stocks. The tension between those two starting points, the surplus that had to move and the child who had to eat, ran through the program from the nineteen thirties onward.

War transformed the political meaning of those meals by giving the nutrition argument a manpower measure. As the United States mobilized for the Second World War, Selective Service examinations subjected large numbers of young men to a common physical screening, and the results gave federal officials a statistical language for conditions that school nurses, teachers, and relief workers had described for years. Rejection figures require careful handling, because different accounts used different denominators and different definitions of a nutrition related cause. A Stanford University account of military nutrition research reported that one ninth of potential draftees were rejected because of apparently nutrition related diseases. That figure measured nutrition related rejection against the pool of potential draftees. Other widely repeated accounts used a different comparison. Draft boards rejected roughly three in ten registrants for physical defects of all kinds, according to an account in Food Safety News that examined the draft record against the popular school lunch origin story, and causes inside that overall share ranged across hearing defects, hernias, dental conditions, infectious disease, and many other findings, with malnutrition playing a role in only part of the total. Precision therefore lies in attribution and definition. The defensible statement is narrower than the slogan that later memory sometimes produced. A substantial share of young men failed military physical standards, nutrition formed one recognized strand among the causes, and Selective Service leadership assigned diet a large importance inside the rejection problem.


The official who carried that assignment into the legislative debate was Lewis B. Hershey, director of the Selective Service System. Hershey appeared before the House Agriculture Committee in the spring of 1945, and accounts of the testimony report that Hershey warned lawmakers that a large share of rejected draftees had been turned away because of poor diet. One account of the 1945 testimony put the figure at as many as forty percent of rejected draftees, attributing the rejections to poor diets. A separate historical account summarized Hershey in similar but broader terms, reporting an estimate that between one third and two fifths of recruits had been rejected for disabilities directly or indirectly connected with nutrition. The two formulations do not yield a single exact rate, and the difference between them cautions against treating either formulation as a settled measurement of nutritional deficiency in the draft age population. The historical significance of the testimony lies in the office of the witness and the inference the witness drew, apart from the precise share selected. The man responsible for supplying manpower told an agriculture committee that the physical condition of recruits reflected feeding long before induction, that childhood diet bore on military readiness, and that health formed a condition of national survival in war or in peace. A school lunch, heard through that testimony, sounded less like a municipal convenience and more like preparation of the population on which mobilization depended.

Hershey gave the nutrition case an institutional weight that nutrition specialists alone did not possess inside a debate over farm legislation. Nutrition science had gained federal standing during the war, and the May 1941 White House National Nutrition Conference for Defense brought nutrition scientists into national planning months before the attack on Pearl Harbor. Recommended dietary allowances emerged from that wartime mobilization of expertise. Hershey connected such arguments to a consequence that legislators responsible for defense could measure in rejected manpower. Agriculture interests reached a compatible conclusion from the opposite direction. Farm organizations and members of Congress from agricultural districts had reason to preserve a domestic outlet for commodities after wartime demand subsided, because experience after the First World War had shown how sharply farm prices could fall when extraordinary demand ended. A permanent school lunch program offered a continuing use for nutritious food, a defense of farm income framed around feeding children, and a market that rested on public purpose rather than on disposal of embarrassing waste. Military readiness and farm income therefore supplied two distinct majorities that could meet inside one bill, and the committee setting in which Hershey testified, the House Agriculture Committee, symbolized the meeting point.

Senator Richard B. Russell of Georgia stood among the central legislative champions of that settlement, and the Congressional Research Service credits Russell with a leading role in passage of the 1946 act and with earlier support for school lunch provision inside New Deal programs, including service as chairman of the agriculture appropriations subcommittee. Support for the statute nevertheless extended well beyond one sponsor or one region. War mobilization had made child feeding a subject on which defense officials could testify, farm state legislators could claim an economic benefit, educators could claim better nourished pupils, and public health advocates could claim preventive value. Opposition and hesitation had their own grounds in the period, including concern over permanent federal involvement in a function that families, charities, and local schools had performed, and concern over the cost of an open ended authorization. The enacted statute answered the permanence question directly. Congress chose a permanent authorization over another round of temporary Depression and wartime expedients, and the choice converted a decade of improvisation into a standing national program.

President Harry S. Truman signed the National School Lunch Act on June 4, 1946, and the measure entered law as Public Law 79-396. The Congressional Research Service states the essential facts plainly, identifying the public law number, the signing date, and the President, and the statute itself recorded the policy in Section 2. The Section 2 declaration deserves close reading because each clause assigned the program a task. National security supplied the stated ground. Safeguarding the health and well being of children of the United States supplied the human object. Encouraging the domestic consumption of nutritious agricultural commodities and other food supplied the agricultural object. Assistance to the states through grants in aid and other means supplied the federal method, and the establishment, maintenance, operation, and expansion of nonprofit school lunch programs supplied the institutional result. The sentence therefore refused to choose between feeding children and supporting consumption of farm products. Federal law treated each purpose as part of the same policy, and administration remained with agriculture rather than with an education or health agency, a placement consistent with the commodity origins of the program and with the farm coalition that helped enact the statute.

The operating provisions translated that declaration into a federal and state structure. The 1946 act permanently authorized appropriations of such sums as might be necessary for the National School Lunch Program, in the formulation reported by the Congressional Research Service. Permanent authorization did not itself appropriate money for any particular year, and Congress retained control through annual appropriations. Funds were apportioned to states by a formula that reflected the number of school age children in a state and the relative need of the state as measured by per capita income, so a poorer state received a different federal share from a wealthier state with the same child population. States in turn distributed assistance to schools, and the Congressional Research Service reports that state distribution occurred monthly on the basis of the number of meals served that met minimum nutritional requirements prescribed by the Secretary of Agriculture on the basis of tested nutritional research. The Secretary also gained authority over the nutritional definition of a reimbursable lunch, which meant that federal money recognized a meal only when the meal satisfied a federal standard rather than whenever a school placed any food before a child.


Eligibility reinforced the institutional character of the settlement. The statute directed federal assistance toward nonprofit school lunch programs, and participating schools operated under that nonprofit condition rather than as commercial vendors selling a federally subsidized product for profit. The Congressional Research Service also reports that the law required participating schools to serve lunches free or at a reduced price to students whom local school authorities judged unable to pay the full cost of a lunch. Local judgment preserved a large measure of local discretion over which children received a free or reduced price meal, and no uniform national income standard defined that judgment in the original program. The provision nevertheless established a principle that inability to pay could not simply exclude a child from the lunchroom of a participating school. Cash assistance under the act supported food purchase, and separate authority addressed equipment, although Congress restricted appropriations for equipment assistance in the years after enactment. The structure thus distinguished the meal, the labor and facilities behind the meal, and the child unable to pay for the meal, while leaving much of the practical administration to state agencies and local schools.

Scale in the first year showed that the permanent statute organized a program of national reach rather than a pilot. The Food and Nutrition Service reported that about 7.1 million children participated in the National School Lunch Program by the end of the first year, from 1946 to 1947. That participation built on the much smaller commodity program counted in March 1937 and on the far larger wartime and relief era operations that preceded the statute, and participation rested on schools that chose to take part and states that administered agreements with those schools. Even in 1946 the lunch program was structured so federal money followed qualifying meals, and that structure formed the entitlement architecture that the 1966 act inherited. Federal assistance moved through states to schools in relation to meals served under federal nutritional standards, commodities supplemented cash assistance, and the number of qualifying meals rather than a fixed local grant alone determined a substantial part of the federal payment generated by a school. The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 therefore inherited more than a popular program. The later statute inherited a declared security purpose, a dual commitment to child health and agricultural consumption, administration centered in the Department of Agriculture, state apportionment influenced by child population and income, nonprofit operation, and payment tied to meals that met standards set by the Secretary of Agriculture. The origins explain the form. A program born from surplus disposal, relief labor, and wartime manpower anxiety entered permanent law carrying all three inheritances, and the 1966 legislation expanded child nutrition policy from inside that architecture rather than by replacing the architecture with a different one.

## What the Statute Added to the Lunch Framework

The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 arrived as a repair and expansion measure for a system that had existed for two decades without reaching many of the children whom federal policy described as most in need. The National School Lunch Act of 1946 had created a permanent federal role in school feeding, with cash assistance and donated commodities flowing through state agencies to schools serving lunches that met federal requirements. By the middle of the 1960s, that framework carried clear limits. It centered on a single midday meal. It offered no federal assistance for breakfast, even for children who left home early, traveled long distances to school, or arrived without a morning meal at home. It depended on kitchens, storage, and serving capacity that many schools in poor areas did not possess. Milk reached children through a separate special milk arrangement with its own history and expiring authority, rather than through a consolidated child nutrition statute. Each gap pointed toward children in low income areas whose schools had the weakest facilities and whose families had the least margin for food costs.

The political setting was the War on Poverty declared by President Lyndon B. Johnson in 1964 and the legislative program that followed it. Federal anti-poverty policy in that period treated nutrition, health, and schooling as connected problems. A child who arrived at school hungry presented an educational problem as well as a welfare problem, and a school meal presented an intervention that reached children at scale through an institution attended every weekday. [The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965](/2014/08/01/elementary-secondary-education-act-1965/) directed federal aid toward schools and districts serving concentrations of children from low income families, and its administration required the counting and mapping of school poverty on a national basis. The same attendance areas that qualified for compensatory education aid contained schools least likely to operate a breakfast service, least likely to own adequate kitchen equipment, and most likely to enroll children who qualified for free meals that local resources could not fully support.

The Johnson administration framed its child nutrition proposals as an extension of experience rather than an experiment. Secretary of Agriculture Orville L. Freeman, testifying before the House Committee on Agriculture on the administration proposals, presented the package as a comprehensive effort to broaden child nutrition programs, grounded in twenty years of administration of the National School Lunch Act and in an assessment of gaps in the nutritional needs of children. The administration did not propose to replace the lunch program or to move school feeding out of the Department of Agriculture. The proposal instead added the missing meal of the school day, consolidated the milk program, paid for equipment that poor schools lacked, funded the state offices that administered the programs, and strengthened assistance aimed at needy children, all inside and alongside the established lunch structure. The bill that carried the package bore the Senate designation S. 3467.

The formal identity of the measure is stated once and plainly. The statute is the Child Nutrition Act of 1966, enacted as Public Law 89-642 and printed at 80 Statutes at Large 885. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the act on October 11, 1966. The act was codified principally at title 42 of the United States Code, section 1771 and following sections, in a chapter separate from the chapter that carried the National School Lunch Act at section 1751 and following sections. Section 1 supplied the short title. Section 2 supplied a declaration of purpose that tied food and good nutrition to the capacity of children to develop and learn, invoked the cumulative experience under the national school lunch program, and declared a congressional policy of extending, expanding, and strengthening those efforts under the authority of the Secretary of Agriculture, through grants in aid and other means to the states, as a measure to safeguard the health and well being of children and to encourage the domestic consumption of agricultural and other foods. 

### What did the Child Nutrition Act change for school meals?

The act added a pilot school breakfast program, placed the special milk program on a renewed statutory footing, funded food service equipment for schools in poor areas, paid state administrative costs, and strengthened free and reduced price service for needy children within the existing lunch framework.

The school breakfast pilot occupied Section 4 of the act and represented the clearest addition to the school day. Before 1966, federal school food assistance recognized lunch and, through the separate milk arrangement, milk served outside a full meal. No federal program assisted a school that wished to feed children before classes began. Section 4 authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to formulate and carry out, on a nonpartisan basis, a pilot program to assist states through grants in aid and other means to initiate, maintain, or expand nonprofit breakfast programs in schools. The pilot character of the authorization was written into both its duration and its figures. The act authorized appropriations not to exceed seven million five hundred thousand dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1967, and not to exceed ten million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1968. 

The distribution rules of Section 4 targeted the pilot at children most at risk of arriving at school unfed. Of the funds appropriated, the Secretary apportioned a fixed sum equally among the states, with a smaller equal division for Guam, the Virgin Islands, and American Samoa, and apportioned the remainder under the formula used in section 4 of the National School Lunch Act. State educational agencies then disbursed funds to schools the agencies selected, to reimburse the cost of obtaining food for consumption by needy children in a breakfast program, including costs of processing, distributing, transporting, storing, and handling that food, at rates per meal or on another basis prescribed by the Secretary. In selecting schools, the state educational agency was directed, to the extent practicable, to give first consideration to schools drawing attendance from areas in which poor economic conditions existed and to schools to which a substantial proportion of the children enrolled had to travel long distances daily. 

Section 4 also built need based assistance into the breakfast service itself. Breakfasts had to consist of a combination of foods and meet minimum nutritional requirements prescribed by the Secretary on the basis of tested nutritional research. The act required that breakfasts be served without cost or at a reduced cost only to children whom local school authorities determined to be unable to pay the full cost, and the text directed those authorities, to the extent practicable, to consult with public welfare and health agencies in making such determinations. A companion clause barred any physical segregation of, or other discrimination against, a child because of inability to pay, a protection against the visible separation of poor children in the cafeteria. For schools in circumstances of severe need, where the rate per meal established by the Secretary was judged insufficient to carry on an effective breakfast program, Section 4 authorized financial assistance up to eighty percent of the operating costs of such a program, including the cost of obtaining, preparing, and serving food, and applicant schools had to justify the need for that assistance to the state educational agency. 

Special milk assistance, in Section 3, consolidated an older program rather than creating a fresh one. The Special Milk Program had functioned since 1954, encouraging consumption of fluid milk by children in schools and institutions, and the Congressional Research Service traced the program in the 1966 act to predecessor Department of Agriculture school milk efforts reaching back to the 1940s. Section 3 authorized appropriations not to exceed one hundred ten million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1967, not to exceed one hundred fifteen million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1968, and not to exceed one hundred twenty million dollars for each of the two succeeding fiscal years, running the authorization through the fiscal year ending June 30, 1970. Section 3 directed the Secretary to administer the special milk program, to the maximum extent practicable, in the same manner as the program administered under Public Law 85-478, as amended, during the fiscal year ended June 30, 1966, which preserved administrative continuity for schools and institutions that depended on the reimbursement.

The eligible reach of the milk authorization extended beyond schools in the narrow sense. Section 3 covered children in nonprofit schools of high school grade and under, and in nonprofit nursery schools, child care centers, settlement houses, summer camps, and similar nonprofit institutions devoted to the care and training of children. 

Nonfood assistance, the equipment program of Section 5, addressed the physical bottleneck identified in poor schools. The section heading in the enrolled text named the program directly, as a nonfood assistance program authorization. Section 5 authorized appropriations not to exceed twelve million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1967, not to exceed fifteen million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1968, and not to exceed eighteen million dollars for each of the two fiscal years ending June 30, 1969, and June 30, 1970, with later fiscal years left to such sums as Congress might authorize afterward. The Secretary was to assist the states, through grants in aid and other means, to supply schools drawing attendance from areas in which poor economic conditions existed with equipment, other than land or buildings, for the storage, preparation, transportation, and serving of food, so that such schools could establish, maintain, and expand school food service programs. 

The terms of Section 5 tied equipment money to demonstrated local need and to a local financial contribution. The Secretary apportioned appropriated funds among the states on the same basis as apportionments under section 4 of the National School Lunch Act for supplying agricultural and other foods. Payment to a state carried the condition that at least one fourth of the cost of any equipment financed under the section be borne by state or local funds, limiting the federal share to three fourths of equipment cost. The state educational agency disbursed funds to assist schools that drew attendance from areas in which poor economic conditions existed and that had no equipment, or grossly inadequate equipment, to conduct a school food service program. An applicant school had to justify the need for assistance and its inability to finance the equipment required, and disbursement, by advance or reimbursement, followed state agency approval of a request that described the equipment in detail and set out plans for its use in meeting the nutritional needs of children in the school. For nonprofit private schools, equipment financed with federal funds served principally in connection with child feeding programs authorized under the 1966 act and the National School Lunch Act, and if the equipment ceased to be so used, the federally financed portion, or its residual value, reverted to the United States.

State administrative expense funding appeared in Section 7, and its presence signaled that Congress understood administration as a program cost rather than an overhead afterthought. Section 7 authorized the Secretary to use funds appropriated under the section for advances to each state educational agency, for use for its administrative expenses in supervising and giving technical assistance to local school districts conducting programs under the act. Advances were limited to amounts and to the extent determined necessary by the Secretary, and the stated object was assistance with additional activities that state agencies undertook under section 11 of the National School Lunch Act and under sections 4 and 5 of the 1966 act. The act authorized such sums as might be necessary for the purposes of the section, without a fixed ceiling of the kind that sections 3, 4, and 5 carried. Section 6 supplied payment machinery through certification to the Secretary of the Treasury.

The remaining sections assembled the operating rules that held the new programs together. Section 8 required each school participating under Section 4, insofar as practicable, to use foods designated by the Secretary as being in abundance, and authorized donation of foods available through the commodity authorities the text cited, in accordance with needs determined by local school authorities. Section 9 imposed the nonprofit requirement in general form, providing that food and milk service programs in schools and nonprofit institutions receiving assistance under the act be conducted on a nonprofit basis. Section 10 empowered the Secretary to prescribe regulations necessary to carry out the act. Section 11 barred the Secretary and the states, in carrying out sections 3 through 5, from imposing requirements respecting teaching personnel, curriculum, instruction, methods of instruction, and materials of instruction. The same section provided that the value of assistance to children under the act not be considered income or resources for any purpose under federal or state law, including laws on taxation, welfare, and public assistance, and that expenditures from state and local sources for the maintenance of food programs for children not be diminished as a result of funds received under the act. Section 16 closed the act with an accounts and records duty for participating states, agencies, schools, and institutions, with records open to inspection and audit and preserved for a period set by the Secretary, not in excess of three years.

The strengthening of service to needy children ran through the act as a pattern rather than as a single freestanding program. The Congressional Research Service narrative of the lunch program records that special assistance for schools in poor economic areas had been authorized in the National School Lunch Act in 1962, through its section 11, without funding until 1966. The 1966 legislation therefore arrived in the same year in which special assistance for lunches moved from authority toward funded practice, and the new act aligned its own provisions with that direction. Breakfast funds flowed first to schools in areas of poor economic conditions. Severe need schools could receive up to eighty percent of operating costs. Equipment funds flowed only to schools in poor areas with absent or grossly inadequate facilities. Free and reduced price breakfast service attached to a local determination of inability to pay, protected by the bar on segregation and discrimination. The act stopped short of uniform national income eligibility standards for free and reduced price meals, leaving determination to local school authorities under the framework the text prescribed, and later amendments carried the work of standardizing eligibility beyond the point the 1966 text reached.

Coverage under the act followed its definitions, in Section 15. The term state meant any of the fifty states, the District of Columbia, the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, Guam, and American Samoa. The term state educational agency meant, as the state legislature might determine, either the chief state school officer, such as a state superintendent of public instruction or commissioner of education, or a board of education controlling the state department of education. The term nonprofit private school meant a private school exempt from income tax under section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. The term school meant any public or nonprofit private school of high school grade or under, including kindergarten and preschool programs operated by such a school and, with respect to Puerto Rico, nonprofit child care centers certified as such by the Governor of Puerto Rico. Sections 4 and 5 each routed assistance for nonprofit private schools through the withholding and disbursement mechanism of section 10 of the National School Lunch Act, exclusive of its matching provisions, preserving a channel for private school participation where state law or state practice complicated direct state payment.

Two further provisions fixed the institutional home of the programs and the outer edge of their reach in 1966. Section 13 assigned authority for the conduct and supervision of federal programs to assist schools in providing food service programs for children to the Department of Agriculture, and directed other federal agencies administering programs under which funds were to be provided to schools for such assistance, to the extent practicable, to transfer such funds to the Department of Agriculture for distribution through the administrative channels and standards of the 1966 act and the National School Lunch Act. Section 14 authorized appropriations for the Secretary administrative expense under the act in such sums as might be necessary for any fiscal year. Section 12, headed as a provision on preschool programs, empowered the Secretary to extend the benefits of all school feeding programs conducted and supervised by the Department of Agriculture to include preschool programs operated as part of the school system. The preschool authority was permissive rather than programmatic, it created no separate appropriation and no separate reimbursement structure, and its reach was confined to preschool programs operated as part of a school system rather than to independent child care settings as a class.

That distinction resolves a point that later program histories can blur. The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 did not create a child care food pilot. Milk assistance under Section 3 reached nonprofit nursery schools and child care centers, and Section 12 allowed extension of school feeding benefits to school based preschool programs, but neither provision funded meals in child care settings as a dedicated program. The dedicated pilot arrived two years afterward, in 1968, when Public Law 90-302 authorized the Special Food Service Program for Children, a pilot directed to meals in summer and child care settings and targeted to areas with poor economic conditions and large numbers of working mothers, as the Congressional Research Service account of the period records. That 1968 pilot supplied the first federal assistance for summer meals and the first dedicated assistance for meals served in child care settings, and separate legislation in the following decade divided that pilot into distinct child care and summer programs. 

The limits of the 1966 act were as deliberate as its additions. The act did not create the school lunch program, which rested on the National School Lunch Act of 1946 and remained governed by that statute, its appropriations, and its reimbursement structure. The 1966 act did not repeal, absorb, or recodify the lunch program, and the two statutes operated side by side, one as the foundation and the other as the extension. The act did not create an individual entitlement for any child. No child acquired a personal legal right to a breakfast, to milk, or to equipment supported service enforceable against a school or a state. Participation ran through institutions, schools applied or were selected, state educational agencies disbursed funds within appropriations, the breakfast authorization carried fixed ceilings for two fiscal years, and a child received a free or reduced price breakfast only where a school operated a program and local authorities determined inability to pay. The act also left the nutrition of women, infants, and preschool children outside school systems largely untouched, apart from the milk reach into nursery schools and child care centers and the permissive preschool authority of Section 12.

The supplemental program for women and infants therefore belongs to the amendment history of the act rather than to the 1966 text. In the early 1970s, specifically through a 1972 amendment enacted as Public Law 92-433, Congress added a special supplemental food program for women, infants, and children as a pilot, placed in section 17 of the Child Nutrition Act, and the program acquired permanent form later in that decade. Read on its own terms, the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 performed a defined set of tasks. It added breakfast as a pilot, renewed milk as a consolidated authorization, bought equipment for the schools least able to buy equipment, paid states to administer the expansion, tightened the focus of federal food assistance on needy children without converting that focus into an entitlement, and left both child care feeding and maternal and infant supplementation to later Congresses acting by amendment.

## The Supplemental Program and the Appropriation Line

The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 was, in its original form, a statute about meals served in institutions, and the program added to it in 1972 broke that pattern in two respects. The Special Supplemental Food Program for Women, Infants, and Children, known by its initials as WIC, reached persons who were not gathered in a school cafeteria, and it reached some of them before birth, through the nutrition of a pregnant woman. WIC was built as a grant program financed by annual appropriations, not as an entitlement under which federal payment followed each qualifying case as a matter of legal right. Eligibility rules defined who could be served. The appropriation defined how many would be served. That separation between qualification and payment is the defining structural fact of Section 17.

Creation came by amendment. Public Law 92-433, enacted on September 26, 1972, amended the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 and added the authority codified as Section 17 of that act, at 42 U.S.C. 1786 in the codification cited by the Congressional Research Service. The Congressional Research Service primer on WIC and the Department of Agriculture timeline agree that the 1972 legislation established WIC as a two-year pilot program, and the senator most closely associated with the legislation was Hubert Humphrey of Minnesota, named as sponsor in standard program histories. Congress authorized a test of a proposition pressed since the 1969 White House Conference on Food, Nutrition and Health, which had recommended special attention to the nutritional needs of low-income pregnant women and preschool children, according to the Department of Agriculture timeline.

The pilot was limited in scale, in duration, and in the categories it covered. Its authorized duration was two years. Eligibility extended to children only up to age four, and non-breastfeeding postpartum women were excluded, according to the program history summarized from Department of Agriculture sources. The Department of Agriculture timeline records that the first WIC clinic opened in Pineville, Kentucky, in 1974, and the 1972 law mandated an evaluation of the pilot, so Congress would judge measured results before committing the program to permanence. By the end of 1974, WIC was operating in 45 states, according to the Department of Agriculture based program history, and in fiscal year 1974 the program served an average of 88,000 participants per month, a figure reported by the Economic Research Service of the Department of Agriculture.

On October 7, 1975, Public Law 94-105 established WIC as a permanent program, according to the Congressional Research Service primer and the Department of Agriculture timeline. The 1975 legislation also widened the categories the pilot had narrowed. Eligibility extended to non-breastfeeding women up to six months postpartum, and the age limit for children rose from four years to five years, so that a child could participate up to the fifth birthday. Eligibility rested, after 1975, on three elements acting together: income, categorical status defined by life stage, and nutritional risk. In 1978, Public Law 95-627 defined nutritional risk in statute and established income eligibility standards linked to the standards for free and reduced price school meals. In 1989, Public Law 101-147 aligned income treatment with participation in [the food stamp program](/2019/09/01/food-stamp-act-1964-snap-history/), Medicaid, and Aid to Families with Dependent Children, simplifying the application process.

The first component was a supplemental food package, prescribed for the participant category rather than chosen freely by the household. The Department of Agriculture timeline records that two food packages were developed in 1973, one for infants and one for children and pregnant or breastfeeding women, including infant formula, milk, cheese, eggs, infant and adult cereals, and fruit juice. Food was delivered through instruments that specified what could be obtained, described in Congressional Research Service reporting as vouchers or checks listing the specific foods, redeemed at authorized retailers. The second component was nutrition education, offered through the clinic relationship that certified the participant. The third component was referral to health care and to other social services.

Administration followed the health setting rather than the school or welfare office. The Food and Nutrition Service in the Department of Agriculture administered the program federally and made grants to state agencies, which in the main were state health agencies, and those agencies worked through local agencies and clinics. A health professional assessed nutritional risk, staff verified income or adjunctive eligibility, and the clinic issued the food instrument for a certification period matched to the category. Pregnant women were certified for the duration of pregnancy and a short postpartum period, breastfeeding women in periods extending up to one year after delivery, and non-breastfeeding women only up to six months after delivery, according to the Congressional Research Service primer. Children were eligible until the fifth birthday.

Eligibility was a compound test, and each element did different work. Categorical eligibility fixed the population: pregnant women, postpartum women, breastfeeding women, infants, and children up to age five. Income eligibility fixed the economic band. Section 17 required income criteria to be used together with nutritional risk criteria, and the operative income limit was 185 percent of the federal poverty guidelines, the same standard prescribed for reduced price school meals under Section 9(b) of the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act, as Department of Agriculture guidance states in citing Section 17(d)(2)(A) of the Child Nutrition Act. Adjunctive eligibility supplied an alternative route to the same income conclusion. An applicant who participated in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, Medicaid, or Temporary Assistance for Needy Families could be treated as automatically income eligible, because participation in those programs rested on an income determination already made. The adjunctive route simplified proof; it did not waive the remaining elements, and the nutritional risk determination applied to adjunctively eligible applicants in the same manner as to applicants who documented income directly.

Nutritional risk was the element with no counterpart in the school meal programs, and it inserted a professional judgment into a federal eligibility rule. A competent professional at the clinic determined whether an applicant was at nutritional risk, on grounds that Congressional Research Service reporting described as clinical measurements, documented nutritionally related medical conditions, and dietary deficiencies. Public Law 95-627 in 1978 gave the concept a statutory definition, and in 1999 the program standardized nutrition risk criteria and began assigning individual nutrition risk priority levels, according to the Department of Agriculture based program history. Eligibility, risk assessment, and rationing were thus built from a single set of findings.

### Is WIC an entitlement program or a discretionary program?

WIC is a discretionary grant program, not an entitlement. Congress appropriates a fixed sum each year, the Food and Nutrition Service allocates that sum to state agencies, and clinics enroll eligible applicants only within the funded amount, using waiting lists and priority ranking when applications exceed available funds.

The mechanics of the discretionary structure run in a chain, and each link is a place where a fixed sum is divided rather than a place where a claim is paid. Congress acts first, in an annual appropriations law, and sets the funding level for the fiscal year. The authorization in Section 17 permits the program to exist and defines whom it may serve; the appropriation determines the resources available, and no spending flows from the authorization alone. The Food and Nutrition Service acts second, allocating the appropriated total among state agencies under formulas described in federal regulations. The Congressional Research Service primer describes those formulas as designed to guarantee a state enough money to maintain the prior year operating level and caseload, with adjustments for inflation, and then to distribute additional dollars toward grantees receiving less than a fair share, measured against the income eligible population and the estimated cost of projected participation. The state agency acts third, assigning caseload and funds to local agencies within the state grant. The clinic acts last, certifying applicants against the eligibility rules, but only into the participation level the assigned funds support.

The legal consequence sits at the end of that chain. A person who satisfied the categorical, income, and nutritional risk tests held eligibility, and eligibility was a necessary condition for benefits, but eligibility was not a legally enforceable entitlement to benefits. The Congressional Research Service primer states the reason in funding terms: because WIC funding is discretionary, the number of participants served is limited by the level of funding appropriated by Congress and by the allocation of those funds to each state. No provision required the federal government to appropriate additional money when eligible applications exceeded the funded caseload, and no applicant could compel payment from an exhausted grant in the manner available under an entitlement statute. State agencies could slow or stop new certifications, hold eligible applicants on waiting lists, apply the federal priority system so that funded places went first to applicants at highest nutritional risk, or, in severe cases described in Food and Nutrition Service caseload guidance, discontinue benefits beginning with the lowest priority categories after other alternatives were exhausted.

The school lunch structure in the same statutory family operated on the opposite principle, and a point by point comparison isolates what the appropriation line changed. In the National School Lunch Program, federal aid took the form of a cash reimbursement for every meal a participating school served that met federal requirements, with the largest subsidies attached to free and reduced price meals served to eligible children, as Congressional Research Service reporting describes the structure. The unit of federal obligation was the meal, and the obligation arose when the meal was served and claimed. If participation rose, federal reimbursement followed the additional meals at the rates fixed in law, and schools were also entitled by law to a quantity of Department of Agriculture foods per lunch served.

WIC inverted each element of that arrangement. The unit of federal support was the grant, allocated before participation occurred, rather than a reimbursement generated by each act of service. The decision that limited service sat with the appropriations process in WIC, made well before a particular applicant appeared at a clinic, whereas in school lunch the limiting decisions were the eligibility rules and the meal pattern requirements themselves. Under the reimbursement structure, the federal government absorbed the financial risk of higher than expected participation, because each additional qualifying meal generated an additional payment. Under the WIC grant structure, the risk of a shortfall fell on state agencies, on clinics, and ultimately on eligible applicants who waited, because the grant did not expand when applications did. WIC contained federal cost by fixing the sum and rationing within it, while school lunch contained cost through per meal rates, eligibility categories, and verification rules applied to an open ended flow of claims.

The Economic Research Service history reported average monthly participation rising from 88,000 in fiscal year 1974 to 1.9 million in 1980 and 4.5 million in 1990, reaching 7.41 million in 1997. Congressional Research Service tabulations of Department of Agriculture data extended the series: participation averaged 8.285 million in fiscal year 2007 and 9.175 million in fiscal year 2010, before declining to 8.663 million in fiscal year 2013 and 7.696 million in fiscal year 2016. Federal spending followed the same arc in the Congressional Research Service table, rising from 3.837 billion dollars in fiscal year 1997 to 6.671 billion dollars in fiscal year 2010, and then falling to 5.960 billion dollars in fiscal year 2016.

Two supplementary sources of value stretched the appropriated dollar. The first was the infant formula rebate system. Under competitive contracts, the manufacturer selected as the sole provider of formula for a state WIC program paid rebates on formula purchased with WIC instruments, and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities account explains that the state reimbursed the retailer at the retail price and then billed the contract manufacturer for the rebate, leaving the net cost to WIC well below the retail price. A Government Accountability Office figure quoted in program analyses placed rebate savings at about 800 million dollars in 1990, rising to more than 1.6 billion dollars in 1997, and rebates in 2007 amounted to about 1.9 billion dollars, close to 26 percent of total program expenditures for that year in the same analyses. The second source was time. Funds unspent in one fiscal year could carry over into the next, recovered funds could be reallocated, and a contingency fund stood behind the annual appropriation. Neither converted a capped grant into an open ended claim.

The periods of strain showed where the pressure landed. Before the rebate system matured, funding limits were visible at the clinic level: a Food and Nutrition Service official quoted in program analyses recalled that, before the infusion of infant formula rebates, many local agencies had waiting lists, and some could not certify older children, meaning children ages three and four, because of lack of funding. The mid 1990s then contested the structure itself. Proposals advanced in 1995 to fold WIC into a broader family nutrition block grant drew a Clinton administration analysis, preserved in the Clinton presidential library records, estimating that the block grant funding level for fiscal year 1996 would force states to remove 168,000 women, infants, and children from WIC and would leave the designated amount supporting an average annual caseload of 7.1 million participants against a projected year end caseload of 7.27 million. The estimate was an administration argument against a proposal rather than a description of an enacted outcome, and the block grant conversion was not enacted.

The government shutdown that began on October 1, 2013, supplied the clearest administrative demonstration of the distinction, because a lapse in appropriations acted on WIC and on entitlement programs through entirely different channels. The shutdown lasted 16 days, at the opening of fiscal year 2014, and WIC entered the lapse with no new annual appropriation in place. A Department of Agriculture memorandum summarized in National Agricultural Law Center reporting warned that states might have funds from rebates, spend forward authority, or other sources to continue operations for a week or so, and that contingency funds would help states without fully mitigating a shortfall for the entire month of October. The department released 100 million dollars in contingency funds, out of 125 million dollars on hand when the impasse began, and worked with states to distribute about 280 million dollars in unexpended funds from fiscal year 2013, figures entered in the Congressional Record in October 2013. The president of the National WIC Association, the Reverend Douglas Greenaway, stated in October 2013 that the combination should give state agencies enough money to keep clinics open until the end of that month, while warning that clinics would face closure and participants would be turned away if Congress failed to act before November 1.

Congress and the department built those bridging devices deliberately, and their design confirms the underlying classification. Carryover authority allowed a state agency to apply funds remaining from the prior year appropriation to the next year, smoothing the boundary between fiscal years and allowing underspending to offset pressure in a later period through recovery and reallocation. The contingency fund, maintained within the WIC account and available in any year when the appropriation and carryover proved insufficient, functioned as a reserve against forecasting error in food costs and participation. The Congressional Research Service primer describes the account in those terms, noting that obligated funds in a given year could include the annual appropriation, carryover, and contingency spending together.

Against that record, the broad pattern from the late 1990s onward requires a precise statement, because the pattern is easy to misread as a legal feature. The Congressional Research Service primer records that, since the late 1990s, funding provided was adequate to serve all eligible applicants, and that the priority system largely went unused during that period. The resemblance rested on a recurring political and appropriations outcome: estimators projected participation and food costs, appropriators funded the projection, and the department managed deviations with reallocation and reserves. The cap had not disappeared, and the legal position of the next applicant in a shortfall year remained what Section 17 and the appropriations structure made it, that of a qualified person waiting for a funded place.

The classification matters beyond WIC because the same question, entitlement or appropriation, decides the security of every adjacent program in the child nutrition and food assistance field, and programs serving overlapping families answer it differently. A household could hold a school lunch reimbursement claim generated by a meal served at school, a Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefit structured as an entitlement for eligible households, and a WIC certification that depended on a funded caseload slot, all at the same time and all administered within the same department. A recession, a food price increase, or a lapse in appropriations acted on each component through a different mechanism: meal reimbursements and entitlement benefits expanded or continued under their own payment rules, while the WIC grant remained the sum Congress had fixed until Congress acted again. The choice between structures therefore allocated forecasting risk between the federal Treasury and the applicant, determined whether a funding dispute in Congress could reach a clinic waiting room within weeks, and decided whether rationing would occur openly through eligibility rules or administratively through queues.

WIC waiting list practice shows what rationing inside a grant looks like when the queue becomes necessary, because federal regulations required state agencies to use a priority system to manage waiting lists when limited funding prevented all eligible applicants from being served. The system ranked applicants by nutritional risk rather than by order of application. The highest priority covered pregnant and breastfeeding women and infants with medical problems demonstrating a need for supplemental foods. The next priority covered infants whose mothers had medical problems during pregnancy demonstrating that need, or whose mothers had been program participants. Children with medical problems demonstrating a need for supplemental foods followed, then infants and pregnant or breastfeeding women at nutritional risk because of an inadequate dietary pattern, then children at nutritional risk because of an inadequate dietary pattern, then postpartum women with any nutritional risk. The lowest priority covered individuals certified solely because of homeless or migrant status and participants whose medical or dietary problems might develop without program benefits.

Section 17 thus contains two programs in a single text, one described by its eligibility rules and one described by its funding rule, and the second governs the first whenever money runs short. The funding program delivers that package only within a sum that Congress sets each year, that the Food and Nutrition Service divides by formula, and that state agencies translate into caseload. Between the pilot that Public Law 92-433 authorized in 1972 and the permanent program that Public Law 94-105 established in 1975, Congress settled the eligibility design quickly and left the funding question to be answered annually, and every later episode, from the waiting lists of the rebate era to the contingency releases of October 2013, was an answer given under that original settlement. The namable claim of the Child Nutrition Act, in its supplemental program, is that qualification and guarantee are different legal events, and that a statute can extend the first to millions of women, infants, and children while withholding the second from every one of them.

## Three Prices for One Lunch: Tiers, Thresholds, and Reimbursement

Behind a single menu in a participating school stood three federal categories, each with a different price for the child, a different obligation for the household, and a different payment for the school. A child certified for free meals paid nothing. A child certified for reduced price meals paid a capped charge set in federal law. A child in the paid category paid a price set largely by the local school food authority, subject to federal pricing rules. The categories formed the financial engine of the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 and of the National School Lunch Program that the 1966 act amended and extended, because the federal government paid a different amount for every meal in every category, every school day.

### How is eligibility for free and reduced price school meals determined?

Eligibility rests mainly on household income measured against federal poverty guidelines. In school year 2018 to 2019, free meals required income at or below 130 percent, reduced price meals allowed income above 130 percent and at or below 185 percent, and paid meals covered all other children in participating schools.

The free category carried the largest federal payment. For the child, a free meal meant a complete reimbursable lunch or breakfast with no charge at the point of service and no separate line, token, or public list identifying the child as needy, a protection against overt identification that federal law carried from the amendments of 1970 forward. For the household, free status meant that income, household size, or participation in another assistance program had placed the household at the lowest rung of the eligibility scale. For the school, a free meal drew the highest per meal cash reimbursement of the three categories, plus the value of federal commodity foods attached to each lunch served.

The reduced price category occupied the middle ground, and the middle ground had a price written into law. Under Title VIII of the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, the maximum charge rose from 20 cents for a reduced price lunch in school year 1980 to 1981 to 40 cents for a reduced price lunch from school year 1981 to 1982 forward, and from 10 cents for a reduced price breakfast in school year 1980 to 1981 to 30 cents for a reduced price breakfast from school year 1981 to 1982 forward. For the school, a reduced price meal drew a federal cash payment set at 40 cents less per lunch in the rates for school year 2018 to 2019 than the payment for a free lunch, a gap that matched the maximum charge to the child.

The paid category completed the structure. A paid meal went to a child whose household did not apply, whose household applied and was denied, or whose household income placed the child outside both subsidized bands. The child paid the local price, and the school received a comparatively small federal cash payment. The Congressional Research Service primer on school meals programs described paid reimbursement as approximately 30 cents per lunch in the rates summarized for school year 2018 to 2019, against approximately 3 dollars per lunch for a free meal in the same summary for school year 2018 to 2019.

The thresholds that separated the categories were percentages, and the percentages had a history that moved in two directions. Public Law 91-248, approved on May 14, 1970, changed the basis of the decision. The 1970 amendments directed that determinations rest on income poverty guidelines prescribed by the Secretary of Agriculture, required that by January 1, 1971, a child in a household with annual income at or below the applicable family size level in those guidelines receive meals free or at reduced cost, placed a ceiling of 20 cents on a reduced price meal in the 1970 statute, barred overt identification of recipients, and gave first priority to providing free meals to the neediest children. The Congressional Research Service described Public Law 91-248 as the law that established the first national eligibility guidelines, set at 100 percent of the federal poverty level, extended special assistance to all participating schools, and reduced the state matching requirement.

During the decade after 1970, Congress raised the thresholds in steps. The Congressional Research Service chronology recorded that the free threshold rose later in the 1970s to 125 percent of the federal poverty guidelines and the reduced price threshold rose to 195 percent. A separate Congressional Research Service history supplied intermediate steps: Public Law 92-433 in 1972 gave states an option to set free eligibility at up to 125 percent and reduced price eligibility at up to 150 percent, the reduced price ceiling rose to 175 percent in 1973 under Public Law 93-150, amendments in 1975 under Public Law 94-105 required schools to offer reduced price meals to children in households under 195 percent, and the Child Nutrition Amendments of 1978, Public Law 95-627, set the national standard for free meals at 125 percent starting in 1979.

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, reset the percentages to the figures that defined the system through school year 2018 to 2019. Title VIII of the 1981 act restricted reduced price eligibility from 195 percent of the federal poverty guidelines to 185 percent and set free eligibility at 130 percent, according to the Congressional Research Service history of child nutrition programs. The same act formed part of a larger reduction that the Congressional Research Service history measured at 1.4 billion dollars in the child nutrition budget for fiscal year 1982. The free threshold rose from 125 percent to 130 percent, a small expansion for the poorest band, while the outer boundary of the subsidized system contracted from 195 percent to 185 percent, removing the highest income slice of the reduced price population.

Percentages alone did not operate a cafeteria. Each year the percentages became dollar amounts through the income eligibility guidelines published by the Department of Agriculture. The federal poverty guidelines originated with the Department of Health and Human Services, and the Food and Nutrition Service multiplied the guidelines by 1.30 for free meals and by 1.85 for reduced price meals, rounded the results, and published tables by household size in the Federal Register for the school year that began on July 1. For a household of four in the 48 contiguous states and the District of Columbia, the guidelines effective from July 1, 2019, through June 30, 2020, set the free limit at 33,475 dollars in annual income for school year 2019 to 2020 and the reduced price limit at 47,638 dollars in annual income for school year 2019 to 2020. Separate and higher tables applied in Alaska and Hawaii, where living costs and the underlying poverty guidelines differed.

For most households, entry into a tier began with an application, and the application performed two functions at once. The form, submitted on paper or through an online system operated by the district, asked for the names of household members, the income of each household member and the frequency with which the income was received, the household size, a case number for a household that received benefits through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program or another covered program in place of income information, and a signature by an adult household member attesting to the information provided. A school official compared reported income and household size with the published guidelines and approved the household for free meals, approved the household for reduced price meals, or denied the application, with notice and a right to appeal. The same determination set the price a child paid in the lunch line and the rate at which the federal government reimbursed the school for that child. Approval of an ineligible household drew federal payments at a rate the household had not qualified for, and denial of an eligible household shifted cost to a household the statute aimed to relieve and reduced federal revenue to the district.

Verification tested a sample of approved applications after approval. Under Section 9 of the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act, as amended by the Child Nutrition and WIC Reauthorization Act of 2004, Public Law 108-265, local educational agencies verified a sample drawn under rules the 2004 act specified, with the standard sample described in Food and Nutrition Service guidance dated July 25, 2006, as the lesser of 3 percent or 3,000 of the approved applications on file on October 1, selected from error prone applications, with alternate sample sizes available to agencies that met conditions in federal regulation. A household selected for verification had to supply documentation of income, such as pay records or benefit statements, within a deadline set by the district. A household that failed to respond, or whose documents contradicted the approved status, lost the approved benefits or moved to a lower benefit level, subject to notice rules.

The Food Research and Action Center, a national antihunger organization, argued across reauthorization debates that household applications deterred eligible children from receiving meals and stigmatized children whose households qualified for assistance. The Department of Agriculture evaluation of the Community Eligibility Provision, released in 2014, supplied evidence that advocates cited: the evaluation found that eliminating household applications reduced administrative burdens on households and, to a lesser extent, on school districts. The School Nutrition Association, representing school nutrition professionals, pressed operator concerns in reauthorization materials, including staff time consumed by processing applications, conducting verification, and collecting unpaid meal charges. On the other side, the Government Accountability Office, in a report dated May 2019 and numbered GAO-19-389, held that the school meals programs had reported high estimated improper payment rates, that the Department of Agriculture had reported steps intended to lower error rates, and that the department had not comprehensively assessed fraud risks. Department officials who defended verification described sampling and documentation as safeguards for a program in which a self reported form unlocked a federal payment, and the prototype application work described in the May 2019 Government Accountability Office report aimed to reduce applicant error rather than to abandon the application itself.

Direct certification offered an exit from the application for children whose need had already been established by another program. A state agency or a school district matched enrollment records against records of participation in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and other covered programs, and a matched child was certified for free meals without a household application for school meals. The Child Nutrition and WIC Reauthorization Act of 2004, Public Law 108-265, required local educational agencies to directly certify children in food stamp households, the program renamed the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program in 2008, and required state agreements between the child nutrition agency and the food stamp agency to carry out the matching. Food and Nutrition Service guidance described a phased implementation that began for the largest school districts in July 2006 and covered all school districts by July 2008. The Food and Nutrition Service reported that direct certification rates rose from about 68 percent for school year 2007 to 2008 to nearly 90 percent in the school year reported in the department summary of direct certification studies released in the period before 2019. The matching machinery behind that result belonged to [the wider administrative law governing nutrition assistance](/2019/12/01/snap-administration-law/).

The effect of direct certification reached beyond convenience. A directly certified child was not subject to verification of a household application, because no household application for school meals existed to verify. Direct certification also exposed a boundary problem. A household could meet the income test for free meals and receive no benefit through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, because the household had not applied for that program, because the household failed a separate rule of that program, or because administrative records contained an identifier that failed to match. For such a household, the school application remained the only path to a free meal, so direct certification reduced dependence on applications without eliminating the application system.

Categorical eligibility overlapped with direct certification and extended beyond income. Under the rules in place for school year 2018 to 2019, a child was automatically eligible for free meals, without a separate income test, when the child belonged to a household that received benefits through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, or Temporary Assistance for Needy Families cash assistance, or when the child was enrolled in Head Start, was in foster care, was a migrant child, was a runaway child, or was a homeless child. A household could establish categorical eligibility by listing a case number on the household application, and a district could establish categorical eligibility through direct certification or through documentation supplied by a liaison or an official responsible for the relevant program, including a homeless liaison operating under the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act and a migrant education program coordinator. The Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010, Public Law 111-296, placed foster children into statute as categorically eligible for free school meals, a provision the Congressional Research Service summary of the 2010 act described as placing into law a rule similar to the regulation that had preceded the 2010 act.

Reimbursement mechanics translated the tiers into money through formulas published each year. The Department of Agriculture announced national average payments and maximum reimbursement rates for lunches and breakfasts each July, with adjustments prescribed on July 1 based on changes in the Food Away From Home series of the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics of the Department of Labor. For school year 2018 to 2019, the federal reimbursement for many schools in the contiguous states stood at 3 dollars and 37 cents for a free lunch in school year 2018 to 2019, 2 dollars and 97 cents for a reduced price lunch in school year 2018 to 2019, and 37 cents for a paid lunch in school year 2018 to 2019, figures published in the Federal Register on July 19, 2018. The 40 cent gap between the free and reduced price lunch rates in school year 2018 to 2019 matched the maximum reduced price charge, and the gap between either subsidized rate and the paid rate showed how heavily the system weighted certified need over universal support.

Inside the cash payment, two statutory layers did distinct work. Section 4 of the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act provided general cash assistance for lunches served, at two payment levels: a lower level for school food authorities in which less than 60 percent of lunches served during the second preceding school year had been served free or at a reduced price, and a higher level for authorities at or above 60 percent on the same measure, a differential that federal payment analyses for school year 2008 to 2009 illustrated at 2 cents per lunch for school year 2008 to 2009. Section 11 of the same act provided special assistance payments that supplemented Section 4 payments for free and reduced price lunches, with the Section 11 factor for a reduced price lunch set at 40 cents less per lunch in the rates described for school year 2018 to 2019 than the factor for a free lunch. Special assistance descended from the amendment that added Section 11 in 1962 under Public Law 87-823, which authorized additional assistance for schools in poor economic areas, and from the expansion in Public Law 91-248 in 1970, which extended special assistance to all participating schools.

Geography altered the rates through separate schedules for Alaska and Hawaii. Federal payment notices stated the reason in plain terms repeated across years, including the notice for the period from July 1, 2008, through June 30, 2009: average payments and maximum reimbursements for Alaska and Hawaii were higher than the payments and reimbursements for all other states because of a higher cost of living in Alaska and Hawaii during school year 2008 to 2009. The Alaska and Hawaii schedules applied the same tier logic at higher dollar levels, so a free lunch in Alaska drew a larger federal payment than a free lunch in the contiguous states in the same school year, and the differential reflected freight, food, and labor costs rather than a different judgment about the need of the child.

A third cash element arrived with the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010. Section 201 of Public Law 111-296 created performance based cash assistance for school food authorities certified by a state agency as compliant with the updated meal pattern and nutrition standards, set at an additional 6 cents per reimbursable lunch available beginning October 1, 2012, and adjusted annually thereafter. The performance payment differed from the tier payments in its trigger: tier payments followed the certified status of the child who ate the meal, while the performance payment followed the certified compliance of the authority that served the meal. Federal payment notices recorded that the performance amount had risen through annual adjustment to an additional 7 cents per lunch available beginning July 1, 2019, for school year 2019 to 2020.

Commodity support supplied food rather than cash, through an entitlement layer and a bonus layer. Under Section 6 of the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act, schools were entitled to a per meal value of Department of Agriculture foods, called entitlement commodities, in an amount set by law and adjusted each July 1 by the annual percentage change in a three month average value of the Price Index for Foods Used in Schools and Institutions. The Food and Nutrition Service notice for the period from July 1, 2018, through June 30, 2019, set the national average minimum value of donated foods, or cash in lieu of donated foods, at 23.50 cents per lunch for school year 2018 to 2019, an increase of one quarter of a cent per lunch from the rate for school year 2017 to 2018. States received an entitlement amount based on reimbursable lunches served in the prior year and selected foods from Department of Agriculture offerings within that amount, so the entitlement behaved like a restricted credit for food. Bonus commodities stood outside the entitlement. The department acquired bonus foods through price support and surplus removal operations, including purchases financed through Section 32 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1935, and offered bonus foods to schools without charge against the entitlement amount.

The money side of the system operated as an entitlement keyed to meals served, and that feature explained the financial preoccupation with participation. Federal reimbursement followed a count: schools recorded daily counts of meals by category, reported monthly counts to the state agency, and received payment for meals that met program requirements and matched a certified category. No meal served meant no reimbursement for that meal, and a child who was absent, brought food from home, bought a competitive food item, or left campus for lunch generated no lunch reimbursement for the district. A rise in participation among children certified for free meals raised federal revenue at the highest per meal rate. A fall in participation among paying children reduced meal counts while cafeteria labor, equipment, and facility costs remained largely fixed for the school year. District food service directors therefore read participation reports as revenue reports, and outreach that raised participation among eligible children, through application assistance, direct certification matching, or categorical certification of homeless and foster children, functioned as revenue work as well as nutrition work.

## From Pilots to Permanence: The Program Family Grows

The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 was built as a framework of experiments. Congress authorized a school breakfast pilot that lasted two fiscal years, carried a milk program with older roots into the new statute, and left the lunch program under the National School Lunch Act of 1946 as the established center of federal child feeding. Two years later, Congress added a second experiment, a special food service pilot for children outside school hours and outside the school year. The expansion era that followed tested each experiment in sequence. Some pilots were extended and then converted into permanent law. One pilot divided into two programs with different settings and different funding histories. Permanence rarely arrived in a single vote. Permanence accumulated through repeated extensions, through splits that gave each activity a clearer identity, and through periodic reauthorization acts that revised permanent programs and time limited programs in the same package.

School breakfast supplied the clearest case of a pilot that earned permanent status. The Congressional Research Service account records that the Child Nutrition Act of 1966, Public Law 89-642, authorized the School Breakfast Program for two fiscal years only, and that states were directed to give priority to schools drawing attendance from areas in which poor economic conditions existed and to schools to which a substantial proportion of enrolled children had to travel long distances each day. The authorization was short, and the first claim on limited funds belonged to schools identified by poverty or by distance. Breakfast entered federal law as a targeted remedy for children whose morning nutrition was thought to be weakest. The same statute gave the Secretary of Agriculture authority to provide higher reimbursements to schools in severe need, an acknowledgment that a single national rate would not carry the same weight in a school serving very poor children as in a school with a more affluent enrollment.

Expansion came first through a widening of the priority rules. According to the Congressional Research Service, Congress expanded the breakfast priority to include schools in which there was a special need for improving the nutrition and dietary practices of children of working mothers and children from low income families, a change carried in Public Law 92-32 in the early 1970s. The amendment also recognized a household condition, the absence of a parent at home in the morning because of work. Repeated extensions carried the pilot through the intervening years, and the decisive change arrived in 1975. The National School Lunch Act and Child Nutrition Act Amendments of 1975, Public Law 94-105, enacted on October 7, 1975, extended the authorization for appropriations for the school breakfast program indefinitely, in the language summarized by Congress for the conference report on the bill. Congressional Research Service histories describe the same event in plainer terms: the breakfast program, piloted in 1966 and regularly extended, was made permanent in 1975. The 1975 act also stated a national policy that breakfast should be made available in all schools where breakfast was needed, and directed the Secretary of Agriculture to carry out a program of information to advance that policy.

The severe need device survived the transition to permanence and became a durable feature of breakfast finance. Under the provisions described by the Congressional Research Service, a school qualified as a severe need school for breakfast purposes when 40 percent or more of the lunches served at the school were served free or at a reduced price, measured by reference to the second preceding school year. A school crossing that threshold received higher reimbursement for free and reduced price breakfasts than the standard rate provided. Eligibility turned on a count of subsidized lunches that school food authorities already recorded for the lunch program. The design concentrated extra federal money where subsidized lunch service was heaviest, which was the operational meaning of severe need.

Participation figures published by the Food and Nutrition Service of the Department of Agriculture trace the effect of permanence. Average daily participation stood at 0.5 million children in fiscal year 1970, before permanence, and reached 1.8 million children in fiscal year 1975, the year of the permanent authorization. Participation reached 3.6 million children in fiscal year 1980, eased to 3.4 million children in fiscal year 1985, and then resumed a long climb, reaching 4.0 million children in fiscal year 1990, 6.3 million children in fiscal year 1995, and 7.5 million children in fiscal year 2000. The fact sheet reporting those figures also recorded 9.7 million children participating each day in fiscal year 2006 and 10.1 million children in fiscal year 2007, with 8.1 million of the 2007 participants receiving meals free or at a reduced price. The figures describe a program that grew rapidly after permanent authorization, contracted slightly in a period of federal budget restraint, and then grew for two further decades.

Summer food service grew from the second pilot, and its history turned on the calendar. The problem was structural. A child who received a subsidized lunch, and later a subsidized breakfast, during the school year lost that source of meals when school closed for the summer, even though household income and nutritional need did not change with the season. The Congressional Research Service records that in 1968 Congress authorized the Special Food Service Program for Children, a pilot program to fund meals in summer and child care settings, in Public Law 90-302. The Congress record for that statute identifies Public Law 90-302 as an act to amend the National School Lunch Act to strengthen and expand food service programs for children, which fixes the pilot in the lunch act rather than in the Child Nutrition Act. The National Academies timeline describes Public Law 90-302 as a three year pilot, and records a further extension in Public Law 92-433, which carried the pilot for another three years. The pilot provided the first federal assistance for summer meals and the first dedicated federal assistance for meals in child care settings, according to the Congressional Research Service, and the pilot was targeted to areas with poor economic conditions and a high proportion of working mothers. Two distinct needs had been placed inside one pilot, one seasonal and one continuous.

The division of the pilot in 1975 gave each need a program of its own. Public Law 94-105, the same 1975 statute that made breakfast permanent, split the special food service pilot into the separate Child Care Food Program and the Summer Food Service Program, according to both the Congressional Research Service and the National Academies timeline. The summer successor retained a focus on institutions in low income areas, and meals at summer sites were provided free to all children at the site, a design that avoided individual eligibility determinations during a short season. The permanence question for the two successors then diverged in form, although the Congressional Research Service primer groups both programs together and describes separate programs authorized in 1975 as made permanent in 1978. The Child Nutrition Amendments of 1978, Public Law 95-627, enacted on November 10, 1978, supplied that date. For the summer program, a later Congressional Research Service overview draws a finer distinction. Under that overview, the authority for the Summer Food Service Program itself is permanent, while the authorization of appropriations for the program was time limited and expired on September 30, 2015 in the cycle that the overview described, with the program continuing to operate because appropriations acts provided funding.

The child care branch of the 1968 pilot followed the cleaner path to permanence. The Government Accountability Office report on the Child Care Food Program issued in 1980 stated that the program began as a temporary program in 1968 and became permanent in 1978 when Congress enacted the Child Nutrition Amendments of 1978, Public Law 95-627. The same report stated that the 1978 legislation changed reimbursement methods to provide greater financial assistance to providers and provided start up and advance funding to encourage expansion. The National Academies timeline adds that the 1978 amendments eliminated the means test for family child care homes and separated reimbursement of sponsor administrative costs from meal reimbursement for those homes, changes that lowered barriers for small home based providers. In fiscal year 1969 the child care food effort served about 8 million meals to almost 40,000 children, figures quoted in the Government Accountability Office report. By fiscal year 1979, after permanence, expenditures reached about 192 million dollars and more than 650,000 children were served approximately 400 million meals.

The program then crossed a boundary that the 1968 pilot had not contemplated, the boundary between child care and adult care. The Adult Day Care Handbook of the Department of Agriculture records that the Older Americans Act Amendments of 1987 authorized participation of eligible adult day care centers, and a Department of Agriculture memorandum of August 7, 2012 states that the adult component was authorized by that statute and codified at 7 CFR 226.19a. The Congress summary of the 1987 statute, Public Law 100-175, describes the operative amendment directly: the act amended the National School Lunch Act to make adult day care centers eligible under the child care food program for reimbursement for meals or supplements served to elderly persons or chronically impaired disabled persons. Two years later, the program name caught up with the change. The Pennsylvania Department of Education program history records that Public Law 101-147, the reauthorization act of 1989, changed the name of the Child Care Food Program to the Child and Adult Care Food Program, and the amendment notes to the governing statute, 42 U.S.C. 1766, confirm that Public Law 101-147 substituted the new heading for section 17 of the National School Lunch Act. A program born in a 1968 pilot for children had become, by 1989, a program serving children in centers and homes and adults in day care under one statutory section.

A further change in 1996 altered how generously the program treated one class of provider, and the change came from welfare legislation rather than from a child nutrition bill. The Economic Research Service of the Department of Agriculture reported that [the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996](/2019/08/01/welfare-reform-act-1996-guide/), Public Law 104-193, established a two tier structure of meal reimbursement rates for family child care homes. Homes located in low income areas, or operated by persons with incomes at or below 185 percent of the federal poverty guideline, were designated as Tier 1, and rates for Tier 1 homes were comparable to the rates that had existed for all homes before the 1996 act. Homes that did not meet the low income criteria were designated as Tier 2 and received lower reimbursements, although a Tier 2 home could be reimbursed at Tier 1 rates for meals served to children whose household income met the same threshold. The same research program found an early cost. Participation by child care homes fell 6 percent between 1997 and 1998, and the number of sponsoring organizations fell 2 percent, a decline larger than the trend in earlier years would have predicted, although the researchers stated that the share of the decline caused by tiering, as against other changes in the child care market, could not be determined. Tiering stands in the expansion era as a counterpoint to growth.

The Special Milk Program moved in the opposite direction from every other member of the family, and the reason was success elsewhere. The Congressional Research Service primer records that the Special Milk Program was created in 1954, regularly extended, and made permanent in 1970, and the Congressional Research Service background report adds that the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 formally authorized the program and based the program on predecessor school milk efforts that had operated since the 1940s. The program reimbursed milk served in schools and institutions that did not take part in the meal service programs. Each school that joined the lunch program, and each school that added breakfast, removed children from the population that milk alone served, because a full meal under the lunch or breakfast program already included milk. A fact sheet of the Food and Nutrition Service stated the causal link directly: expansion of the National School Lunch Program and the School Breakfast Program, which include milk, had led to a substantial reduction in the Special Milk Program since a peak in the late 1960s. The program served nearly 3 billion half pints of milk in 1969, 1.8 billion half pints in 1980, and 181 million half pints in 1990, and in fiscal year 2012 the program served over 61 million half pints. The milk program contracted to a residue while the meal programs absorbed the mainstream of school feeding.

### Is the Special Milk Program an entitlement program?

The Congressional Research Service classified the Special Milk Program with the other child nutrition meal programs as an appropriated entitlement in its funding tables. Federal money followed each qualifying half pint of milk at set rates, although the obligation depended on annual appropriations and on milk served outside the other meal programs.

Entitlement status across the family rests on a distinction that the Congressional Research Service explained for the child nutrition account. Most child nutrition programs are appropriated entitlements, a term that means the authorizing statute establishes a legal obligation to make payments, while the money that fulfills the obligation is provided in annual appropriations acts. Applied program by program, the funding table published by the Congressional Research Service classified each of the following programs in the appropriated mandatory category, the budget category that corresponds to appropriated entitlement funding: the National School Lunch Program, the School Breakfast Program, the Child and Adult Care Food Program, the Summer Food Service Program, and the Special Milk Program. For lunch, breakfast, summer food, and child and adult care food, federal reimbursement follows qualifying meals and snacks served to eligible children, and for the adult component, qualifying meals served to eligible adults in day care. For milk, federal reimbursement follows qualifying half pints. The classification depends on the mechanism, an open ended per unit reimbursement funded through appropriations in amounts driven by the volume of qualifying service.

Two qualifications keep that classification accurate. The first concerns permanence of appropriations authority. A Congressional Research Service overview of reauthorization stated that many child nutrition programs are permanently authorized and hold permanent authorizations of appropriations with no expiration date, and that the main functions of the lunch, breakfast, child and adult care food, and Special Milk programs fall in that class. The same overview placed the Summer Food Service Program in a different class: program authority permanent, authorization of appropriations time limited. The second qualification concerns a program that sits beside the meal programs in the same reauthorization bills without sharing entitlement status. The Special Supplemental Food Program for Women, Infants, and Children, known as WIC, was reauthorized together with the child nutrition programs in 1989, 1998, and 2004, and the Congressional Research Service funding categorization lists WIC funds as discretionary, meaning that annual appropriations set the level of service rather than following an open ended obligation.

Reauthorization supplied the rhythm by which this family was maintained. The Child Nutrition and WIC Reauthorization Act of 1989, Public Law 101-147, required the Department of Agriculture to create a standardized process for state review of school food authority administration of the lunch and breakfast programs, according to the Congressional Research Service, and the same act performed the renaming of the child care food program. The William F. Goodling Child Nutrition Reauthorization Act of 1998, Public Law 105-336, enacted on October 31, 1998, extended expiring authorizations for child nutrition and commodity assistance programs and the WIC program through fiscal year 2003, and significantly expanded federal subsidies, through the lunch program and the child and adult care food program, for snacks served in after school programs. The Child Nutrition and WIC Reauthorization Act of 2004, Public Law 108-265, enacted on June 30, 2004, extended virtually all expiring authorities through fiscal year 2009, in the summary given in a Congressional Research Service account, and placed program integrity at the center of the school meal changes, setting a sample size for verification of household applications for free and reduced price meals, focusing verification on error prone applications near the income eligibility thresholds, and requiring added state administrative reviews of school food authorities with high levels of administrative error.

A permanent program does not need a reauthorization act in order to exist, and a lapse in reauthorization does not by itself end reimbursement for free lunches or breakfasts, because appropriations acts can carry a permanently authorized program forward. Reauthorization instead gives Congress a scheduled occasion to amend permanent law in a coordinated way, adjusting eligibility rules, reimbursement structures, verification duties, and administrative requirements across several programs at once, and to extend the authorities that are not permanent, among which the Congressional Research Service lists the summer program appropriations authorization, state administrative expense assistance, and WIC. A Congressional Research Service report written during the 105th Congress observed that appropriations authority for several programs, including the summer food program, was scheduled to expire, prompting the 1998 action, and that while other programs such as school lunch and breakfast were permanently authorized, significant changes in the permanent programs were also considered as part of the reauthorization legislation. Reauthorization was the regular legislative instrument by which a permanent structure was revised and its time limited companions were carried forward in the same bill. That legislative rhythm had parallels in [the recurring renewal structure of farm legislation](/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/).

Jurisdiction over that instrument was divided in a pattern that surprises readers who assume that a Department of Agriculture program belongs wholly to agriculture committees. The Congressional Research Service primer states that congressional jurisdiction over the three governing laws, the National School Lunch Act in its renamed form, the Child Nutrition Act, and Section 32 of the act of August 24, 1935, has typically been exercised by the Senate Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry, by the House Committee on Education and the Workforce, and, to a limited extent, by the House Committee on Agriculture in matters relating to commodity food assistance and Section 32. The Senate agriculture committee thus held authorizing jurisdiction in that chamber, while authorizing jurisdiction in the House rested with the education committee. By the end of the expansion era, the family that the 1966 act had sketched in pilots had settled into permanent law for lunch, breakfast, child and adult care food, and milk, a permanent summer authority with a recurring appropriations question, and a reauthorization calendar that returned the whole family to the same two committees at regular intervals for revision.

## Feeding Everyone in the Building: Community Eligibility and the Standards Fight

The Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010, Public Law 111-296, signed on December 13, 2010, changed the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 in two directions at once. One direction concerned access, and its central device was a meal service option that allowed a high poverty school to serve breakfast and lunch at no charge to every enrolled student. The other direction concerned content, and its central device was a directive to the Department of Agriculture to rewrite the nutritional composition of the meals themselves, with a second directive covering foods sold outside the meal program during the school day. Section 104 of Public Law 111-296, titled Eliminating Individual Applications Through Community Eligibility, supplied the access device. Section 201 supplied the meal pattern directive, and Section 208 supplied the authority over other foods sold in school. The two directions interacted through September 15, 2019, and the argument over the standards drew on participation, cost, dietary quality, and discarded food, with each body of evidence assembled by named institutions and researchers holding opposed positions on federal prescription.

### How does community eligibility decide which schools qualify?

A school, a group of schools, or an entire district qualifies when identified students, meaning students certified for free meals without a household application through programs such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, make up at least 40 percent of enrollment, measured with direct certification data on a four year cycle.

The statutory term was identified students, and Section 104(a) defined the category by documentation rather than by application. An identified student was a student certified for free meals based on documentation of benefit receipt or categorical eligibility, without a household application. The routes named in program guidance included direct certification through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families program, participation in the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, and categorical eligibility attached to foster care placement, Head Start enrollment, and homeless, migrant, or runaway status. The share of enrolled students who were identified students was termed the identified student percentage. A local educational agency could elect the option for an individual school, for a group of schools, or for all schools in the district, and the percentage could be calculated for a grouping as a whole, so that a school below the threshold on its own figures could participate within a grouping whose combined percentage reached 40 percent. Election ran in four year cycles, with annual data updates permitted, a higher percentage usable when data showed an increase, and the original percentage retainable for the remainder of the cycle when data showed a decrease.

Reimbursement under community eligibility did not follow the free, reduced price, and paid tiers that governed conventional operation. The statute instead supplied a formula. The identified student percentage was multiplied by a statutory multiplier of 1.6, and the product, capped at 100 percent, became the percentage of meals claimed at the free reimbursement rate, with the remaining meals claimed at the paid rate and no meals claimed at the reduced price rate. A school at the 40 percent threshold therefore claimed 64 percent of its meals at the free rate and 36 percent at the paid rate, and a school at 62.5 percent or higher claimed every meal at the free rate. The multiplier rested on an administrative estimate, described in Department of Education guidance, that the count of identified students understated the count who would qualify for free or reduced price meals through applications, because some eligible households did not participate in the underlying benefit programs. Section 104(a) set the multiplier at 1.6 and authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to adjust the figure. Any difference between formula reimbursement and operating cost remained a local responsibility, as it had under the earlier Provision 2 and Provision 3 options, which had required household applications in a base year.

Implementation proceeded in stages administered by the Food and Nutrition Service. The option opened in the 2011 to 2012 school year in Illinois, Kentucky, and Michigan. The District of Columbia, New York, Ohio, and West Virginia joined for the 2012 to 2013 school year, and Florida, Georgia, Maryland, and Massachusetts joined for the 2013 to 2014 school year, with jurisdictions selected from among states that applied for early implementation. The option became available in every state on July 1, 2014, for the 2014 to 2015 school year. The Food Research and Action Center, an anti hunger research and advocacy organization that tracked participation, reported more than 14,000 participating schools in that year. Its report titled Community Eligibility: The Key to Hunger-Free Schools, released on June 1, 2019, counted 28,614 schools in 4,698 school districts participating in the 2018 to 2019 school year, with more than 13.6 million children attending participating schools, a 14 percent increase over the 2017 to 2018 school year, participation by 64.6 percent of the schools the organization classified as eligible, and participation rates of 80 percent or more among eligible schools in thirteen states.

The case made for the provision by its sponsors and by organizations promoting adoption rested on one administrative claim and one social claim, and both appeared in the legislative record of Public Law 111-296. The Senate committee report accompanying the legislation described community eligibility as a device to increase access to free meals, to reduce paperwork for schools and families by eliminating applications, and to eliminate stigma attached to free meals in the cafeteria. Conventional operation required a school food authority to distribute, collect, process, and verify household applications each year, to maintain eligibility rosters, and to categorize each meal by the eligibility status of the student who received it. Community eligibility removed the application process, removed eligibility determination at the point of service, and reduced the claiming record to a count of meals served multiplied by a fixed claiming percentage. James Weill, president of the Food Research and Action Center, stated in connection with the June 2019 report that participation increased under community eligibility and that removing the distinction between students who paid and students who received free meals removed the perception that school meals served only low income children. The countervailing consideration was fiscal. Because the formula reimbursed a fixed share of meals at the paid rate in any school below 62.5 percent, a district weighing election had to project whether formula reimbursement, together with state or local funds, covered the cost of serving all students without charge, and districts near the 40 percent threshold faced the largest projected gap.

The standards half of the 2010 statute proceeded from a different evidentiary base. Section 201 directed the Secretary of Agriculture to promulgate regulations updating the meal patterns and nutrition standards for the National School Lunch Program and the School Breakfast Program based on the recommendations of the Institute of Medicine of the National Academies and consistent with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. The Institute of Medicine released its report, School Meals: Building Blocks for Healthy Children, in October 2009, recommending patterns aligned with the 2005 Dietary Guidelines for Americans, with increased fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, milk limited to low fat and fat free varieties, calorie levels by age group, and staged reductions in sodium. The Department of Agriculture described the rulemaking as the first major revision of the meal patterns in more than fifteen years. The proposed rule appeared in January 2011, and the final rule, titled Nutrition Standards in the National School Lunch and School Breakfast Programs, was published on January 26, 2012, at 77 Federal Register 4088, with implementation phased from July 1, 2012.

The January 2012 final rule reorganized the lunch and breakfast patterns around separate components and grade group ranges. Fruits and vegetables became separate components, each offered daily at lunch, with weekly requirements for vegetable subgroups covering dark green, red and orange, beans and peas, starchy, and other vegetables. Grain requirements moved in two steps, with at least half of grains offered required to be whole grain rich in the initial period and all grains offered required to be whole grain rich by the 2014 to 2015 school year, with whole grain rich defined as a product containing at least 50 percent whole grains, with any remaining grain enriched. Milk requirements limited flavored milk to fat free varieties and unflavored milk to low fat or fat free varieties. The rule set minimum and maximum calorie ranges for meals averaged over the school week for three grade groups, kindergarten through grade five, grades six through eight, and grades nine through twelve, and set limits on saturated fat and trans fat. Sodium requirements operated as staged maximums termed Target 1, Target 2, and a final target, with Target 1 effective in the 2014 to 2015 school year, Target 2 scheduled for the 2017 to 2018 school year, and the final target scheduled for the 2022 to 2023 school year. Companion breakfast provisions followed a parallel structure, with the full fruit and whole grain requirements for breakfasts phased one year behind the lunch schedule in the initial steps.

Section 208 extended federal nutrition standards beyond the reimbursable meal to all foods sold in school during the school day, the category termed competitive foods. The Department of Agriculture published the interim final rule, titled Nutrition Standards for All Foods Sold in School and known as Smart Snacks in School, on June 28, 2013, effective July 1, 2014. The interim final rule applied to a la carte items, vending machines, school stores, and snack bars during the school day, and it required a food to be a whole grain rich product, to have a fruit, vegetable, dairy product, or protein food as its first ingredient, or to be a combination food containing a stated quantity of fruit or vegetable, together with nutrient limits on calories, sodium, fat, and sugar. Beverage standards limited portion sizes by school level, with additional beverages permitted in high schools. The department issued a final competitive foods rule on July 29, 2016, that maintained the interim final standards with limited modifications. Fundraiser sales were addressed through state discretion to exempt infrequent fundraisers, and foods brought from home were outside the rule.

The 2010 statute paired the standards with a payment device implemented by an interim final rule on certification published on April 27, 2012. A school food authority certified by its state agency as complying with the updated standards received an additional six cents for each reimbursable lunch served, available from October 1, 2012, and adjusted annually for inflation in later years, with the payment withheld if a subsequent administrative review found the authority out of compliance. The Senate committee report recorded the Institute of Medicine estimate that compliance with its recommended patterns would raise food costs by between four percent and nine percent per lunch, a range against which the adequacy of the six cent increment was debated throughout the implementation period.

The public health case for the standards began with meal composition. The School Nutrition and Meal Cost Study, conducted for the Food and Nutrition Service by Mathematica, compared menu data from the 2009 to 2010 school year, collected in the fourth School Nutrition Dietary Assessment Study, with menu data from the 2014 to 2015 school year, and its findings released in April 2019 reported that total Healthy Eating Index 2010 scores rose from 58 percent of the maximum score to 82 percent for lunches and from 50 percent to 71 percent for breakfasts. Gearan and Fox reported component scores for whole grains, refined grains, and empty calories each rising by more than twenty points in the same comparison. A study by Donna Johnson and colleagues at the University of Washington, published in JAMA Pediatrics in 2016, measured meals selected by students in a Washington district before and after implementation and reported improvement in mean nutritional quality, measured by mean adequacy ratio and energy density, alongside participation rates that held steady in the district studied. Juliana Cohen of the Harvard T. H. Chan School of Public Health and Merrimack College and Marlene Schwartz of the Rudd Center for Food Policy and Obesity at the University of Connecticut argued in their published analyses that improvement in what schools served was matched by improvement in what students ate, pointing to School Nutrition and Meal Cost Study data showing participation of 61 percent in schools serving the highest scoring lunches against 50 percent in schools serving the lowest scoring lunches. Agriculture Secretary Tom Vilsack, announcing the January 2012 final rule with First Lady Michelle Obama at an elementary school in Alexandria, Virginia, presented the standards as the application of Institute of Medicine recommendations to a program serving more than thirty million children on a school day.

The local control and cost case began with participation and revenue. The Government Accountability Office reported in January 2014, in report GAO-14-104, that nationwide participation in the National School Lunch Program declined by 1.2 million students, or 3.7 percent, from the 2010 to 2011 school year through the 2012 to 2013 school year, after a long period of steady increase, driven by a decrease of 1.6 million students who paid full price, partly offset by increases among students receiving free meals. A follow up study released in October 2015 reported a decline of 1.4 million students, or 4.5 percent, between the 2010 to 2011 and 2013 to 2014 school years, with the share of enrolled students participating falling from 62 percent to 58 percent, and seven of eight states interviewed reporting that challenges with student acceptance of changes made to comply with the new requirements contributed to the decrease, with required increases in paid lunch prices identified as an additional factor. Patricia Montague, chief executive officer of the School Nutrition Association, stated in releasing the association 2015 position paper that some regulations under the statute had increased costs and waste and had caused students to leave the meal program, and the association asked Congress for increased funding and for flexibility in menu planning. Julia Bauscher, president of the association and nutrition director for Jefferson County Public Schools in Louisville, Kentucky, stated that school nutrition professionals supported the goals of the statute and were concerned about the participation decline under its regulatory requirements. The position paper specified the flexibility requested, covering maintenance of Target 1 sodium levels pending further study, restoration of the half whole grain rich requirement in place of the all whole grain rich requirement, local discretion over whether a student was required to take a fruit or vegetable for a meal to be reimbursable, and permission for items served in the reimbursable meal to be sold a la carte. On cost, the association cited a Department of Agriculture estimate that the new rules added 1.2 billion dollars to the cost of preparing school meals in fiscal year 2015 alone, and survey results in which more than half of responding operators anticipated that their programs would not break even. The School Nutrition and Meal Cost Study reported average reported cost of 3.81 dollars for a reimbursable lunch in the 2014 to 2015 school year against average federal cash reimbursement of 3.32 dollars. Lynn Harvey, chief of school nutrition services for the North Carolina Department of Public Instruction, stated to an association briefing in June 2015 that North Carolina programs had complied from 2013 onward while the state recorded a five percent decline in lunch participation and a decline of twenty million dollars in a la carte revenue under the Smart Snacks requirements.

Plate waste supplied the most contested evidence in the standards argument, and the published record contained findings in both directions, measured by different methods in different populations. Juliana Cohen and colleagues measured plate waste among 1,030 students in four elementary and middle schools in an urban, low income district in the fall of 2011 and the fall of 2012, and the study, published in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine in April 2014, reported that fruit selection increased by 23 percent after implementation, that consumption of selected vegetables increased by 16.2 percent, and that the standards did not result in increased food waste on the measures used. Cohen and colleagues also reported the level of waste in the same data, with students discarding between 60 percent and 75 percent of vegetables served and about 40 percent of fruit served, both before and after implementation. Marlene Schwartz and colleagues tracked twelve middle schools in New Haven, Connecticut, from the spring of 2012 through the spring of 2014, and the study, published in Childhood Obesity in 2015, reported that the share of students selecting fruit rose from 54 percent to 66 percent, that the share of selected vegetables consumed rose from 46 percent to 64 percent, that the share of entrees consumed rose from 71 percent to 84 percent, and that total plate waste did not increase.

Findings and reports in the opposed direction measured waste levels that the flexibility case cited as consequences of the standards. Carmen Byker and colleagues measured food waste over one week in March 2013 in prekindergarten and kindergarten classes at one elementary school, after the standards took effect, and the study, published in the Journal of Nutrition Education and Behavior in 2014, reported that 45.3 percent of food served was wasted, with vegetables wasted at 51.4 percent and entrees at 51.0 percent. The School Nutrition Association 2015 position paper cited research by Cornell University and Brigham Young University researchers on the requirement that students take a fruit or vegetable with each reimbursable meal, reporting the researchers estimate that the requirement had increased fruit and vegetable waste and attributing an estimated 684 million dollars in fruits and vegetables discarded in a school year to the requirement. The Government Accountability Office recorded the operational version of the claim in its January 2014 and October 2015 studies, in which school food authorities reported ongoing challenges with plate waste, described as students taking required foods and not eating them. The two bodies of waste evidence differed in design, with the Cohen and Schwartz studies measuring change against a pre implementation baseline and the Byker study and the district reports measuring levels after implementation without a comparable baseline in the same setting, and the record through September 15, 2019, contained no national pre and post measurement resolving the difference.

Congressional and administrative revisions after 2012 arrived as dated enactments and rules that altered particular requirements without repealing the standards structure. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2015, Public Law 113-235, enacted in December 2014, contained two provisions in Sections 751 and 752. The whole grain provision required the Department of Agriculture to allow states to exempt school food authorities demonstrating hardship from the all whole grain rich requirement from December 2014 through the 2015 to 2016 school year, with exempt authorities held to the half whole grain rich standard. The sodium provision barred a sodium reduction below Target 1 levels until scientific research established that the reduction was beneficial for children, postponing Target 2. Appropriations acts for fiscal year 2016, Public Law 114-113, and fiscal year 2017, Public Law 115-31, extended the whole grain exemption mechanism and the sodium postponement, and the fiscal year 2017 act added a provision allowing states to grant exemptions permitting flavored low fat milk. The Department of Agriculture issued an interim final rule on November 30, 2017, at 82 Federal Register 56703, carrying the flexibilities through the 2018 to 2019 school year as regulations. Secretary of Agriculture Sonny Perdue, who had issued a proclamation on May 1, 2017, committing the department to operational flexibility, announced the culminating rule on December 6, 2018, as a response to operational challenges reported by local operators regarding product availability, menu planning, and student acceptance.

The final rule, titled Child Nutrition Programs: Flexibilities for Milk, Whole Grains, and Sodium Requirements, was published on December 12, 2018, at 83 Federal Register 63775, took effect on February 11, 2019, and applied from the 2019 to 2020 school year. On milk, the rule allowed local operators to offer flavored low fat milk without a hardship exemption, reversing the January 2012 limitation of flavored milk to fat free varieties, and extended the same option to participants age six and older in the Special Milk Program and the Child and Adult Care Food Program. On grains, the rule replaced the requirement that all grains offered be whole grain rich with a requirement that at least half of the weekly grains offered be whole grain rich, with remaining grains enriched. On sodium, the rule retained Target 1 limits through the 2023 to 2024 school year, set Target 2 limits to take effect in the 2024 to 2025 school year, and eliminated the final target scheduled for the 2022 to 2023 school year. No other element of the January 2012 meal patterns, and no element of the Smart Snacks standards, was changed by the December 2018 rule, and the record through September 15, 2019, closed with the dietary quality findings, the participation and cost findings, and the opposed plate waste findings each in print, each attributed to named holders, and none withdrawn.

## The Classification That Outlasts the Menu

Entitlement or appropriation decides everything, and the history assembled in the preceding sections gives that claim a precise legal meaning rather than a rhetorical one. A program structured as an entitlement paid for each unit of qualifying service, and growth in participation generated growth in federal payment under rates fixed in law. A program structured as a discretionary grant received a sum fixed before service began, and growth in eligible applications generated waiting lists, priority rankings, and administrative rationing inside the sum. The Child Nutrition Act and the statutes around the act contained both structures at once. Lunch, breakfast, milk, summer food, and child and adult care food operated on the reimbursement side of the line. The supplemental program for women, infants, and children and the equipment assistance for school kitchens operated on the grant side of the line. Families encountered the difference as a practical fact long before any family encountered the vocabulary, because a child in a participating school received a meal priced by a tier, while a qualified mother at a clinic received benefits priced by a caseload.

An honest account of the record also requires a clear statement of what the statute did not do. The 1966 act did not create school lunch, which rested on legislation from 1946 and kept a separate codification, a separate appropriations history, and a separate statutory identity. The act did not create an individual right to a meal enforceable by any child against any school, and participation depended on institutional decisions at every level, from a state agreement to a local choice to operate a program. The act did not reach the nutrition of women and infants in the original text, and that reach arrived only through an amendment six years later, in a discretionary form that withheld any guarantee of service from every eligible applicant. Equipment assistance covered only part of equipment cost and only for schools that proved need and inability to pay. Eligibility lines drawn at percentages of the poverty guidelines left households just above each boundary with a materially different price for the same meal.

The standards record carried a similar mixture of achievement and limit. The 2010 revision raised measured dietary quality by a wide margin in the comparison between the 2009 to 2010 and 2014 to 2015 school years, and the same period produced a sustained decline in paid participation, operator reports of higher cost and discarded food, and a sequence of legislative and regulatory steps that restored flexibility on flavored milk, whole grains, and sodium targets. Neither side of that argument held a complete account on the evidence available through September 15, 2019. The dietary quality studies measured menus and consumption in defined settings, the participation figures measured national enrollment in the meal programs, and the waste studies measured different quantities under different designs, so each finding answered a narrower question than the public argument asked.

Community eligibility illustrated the direction that federal policy took when administrative proof became the obstacle. Rather than refining the household application further, the 2010 legislation allowed high poverty schools to abandon the application for meal purposes, serve every student without charge, and accept a formula payment built from directly certified enrollment. The device traded precision about individual households for certainty about the character of a school population, and the trade exposed the underlying judgment that had driven direct certification and categorical eligibility in earlier decades. Where need was concentrated and documented through other programs, repeated proof of the same fact cost more than the proof protected.

Readers tracing the family further have several productive directions available. The lunch program rewards study as the foundation statute whose reimbursement mechanics every later program either copied or deliberately refused. The supplemental program rewards study as the clearest case in American social legislation of a permanent authorization paired with discretionary funding, a pairing whose consequences appeared in rebate finance, carryover authority, contingency reserves, and shutdown planning. For study of that kind, [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) as the record grows from one statute into the full family. The menus changed with each decade, the tiers and thresholds changed with budget legislation, and the standards changed with dietary science and operator pressure. The classification underneath each program changed least of all, and the classification decided, in every funding dispute, whether the federal promise expanded with need or stopped at a number fixed in advance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 create?

The act created a pilot school breakfast program under Section 4, renewed and consolidated the Special Milk Program under Section 3, established nonfood assistance for food service equipment in schools in poor areas under Section 5, and funded state administrative expenses under Section 7. The act also strengthened free and reduced price service for needy children inside the existing lunch framework. The act operated alongside the National School Lunch Act of 1946 rather than replacing that statute, and the lunch program kept a separate statutory identity. Breakfast operated as a two year pilot with fixed authorization ceilings, equipment aid required a state or local share of cost, and no provision created an individual entitlement for any child.

### Q: Was school lunch a national security measure before the Child Nutrition Act?

Federal law described school lunch in national security terms two decades before the 1966 statute. Section 2 of the National School Lunch Act of 1946 declared the policy of Congress, as a measure of national security, to safeguard the health and well being of children and to encourage domestic consumption of nutritious agricultural commodities. The language rested on wartime experience. Selective Service examinations during the Second World War linked poor childhood diets to rejected recruits, and Lewis B. Hershey, director of the Selective Service System, carried that argument into congressional testimony in 1945. Agriculture interests supported permanence from the opposite direction, because a standing lunch program preserved a domestic outlet for farm commodities after wartime demand ended.

### Q: Is WIC an entitlement under the Child Nutrition Act?

WIC is not an entitlement. Congress added the Special Supplemental Food Program for Women, Infants, and Children to the Child Nutrition Act through an amendment in 1972, codified as Section 17, and made the program permanent in 1975. The funding structure remained discretionary throughout. Congress appropriates a fixed sum each year, the Food and Nutrition Service allocates that sum to state agencies by formula, and clinics certify eligible applicants only into the participation level that assigned funds support. Eligibility based on category, income, and nutritional risk is a necessary condition for benefits, but eligibility carries no legally enforceable right to benefits, and state agencies use waiting lists and a federal priority system when applications exceed funded caseload.

### Q: Who qualifies for free meals under the Child Nutrition Act?

A child qualifies for free meals when household income falls at or below 130 percent of the federal poverty guidelines, under the thresholds in place for school year 2018 to 2019, or when the child qualifies through a categorical route that carries no separate income test. Categorical routes covered children in households receiving Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits, Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations benefits, or Temporary Assistance for Needy Families cash assistance, and covered children enrolled in Head Start, children in foster care, and migrant, runaway, and homeless children. Direct certification could establish free status through a records match with another program, without a household application for school meals. A free meal carried no charge at the point of service and protection against overt identification.

### Q: What is community eligibility in the Child Nutrition Act?

Community eligibility is a meal service option created by Section 104 of the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010. A school, a group of schools, or an entire district qualified when identified students made up at least 40 percent of enrollment. Identified students were students certified for free meals without a household application, through direct certification or categorical eligibility. An electing school served breakfast and lunch at no charge to every enrolled student and claimed reimbursement by formula, with the identified student percentage multiplied by 1.6 to set the share of meals claimed at the free rate and the remainder claimed at the paid rate. Election ran in four year cycles, removed household applications, and removed eligibility determination at the point of service.

### Q: Did the Child Nutrition Act create school breakfast?

The act created federal assistance for school breakfast as a pilot program. Section 4 authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to assist states, through grants in aid and other means, to initiate, maintain, or expand nonprofit breakfast programs in schools. The authorization covered two fiscal years, with ceilings of seven million five hundred thousand dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1967, and ten million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1968. State agencies gave first consideration to schools in areas of poor economic conditions and to schools whose pupils traveled long distances. Repeated extensions carried the pilot forward, and legislation in 1975 made the School Breakfast Program permanent and stated a national policy favoring breakfast availability in schools where breakfast was needed.

### Q: Who sets standards under the Child Nutrition Act?

The Secretary of Agriculture sets the standards that define a reimbursable meal. The 1966 act and the lunch statute beside the act required meals to meet minimum nutritional requirements prescribed by the Secretary on the basis of tested nutritional research, and later legislation directed the Secretary to update meal patterns and nutrition standards for lunch and breakfast based on Institute of Medicine recommendations and consistent with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. The resulting rules, issued in final form in January 2012, governed components, calorie ranges, grain and milk requirements, and staged sodium limits. The Secretary also prescribed regulations for program administration, and state agencies enforced the standards through reviews of school food authorities, with a performance payment tied to certified compliance.

### Q: How are schools reimbursed under the Child Nutrition Act?

Schools receive federal payment per meal, at rates that differ by eligibility category and by meal type. A school records daily counts of meals by category, reports monthly counts to the state agency, and receives cash reimbursement for meals that met program requirements, with the highest rate attached to free meals and a far smaller rate attached to paid meals. For school year 2018 to 2019, the federal reimbursement for many schools in the contiguous states stood at 3 dollars and 37 cents for a free lunch, 2 dollars and 97 cents for a reduced price lunch, and 37 cents for a paid lunch. Schools also received Department of Agriculture foods at an entitlement value per lunch, plus bonus commodities outside that entitlement amount.

### Q: What is the Special Milk Program and who can take part in it?

The Special Milk Program encourages consumption of fluid milk by children in schools and institutions that lack other meal service. The program began in 1954, received formal authorization in the Child Nutrition Act of 1966, and became permanent in 1970. Eligible settings included nonprofit schools of high school grade and under and nonprofit nursery schools, child care centers, settlement houses, summer camps, and similar nonprofit institutions devoted to the care and training of children. Federal payment followed each qualifying half pint of milk at set rates, and the Congressional Research Service classified the program with the other meal programs as an appropriated entitlement. Participation contracted sharply across later decades because lunch and breakfast service already included milk.

### Q: What does WIC provide to the women and children it serves?

WIC provides three components through a clinic relationship. The first component is a supplemental food package prescribed for the participant category, delivered through vouchers or checks that list specific foods for redemption at authorized retailers. Packages covered items such as infant formula, milk, cheese, eggs, cereals, and fruit juice, with contents matched to infants, children, and pregnant or breastfeeding women. The second component is nutrition education offered through the clinic that certified the participant. The third component is referral to health care and to other social services. A health professional assessed nutritional risk at certification, and certification periods matched the category, with children eligible until the fifth birthday and breastfeeding women covered in periods extending to one year after delivery.

### Q: How does direct certification qualify a child for free meals?

Direct certification qualifies a child through a records match rather than through a household application. A state agency or a school district compares enrollment records against records of participation in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and other covered programs, and a matched child receives certification for free meals automatically. Legislation in 2004 required local educational agencies to directly certify children in food stamp households and required agreements between the child nutrition agency and the food stamp agency to carry out the matching, with phased implementation that covered all districts by July 2008. A directly certified child faced no verification of a household application, because no application existed. Reported direct certification rates rose from about 68 percent in school year 2007 to 2008 to nearly 90 percent in later department summaries.

### Q: What is the Summer Food Service Program and whom does it feed?

The Summer Food Service Program feeds children during school closures, when subsidized lunch and breakfast service pauses and household need continues. The program grew from a pilot authorized in 1968 for summer and child care settings, and legislation in 1975 divided that pilot into separate summer and child care programs. Summer service operated through sponsoring organizations that ran sites in low income areas, and meals at those sites were provided free to all children at the site, a design that avoided individual eligibility determinations during a short season. Federal payment followed each qualifying meal at set rates. Program authority was permanent, while the authorization of appropriations carried a time limit in the reauthorization cycles that later overviews described.

### Q: What is the Child and Adult Care Food Program?

The Child and Adult Care Food Program reimburses meals and snacks served in child care centers, family day care homes, and adult day care centers. The program began as part of a 1968 pilot for summer and child care settings, became a separate program in the 1975 division of that pilot, and reached permanence in 1978, when legislation also changed reimbursement methods and provided start up and advance funding. Legislation in 1987 added eligible adult day care centers serving elderly persons or chronically impaired disabled persons, and legislation in 1989 changed the program name to reflect the adult component. Federal reimbursement follows qualifying meals, and family day care homes receive tiered rates under rules added in 1996.

### Q: What did the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act change in school meals?

The Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010, Public Law 111-296, changed access and content at the same time. Section 104 created community eligibility, allowing high poverty schools to serve all students without charge under a formula payment. Section 201 directed updated meal patterns and nutrition standards for lunch and breakfast, which the Department of Agriculture issued in final form in January 2012, with separate fruit and vegetable components, whole grain requirements, milk limits, calorie ranges, and staged sodium targets. Section 208 extended standards to foods sold outside the reimbursable meal during the school day. The act also placed foster children into statute as categorically eligible for free meals and created a performance payment of six cents per lunch for authorities certified as compliant with the updated standards.

### Q: What is the identified student percentage in community eligibility?

The identified student percentage is the share of enrolled students in a school, a group of schools, or a district who count as identified students for community eligibility. An identified student is a student certified for free meals based on documentation of benefit receipt or categorical eligibility, without a household application. Routes included direct certification through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, participation in the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, and categorical eligibility tied to foster care, Head Start enrollment, and homeless, migrant, or runaway status. A percentage of at least 40 percent qualified an electing unit, and the percentage, multiplied by 1.6 and capped at 100 percent, set the share of meals claimed at the free reimbursement rate.

### Q: How do the federal poverty guidelines set meal eligibility each year?

Each year the Department of Health and Human Services issues federal poverty guidelines, and the Food and Nutrition Service converts the guidelines into dollar limits for free and reduced price meals by household size. The conversion multiplies the guideline by 1.30 for free meals and by 1.85 for reduced price meals, rounds the results, and publishes tables in the Federal Register for the school year beginning July 1. Separate and higher tables apply in Alaska and Hawaii. For a household of four in the contiguous states, the limits effective from July 1, 2019, through June 30, 2020, set free eligibility at 33,475 dollars in annual income and reduced price eligibility at 47,638 dollars. Schools compare reported household income and size against the published table when deciding an application.

### Q: What happens when WIC funding falls short of the number of eligible applicants?

A funding shortfall converts eligibility into a queue. Because WIC operates as a discretionary grant program, the annual appropriation fixes the total available, the Food and Nutrition Service allocates that total among state agencies, and state agencies assign caseload and funds to local agencies. When eligible applications exceed funded places, state agencies slow or stop new certifications, hold eligible applicants on waiting lists, and apply the federal priority system so funded places go first to applicants at highest nutritional risk. Federal rules rank pregnant and breastfeeding women and infants with medical problems at the top of that system. Carryover funds, recovered funds, reallocation, and a contingency fund soften a shortfall without removing the cap, and no applicant can compel payment from an exhausted grant.

### Q: What role do state agencies play in running the school meal programs?

State agencies form the middle layer between federal payment and local service. A state educational agency enters agreements with the Department of Agriculture, receives apportioned funds, selects or approves participating schools, disburses reimbursement based on meal counts reported by local authorities, and provides supervision and technical assistance. State agencies also conduct administrative reviews of school food authorities, carry out direct certification matching under agreements with other assistance agencies, publish eligibility information, and enforce meal pattern and record keeping requirements. The 1966 act funded state administrative expenses directly under Section 7, treating administration as a program cost. Local school food authorities operate the cafeterias, certify most household applications, count meals by category each day, and submit the claims on which state disbursement rests.

### Q: Where is the Child Nutrition Act codified in the United States Code?

The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 is codified principally at title 42 of the United States Code, section 1771 and following sections. Section 17 of the act, the authority for the Special Supplemental Food Program for Women, Infants, and Children, appears at 42 U.S.C. 1786. The codification sits in a chapter separate from the chapter carrying the National School Lunch Act at section 1751 and following sections, a separation that reflects the distinct statutory identities of the two measures. The lunch act later acquired the name of Senator Richard B. Russell. Program regulations appear in title 7 of the Code of Federal Regulations, and the adult component of the child and adult care program carries a citation at 7 CFR 226.19a in department materials.

### Q: What did the act change about equipment in school kitchens?

Section 5 of the 1966 act created nonfood assistance, a program that paid for food service equipment in schools that lacked the physical capacity to run a meal program. Eligible schools drew attendance from areas in which poor economic conditions existed and had no equipment, or grossly inadequate equipment, for storage, preparation, transportation, and serving of food. The federal share covered at most three fourths of equipment cost, with at least one fourth borne by state or local funds. An applicant school had to justify need, describe the equipment in detail, set out plans for use in meeting the nutritional needs of children, and obtain state agency approval before disbursement by advance or reimbursement. Equipment for nonprofit private schools served principally in connection with authorized child feeding programs.
