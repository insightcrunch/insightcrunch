## Cold Open

![Nightjar: Marked Hands and the Men Who Keep Them Hidden - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-44.webp)

Ferro Cant comes in by the side door, as he always comes in, because the side door opens onto the narrow lane that runs down from the counting houses to the water, and because a man who has walked the same lane for twenty years knows which stone lifts after rain and which shutter will be open and which will be shut. He comes in at his hour, which is not written anywhere in the restaurant and does not need to be, because the kitchen knows it and the room knows it and the chair knows it in the way that furniture knows a weight that returns. He hangs his coat on the second peg, not the first, because the first peg has leaned for as long as he can remember and he does not trust a leaning thing with a good coat. He nods to the room without looking for anyone in particular. He crosses to his table.

The table stands by the wall that faces the harbour windows but is not itself beside a window, which is a distinction Ferro has explained to no one and has never needed to explain. From his chair he can see the door he came in by and the door to the kitchen and the length of the room, and his back is to a wall of plaster and old panelling that has never held a door or a hatch or a serving gap. The chair is the third from the wall on that side, though the count means nothing to anyone else, because the other chairs move and his does not, or rather his moves only in the small arc that his own settling has worn into the floorboards, a pale crescent that the broom reveals each morning and that the evening covers again. He sits. He settles. The chair takes him with a sound so familiar that he would notice its absence sooner than its presence.

He lays his hands flat for a moment on either side of the empty place setting, not from any ceremony he would name, but because a man who keeps books learns to begin with his hands still. Then he draws the setting toward him by the width of two fingers, because the setting is always laid a fraction too far, whoever lays it, and because the correction is part of arriving. The napkin he unfolds once and lays across his knee. The glass he turns so that a small flaw in its base faces away from him. He does not know when he began turning the glass. He knows that he would feel the evening had started wrong if he drank from it unturned.

The waiter is new.

Ferro has watched enough new men in enough counting houses to know newness when he sees it carried into a room. The boy, for he is hardly more than a boy beside the men who usually work these tables, holds his tray too high and too level, as if the tray were a document that might be inspected. He comes to the table from the wrong side, corrects himself with a small, wasted step, and sets down bread that Ferro has never ordered at this point in the meal and has never been given at this point in the meal in twenty years. He pours water before he is asked for water. He asks after the order before Ferro has looked at the card, though the card has not changed in its essentials since the spring, and though Ferro orders from memory and from habit and only in part from hunger.

Ferro is kind about it, in the spare way of a man who has trained clerks and knows that correction given too early teaches nothing but fear of the corrector. He asks for the soup he always asks for first. He asks for it by name. He watches the boy repeat the name back with the stress on the wrong syllable, and he lets that pass as well. What he does not let pass, because noticing is not a choice for him but a trade worn into the bone, is the watching.

The boy watches his table more than a waiter should watch a table that has ordered nothing unusual and wants nothing yet. Between journeys to the kitchen he looks across the room, not at the door, not at the harbour windows, but at Ferro, and the look is not the look of a man memorising a regular customer for the sake of better service. It is quicker than that and it is more afraid than that. Once, when Ferro lifts his glass, the boy starts, actually starts, as if a glass raised at a table where a man eats alone were a signal he had been told to expect and had half hoped would not come. Once, when the kitchen door swings, the boy looks at Ferro first and at the door second, which is the wrong order for a waiter and the right order for a man who believes that whatever enters a room will enter it for the sake of the man at this table.

Ferro eats his soup slowly. He has always eaten slowly in this place, because the hour is his and because a meal eaten slowly in public, alone, in the same chair, is a kind of statement that he made long ago without words and has renewed every evening since. He is not a large man. His hair has thinned to a careful arrangement that he no longer thinks about. His suit is dockside good, which is to say that it would pass in a counting house and would not be remarked in a warehouse, and his shoes are cleaned by a boy at the corner of the lane who knows him by his hour as surely as this room does. Men who keep books for shipping firms are not, in the common telling, men whose evenings matter. Ferro knows better, and he knows that knowing better is precisely his difficulty.

The books matter. That is the plain shape of it, held under the soup and the bread and the small ceremonies of the chair. Through his books, in his hand, in columns he can still see when he closes his eyes, passed the alloy consignments of the last seasons, each one entered as it should be entered, weight and mark and vessel and consignee, each one clean on its face. A book that is clean on its face can still be the only place where two clean entries, laid side by side, show a space between them that no single entry shows. Ferro saw the space. He did what a bookkeeper of his training does with a space of that kind. He kept a second record of the seeing, not a copy of the books, which would have been theft and folly together, but a record of the intervals, of the places where the clean columns did not meet. Then, because he is not a fool, he told the harbour authority that such a record existed, and the harbour authority, because it is not composed of fools either, told others, and now his evenings are a matter that men discuss in rooms he will never enter.

Men have asked about his evenings. Not to him. That is another thing his trade has taught him to notice, the shape of a question asked at one remove. The landlord of his lodging house mentioned, with a casualness that had been rehearsed badly, that a man had wanted to know whether Ferro kept regular hours. A clerk from a firm that is not his firm bought him a drink he had not asked for and wondered aloud whether a man alone did not find the same restaurant wearisome after so many years. Wearisome. Ferro had turned the word over for a full evening in this chair. A man who wants to know whether a habit will hold does not call it wearisome by accident. He calls it that to see whether the keeper of the habit will agree that it might change.

It will not change. Ferro understands, with a clarity that costs him something each evening and that he pays willingly, that changing his restaurant or his hour or his chair now would tell whoever watches that he is afraid, and that a frightened witness is a witness whose evidence shrinks in the telling, first in his own mind and then in the minds of those who hear it. He also understands the simpler truth beneath that one. This place is his. Twenty years of evenings are his. He ate here when his wife was alive and came sometimes to share the second half of the hour, and he ate here in the months after her death when the chair opposite was a fact he had to learn again each evening, and he ate here through the winter when the harbour froze at its edges and the kitchen served what it could get and apologised for it, and he has no intention of surrendering the one room in Valtessa that asks nothing of him except that he arrive.

The boy brings the fish. He brings it on a plate that is too hot and warns Ferro about the heat three times, which is two times more than the warning needs and exactly the number that tells Ferro the boy is speaking to fill a silence he fears. While he speaks he looks at the door. While Ferro thanks him he looks at the door again. When he retreats he takes a path that keeps Ferro's table in his sight for as long as the room allows, walking backward for two steps in a way that he then corrects, too late, with a flush that rises from his collar.

Ferro cuts his fish. He thinks, as a man thinks whose work is the reading of entries, that the boy is an entry that does not belong in this ledger. Not wrong in the way a false figure is wrong. Wrong in the way a figure in a strange hand is wrong, inserted by someone who knows the shape of the column but not its habits. The boy knows where to stand to watch this table. The boy does not know when the bread comes. A man can be taught the first in an afternoon. The second takes a season. Someone, or some fear of his own, has taught the boy the first and left the second to chance, and Ferro, eating alone in his chair at his hour, files the distinction away in the part of his mind where intervals are kept, between the clean columns, where the true shape of a thing so often lives.

Outside, the lane darkens toward the water. Inside, the lamps are lit in their order, window end first, as they have been lit for twenty years, and the room settles into the part of the evening that Ferro likes best, when the tables fill with dock people and counting house people and the noise rises enough to make a man alone feel accompanied without being disturbed. He orders nothing more yet. He sits in the sound of it. The boy hovers at the kitchen door, watching, and Ferro, who has decided to be kind and to be watchful in equal measure, raises his glass, flaw turned away, and drinks, and gives the boy nothing at all to report except a man keeping his habit in a room that has kept it with him.


## Tasking

Petra Dunmore kept the tasking short in the way that meant she had read the papers twice and had disliked them the second time more than the first. She laid three things on the table in the small room in Carrowmere that the Office used for such conversations, a room with no harbour view and no distractions, and she laid them in a row, as if the order itself were part of the instruction. A Valtessan watch report, folded once. A copy of an agreement, signed on both sides, with the Valtessan seal heavier than the Office one. A set of restaurant papers, thin, of the kind that port authorities exchange without enthusiasm and without comment, until someone reads them closely.

The witness was Ferro Cant, a shipping agent in Valtessa, whose dockside books had carried the alloy consignments and whose second record, the record of intervals between clean entries, was the evidence the Office needed and could get from no other living hand. He was under threat. The threat had not yet taken a shape the Office could act on, which Dunmore said in the tone of a woman naming a weather she could see coming across a harbour. Men had asked about his evenings. Men had asked about his hours. A man had been seen, more than once, in the places where a man would stand if he were learning those evenings from the outside.

Mira looked at the watch report first, because Dunmore had placed it first, and because the first thing placed is usually the thing the placer wants understood before any argument begins. The report was Valtessan, written in the dry hand of a port watch that recorded what it saw and resisted what it concluded, and Mira respected it for that before she had finished the opening lines. A man of middle height and middle build, unremarkable except in two respects. He wore a heavy dock coat in all weathers, the report said, a coat made for a winter quay, worn buttoned on a mild evening and worn indoors in a dockside office where he had presented a paper that proved, on examination, to be a copy of a public notice that anyone might carry. And he kept one hand in his coat pocket the entire time. At rest and walking. Indoors and out. When he took the paper from an inside pocket he took it with the free hand, awkwardly, across his body, rather than free the pocketed one. When he was jostled at a doorway, in the ordinary press of a port office at the changing of a watch, he turned his body to protect the pocketed side and took the jostle on the other shoulder. The watch had noted it without emphasis, which gave it more weight in Mira's mind than emphasis would have done. Men protect what costs them. Men also hide what identifies them. A hand kept hidden through weather and crowding and inconvenience is doing one of those two kinds of work, and sometimes, she thought, it is doing both.

Dunmore let her read it twice. Then she unfolded the agreement, and the tasking changed its temperature.

Valtessa insisted that the detail was theirs. Dunmore said it without apology and without relish, as a fact of law and of place. In its own ports Valtessa had primacy, and the Office acted there by permission, and permission in this case had been given in a precise and narrow form. The protection detail around Ferro Cant would be a Valtessan detail, commanded on the Valtessan side, staffed by Valtessan officers, arranged according to Valtessan judgement of a Valtessan port. The Office might observe. Mira would be present as an observer. She might read the room and the ground, she might report what her practice gave her, she might advise when advice was asked for, and she might do none of the things that her training would otherwise make instinctive. She could not act. She could not direct the detail. She could not move the protected man, or alter the ground plan, or lay a hand on either, until something happened that dissolved the condition in fact rather than in her judgement of what was coming.

Dunmore named what that meant, plainly, in the way the spec of a cost should be named before the cost is incurred and not discovered inside it. Mira would watch a man be threatened in a place she had read. She would sit with her hands kept off it. She would know the room in the way only she in that detail would know it, its anchors and its accumulations, and she would be forbidden the use of the knowledge at the moment when knowledge most wants to become movement. If she acted, she would break a condition the Office had agreed to, in writing, with a seal on it, and she would answer for the breaking herself. Not the Office in the abstract. Herself, by name, in a proceeding whose outcome Dunmore did not trouble to predict because prediction was not the point. The point was that the price was personal and was known in advance.

Mira thought of the register then, because the room and the hour made a place for the thought whether she invited it or not. Her private register of absences, the notebook she had kept against instruction, was no longer hers to carry into a room like this one. She had given it voluntarily to Harrand, and it now sat in oversight's keeping as an Accord document, with a numbered Accord label on its cover, and the giving had been right and the loss of it was still a loss, in the practical sense that a tool she had built entry by entry was gone from her hand and in the other sense that she did not name to Dunmore. The reprimand from the Scour crossing sat beside it in her mind, the formal reprimand Harrand had written before she crossed and had handed to her on her return, a document whose language she could still summon whole, the way one summons the wording of a charge in a quiet room. Dunmore, who missed very little that crossed a face she was watching, noted both without naming either beyond a single sentence. The Office was sending her into Valtessa with a reprimand already on her record from the weeks just past, she said, and with her register in other hands, and Mira should understand that a second mark, earned in a port where the Office was a guest, would be read by oversight in the light of the first. It was not a threat. It was an accounting. Mira preferred it as an accounting.

The restaurant papers were the last of the three, and the slightest, and Dunmore saved them for last with the air of a woman who knows that slight papers are where details hide. The restaurant where Ferro ate, the same restaurant, the same chair, the same hour, for twenty years, was licensed and returned and inspected in the ordinary Valtessan way, and its return listed its staff. Against the newest name, a waiter, Tam Rezik, engaged within the season, the Valtessan clerk had written a note in a smaller hand. Papers not yet in order. Licence status to be confirmed. Juno's people, who had supplied the set, had flagged the note without comment. A new waiter, at the very table, with his papers not in order. Mira filed it where Dunmore intended her to file it, in the open part of her mind, as a fact established and unexplained, the kind of fact that either dissolves into the ordinary untidiness of port paperwork or becomes, in retrospect, the first line of a different and worse account.

Juno Sarte would command the Valtessan side. Dunmore said the name and let Mira place it, and Mira placed it at once, in the warehouse and the seal case, where Juno had commanded the Valtessan side and her photographs had gone in with the register. A competent officer. A proud one, in the professional sense that had nothing to do with vanity and everything to do with a port that knew its own ground. Mira would be her guest and her burden at once, an observer who could see things the detail could not and was forbidden to act on most of them.

Her state entering was full, and Dunmore said that too, as if it were a line in an inventory, which in the Office sense it was. No heavy read in the days behind her. Sleep taken. The cold that followed a deep read absent from her hands. She would go to Valtessa whole, and the wholeness was part of what was being spent, because a week of watching a threatened man in a read room would spend it whether she acted or not, and Dunmore knew that and Mira knew it and the agreement on the table, with its two seals, took no account of it at all. She accepted the tasking. She took the watch report and the restaurant papers and left the agreement where it lay, because the agreement was not hers to carry. It was hers to obey, which was a heavier carrying, and she knew the weight of it before she reached the door.


She asked, before she rose, what the Office expected her report to contain if the week passed without an attempt, and Dunmore answered with the honesty that made her taskings bearable even when their terms were hard. A report that said the witness lived, the detail held, the observer observed, and the threat retired unidentified would be a successful week by every measure the agreement recognised, and a frustrating one by every measure that mattered to the people in the room, and Mira was to write it in exactly those terms if it came to that and not to reach, in prose, for a shape the week had not supplied. She asked also what would be made of the pocketed hand if it never appeared again, and Dunmore said that the watch report would go into the Valtessan file and into the Office copy of it, and that a hand a man would not show, in a port, in a case of this kind, would be remembered by the people whose work was remembering, long after the detail stood down. Ports had long memories, Dunmore said, and so did the Office, and the two memories did not always hold the same things, which was another reason an observer was worth her keep. Mira took the point as it was meant. She was to be a second memory in the room, a recording instrument of a different kind from the watch log, and the condition that bound her hands did not bind her attention, which the Office expected to be total, and which she intended, walking out into the Carrowmere morning with the papers under her arm, to give it without remainder.

## Ground Truth

Valtessa in the morning had the particular clarity of a port that worked before it woke, the quays already loud with the iron language of cargo while the streets behind them were still delivering bread and paper and coal in carts whose drivers knew every yard of the gradient. Mira came down to the restaurant before its hour, by Juno's arrangement and under Juno's permission, with Reni Vosk beside her and a Valtessan officer at a distance that was meant to be unobtrusive and succeeded only in being exact. She was an observer. She observed the distance as carefully as she observed everything else, because the distance was the agreement made visible, measured out in paces on a dockside street.

The detail, as Juno ran it, was a study in a port knowing itself. There were officers at the lane's two ends, not in pairs, which would have advertised them, but singly and at staggered hours, one with a reason to be mending a lamp fitting that took him most of the week, one with a chandlery account that required daily attention, one whose sister kept the shop opposite the side door and whose visits no one in the lane would ever count. There were officers inside the restaurant at the tables that turned over, never the same faces on consecutive evenings, never a face at the table nearest Ferro's, because Juno understood that a protection that altered the protected man's room would either drive him from it or teach whoever watched the room that the room had changed its meaning. There was a rotation for the walk from Ferro's lodging house, and a rotation for the walk from his office, and the two rotations never used the same officer on the same day, so that no single Valtessan back became a landmark by which his movements could be read. There was a man in the kitchen, which Ferro did not know, and a woman at the harbour authority's window that overlooked the lane, which Ferro did know and had pretended, with a courtesy Mira admired when Juno reported it, not to know.

It was competent. Mira recorded the competence without reservation in the part of her mind that kept professional accounts, and she recorded beside it the limit that the competence could not cross. The detail could watch the room. It could not read it. When Juno gave her the hour before opening, with the chairs still upended on the tables and the floor still damp from the mop, Mira walked the room slowly, as an observer permitted to read and to report, and put her attention where her practice required it to go, to the anchors, the places where weight gathered and held.

She began with the chair.

Ferro's chair was not the oldest chair in the room and was not, to any ordinary examination, different in kind from its neighbours, but weight does not keep the accounts that furniture keeps, and what it held stopped her at the table's edge with her hand not yet raised. Twenty years of one man's habits in a single chair lay in that wood and in the floor beneath it in a density so even, so unbroken by variation, that her first sense of it was not of events at all but of a note held without wavering, a single tone of return. Repetition is the strongest thing weight records after violence, and here repetition had been laid down evening upon evening, in the same settling of a body, the same drawing of a setting by the width of two fingers, the same turning of a glass so that its flaw faced away, the same solitude practised in public with a dignity that had long ago stopped being defiance and had become simply the shape of a life. She felt the early years of it, when another presence sometimes shared the second half of the hour, a warmth at the table's far side that the weight held gently and without detail, because weight records intensity and affect and not identity, and what it held of the wife was not her name or her face but the affect of companionship settling into a chair that would later hold its absence. She felt the months when the opposite chair held that absence instead, evening upon evening of a man learning a room again with one place in it emptied, and the grief in the wood was old and worked through and had become part of the grain of the habit rather than a wound upon it. She felt the winters and the lean seasons and the evenings of no significance at all, which were most of them, and which mattered most to what she had come to learn, because a habit made only of significant evenings can be interrupted, and a habit made of ten thousand insignificant ones cannot. It has no hinge. It does not argue with itself. Ferro Cant would come to this chair at this hour on the evening when he was most frightened exactly as he came on the evenings when he thought of nothing but fish and bread and the harbour news, not from courage and not from folly, but because the habit was total, deeper than decision, laid down in wood and floor and in the man's own frame in a single unbroken deposit that she could read as plainly as Juno could read a tide table.

The table held the same account in a lighter register, metal and wood together, the cutlery's small ceremonies and the glass's turning preserved in the surface as a pattern of minute, repeated intentions, none of them large, all of them his. The door he entered by held a different quality, the affect of arrival and of a day set down at a threshold, twenty years of a man coming in from the lane with the counting house still on him and leaving it, deliberately, at the peg where he hung his coat. She read the three anchors in turn and then stood for a while in the middle of the empty room with the cold coming into her hands in the familiar way, mild as yet, the price of a read of this evenness being not shock but a slow chill that settled and stayed, and she understood the case in its fixed geography with a certainty that no report could have given her. His habit was total and would not change. Therefore the attempt, if it came, would come here, at this hour, to this chair, because a man who cannot be moved from his ground fixes the ground for everyone, for his protectors and for his enemies alike. The detail did not need to guess where. The chair had already told them. What remained was who, and when, and with what in his hidden hand.

She reported the read to Juno in those terms, standing among the upended chairs, and Juno listened with the attention of an officer hearing a tide report from a source she had not chosen and could not verify by her own instruments, and asked two questions, both practical, about whether the read altered the ground plan Mira would advise. It did not. It confirmed it. The room was the ground. The plan already held the room.

Ferro's firm occupied the second floor of a counting house whose ground floor smelled of rope and tallow and whose stairs were worn in the centre by three generations of clerks. His books were kept there, and the copies and the day books and the consignment records of the alloy trade that passed through Valtessa in quantities that made the port's fortune and its temptations in equal measure. Mira was not permitted to examine the books herself, which was another face of the same primacy, but Juno's people had examined them under the harbour authority's warrant and Juno laid the shape of the finding before her with a fairness that Mira noted. The books were clean. That was the finding, and it was also the problem, because Ferro's evidence was precisely that the cleanness was constructed, that consignments had passed whose true marks could be discovered only in the intervals between entries, and that his second record, kept in his own hand over seasons, mapped the intervals. If his evidence were given, men who had profited from the spaces between clean columns would face the Accord's accounting. If his evidence died with him, the columns would stay clean forever, and the profits already taken would harden into fortunes, and the men who took them would sleep in the untroubled way of men whose only witness had kept a habit so regular that his ending could be arranged like a consignment, to an hour and to a chair.

Who profits was therefore not a riddle. It was a list, and Juno had the list, and the list had on it partners in his own firm who had countersigned entries they could not now explain, and a broker across the harbour whose house had grown faster than the trade had grown, and two consignees whose names appeared in the intervals more often than chance allowed. Any of them could pay for a threat. Any of them could pay for more than a threat. The port also kept its quieter knowledge, which Juno shared in the same even tone, of hired men. Ports keep such knowledge the way they keep knowledge of unmarked rocks, not in writing and not from approval, but because navigation requires it. There were men who could be hired from outside, through brokers in other harbours, men with no local face and no local debts, of the kind that arrived on one vessel and left on another and were never entered in any book except, perhaps, in the interval of a book. The Valtessan watch kept its informal account of such arrivals. The man with the pocketed hand had entered that account without entering any other, which was itself a kind of entry, and Juno's people were working the vessel lists against his first sighting with the patience of a port that had done this work before.

So the possibilities stood, three of them, live and fairly stated, and Mira set them out in her own mind in the order in which the evidence, such as it was, allowed them to stand. The first was a professional, hired from outside, of the kind the port kept its quiet lists of, a man brought in precisely because he had no place in the local pattern and could therefore move through it unread, whose hidden hand might be a professional's caution or a professional's mark. The second was someone from Ferro's own firm, who knew his habits from inside, who had eaten perhaps in this very restaurant in years past and knew the chair and the hour not by watching but by memory, and who profited directly and largely if the evidence died with the witness. The third was the waiter. Tam Rezik, new at the restaurant, nervous, whose papers were not in order, who watched Ferro's table more than a waiter should, who had arrived within the season and whose arrival sat in the papers with a clerk's small, unexplained note beside his name. Each of the three could be true. None of the three could yet be excluded by anything stronger than preference, and Mira had been trained, at some cost, to distrust the order in which her preferences arranged themselves.


Juno Sarte took her coffee standing, in the small office above the chandlery that the detail used as its harbour side base, with the window open to the noise of the quay and a chart of the restaurant's street weighted at its corners by a mug, a knife, a box of cartridges and a stone. She had not asked Mira to sit. She had not asked her anything at all for the first minute, which Mira understood as a courtesy of a particular kind, the courtesy of an officer deciding how much of her mind to show an observer whose presence she had not requested and whose usefulness she did not dispute. Juno was as Mira remembered her from the seal case, where she had commanded the Valtessan side and her photographs had gone in with the register, a woman whose competence expressed itself as economy, of words, of movement, of the number of times she allowed a fact to be repeated in her hearing.

The detail is mine, Juno said at last, not as a challenge but as a surveyor names a boundary before discussing the land. Not because I asked for it to be mine, and not because the Office is unwelcome in Valtessa. Because the law of this port says that a Valtessan witness, threatened in a Valtessan street, is protected by Valtessan officers answerable to a Valtessan authority, and because that law is not a courtesy the port extends when it is convenient. It is the condition on which the port belongs to the Accord at all. She turned a page of the watch log without reading it. Your Office sees the Accord from its seat. From Carrowmere, primacy looks like a formality, a paragraph in an agreement. From this window it looks like the only reason a harbour master in Valtessa can order a foreign vessel searched and be obeyed. Take it away in one case, for good reasons, with the best intentions in the world, and you have not made an exception. You have published a price. Every master and every broker and every firm on this quay will know that Valtessan authority holds until the Office wants something enough, and they will govern themselves accordingly, in matters that have nothing to do with your witness and everything to do with whether this port can be policed at all.

Mira said nothing, because the scene was Juno's ground and because some of it was true in a way that required no answer.

Juno was right about one thing in particular, and the rightness of it sat in the room between them with more weight than the disagreement did. The detail was competent. Mira had walked it and read around it for two days and had found no gap that she could name without inventing a standard no detail in any port could meet, and Juno, who had built it out of lamp fitters and chandlery accounts and a woman at a harbour window, knew exactly how good it was and knew that Mira knew. An Office observer who could not keep her hands off a Valtessan detail would end Office access to Valtessan ports for years, Juno said, and she said it not as a threat but as a tide table states a depth, a fact about the water that any navigator might verify. The next witness would have no observer at all. The next firm with clean books and dirty intervals would face a port whose authority had been publicly discounted and whose officers would therefore be obeyed a fraction less, in a trade where a fraction less obedience was measured, eventually, in cargo and in lives. That was a real price. It was not a pretext dressed as one. Mira, who had come into the office prepared to respect the detail and to chafe under the condition in roughly equal measure, found the proportions altered by the hearing, and she let the alteration stand in her mind uncorrected, because a fact does not become less a fact for being inconvenient to the person who must carry it alongside another fact. The other fact was Ferro, in his chair, at his hour. Both facts were true. The week would be the business of holding them both until one of them, in an instant, outweighed the other, and Juno, looking at her across the weighted chart, appeared to understand that arithmetic as clearly as she did, and to trust her with it no further than the agreement allowed, which was, Mira thought, exactly as far as trust should go between professionals who had read each other's work.

When she left, Juno gave her the injury table, because the table was Office material that had travelled with the tasking papers and because Juno, with an officer's instinct for putting a tool in the hand that might need it before the need arrived, thought an observer who read rooms might as well hold the paper that the Office kept for the other kind of reading, the kind that could not be done.

The Office kept a table of known practice injuries, and kept it for a reason that Juno stated as she handed it over and that the table's own heading, in the copy Mira studied that evening, stated in drier language. Practitioners are known by what practice costs them. A Weigher carries cold. A Binder carries the stiffness of the joints that have held too many fixings against their natural settling. Other practices wrote themselves on the body in other ways, and the Office, which could not always name a practitioner, could sometimes name a practice from the shape of its price, and could therefore narrow a room of suspects to a kind of person, if not to a person. The table set three known practice injuries of the hand against four respects, where the injury sits, its pattern, what it does to the hand's use, and how it heals, and Mira learned it the way she learned any instrument, by its limits as much as by its entries. It could say what a hand had not suffered more readily than what it had. It held three rows. The world, she suspected, held more.

She read the table twice in the room Juno lent her, with the harbour noise coming up through the floor, and she thought about the pocketed hand, which she had not seen and which the table could not describe, because the table described only what the Office already knew and the whole shape of the week suggested that whatever was coming for Ferro Cant came from outside the Office's knowledge in at least one respect. She thought also, professionally, of [the seal that was never there, in the warehouse where three perfect seals closed doors on nothing](/2026/11/11/nightjar-seal-that-wasnt-011/), where a perfect surface had been the sign of a wrongness underneath it, and of how Juno's photographs from that case had shown what eyes alone would have passed over. A hidden hand was a perfect surface of a kind. It showed nothing. That was its information, and it was not enough.

The evenings established their rhythm after that, and Mira took her observer's place in it, at the table Juno assigned her, which was not the nearest table and not the farthest, with Reni at another table on the rotation and the room arranged around Ferro like a held breath that had learned to look like ordinary dining. She watched Tam Rezik learn the room by inches and fail to learn it by inches, watched him bring bread at the wrong point and be corrected by the kitchen rather than by Ferro, watched his eyes go to Ferro's table in the gaps between his duties with a regularity that she recorded because recording was her function. She watched the door and the lane beyond it through the harbour windows. Twice in the first days she saw, or was reported by the lamp fitter's successor as having been seen in the lane, a figure in a heavy coat out of season, and once she saw it herself across the restaurant's frontage in the rain, a man passing without looking in, one hand visible and swinging slightly with his walk, the other in his coat pocket, and the asymmetry of the gait stayed with her after he had gone, the way a line of verse with one foot wrong stays in the ear. She did not rise. Rising was not hers to do. She reported the sighting to Juno in the interval, in words, as the agreement required, and Juno's people worked the street and found the street empty and the rain washing it, and the report went into the watch log in Juno's dry hand, and the week moved forward by one more evening toward whatever it was moving toward.

In the quiet of the third morning she thought, uninvited, of [the inert house, where Venn was afraid of something that was not the Accord](/2026/11/13/nightjar-inert-house-013/), and of how fear in a place could point in a direction that no one in authority expected, and she kept the thought beside the waiter's watching eyes and the pocketed hand in no order of importance, because ordering them prematurely was a kind of acting, and acting was the one thing she had agreed not to do. She thought also of [the customs post that stamped convoys which never passed, toward a country made of paper](/2026/11/16/nightjar-scour-crossing-016/), where a whole apparatus of correctness had concealed an absence, and she wondered, watching the restaurant fill for another evening, which of the things in this room that looked correct was concealing an absence of its own, and whether the waiter, whose papers concealed a gap, was the absence or merely a man standing near it, frightened of the light it let in.


The firm deserved its own slow hour of attention, and Mira gave it one on the second day, in the observer's permitted way, which was to say on paper and through Juno's summaries and through the shape of the harbour authority's warrant rather than through any door she opened herself. Ferro's partners were men of his own stamp outwardly, bookkeepers and agents grown into ownership, and the firm's prosperity had been built, as port prosperity usually was, half on competence and half on knowing which consignments to court and which to leave to houses with stronger stomachs. The alloy trade had changed the proportions. Alloy consignments were valuable beyond the ordinary run of cargo, valuable in a way that made the spaces between clean entries worth sums that a clerk's lifetime of evenings could not approach, and the firm's books, clean as they stood, had carried those consignments under marks that Ferro alone had kept whole. Juno's examiners had found no partner who could explain the intervals and no partner who would try, which told its own story in two directions at once. A partner who profits from an interval learns not to look at it, and a partner who has never looked at it cannot explain it, and from a distance the two men are indistinguishable, wearing the same silence for different reasons. One of them, the senior partner, a man who had taken Ferro into the firm when both were young, had sent word through the harbour authority that the firm stood ready to pay for additional protection out of its own funds, an offer that Juno reported without comment and that Mira turned over for a long while. It might be conscience. It might be the purchase of a reputation for concern, at a price far below the value of the intervals, by a man who intended the concern to be remembered when the accounting came. It might be the clumsiness of an innocent man who did not understand that money offered around a witness becomes part of the witness's story. The case could not yet tell her, and she set the offer down in her notes as a fact with three faces, all of them plausible, none of them proven, in the same column where the waiter and the hidden hand already stood.

The port's knowledge of hired men had a texture that no file could have carried, and it came to her in pieces, in the chandlery office and on the lane and once, memorably, from the woman at the harbour authority's window, who had watched the port for longer than the detail's officers had been alive and who spoke of hired violence the way she spoke of certain weathers, as a thing that arrived from elsewhere and was known by its signs before it was known by its landfall. A hired man, she said, spends money in a pattern. He pays in advance for lodging he may not use out the week. He eats where he can watch rather than where the food is good. He learns a street faster than a visitor learns it and slower than a native knows it, and the difference shows at corners, in the half step a man takes when a turning surprises him. He has no errands. That, she said, was the surest sign of all, in a port where every soul on the quay had an errand and could name it. A man without an errand, keeping his hours around another man's hours, was either hired or in love, and the port, which had seen both, seldom mistook the one for the other. Mira carried the description back to the detail's papers and found, in the watch reports, its pieces already present and unassembled, the lodging paid forward, the meals taken at watching tables, the half step at the corner of the lane that the lamp man had recorded without knowing what he had seen. None of it made a name. All of it made a shape, and the shape stood behind the first of her three possibilities and gave it a solidity that the firm, for all its profits, had not yet acquired, because a shape that is being watched assembling itself in the present tense has a claim on the attention that a motive, however large, sitting still in a ledger, does not.

She read other anchors in the restaurant across those days, in the permitted hours, filling out the room's account of itself in the way that a surveyor fills out a chart with soundings that may never be needed and that, once taken, change the meaning of the ones that are. The counter held the affect of service and of money changing hands and of a thousand small negotiations between appetite and price, a cheerful deposit, shallow and broad. The kitchen door held haste and heat and the particular concentration of people working with knives and fire in a small space, and under the haste a steadiness that spoke well for the house. The peg where Ferro hung his coat held, as the door had held, the daily act of arrival, and she found in it, faintly, the trace of the second peg's long disuse of the first, a man's distrust of a leaning thing preserved as a habit of the hand. None of these soundings altered the plan. All of them altered her, in the small cumulative way that reads did, laying a chill along the edges of her attention that she managed by movement and by tea and by the discipline of sleep taken at the hours Juno's rotation allowed, and she noted, with the part of her mind that kept her own accounts as carefully as Dunmore kept the Office's, that she was spending herself slowly on a room she was forbidden to defend, and that the spending would be part of the price whether or not the attempt ever came. A Weigher who reads a ground she cannot act on does not keep her wholeness. She lends it to the ground, evening by evening, and the ground, being only a room, gives nothing back.

On the evenings themselves she learned Ferro at the distance the agreement imposed, and the learning complicated the case in the human direction, which was the direction she trusted least and valued most. He was courteous to the new waiter in his errors and courteous to the detail he pretended not to see, and once, when a child from a neighbouring table wandered to his side and stood looking at his plate with the frankness of the very young, he gave the child a piece of his bread with a gravity that treated the transaction as a matter between equals, and the child's mother, retrieving her, apologised to him as people apologise to a fixture, with the warmth reserved for things that belong to a place. He belonged to the place. That was the whole of it and the difficulty of it in a single sentence. Protecting a man in a place he belongs to is a different art from protecting a man in transit, and Juno's detail, arranged so that the belonging was never disturbed, understood the art, and Mira, watching the bread change hands, understood also that whoever came for him would be coming not merely for a witness but into the middle of a room's affection for its own habits, and that the room itself, its people and its furniture and its worn crescents in the floorboards, would be a party to whatever happened in it, in ways that no ground plan she had ever reported on had quite prepared her to name.

Late on the second evening she walked the lane with Reni after the tables had been set and before the first diners came, at the hour when the port changed its shift and the street belonged briefly to no trade at all, and they spoke, in the shorthand of colleagues who have shared enough rooms to leave most sentences unfinished, about the difference between a detail and a trap. A trap wants the attempt. A detail wants the evening to pass. Juno's arrangement, they agreed, was a detail in the honourable sense, built to make the attempt unnecessary in the attacker's own arithmetic, to raise the price of the chair beyond what any broker's fee would cover, and the pocketed man's continued presence in the lane therefore carried a meaning that neither of them liked. He had seen the lamp man and the chandlery visits and the woman at the window, or enough of them to know that the room was held, and he had not retired. Either he had not seen them, which the woman's account of hired men made unlikely, or he had seen them and priced them and stayed, which meant that the fee, or the obligation behind the fee, was of an order that absorbed the price of a competent detail without flinching. Reni, who had a gift for stating a conclusion at the temperature it deserved, said that the lane felt like a page that had been read too many times by someone learning it by heart, and Mira, who knew exactly what a place felt like when repetition was being laid down in it by an intention not its own, did not disagree, and they went back inside to their separate tables and their observer's patience, and the evening passed, and the passing of it, which was the detail's whole purpose, felt nevertheless like a reprieve granted by an authority that neither of them served.

## The Collapse

On the third day the waiter did not come in.

The fact arrived in the restaurant the way consequential facts in ports usually arrive, sideways, in the course of another conversation, with the kitchen door swinging and a tray going past and the senior waiter, a man of Ferro's own generation who had served the table for a decade before other duties took him to the room's far end, saying to the doorway at large that the new boy had not shown and had not sent word, and that the midday covers would have to be worked short handed. Mira, who was in the room early by Juno's leave to take the measure of the place in its empty hour, heard the sentence with the particular attention that her training gave to any change in a pattern she had been set to watch. A pattern had changed. The nervous waiter, whose papers were not in order, who watched the protected man's table more than a waiter should, had vanished on the third day of a protection detail, in a port, without word.

For half a day it looked like flight from the case. She would write that sentence later in her own accounting and would not soften it, because the half day mattered and the way the detail spent it mattered, and a report that begins by excusing its subject teaches its reader to discount everything that follows. For half a day the reasonable reading of the case rearranged itself around the empty place where Tam Rezik had stood. The waiter had been placed, or had placed himself, at the one table that mattered. The waiter knew the hour and the chair and the order of the meal and the moment in the meal when Ferro's attention was on his plate and the room was at its loudest. The waiter had a gap in his papers that no one had yet explained. The waiter was gone. Under that reading, the detail had been watched from inside its own room, by a man serving soup, and the attempt was not coming but had been rehearsed, evening by evening, in the bringing of bread at the wrong point and the pouring of water unasked, each small error a measurement taken. Juno's people went to his lodging house within the hour. Juno's people went to the harbour lists and to the offices where a man leaving Valtessa by vessel would leave a trace of his leaving. Mira sat in the observer's chair that was not yet, at that hour, a dining chair, and did the thing she was permitted to do, which was to think, and found her thinking running in the channel that the facts had dug for it, toward the waiter, away from the pocketed hand, and she distrusted the ease of the channel even while she could not yet point to where it failed.

It failed in paper. That was the collapse, when it came, and its manner of coming taught her something she would carry further than this case, about the kinds of truth that her practice could not reach and that had to be reached, if they were reached at all, by clerks and landladies and the unglamorous patience of a port police following a licence number through a registry. Weight cannot do this work. She said it to Reni, in the kitchen doorway, while they waited for Juno's return, and she said it without bitterness, as a surveyor might note that a sounding line does not measure distance along a quay. Weight reads intensity and affect in places and objects, laid down by events. It does not read a registry. It does not know whether a licence was granted or refused or never sought. It cannot tell a frightened man from a guilty one when both have stood in the same room and left the same fear in the same chair rail, because fear is what it records, and the reason for the fear is not in the recording. The room had told her that the waiter was afraid. The room had never told her what he was afraid of. She had supplied the object of the fear from the shape of the case around her, and the case, for half a day, had nodded along.

Juno came back in the early afternoon with a folder that had the unmistakable look of a matter finished in another office, thinner than the folders of matters still alive. She laid it open on the table Juno used for the detail's papers, between the watch log and the chart weighted with the stone, and she turned it so that Mira, the observer, could read what the Valtessan side had established by the means that were Valtessa's to use. The licence return came first. It was the fuller version of the note in the restaurant papers, the note that had travelled with the tasking and had sat in the open part of Mira's mind since Carrowmere, unexplained. Tam Rezik held no Carrier's licence. He had never held one. He had never applied for one, which the registry could state with a completeness that it could state about few things, because applications left a record even when they failed, and there was no record. And yet Tam Rezik was a Carrier. The second paper in the folder was a statement from his landlady, taken by a Valtessan officer whose handwriting improved as the statement went on, as if he had begun by doubting its relevance and had ended by understanding it. The boy took parcels that were not parcels. Men from the dockside brought him things wrapped and sealed that a man did not carry the way he carried a parcel, but the way he carried something entrusted, against the body, with both hands when he thought no one watched. He kept hours that were not the restaurant's hours. He was paid in a coin of favours and small money that never gathered into anything a landlord would call an income. He had spoken to the landlady, in the week before the detail arrived, about the Accord in the way that a man speaks about weather that can ruin him, not with hatred and not with defiance, but with the steady fear of a person whose living depends on remaining uninspected. A Carrier is a practitioner who carries weight or bound material for others, Mira knew, and the knowledge needed no folder. What the folder supplied was the other half of the fact, the half that lived in registries rather than in rooms. The licence is how the Accord watches them. A licensed Carrier is an inspected man, his loads declared, his clients named in returns, his practice a matter of record. An unlicensed Carrier runs from the Accord for the simplest of reasons, that the licence is the watching, and to apply for one is to walk into the office of the people he has spent his working life avoiding and to give them his name.

Then the detail had arrived in his restaurant. Officers at the lane's ends. A woman at the harbour window. An Office observer at a table, asking, through Juno's people and in the ordinary course of a protection detail's paperwork, about licence status, about the staff return, about the new man's papers that were not in order. Mira could reconstruct his evenings from that point without any practice at all, from the plain evidence of the folder and from what she had seen with her ordinary eyes across three dinners. A boy who carries unlicensed for dockside clients, whose whole safety lies in never being the most interesting person in any room, finds his room filling with authority. He watches the protected man's table more than a waiter should, yes. He watches it because it is the table the authority has come for, and every look he gives it is a measurement of how close the light is coming to the corner where he stands. He gets small things wrong because his attention is not on the bread. His attention is on the door, and on which of the new faces in the room might, at any moment, turn from the shipping agent's soup to the waiter's papers and ask the question that ends his living and perhaps his liberty. On the third day he reached the end of his nerve, or received a client's warning, or simply understood that a fourth day of it would find him answering questions in a room without windows, and he left the country. Juno's people had the manner of his leaving by then as well, traced through the same patience. He had gone overland toward the Barrow States road with a client's cousin, carrying nothing that anyone saw him carry, which for a Carrier was the surest sign of a man travelling for himself. He had left a week's wages uncollected at the restaurant. He had left, at his lodging, a coat and a second pair of shoes and a bowl he had bought himself in the market, the leavings of a man who expected, when he left that morning, to come back in the evening, and who decided, somewhere in the course of a day whose details no registry would ever hold, that he would not.


A different crime entirely. Juno used the phrase once, in the doorway of the chandlery office, with the folder under her arm, and she used it the way she used most language, as a boundary marker rather than a flourish. Unlicensed carrying was an offence under the Accord's arrangements in Valtessa. It would be pursued, in its own channel, by the officers whose work it was, and if Tam Rezik were found in a jurisdiction that honoured the return, he would answer for it. But it had nothing to do with the threat to Ferro Cant. Nothing in the folder, nothing in the landlady's statement, nothing in the accounts gathered from two of his carrier clients, men who had come forward only when Juno's people made it plain that the waiter was gone and that their names would be kept out of the protection papers, connected the boy to the firm, to the broker across the harbour, to the alloy consignments, or to any man who had asked about a shipping agent's evenings. His clients were dockside small, a widow moving a bound keepsake out of a house she could no longer bear to sleep in, a foreman carrying the weight of a warehouse accident away from the warehouse in portions, work of that order, sad and ordinary and illicit only in its lack of a licence. He had not been placed in the restaurant. He had been hired by the restaurant, months before Ferro's name was spoken in any office, through the ordinary channel by which restaurants hire boys who can carry a tray, and if his watching had a design in it, the design was his own preservation and nothing larger. The collapse of the waiter reading was total, and Mira, sitting with the folder's contents settling into a new arrangement of the case, felt the particular professional sensation that she had learned to value more than confidence, the sensation of a floor giving way under a structure she had not built but had stood on for half a day, and of the ground beneath the floor proving, on inspection, to be the true ground she should have been standing on all along.

What remained was narrower and, being narrower, worse. Two possibilities stood where three had stood. Someone from Ferro's own firm, who knew the chair and the hour by memory and profited if the evidence died. Or a professional, hired from outside, of the kind the port kept its quiet lists of, brought in through a broker's hands to do in a restaurant what a local man might refuse to do in his own streets. Between the two, the evidence now leaned by a weight that was not practice and not intuition but the accumulated shape of the reports. A man from the firm would not need to be seen learning the room. A man from the firm would not need to stand in lanes and present copies of public notices in dockside offices, keeping one hand hidden through weather and crowding. The watching that the Valtessan watch had recorded was the watching of a man acquiring a ground he did not already possess. And through every line of the watch reports, early and late, ran the one fact that the waiter's collapse now left standing in the centre of the case, unobscured by any nearer and easier explanation. The pocketed hand.

Juno's people brought the later sightings into order that evening, in the interval before the restaurant filled, laying the watch log open at a new page and entering, in sequence, the reports that the detail had gathered while its attention had been divided by a vanishing waiter. A man in a heavy dock coat had been seen at the upper end of the lane on the second evening, after the rain, standing where the lamp fitting's repair gave the detail's own man a reason to be standing also, so that the two of them had kept their separate posts for the length of a cigarette neither of them smoked, in a companionship of mutual disregard that the detail's man had reported with some dryness. A man answering the description had asked at the harbour authority, on the third morning, after the sailing times of a vessel whose name he pronounced as a landsman pronounces a name he has been given rather than learned. A man, and Juno's officer had underlined the relevant line twice, had eaten at a house two streets from the restaurant on the evening of the waiter's leaving, alone, at a table from which the restaurant's side door could not be seen but from which its lane could, and had kept one hand in his coat pocket while he ate, working his food one handed, refusing, when the serving woman offered to take the coat in the warmth of the room, with a politeness that she remembered because it had been absolute and because the room had been, in her words as the officer recorded them, warm enough to make the refusal a remark. Mira read the sequence twice. The hidden hand was not an accident of a single sighting, to be explained by a cold morning or an injury of the ordinary kind that a man might favour for a day. It was a constant. Indoors and out, at rest and walking, eating and enquiring, the hand stayed hidden, and the coat stayed on the man as the pocket stayed on the hand, a heavy dock coat worn in all weathers, which Juno's people now described in their reports with the care of men who have understood that a garment in a case like this is not clothing but equipment.

So the detail planned around the hidden hand, because the hidden hand was now the shape of the attempt. Whoever came would come with one hand hidden. That was the sentence Juno wrote at the head of the new page, in her dry hand, and under it the arrangements rearranged themselves with the quiet efficiency that Mira had admired from the first morning and admired still, even as she felt her own place in them shrink to the observer's measure. The door positions changed their emphasis from faces to hands. The officer inside the room nearest the side door was given the lane window as well as the room, so that a coat seen in the lane could be called before it reached the door. The kitchen man was briefed to watch for a man who would not surrender his coat. Ferro's own part was discussed and, to Mira's relief, was not altered, because altering it was the one thing that neither Juno's authority nor the Office's need could accomplish against twenty years of deposit in a chair, and because Juno, to her credit, said so in plain words rather than pretending a plan existed where none could. He would sit in his chair. He would eat at his hour. The room would be ready around the fact, and the readiness would have to be enough.

Mira's part in the readiness was to read, and she read the room again in its empty hour on the fourth morning, not for Ferro this time but for the room as a field of movement, the distances between the side door and the chair, the counter's length and the kitchen doorway's swing, the tables whose positions were fixed by habit and the chairs that could be moved and the chairs that, by the evidence of their own worn crescents in the floorboards, would resist moving in the small, treacherous way of furniture that has settled. She reported what she found in words, to Juno, standing among the tables, and she kept her hands at her sides while she reported it, because her hands were the agreement's whole substance. She could say which table would turn if it were pushed and which would slide. She could say that the kitchen door had begun, in the damp of the week, to stick at the top of its swing, a small fault she had found with her shoulder in the ordinary course of passing through it, and that it cost a breath to force. She could not put her shoulder to it in earnest, not yet, not until something happened. The distinction had begun to feel, by the fourth evening, like a line drawn across her own body rather than across a page in Carrowmere, and she carried it back to her observer's table each evening and sat behind it and watched the room fill, and watched Ferro arrive by the side door and hang his coat on the second peg and draw his setting toward him by the width of two fingers, and understood that the case had narrowed to a waiting that everyone in the room, except the man in the chair, was performing as a profession.

Reni, at his table on the rotation, caught her eye once on the fifth evening over the rim of a glass and held it for exactly as long as a colleague may, and what passed in the look was not reassurance and was not meant to be. It was an acknowledgement, between two people who had worked together long enough to dispense with the forms of encouragement, that the possibilities had narrowed to the professional and the firm, that the hidden hand had been seen again and would be seen again, and that when it was seen in this room, at Ferro's hour, with Ferro in his chair, the agreement on the table in Carrowmere would meet the fact it had been written to govern, and one of them would give way. Mira looked back at the room. The lamps were lit in their order, window end first. The new waiter, a woman of middle years supplied by an agency and possessed of papers so complete that Juno's clerk had remarked on them, brought Ferro his soup without error and without watching him at all, and the correctness of it, after the boy's frightened errors, felt for a moment like another kind of absence in the room, a place where a frightened man's fear had been and was no longer, leaving the true shape of the threat standing alone in the quiet, with one hand in its pocket, somewhere out in the lane or the harbour dark, learning the last of what it needed to learn.


There remained the firm to be weighed again in the new light, and Mira weighed it in Juno's office on the fourth morning with the folder closed and the watch log open, in a conversation that both of them conducted with the care of officers who know that an excluded possibility, wrongly excluded, is more dangerous than a possibility never considered. Could a man from the firm have hired the pocketed stranger? Plainly he could. The two remaining possibilities were not rivals in the way she had first set them out. They could be layers of a single arrangement, a partner's money and a stranger's hand, the firm supplying the knowledge of the chair and the hour and the outside man supplying the errandless presence in the lane. Juno said it before Mira did, which Mira noted as another mark of the detail's quality, and the saying of it changed the planning less than might have been expected, because the plan had never depended on who paid. It depended on where, and on the shape of the arrival, and both of those the chair and the hidden hand had already fixed. What the layering changed was the meaning of the evidence after the week, if the week ended well. A stranger taken in the act would be a beginning and not an ending, a thread that led back through a broker's hands to a counting house desk, and Ferro's intervals would still have to do their work in a hearing room against men who would say that a hired stranger proved nothing about clean columns. Mira found that she wanted the attempt, if it came, to be legible, and she distrusted the wanting as soon as she recognised it, because legibility was an investigator's desire and she was not, in Valtessa, an investigator. She was an observer attached to a protection detail, and the detail's desire was simpler and better. It wanted an ordinary evening, and then another, until the hearing took Ferro out of his chair by lawful summons and into a room where his evidence, and not his body, was the thing at risk.

The waiter's leaving had one more consequence, quieter than the rest, which she recorded in her notes that night because it seemed to her to belong to the true account of the case and because no one else in the detail was placed to record it. The restaurant was sadder for his going. The staff, who had complained about his errors in the cheerful way of people training a newcomer they expect to keep, spoke of him after his flight with a guilt that none of them had earned, each of them rehearsing some small unkindness, a correction given sharply, a tray taken from his hands without explanation, as if a frightened boy's flight could be laid to a sharp word's account. The senior waiter set the boy's bowl, the one left at the lodging, which Juno's people had returned to the restaurant as a thing of no evidentiary worth, on a shelf in the kitchen where the staff kept their own cups, and no one, in Mira's hearing, proposed moving it. She thought that the Accord, in its vast and necessary machinery for watching the people who carried weight for others, had no column for the bowl on the shelf, and that the absence of the column was not a failure of the machinery but a reminder of its nature. It counted licences. It could not count the cost of being unlicensed and afraid in a room full of officers who had come, in fact, to protect someone else entirely, and who had frightened him out of his livelihood and his country as a by product of an arrangement he never knew existed. She carried the bowl in her mind alongside the pocketed hand for the rest of the week, the two facts in no competition, the small collateral sorrow and the approaching danger, and she thought the case was truer for holding both than it would have been holding either alone.

She slept that night with the case rearranged and woke with it still rearranged, which she had learned to take as the sign of a true collapse rather than a passing doubt, the false structures of a case having a way of rebuilding themselves overnight if any part of their foundation was sound. No part of the waiter's foundation had been sound. It had rested on a clerk's small note, a boy's frightened eyes, and a profession's readiness to find its threat close at hand, and all three supports, examined, turned out to be supports for a different structure entirely, a story about the Accord's own shadow falling across a boy who carried other people's sorrows for small money. She wrote the lesson into her notes in a single line, that fear in a room points where it points and not where the Office expects, and then she closed the notebook and went down to the restaurant to watch a man eat breakfast in his lodging house's parlour, under a rotation, at an hour that was not his hour, in a chair that was not his chair, and to wait for the evening to restore him to both.

## Pressure

The room filled in its usual order. Coats came off at the door. Chairs scraped. The counter took its first orders and the kitchen sent out heat and noise in short bursts. Ferro Cant came in by the door he always used and crossed to the table he always used and sat in the chair he always used, and the whole room settled around that fact as if it had been waiting for it. Mira watched from the place Juno had given her, near the wall where the service passage narrowed, where an observer could see the door and the counter and Ferro at once and could be seen seeing nothing in particular. She had a cup in front of her that she did not want. She had a clear view of the street through the glass beside the door. She had her hands flat on the table where anyone could see them, because hands flat on a table were part of what observe only looked like from the outside.

Reni was two tables away with a newspaper folded to the shipping pages. Two of Juno's officers were at the counter, out of uniform and doing a poor job of looking hungry. Another stood outside under the awning where the rain from earlier still dripped off the edge in a thin line. The detail had the room in the way a hand holds a cup, loosely and without showing effort, and Mira had spent the week learning to trust that hold even while she hated her place inside it. Ferro ordered without looking at the menu. The waiter who served him was not Tam Rezik. That absence had its own shape now. The new man was older, slower, correct in every small thing, and he did not watch Ferro's table any longer than he watched any other, and that correctness told its own story about how much fear the week had put into the staff.

The light outside thinned. Lamps came on along the dock road. A cart went past with wet wheels. A group of men from a late boat came in laughing and took the large table near the window and ordered loudly and then quieted, as men do when food arrives and the day catches up with them. Ferro ate in his small exact ceremonies. Knife set down. Glass lifted. Napkin folded once and set beside the plate at an angle he would correct without thinking before the next course. Mira had read those ceremonies in the chair and in the table and in the door on the first day, twenty years of them laid down in repetition until the wood and the stone remembered a man better than most people could, and she knew with a cold certainty that he would not move from that chair for any warning she was allowed to give. If it came, it would come to that chair. The detail knew it. Juno knew it. Ferro, in his own way, knew it too and had chosen the chair anyway, because it was his.

She felt the week in her shoulders first. Then in her eyes. The read of the restaurant had never quite left her. Cold had lived in her hands since the first morning, a low and patient cold that sleep did not clear, because she had slept poorly all week in the narrow room above the chandlery with the harbour bell marking hours she did not need marked. Watching had done the rest. Watching was work when you were forbidden to act on what you saw. Every evening she had marked the door, the counter, the passage to the kitchen, the distance from Ferro's chair to the nearest cover, and had filed each observation away unused, and the filing away had worn her thinner than acting would have. She was not dull. She was not careless. She was depleted, and she knew the shape of that depletion from other cases, the way it narrowed the margin between seeing a thing and reaching it.

The door opened.

Cold air came in with the smell of wet stone and rope and fried oil from the stall across the road. A man stood in the doorway long enough to let his eyes adjust, and in that pause Mira saw the coat first. A heavy dock coat, too heavy for the mild evening, worn as it had been worn in every report. Then she saw the hand that was not visible. One hand hung loose at his side. The other was in the coat pocket, pushed deep, the cloth drawn tight across it, and the pocket did not swing as he moved. Sabin Roche stepped inside and let the door close behind him.

He did not look at Ferro. That was the first wrong thing done well. He looked at the counter. He looked at the large table. He looked at the menu board as if prices interested him. He took two steps toward the counter and stopped where a man might stop to wait for a table, in a place that gave him the whole room at an angle. His free hand rested on the back of an empty chair. The pocketed hand stayed where it was. Mira felt her own breath go shallow and she made it slow again by counting the bottles on the shelf behind the counter, not for calm but for time. Time was the thing she needed to spend correctly.

Reni had seen him too. The newspaper did not move, but Reni's stillness was different from reading stillness, and Mira had worked beside that stillness long enough to read it without turning her head. At the counter one of Juno's officers set his cup down with care. The man outside under the awning had gone from view, called in by some signal she had not caught or moving to cover the back way as the plan required. The plan required many things. The plan did not include her.

She ran the room again the way she had run it every evening, only faster now. Ferro at his table, back half turned to the door in the way twenty years had taught him was comfortable. The counter to his left, solid, with a gap at the end where staff passed through. The kitchen doorway beyond that gap, curtained, with heat coming through the curtain in waves. Tables between Roche and Ferro, three of them occupied, one empty with chairs tucked in. The service passage behind her, narrow, leading to a back door and a yard she had walked twice under Juno's eye. Distances she had measured in steps when the room was empty now had to be measured in people and noise and a man who would not wait once he began.

Roche asked the counterman a question. The counterman pointed toward the back as if directing him to a washroom or a telephone. Roche nodded. He smiled with the part of his face that smiling uses when it means nothing. He took his free hand off the chair back. He started to move, not toward the back, but on a line that would bring him past the empty table and then clear, with nothing between him and Ferro but the last occupied table where two women were sharing a plate and arguing quietly about a bill.

Mira made the decision standing still.

She felt it arrive not as heat but as clarity. The observe only condition had been written and agreed and named to her by Dunmore with the price attached, and she had carried that price all week like a coin in a closed fist. If she acted she broke a condition the Office had given its word on. She would answer for it herself. A second reprimand inside two weeks would go on her record beside the first, the one Harrand had written before she crossed into the Scour and handed to her on her return, and this one would be harder to argue with because it would be accurate in every line. She accepted that in the space between one breath and the next, not bravely and not bitterly, but as a cost counted and paid before the spending showed. Ferro's evidence mattered. Ferro's life mattered more, and the two were in the same chair, and the man with the pocketed hand was done pretending to wait for a table.

She stood up.

Her chair scraped. The sound was small and the room did not notice it. She moved before Roche's line became a run, keeping her own table between them for the first two steps, speaking as she moved in a voice pitched for Ferro alone.

Ferro. Up. Now. Leave the plate.

He looked at her with more annoyance than fear. Twenty years of habit held him in the chair for a full second that she could not spare. She did not reach for Roche. She did not look at Roche. She put her hand on Ferro's shoulder and then on the back of his chair and pulled the chair out and sideways in one motion, so that habit lost its anchor and the man had to stand or fall. He stood, startled, his napkin still in his hand. She turned him by the shoulder toward the counter gap. She kept her body between him and the room without making a wall of it.

Move to the kitchen door. Do not stop at the counter. Go through the curtain.

He went. He was not a fool and fear, when it finally arrived, arrived in him as obedience to a voice that sounded certain. But she was late to the second movement. That lateness had a cause and she knew it even as it happened. The week of watching and the cold that had never left her hands and the poor sleep above the chandlery had narrowed her, and her second step, the step that should have taken the table with her as she went, came half a second after it should have come. Ferro caught the edge of his own table with his hip as he turned. Crockery jumped. A glass fell and broke on the floor with a bright sound. Ferro stumbled against the counter and put a hand out and knocked a stack of small plates, and one of them cut his palm shallowly as he steadied himself, no worse than a kitchen cut, but enough to make him hiss and clutch the napkin around his hand and lose another breath before the curtain. The kitchen door, when she reached it ahead of him and hauled at it to clear his path, stuck on its swollen frame for a single breath before it gave, and that breath cost them too. None of it was error. Her plan had been right in every line. Her body, spent by the week, executed it a fraction slower than the room required, and the room charged her for the fraction in noise and delay.

Roche came across the room then.

That was the attempt, and it took only seconds, and later everyone who described it would make it longer than it was. He crossed the floor fast with his free hand out for balance and the pocketed hand coming out of the coat at last as he reached the place where Ferro had been sitting. The hand came out into the lamplight and every person in the room saw it at once. Scarring crossed the palm and crossed the back of the hand as well, a pattern laid over the whole surface so that the hand looked marked from every side, and the fingers did not open fully as he reached. He reached for a man who was no longer there. His reaching hand struck the edge of Ferro's overturned chair and his weight went into the table Mira had dragged into his path, the table she had pulled sideways as she passed it, so that its legs caught his legs and its top took his hip and the whole arrangement of the room she had read for a week stood between him and the kitchen curtain. Plates slid. A jug went over. He recovered with the quickness of a man who had rehearsed a simpler room than this one, and he turned toward the counter gap with his marked hand raised and his other hand coming up, and by then Juno's officers were off their stools.

They took him from two sides as officers are trained to take a man in a crowded place, one at the free arm and one at the shoulder and coat, driving him down and back against the counter where his marked hand struck the wood and left him gasping more with surprise than pain. A third officer came through the door from the street. Chairs went over. The large table by the window erupted in shouting. One of the women at the last table screamed once and then put both hands over her mouth. Roche fought briefly and with real strength, the kind that comes from a plan collapsing all at once, and he tried to turn his marked hand toward the officer who held his coat, and the fingers would not close as he wanted them to close, and that failure seemed to frighten him more than the hands on him. Then he was on the floor with an officer's knee at his back and his free wrist held and his marked hand pressed open against the boards by an officer's forearm across his wrist, held there not cruelly but firmly, displayed to the room despite him.

Mira did not touch him. At no point in those seconds did her hand meet any part of him. She had Ferro by the sleeve at the kitchen doorway and then she had the curtain held aside and then she had Ferro through it and into the heat and steam of the kitchen, where a cook with a ladle still raised stared at them both. She set her back to the kitchen side of the doorway and listened to the room finish its violence without her. Dishes settled. A chair was righted. Juno's voice came through clear and hard, giving orders in the tone of a woman whose port this was, and the tone said the detail had its man and the room was to sit down and stay sat. Ferro stood beside the steam table breathing hard, napkin wrapped around his shallow cut, his face grey and furious and alive. He looked at Mira as if she had broken something of his that could not be replaced, and perhaps she had. The chair would be righted. The table would be set again. But the twenty years had been interrupted by hands, his own habits used as the map by which a man had found him, and that knowledge would sit at the table with him from now on whenever he ate there, if he ever ate there again.

Through the curtain she heard Roche say Ferro's name once, not loudly, as if confirming an address he had got wrong. Then Juno told him to save his breath for questions. An officer read him the words that Valtessa required read to a man taken in the act, and Roche said nothing after that. Mira kept her hand on the doorframe because the frame was solid and her hands were shaking, not from fear, which had passed through her and gone, but from the accumulated tiredness of the week arriving all at once now that the moment no longer needed her upright. She made herself breathe. She made herself note the room through the curtain gap in order. Roche down. Officers on him. Reni standing now, newspaper abandoned, watching the door as the plan required. The marked hand still visible where the officer held it, palm half turned to the light. She looked at that hand for as long as looking was useful and no longer, and she turned back to Ferro and told him, in a voice that surprised her by being almost gentle, that he could sit down on the stool by the steam table, and that someone would bring water, and that the evening was over in every way that mattered most.


## The Silence

Afterwards there was a room to be given back to its owners and a statement to be given to Juno's people, and between those two duties there was a silence that belonged to Mira alone, in the small back room off the kitchen where someone had set a chair for her and a glass of water she did not drink. The cook had gone back to his pans. Ferro had been taken to be looked at and then to be guarded somewhere quieter than a kitchen. Roche was gone from the floor and the floor had been swept, and through the wall she could hear the ordinary sounds of a restaurant deciding whether it could be ordinary again. She sat in that back room and did not write anything yet. She let the stillness do its work first, because a report written too soon carries the noise of the room in it and loses the shape of what was known and when it was known, and she had learned to lay the fair chance out plainly before she let any conclusion stand.

The pocketed hand came first, as it should. A man had been seen near the restaurant and near Ferro's office keeping one hand in his coat pocket the entire time, in weather and indoors, at rest and walking, and that report had sat in the tasking papers as a detail among details until she saw the same thing with her own eyes in the doorway, the cloth drawn tight, the pocket that did not swing, the hand withheld from every ordinary use a hand is put to when a man enters a room and waits among strangers. It had been the shape of the attempt before the attempt had a face. Whoever came, the detail had agreed after the waiter went, would come with one hand hidden, and he had, and the hidden thing, when it came out at last, was not a weapon anyone could name but a hand marked across palm and back, and that marking was now the fact at the centre of the case and no one in the building could explain it.

The waiter came second, because fairness required her to hold him in the account even though he had left it. Tam Rezik had been new and nervous and had watched Ferro's table more than a waiter should, and on the third day he had left the country, and for half a day that flight had looked like the case breaking open. It had broken nothing. He had left because he was an unlicensed Carrier, carrying weight and bound material for dockside clients under no licence the Accord could inspect, and the arrival of a protection detail with an Office observer asking after licence papers had frightened him out of the country for a crime that had nothing to do with Ferro at all. His fear had pointed at the Accord and away from the witness, and she had nearly read it backwards, and remembering that near error steadied her more than any success in the evening could have done.

And underneath both of those, older and quieter, sat the thing she had carried out of another case and into this one without knowing she would need it here. Venn, in the inert house case, was afraid of something, and what he feared was not the Accord, and that had taught her that fear in a room points where it points and not where the Office expects it to point. The waiter's fear had pointed at the licence and the Accord. Roche's fear, when his fingers would not close as he wanted them to close, had pointed at his own hand. Ferro's fear, when it came, had pointed at a chair and a habit and a life interrupted. She sat with those three fears laid out in a row in her mind and did not force them into a pattern. Patterns forced in silence become errors written in ink later. She drank the water at last, because her hands had stopped shaking and the cold in them was the ordinary cold of a read room rather than the shaking kind, and then she stood and went out to give Juno her statement in the order in which things had happened, no sooner and no later.

## Close

Juno closed the detail the way she had run it, in order and without waste. Ferro Cant was alive, and by morning his evidence had been taken down in full by Valtessan officers with an Office copy sealed for Carrowmere, so that whatever happened to any one set of papers the witness account of the dockside books would survive. His cut palm had been cleaned and bound in the kitchen that night and had needed nothing more than binding, though he complained about it with the energy of a man whose real injury was to his routine. He was moved to rooms the Office was not told about, under a guard the Office did not command, and Mira approved of both arrangements without saying so, because Valtessan primacy in Valtessan ports was not a phrase in a tasking paper but a fact that had just taken an attacker off a restaurant floor while she stood in a kitchen doorway holding a curtain. Sabin Roche was in Valtessan custody, charged under Valtessan law with the attempt, and he had said very little since saying Ferro's name on the floor. What he had said did not include who had sent him, or whether anyone had sent him, or anything at all about his hand.

The coat was brought to her the next day in the same back room, in a canvas evidence bag that Juno's people had sealed and labelled and opened again in her presence, because the coat was an anchor and anchors were her business even under observe only, and observe only, in any case, had already been broken past mending. It was a heavy dock coat, heavier than it looked, with deep pockets and a lining patched in two places by hands that had sewn carefully and without vanity. She laid it across the table and set her own hands on it at the places where weight gathers in a garment, the collar where it is lifted on and off, the cuffs where it is pulled straight, the pocket mouth on the marked side where a hand goes in and out a hundred times without thinking. Then she read it, with Juno watching and Reni at the door and a Valtessan officer taking notes on everything except what she was about to say, because what she was about to say could not be noted into evidence in any form a court would recognise.

The coat gave her waiting. A month of it, laid down day after day in the grain of the cloth, in the patience of a man sitting in rooms and on benches and in the corners of yards where a shipping agent's evenings could be watched without being seen to be watched. It gave her the hour learned by heart and the chair learned by heart and a plan rehearsed against a room simpler than the one she had rearranged. And underneath the waiting, older than the waiting and stronger, it gave her pain. A great deal of pain, carried daily, not the sharp pain of the attempt or of a hand striking a counter, but a chronic pain that had its own hours, worse in the morning when the hand was first asked to work, worse in cold, present in some degree at every hour she could find in the cloth, so that ordinary things, a cup lifted, a coat fastened, a pocket used as a resting place because resting was the only position that eased it, had all been done for a long time through a steady grinding hurt that the man had learned to arrange his life around. She came out of the read cold to the elbows and sat for a while with her hands in her lap and did not trust her voice, and when she did speak she said only what the coat could support. He had waited about a month. He had been in pain the whole of that month and for long before it. The pain was not from the attempt. Nothing in the coat told her who had sent him, because weight records intensity and affect and not identity, and a coat worn by a hired man carries the hiring no more clearly than a chair carries the name of the man who sat in it.

The hand itself she could not read at all. She said that plainly, in the room and later in her report, because the limit mattered more than any guess she might have dressed up as knowledge. Weight does not read a body. It does not read the dead and it does not read the living flesh, not a face, not a scar, and not a hand, however much the hand in question was the one thing in the case she wanted to read. The coat she could read, because a coat is a thing worn and handled and left behind. The hand she could not, because a hand is a person, and persons are closed to her in the way that rooms and objects are not. Roche could have told them what had marked him. Roche did not tell them. The officers' photographs and the doctor's notes were all the Office would ever have of that hand, and neither photographs nor notes could do what her practice could not.

So the Office table had to do the work instead, the table of known practice injuries that the Office keeps because practitioners are known by what practice costs them, and Mira set the photographs beside her memory of the three known injuries and worked through the comparison in the four respects the table uses, in prose and in order, with Juno and Reni listening and the officer writing. In the first respect, where the injury sits, the known injuries are each particular about place. The first sits across the fingers only, where a binding is held too long against the skin. The second sits in the palm alone, a single site where weight was taken in and not discharged. The third sits at the wrist and the base of the thumb, where a carried load settles over years. Roche's scarring sat across the palm and across the back of the hand together, the whole hand marked on both its faces, and no known injury in the table takes both faces of the hand at once. In the second respect, its pattern, the first known injury runs in straight lines that follow the binding. The second forms a single dense patch, round as a coin pressed hard. The third spreads in fine branches from the wrist, fading as it goes. Roche's pattern followed none of those geometries. It crossed palm and back in bands that met at the edges of the hand, as if the marking had gone through the hand rather than across any one surface of it, and the bands were neither straight nor round nor branching, but continuous from one face to the other. In the third respect, what it does to the hand's use, the first known injury stiffens the fingers but leaves the grip whole. The second deadens the palm to touch while strength remains. The third weakens the grip over years but leaves fine work possible for a long time. Roche's hand would not open fully and would not close fully either, as the room had all seen when he reached and when he tried to turn his hand on the officer who held him, and the doctor's notes said the loss sat in the whole movement of the hand rather than in strength or feeling alone. In the fourth respect, how it has healed, the first known injury heals clean and leaves a mark that fades within a few years. The second heals raised and stays raised. The third heals in stages, with fresh marking over old as the carrying continues. Roche's scarring had healed flat and old and all of one age, the doctor said, a single event or a single season of events long finished, with no fresh marking over it and no sign that the cause was still being practised. In respect after respect it matched none of the three known injuries. Nobody in the room could identify it. Juno said so first, flatly, as a fact for the record, and the officer wrote it down, and Mira, who had wanted the table to close the question, found instead that the table had proved the question open beyond argument.

The costs were entered as plainly as the findings, because Dunmore had named the price at the tasking and Mira did not intend to soften it in the closing. She had broken the observe only condition, deliberately and with her eyes open, and a second reprimand inside two weeks would follow as a matter of course, and she had accepted it in advance at the moment of decision and accepted it again now without argument. The other cost was slower and would last longer than any reprimand. She carried away from the coat a month of somebody else's chronic pain, taken on in the read and still with her when she wrote the closing report, a steady grinding hurt in hands that were not injured, worst in the morning and in cold, that made cups and coat fastenings and the turning of pages into tasks to be arranged around, exactly as Roche had arranged his own life around the original of it. It would fade as such costs fade, over weeks rather than days, and it would still be with her into the next case, and she recorded it in those words and no softer ones, because a cost recorded softly is a cost the next officer cannot plan for.

Before the papers left the room Juno asked her one question that had nothing to do with evidence, which was whether the Office would send an observer again if Valtessa ran another detail of this kind, or whether the breaking of the condition had closed that door. Mira answered honestly that she did not know, and that the answer would be made in Carrowmere by people who weighed access against conduct in rooms she was not invited into. Juno received that without offence, as a woman receives weather she had already dressed for, and said that for her part the record would state that the observer had acted when action was the only thing left that protected the witness, and that Valtessa would write its own account of the price and pay its own share of it. It was not absolution. It was something better, an accurate sentence in a file that would outlast both of them, and Mira carried it out of the room with more care than she had carried the reprimand in.

Reni found her after the report was sealed, in the passage outside the back room, and told her what the docks were already saying, with the air of a man reporting weather he did not believe. On the Valtessan docks they said NIGHTJAR had broken the attacker's arm in the fight in the restaurant, broken it with her hands in front of a room full of witnesses. Reni, who was there, told them and told her that she never touched him. Not a blow, not a hold, not a hand on him at any point in the evening. She had moved a witness and a table and a chair and a door, and Valtessan officers had done every bit of the taking, and the arm the docks had given Roche was not even injured beyond the bruising of being held against a counter by men doing their jobs. The story on the docks had invented a touch, and an injury, and a fight, none of which had happened in the room both of them had stood in. Mira listened and said only that the correction mattered more than the story, and Reni said he would keep making it, in the places where the story was told, for as long as the story lasted.


## The Notch

The register went back to Carrowmere with the closing papers, the private register she had kept against instruction and given voluntarily to Harrand, now an Accord document in oversight keeping with a numbered Accord label on its cover, and in the space left for what the case had added to the season she wrote one line and no more, because one line was all the case had honestly earned. Whoever applies the technique is injured in the same way, on the hands. She read the line twice before she sealed it. It was a rung and not an answer. It said what the photographs and the table together had forced into the open, that the marking on Roche's hand was not an accident of his trade as the Office understood trades, and that the hand was evidence of a practice the Office had no name for, practised by someone who had paid for it in the same coin Roche had paid in, if Roche had practised it at all, which nobody could say. It did not say who. It did not say how. It did not reach one step toward any later claim, and she was careful, in the wording and in the filing, that it should not be made to reach. One notch, cut clean, and the knife put away.

Everything else from the week went into its proper folder and closed. The waiter's name went into a licence file that was not a protection file, with his flight recorded as a separate matter and his fear recorded fairly, so that no later reader could pull him back into a threat he had never been part of. Ferro's name went into a witness file with his evidence sealed and his survival stated in the first line, where it belonged. Juno's commendation of her own officers went in unedited, as Juno had written it, because the taking had been theirs and the record was better for saying so without an Office gloss. The reprimand, when it came, would go into a file Mira did not keep, and she thought about it exactly as long as it deserved, which was the time it took to note that it had been priced in before it was earned. The pain from the coat was still in her hands as she filed, and she filed slowly because of it, turning pages with the care of a woman learning which movements cost and which could still be done carelessly, and she understood, with a sympathy she had not expected to feel for the man in Valtessan custody, how a month of such arranging could teach a person to keep one hand in a pocket where the world could not knock it.

One question carried out of the installment unanswered, and she wrote it in the carried space in the plainest words she had. The hand, and nobody in the Office able to name the injury. Not Juno, whose port had seen more practice injuries than Carrowmere had catalogued. Not the doctor, whose notes described everything except a cause. Not the table, which had been built for exactly this purpose and had returned no match in any of its four respects. Not Mira herself, who could read the coat the hand had lived in and could not read the hand. Somewhere behind the attempt on Ferro Cant there was a technique that marked its practitioner on both faces of the hand in bands that met at the edges, that took the whole movement of the hand and left strength and feeling arguing with each other, that healed flat and old and all of one age, and the Office did not know its name, or its use, or whether Roche was its practitioner or only its product. That not knowing was the true close of the protection detail, whatever the closing papers said about success, and she let it stand as the last entry before the seal.

Reni had the final word on the story, as he had earned it by being in the room. Before the papers left Valtessa he came by the back room one more time to say that the dock talk had already travelled two quays and a ferry, growing as it travelled, and that in the newest version the arm had been broken in two places by a woman half Roche's size who had thrown him across his own table. He reported it without pleasure. Then he corrected it again, in the same plain words he had used on the docks, for the record and for her. She never touched him. He had been there. He had watched her hands all evening, because watching had been his job when hers was forbidden, and her hands had touched a witness, a chair, a table, a curtain and a doorframe, and nothing else that mattered. Mira thanked him and did not ask him to stop correcting it. Stories grow in ports the way damp grows in a hull, whether anyone tends them or not, but a correction made by a man who was there is a dry cloth laid over the damp each time, and it was worth the laying.

The last thing pinned into the file, after the statements and the doctor's notes and the sealed copy of the table, was a photograph of a palm pinned to a board next to three others that look nothing like it.

## Case File

### Q: How is a Carrier defined for licensing purposes under the Accord?

A Carrier is a practitioner who takes weight or bound material into personal custody for another person and moves or holds it under agreed terms. The definition turns on custody for others rather than on the act of carrying alone. A practitioner who moves only material arising from personal work is recorded under a different heading and is not entered as a Carrier. Licensing follows the definition because custody creates a chain that the Accord must be able to inspect. The licence names the Carrier, the classes of material accepted, and the places where custody may begin and end. Work outside those entries is treated as unlicensed work, even where the handling itself was competent and no harm followed from the movement.

### Q: Why does an unlicensed Carrier avoid ports where licence returns are inspected?

An unlicensed Carrier avoids inspected ports because the return is the point at which custody becomes visible. A licence return lists material received, material delivered, and any interval where custody cannot be accounted for in ordinary terms. Without a licence there is no return to file, and a request for papers therefore creates an immediate difficulty that conversation cannot resolve. Flight is seldom a comment on any single consignment. It is more often a response to the prospect of inspection itself, since inspection would expose a pattern of work rather than one movement. Details are instructed to record flight of this kind separately from flight connected with a protected person, because the two carry different meanings for a case.

### Q: What material may a licensed Carrier lawfully carry for another person?

A licensed Carrier may carry weight held in a suitable anchor and bound material whose binding is declared on the accompanying papers. The papers must state the origin of the material, the person for whom it is carried, and the place where custody will end. Material whose origin cannot be stated, or whose binding has not been declared, falls outside lawful carriage even when the Carrier holds a current licence. Certain classes are refused carriage in all ports, and a Carrier who accepts them loses the protection of the licence for that movement. The Office does not direct Carriers in their ordinary trade. It records the limits so that officers can tell lawful custody from concealment when the two appear in the same room.

### Q: How does the Office distinguish carrying from practising in its own right?

The distinction rests on whose purpose the work serves and who bears the effect of the handling. A Carrier receives material that already exists, holds or moves it for another, and delivers it in the condition agreed at receipt. A practitioner working in a personal capacity creates, alters, or applies an effect and answers for the result as an author of the work. Difficulty arises where a Carrier is asked to improve, loosen, or redirect material in transit. At that point carriage has become practice, and the licence for carriage alone does not cover it. Officers are instructed to describe what was actually done, in plain sequence, and to leave the classification to the reviewing desk rather than settle it in the field.

### Q: On what basis does Valtessa claim primacy for a detail inside its own ports?

Valtessan primacy rests on the principle that protection given on Valtessan ground is a Valtessan responsibility, exercised by Valtessan officers under Valtessan command. Membership of the Accord gives the Office standing to be present where permission has been granted, but standing is not command. The port, the streets around it, and the premises where a witness is kept are administered by the member service, which also answers for failures in that administration. Primacy is therefore recorded as a matter of responsibility rather than courtesy. Where the Office believes a detail is being run badly, its remedy is representation through the agreed channel and, in grave cases, withdrawal of its observer. It is not assumption of the detail by the observer on the ground.

### Q: Which decisions remain with the Valtessan commander when an Office observer is present?

When an Office observer is present by permission, decisions on positions, rotations, entry and exit routes, and the movement of the protected person remain with the Valtessan commander. The observer may offer information drawn from a read or from Office papers, and the commander decides what use to make of it. The observer does not assign posts, does not alter a ground plan, and does not give orders to Valtessan officers, even where the observer believes a different arrangement would be safer. This division is written into the permission so that no officer in the room is left uncertain about who may direct the detail. Confusion over command during an incident is treated by both services as a failure of preparation rather than a misfortune.

### Q: How is permission for Office presence in a Valtessan port recorded and renewed?

Permission is recorded in writing before the observer travels, naming the port, the premises covered, the purpose of the presence, and the conditions attached to it. A copy is held by the Valtessan service and a copy by the Office, and the observer carries the conditions in a form that can be shown on request. Renewal is required when the purpose changes, when the detail moves to premises not named in the original grant, or when the period stated in the grant expires. Presence continued beyond the terms of the grant is treated as presence without permission, however welcome the observer may be in practice. Officers are instructed to check the wording before each new phase of a detail rather than rely on a general understanding reached at the start.

### Q: What happens to Office access when primacy is disputed during a live detail?

A dispute over primacy during a live detail is handled by preserving the detail first and arguing the question afterwards through the agreed channel. The Valtessan commander continues to command, the observer continues under the stated conditions, and neither side withdraws protection from the person at risk while the dispute is referred. The Office nevertheless records such disputes with care, because access depends on confidence that permission will be honoured in both directions. A member service that finds its primacy set aside without cause may restrict future grants, require narrower conditions, or refuse presence altogether for a period. Officers are reminded that a single incident can therefore affect access for officers who were not present and cases that have not yet arisen.

### Q: What does an observe only condition permit an officer to do, and what does it forbid?

An observe only condition permits an officer to be present, to watch, to read where reading has been allowed, and to report what is seen to both the commanding service and the Office. It permits advice offered through the commander and warnings given plainly where danger is immediate and specific. It forbids direct action on the protected person, on the ground plan, and on any person approaching the detail. The officer may not move furniture, redirect visitors, seize material, or lay hands on anyone in the course of the detail. The condition is intentionally narrow. Its purpose is to keep the observer useful as a witness and adviser while leaving responsibility for action with the service that holds command of the ground.

### Q: How is an observe only condition agreed before a detail begins?

The condition is proposed by the service holding primacy, set out in writing, and accepted by the Office before the observer is assigned. The written form states what the observer may do, what is forbidden, and the point at which the observer should withdraw rather than act outside the terms. Both sides sign, and the protected person is told in ordinary language that the observer is present to watch and report rather than to intervene. Agreement after arrival is not accepted as a substitute, because conditions settled under pressure tend to be remembered differently by each side. Where time does not allow a written agreement, the Office may decline the presence entirely. A missing observer is considered preferable to an observer working under terms nobody can later produce.

### Q: What must an officer record when she believes an observe only condition should be broken?

An officer who believes the condition should be broken is expected to record the belief before acting where time allows, and in all cases to record it fully afterwards. The record states the specific danger seen, the time available for decision, the alternatives considered, and why reporting through the commander would not have met the danger in time. General unease is not sufficient, and a wish to be useful is not recorded as a ground. The record is made in the officer's own hand on the day concerned and is not revised after discussion with colleagues. Its purpose is not to excuse the breach in advance. It is to give the reviewing authority a contemporaneous account against which the outcome, good or bad, can be judged without reliance on memory reshaped by events.

### Q: What consequence follows when an observe only condition is broken to prevent harm?

Breaking the condition remains a breach even where harm was prevented and even where the protected person survives because of the act. The officer answers for the breach through ordinary discipline, which may include a formal reprimand entered on the file, restriction from similar details, or removal from the tasking concerned. The service holding primacy may also make its own representation, and the Office does not shield the officer from that consequence by pointing to the result. This position is stated to officers before assignment so that the decision, if it comes, is made with the price known. A successful outcome is recorded in the case papers as fact. It is not treated as retrospective permission, and it does not amend the condition for any later detail.

### Q: Why does the Office keep a table of known practice injuries?

The Office keeps the table because practice leaves marks, and marks that recur can be recognised, compared, and taught. Practitioners are often identified less by statements about their work than by what the work has cost them over time. A maintained table allows an officer in the field to describe an injury in shared terms and allows a reviewing desk to test that description against recorded patterns. The table also prevents a common error, which is the confident naming of an unfamiliar injury after the nearest familiar one. Entries are added only after examination and review, and doubtful cases are held outside the table until the pattern is settled. An injury that matches nothing in the table is recorded as unmatched rather than forced into a category.

### Q: How is a practice injury entered in the table, and by what respects is it described?

An entry is made from direct examination notes, photographs where photographs were permitted, and the account of the examining officer, checked by a second officer before filing. Each injury is described under the same respects so that unlike cases can be compared without rearrangement. The respects cover where the injury sits on the hand or body, the pattern it forms, what it does to the use of the part concerned, and how it has healed or is expected to heal. Language is kept plain and measurements are given in words, because the table is used in rooms where precision of description matters more than precision of instruments. An entry that cannot be completed under all respects is marked incomplete and is not used as the sole basis for an identification.

### Q: What does a failure to match any known injury require the detail to do next?

Failure to match requires the detail to preserve the evidence of the injury without naming it. Photographs are taken where permitted, descriptions are written independently by those who saw the injury, and the accounts are filed separately so that later discussion does not produce a false agreement. The person concerned is not pressed to supply an explanation as a condition of custody, and officers do not offer candidate names from the table in questioning, since a name offered by authority is easily adopted by a tired or frightened witness. The unmatched description is sent to the Office for comparison against material not held locally. Until a match is established by review, reports refer to the injury by its described respects only. Speculation is recorded as speculation or not recorded at all.

### Q: How are healed practice injuries distinguished from injuries still changing?

Healed injuries are distinguished by stability. The pattern no longer alters between examinations, the surface has closed in a settled form, and the loss or retention of use can be stated without qualification. An injury still changing may alter in pattern, may affect use differently from one examination to the next, and may carry pain that is reported as moving or increasing rather than constant. The distinction matters because a changing injury suggests recent or continuing practice, while a settled injury may belong to work done long before the case in hand. Officers are instructed not to assign an age to an injury from its appearance alone. They record the state observed, the interval between observations where there was one, and any report of pain in the words used by the person concerned, without translating that report into a conclusion.

### Q: Why does a witness habit of place and hour become a security problem?

A fixed habit removes uncertainty for anyone planning harm. Where a witness sits in the same chair, at the same table, at the same hour, over a long period, an attacker does not need to search, follow, or improvise. The room can be studied in advance, approaches can be rehearsed, and the attempt can be timed to a minute that the witness himself supplies. Habit also narrows the detail, which must defend the same ground repeatedly while an attacker need only succeed once. The Office does not treat habit as foolishness. For many witnesses the routine is the part of life that remains theirs, and a detail that begins by despising it will lose the cooperation on which protection depends. The problem is recorded plainly and planned around with respect.

### Q: How does a detail plan around a habit that the witness will not change?

Planning begins from the assumption that the habit will continue exactly as described, because plans built on promised change fail when the change does not happen. The detail studies the room as the attacker will study it, noting doors, sight lines, the distance from chair to cover, and the moments when staff movement masks an approach. Positions are chosen to alter the outcome inside the room rather than to prevent the witness from entering it. Where the service holding command agrees, small variations that do not disturb the witness may be introduced in the detail own arrangements, such as rotation timing and the placement of officers. The witness is told what the detail needs from him in an emergency, in few words, rehearsed once, and not repeated until the rehearsal itself becomes a source of anxiety.

### Q: What does long repetition at one table tell an observer about where an attempt must come?

Long repetition fixes the target in space, and a fixed target reduces the ground an attempt can usefully occupy. If the witness will be in one chair at a known hour, the attempt must reach that chair during that hour or abandon the advantage the habit provides. An observer who has read the room at its anchors can therefore state, with proper caution, which approaches matter and which are theatre. Repetition also tells the observer what will not happen. The witness will not arrive early to defeat a watch, will not choose another room out of caution, and will not leave by an unfamiliar door without direction. Planning that respects those limits is narrower and stronger than planning that imagines a more convenient witness appearing on the day concerned.

### Q: How should a detail record a witness routine without turning the record itself into a risk?

The routine is recorded in the detail papers held by the commanding service, with access limited to officers who need the information to hold their positions. The record states place, hour, chair or its equivalent, usual arrivals and departures, and the persons ordinarily present, in plain terms and without comment on character. Copies are counted, and no copy is carried as a personal note or left with premises staff for convenience. Where the Office observer contributes a read of the room, that contribution is entered as part of the detail record rather than circulated separately, so that the full picture exists in one controlled place. At the close of the detail the commanding service decides retention. A routine that would endanger the witness if read by strangers is treated with the same care as the witness himself.
