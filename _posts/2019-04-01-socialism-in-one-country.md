Socialism in one country is the doctrine, advanced from 1924, that a socialist society could be constructed inside the Soviet Union alone, without waiting for victorious revolutions in the advanced countries to rescue or complete the work. It was formulated against an older expectation, held across the movement that made the October Revolution, that socialist construction begun in a backward and isolated country could not be finished there, because the material and cultural level such construction required exceeded what one country, and that country in particular, could supply from its own resources. The phrase is short, the doctrine is precise, and the quarrel it started has never entirely closed, because the quarrel was never only about policy. Part of it concerned circumstance: what a besieged state should do when the foreign revolutions it had counted on did not arrive. Part of it concerned texts: what the founders had actually written, and what a particular passage of Lenin could legitimately be made to carry. This article treats the doctrine as a glossary problem before treating it as a political one, because the precision is where the interest lies. A reader who finishes it should be able to state the claim exactly, name the evidence offered for it, and say why that evidence fails in an instructive way.

One fact governs everything that follows, and it is placed here at the start because factional accounts of the doctrine routinely omit it. The doctrine's author, Joseph Stalin, published in 1924 a work codifying Leninism, and in the first edition of that work he stated that socialism could not be victorious in a single country, and that organising socialist production there exceeded what one country's proletarians could achieve unaided. A revised edition, issued months later, asserted the contrary, that socialist society could and must be built within the country's own borders, and the earlier formulation quietly disappeared from the text. Both editions survive. The revision is documented in the scholarly literature beyond serious dispute. Any account of the doctrine that does not mention the reversal is not an abbreviation but a selection, and the selection always serves a side. The name this article gives the problem is the two editions, and the claim attached to it is simple: a doctrine whose founding text asserts a proposition and then, within months, asserts its negation cannot rest its case on the authority of that text. Whatever defence the doctrine has must be built elsewhere, on the argument from circumstance, and that argument deserves to be heard at full strength precisely because the textual one collapses.

## Socialism in one country: what the doctrine claims

Precision begins with the proposition itself, stripped of the slogans that accumulated around it. The doctrine claims that the construction of a socialist society, meaning a society in which the means of production are socially owned, the old exploiting classes have been eliminated as classes, and production is organised for use rather than for private accumulation, can be carried through to completion within the borders of a single state, the Soviet Union, by the efforts of its own working class and peasantry, without that completion depending on prior revolutionary victories abroad. Each element of that sentence does work. Construction to completion is the strong claim, and it is what separates the doctrine from the uncontroversial observation that a revolution can begin in one country, seize power there, and start building. Nobody in the dispute denied the weaker proposition. The argument was always about finishing.

### What exactly did the doctrine claim could be completed within one country?

It claimed that a complete socialist society could be built inside a single state: social ownership established, exploiting classes abolished, and a planned economy functioning, all achieved by domestic effort. The claim concerned completion, not beginnings, and that distinction between starting a construction and finishing it is the hinge on which the entire controversy turns.

Formulations of the doctrine usually carried a qualification, and the qualification matters for fairness. Its author distinguished between building socialism in one country, which he held to be possible, and the final victory of socialism, meaning a guarantee against the restoration of the old order through foreign intervention, which he held to require revolutions in several countries. Critics replied that the distinction was a shelter: the strong claim did the political work in public argument, while the qualification absorbed objections in theoretical debate. Defenders answered that the distinction was genuine and inherited, since earlier writers had also separated the internal completion of a new society from its external security. A glossary article does not settle that exchange, but it must record the qualification, because accounts that omit it caricature the doctrine as a promise of total self-sufficiency, which its careful statements never quite made.

### What did the doctrine not claim about the wider world?

It never claimed that revolutions elsewhere were needless or unwelcome, and its statements kept international revolution as the movement's horizon. What it denied was dependence: completion at home was not to wait upon events abroad. The subordination of foreign parties followed in practice, as a consequence its critics charged, not as a clause in the formula.

The doctrine also had a negative edge that defined it as sharply as its positive claim. It was aimed at the theory of permanent revolution, whose third component held that a revolution in a backward country, having completed its democratic tasks and begun its socialist ones, would collide with limits it could not pass alone, so that its fate would be decided on the international arena. The series treats that theory in its own article, [permanent revolution explained](/2014/09/15/permanent-revolution-explained/), and the comparison belongs there as much as here. What matters for definition is the shape of the disagreement: both sides accepted that revolution could begin in one country; they divided over whether its socialist stage could be sealed within national borders or whether national borders were precisely where its contradictions would gather. Stated as propositions about completion, the dispute is clean. Stated as loyalties, it becomes unintelligible, which is why this article keeps returning to propositions.

A further precision concerns the word country. The Soviet Union was not a nation state of the ordinary kind but a federation spanning many nations, and the doctrine's phrasing slid between the Union as a single country and the national units inside it. The slide was rarely examined at the time, yet it mattered, because arguments about the resources available to one country sound different when the country in question holds a sixth of the earth's land surface and a population drawn from dozens of peoples. Opponents occasionally pressed the point, asking whether the doctrine would have been advanced with the same confidence for a small state, and the question exposed how much of the claim's plausibility rested on scale rather than on principle. The doctrine as stated, however, was general: it spoke of the proletariat of one country as such, and its defenders resisted attempts to reduce it to a comment on Russian size.

## The setting: a revolution left waiting

Doctrines are answers to situations before they are answers to opponents, and the situation here was stark. The October Revolution had been made by men and women who expected it to be the first act of a European drama. The Bolshevik leadership said so repeatedly and in print: the Russian upheaval was the prologue, Germany would supply the main act, and the advanced industrial countries would provide the material foundation that backward Russia lacked. This was not a private hope but the stated premise of the seizure of power, repeated in speeches, resolutions and the party's programme. When the German revolution of 1918 and 1919 was defeated, when the Hungarian Soviet Republic fell within months, when the Red Army was turned back before Warsaw in 1920, the premise failed in fact while remaining intact in theory. The state that resulted was isolated, invaded during its civil war by a shifting coalition of foreign powers, blockaded, famine struck, and governed by a party whose founding expectation had been falsified by events.

### Why did the question become urgent when it did?

Because the expected European revolutions had failed and the isolated Soviet state had to decide what its own activity meant. If construction could not be completed alone, policy became a holding operation sustained by hope. If it could, the state possessed a positive task. The doctrine answered a governing party's need for a defensible account of its daily work.

The New Economic Policy sharpened the question rather than settling it. A partial restoration of market relations, adopted as a retreat, could be described in two ways: as a breathing space before an international rescue that would restart the advance, or as the early terrain of a long national construction that would proceed at its own pace. The first description made the present provisional; the second made it foundational. After Lenin's death in January 1924, the succession struggle inside the party gave the theoretical question an institutional edge, since whichever account prevailed would define the legitimacy of the leadership that governed in its name. It is conventional, and not wrong, to note that the doctrine served its author's factional position. It is equally true that the question would have forced itself on any leadership, because a state cannot be administered indefinitely as an anteroom. The circumstance was real before it was useful, and any assessment that treats it as a mere pretext has not looked at the map of Europe in 1924.

Isolation also had an economic texture that the argument from circumstance would later draw on. The country needed machinery it could not yet manufacture, specialists it had not trained, and a peasantry whose cooperation could not simply be commanded without repeating the disasters of requisitioning. Building socialism in such a setting meant, in practice, deciding the tempo of industrialisation, the terms on which grain moved from village to town, and the degree of coercion the state would apply to its own population. The doctrine did not answer those questions, but it licensed them as questions of construction rather than questions of survival, and that licence changed the moral register of every subsequent policy. A sacrifice demanded for survival is one thing; a sacrifice demanded as a stage in building a new society is another, and the doctrine's career inside Soviet life cannot be understood apart from that shift in register.

## The two editions: the centre of the textual question

In the spring of 1924, Stalin delivered a course of lectures at Sverdlov University setting out the foundations of Leninism, and the lectures were published as a book that became the standard codification of the doctrine of the party and the revolution for a generation of communists. The work, Foundations of Leninism, was a textbook in the literal sense: it defined terms, arranged propositions, and taught readers what the correct positions were. Its authority inside the movement was immense precisely because it presented itself not as one man's opinions but as a faithful exposition of Lenin. That is the setting in which the reversal occurred, and the setting is what makes the reversal more than an author's change of mind. A private scholar may revise a view as evidence accumulates. A codifier who revises the codification, silently, within months, while the codification circulates as the movement's standard, has altered the record on which his own authority rests.

### Did the doctrine's author change his position?

yes, within a year. The first edition of his 1924 codification of Leninism stated that the victory of socialism in one country was impossible; a revised edition published later the same year stated the opposite. Both texts survive and the revision is documented.

### What did the first edition say about victory in one country?

It said that organising socialist production in a single country was impossible, that the efforts of one country's proletarians were insufficient for the victory of socialism, and that this victory required the combined efforts of the proletarians of several advanced countries. The passage presented this not as a concession but as settled doctrine, supported by quotations from Lenin.

The first edition's reasoning deserves to be followed rather than merely quoted against its author. It argued from the uneven development of capitalism, the very law that explained why revolution could break out first in a backward country, to the conclusion that the same unevenness limited what that country could achieve unaided. The overthrow of the bourgeoisie in one country was possible; the organisation of socialist production there was another matter, requiring a level of productive forces and of culture that a single backward country did not possess. The text treated this as orthodox Leninism, and in the spring of 1924 it was: the proposition it stated had been the common property of the party, repeated by leaders who would soon be on opposite sides of the quarrel. Nothing in the first edition reads as hesitation. It is written in the confident, enumerating manner of a man summarising what everyone in his audience already believes.

### What did the revised edition say instead?

It declared that the proletariat could and must build a socialist society within a single country, and treated denial of that capacity as a failure of faith in the working class. The spring passage was rewritten, its conclusion inverted, and readers were given no notice that an earlier version had ever circulated.

The revised edition appeared before the end of 1924, attached to a developing polemic. By December of that year the doctrine had been stated publicly as a positive thesis, in a polemical article of that December, where the possibility of building socialism in the Soviet Union was asserted against the theory of permanent revolution by name. The sequence is worth holding in order: a textbook states the old position in spring; the same textbook, revised within the year, states the new one; a polemical article then generalises the new position into a doctrine. Scholars who have reconstructed the chronology, most thoroughly in the bibliographical literature on Stalin's writings, treat the two texts as fixed points. The first edition is the harder of the two to obtain in English, a point taken up in the note on sources below, but its text is established, quoted in the scholarly literature, and not denied by any party to the dispute who has examined it.

### How do we know the revision happened?

Because both printed texts survive for comparison, because the editions' bibliographical record is documented, and because participants in the polemics referred to the alteration while it was fresh. The evidence is of the strongest kind intellectual history affords: two dated artefacts that differ at precisely the passage where doctrine was later claimed.

The namable claim of this article, the two editions, is therefore not an interpretation but a bibliographical fact with an interpretive consequence. The fact is that contradictory positions were published in successive editions of the same work within a single year. The consequence is that the textual case for the doctrine cannot rest on the authority of that work, since the work's authority is precisely what the contradiction dissolves. A reader who trusts the codification must explain why its second word overrides its first; a reader who does not is left asking what other ground the doctrine stands on. That question, properly pressed, relocates the entire debate. The doctrine's defenders, at their best, accepted the relocation and argued from circumstance. Its defenders at their worst pretended the first edition did not exist, and much popular writing on the subject still performs that pretence, which is why a glossary article must spend its centre on two printed pages.

There is a further subtlety that honest treatment requires. Changing one's mind is not a crime in a theorist, and the mere fact of revision does not refute the revised position. A doctrine is not false because its author once denied it. What the revision destroys is a specific kind of argument: the argument that the doctrine is simply Leninism faithfully transmitted, certified by the codifier's office. If the transmission altered the message within a year, then the doctrine must be judged as a new position answering a new situation, not as an inheritance. Judged that way it may still be defensible. But the defence must be made in the open, on grounds of circumstance and consequence, and it forfeits the borrowed authority of the founder. The two editions thus discipline both sides: they deny the doctrine its textual pedigree, and they deny its critics the lazy inference that a reversed author is a refuted one.

## The textual question table

The whole dispute can be laid out on one page, and laying it out is the fastest way to see where the weight falls. The textual question table sets four items in a row: the first edition of the codification, the revised edition, the passage of Lenin cited in its support, and the doctrine as it was finally stated. For each item the table records what the text says, what it means by victory, and what follows for the argument. Read down the third column and the pattern declares itself. Two of the four items use victory to mean a revolution's taking and keeping of power; the doctrine needs the word to mean the completion of construction; and the revised edition moves between the two meanings without marking the transition. The table is the article in miniature, and a reader pressed for time could stop after studying it and still possess the essential finding, though the argument from circumstance, which the table cannot hold, would be missing.

Tables discipline arguments because they forbid the drift that prose permits. In prose, a writer can quote the Lenin passage, allow its prestige to settle over the page, and move on as though the doctrine had been endorsed by it. In the table, the passage must declare what its key word denotes, and once declared, the gap between the warrant and the claim is visible to any reader. That is the service the artifact performs for students in particular: it converts a quarrel of authorities into an inspection of meanings. The same discipline applied to the two editions shows why the reversal matters independently of the Lenin question. Even if the passage had said what the doctrine needed, the codifier's own contradiction would remain, and the work that transmitted the doctrine to millions of readers would still have taught both positions within a single year.

### In what sequence does the table lay out its four texts?

It begins with the codification's first statement, moves to the revision that replaced it, turns to the founder's passage cited as authority, and ends with the doctrine itself. Against each entry it sets what the text says, what its central word denotes, and what follows, so that substitutions a prose narrative can hide become visible at a glance.

One more row could be imagined for the table, and its absence is instructive. The opposition's texts, Trotsky's writings on permanent revolution above all, are not rows in it, because the table's subject is the doctrine's own warrant, not the rival case. The rival case enters this article later, in the objection, where it belongs. Keeping the artifact narrow is what makes it sharp: it answers one question, whether the doctrine's textual foundation supports the doctrine, and it answers that question negatively on evidence that no party has succeeded in disputing. Wider questions, whether the doctrine was wise, whether its consequences were tolerable, whether its rival would have governed better, are real questions, but they are not textual ones, and the precision this series exists to supply consists largely in refusing to let one kind of question impersonate another.

## The Lenin passage: what victory denotes

The warrant offered for the doctrine, once the codification's own authority is set aside, is a passage Lenin wrote in August 1915, in an article attacking the slogan of a European federation. There Lenin argued that uneven economic and political development is an absolute law of capitalism, and drew the conclusion that the victory of socialism is possible first in several or even in one capitalist country alone. The sentence is genuine, its date is certain, and its author is beyond dispute. Everything in the controversy turns on what the sentence is about. Read in its setting, the passage addresses the order in which revolutions might occur across countries: against a slogan implying that socialist transformation must arrive everywhere simultaneously or nowhere, Lenin insisted it could arrive in one place first. The victory named there is the victory of a revolution in a country, the seizure of power by its working class and the establishment of its rule. The passage says nothing about completing the construction of a socialist society inside that country's borders unaided, because that question, in that form, was not the question under discussion.

### What did the disputed passage actually discuss?

It discussed the sequence of revolutions under the law of uneven development: whether socialism could triumph in one country before others, against a slogan that implied simultaneity. Its subject was the outbreak and victory of revolution, the taking of power, and not the subsequent completion of socialist construction within the victorious country, which the passage neither raises nor answers.

The distinction is not a pedantic rescue invented by the doctrine's opponents after the fact. Lenin returned to the theme in 1916, writing on the military programme of the proletarian revolution, and there the same structure appears: victory in one country means a revolution that has taken power and must then defend itself, including by war, while awaiting the spread of the upheaval. In the years after October he spoke repeatedly of the final victory of socialism as requiring the combined efforts of several advanced countries, and of the Russian revolution's survival as depending, in the long run, on revolutions in the West. Defenders of the doctrine could and did assemble countervailing sentences, remarks about building the foundations of a socialist economy, about the possibility of a long period of construction during a breathing space. The honest summary is that Lenin's corpus contains materials for both sides of the later quarrel, because he died before the quarrel's question was posed in its sharp form. What the corpus does not contain is a statement that the 1915 passage, or any passage, settles the question of completion. The passage settles the question of beginnings, which was never in dispute.

### Why does the meaning of one word decide the dispute?

Because the textual case is an argument from a quotation, and such an argument inherits the quotation's meaning. If victory in the passage denotes taking power, the passage proves a proposition every disputant accepted and leaves the distinctive claim, completion, unsupported. The controversy therefore reduces to reference: what the word points to in its sentence.

There is a general lesson here that the series has met before, in other doctrines and other founders. Movements that govern in a founder's name develop a citation practice in which the founder's sentences function as warrants transferable between questions. A sentence uttered against one opponent, inside one argument, is lifted and set down inside another, where its words fit and its meaning does not. The practice is not unique to communism, and it is rarely conscious fraud; it is the natural behaviour of a tradition that treats its canon as a single timeless utterance rather than a series of dated interventions. The antidote is the habit this article is built to teach: before asking whether a founder said the words, ask what the words were doing where he said them. Applied to the 1915 passage, the habit dissolves the doctrine's textual case in a paragraph. Applied generally, it is the difference between scholarship and liturgy.

### Did anyone dispute that power could be seized in one country?

No participant in the controversy denied it, since the October Revolution itself was the standing proof, and the opposition's theory presupposed a seizure of power in a backward country as its starting point. The disputed territory began after the seizure: whether the regime that resulted could finish building a new society alone, or whether its completion awaited revolutions elsewhere.

It is worth pausing on the strangeness of a controversy in which the famous quotation supports the proposition nobody contested. That strangeness explains the heat of the polemics. Each side experienced the other as dishonest: the doctrine's defenders heard opponents deny what Lenin had plainly written, and the opponents heard defenders pretend that a passage about seizing power was a passage about completing construction. Both experiences were sincere, because both sides were reading the same sentence with different questions in mind. A glossary article cannot reconcile the parties, but it can perform the separation they failed to perform, assigning each proposition to its proper evidence. Beginnings: proven by October and stated by Lenin in 1915. Completion: asserted by the doctrine from 1924, denied by the codification's first edition, and supported by no founder's text that addresses it directly. With the separation made, the textual question is closed, and the real argument, the argument from circumstance, can begin on its own merits.

## Reading the revision: how the two texts are handled

The scholarly handling of the two editions forms a small literature of its own, and its main lines are worth recording because they show how a documented fact gets absorbed, deflected or ignored according to the needs of the writer. Historians of the Soviet Union across the political spectrum, from Isaac Deutscher in his biography of Stalin to E. H. Carr in his history of the interregnum, register the change of text and date it. Biographers sympathetic to the doctrine's author have generally minimised the episode, treating the revision as the correction of an early error once the question had been clarified in discussion. Trotsky's own writings made the reversal a centrepiece of the opposition's case, returning to it as proof of an improvisation dressed as orthodoxy. Later textbook treatments inside the Soviet Union simply used the revised text, and generations of readers there never learned that another version had existed. The pattern generalises: the fact is available to anyone who looks, and most accounts that omit it omit it because it complicates a story they are telling in one direction or the other.

### Why do factional accounts omit the revision?

Because the episode embarrasses both simplifications. For the doctrine's heirs it shows the founding text contradicting the doctrine within months of its birth. For a purely factional reading of the opposition it shrinks a world historical argument to a bibliographical point. Committed writers pass over it, and their readers inherit a controversy missing its most checkable fact.

A word on method is appropriate in a series that asks readers to verify. The two editions can be compared by anyone with access to a research library holding the early printings, and the relevant passages are reproduced in the scholarly literature with edition and page references. The first edition in English is the harder text to obtain, since the standard English translation of Foundations of Leninism follows the later, revised text; readers of English usually meet the original passage through quotation in historical works. That asymmetry has itself shaped the Anglophone debate, in which the doctrine's version of its own history circulated in the author's canonical text while the evidence against it travelled in footnotes. Stating where a text can be consulted is part of stating a claim responsibly, and this article does so in its note on sources rather than leaving the reader to trust the summary given here.

The revision also illuminates something about codifications as a genre. A codification presents a living argument as a settled system, and its usefulness to a movement depends on the appearance of settlement. When the system must change, the genre therefore prefers silent substitution to open correction, since an acknowledged correction admits that the settlement was provisional. The two editions are a nearly perfect specimen: the change is total at the decisive passage, the manner of the change is silence, and the authority claimed for the book is undiminished. Recognising the genre explains the episode without excusing it, and it arms the reader against other codifications, in other traditions, that perform the same operation. Wherever a text claims merely to transmit, the reader should ask which edition is being transmitted, and what the earlier ones said.

## The argument from circumstance, at full strength

Set the texts aside, as the two editions oblige us to do, and the doctrine's real case comes into view. It runs as follows, in the terms its proponents used. The revolution had been made. The European working class, through defeat, exhaustion and the choices of its own leaderships, had not followed. The Soviet state existed, held power, and faced a population that needed bread, goods, schools and security rather than a theory of waiting. A government in that position has three conceivable orientations. It can treat itself as a garrison, holding a position until relief arrives, organising its life around an event it does not control. It can despair of its own project and manage a retreat toward the old order by instalments. Or it can declare that the work in front of it is the work itself: that building a socialist society in the country it actually governs is a possible and honourable task, demanding the same devotion that the seizure of power demanded. The doctrine is the third orientation given theoretical form. Its proponents argued that the first orientation corrodes a ruling party from inside, since a garrison mentality turns every domestic question into a postponement and every compromise into a betrayal, and that the second was simply capitulation under a technical name.

### What is the argument from circumstance in its strongest form?

It holds that a revolutionary government, abandoned by the revolutions it expected and ringed by hostile states, must find a positive task within its own borders or decay in suspension. Building at home supplied that task, rallied the population to construction rather than endurance, and rested on real assets: territory, population, and a state already held.

The argument gains strength from its opponents' difficulty, which was genuine and which honest critics acknowledged. The theory of permanent revolution explained, with great power, why the Russian revolution would stall or degenerate if the European revolution failed to arrive. What it offered the Soviet population of 1925 was a diagnosis rather than a task. Its practical corollaries, an aggressive international orientation and a refusal to consolidate, had to be weighed against a countryside that had already endured requisitioning, famine and civil war, and against an encirclement that was not a metaphor. Bukharin, who gave the doctrine its most intellectually serious defence in the middle of the decade, framed the choice in terms of tempo and alliance: the working class of a peasant country could build, slowly, in alliance with the peasantry, using the market as scaffolding, and the alternative tempos on offer would shatter the alliance and with it the state. Whatever one thinks of that programme, and its author was later destroyed by the regime he helped to theorise, it was an answer to a real question, posed by real scarcities, and it cannot be waved away as a bureaucratic excuse without falsifying the decade.

### What would the alternative have required in practice?

It would have required the government to organise its economy and legitimacy around an expectation it could not schedule: revolution in the advanced countries. Investment, schooling and sacrifice would all have been justified as preparation for an external event. The doctrine's proponents asked how long a population can be asked to live in a present defined by a future others control.

Circumstance also included a fact about the international movement that the doctrine's critics preferred to state abstractly. By the middle of the decade the Communist International was no longer a gathering of mass revolutionary parties on the verge of power; it was an organisation whose strongest sections had been defeated and whose centre of material gravity was the one state where communists governed. To say that this state should subordinate its construction to the movement's prospects was to say, in concrete terms, that the only existing workers' state should treat itself as an auxiliary to parties that had repeatedly failed to take power. The doctrine reversed the polarity, and the reversal felt, to many communists of the period, like simple honesty about where the movement's real assets lay. That feeling is data for the historian even where the doctrine is rejected, because doctrines rule through the plausibility they offer to those who must act under them, and this one's plausibility was earned in the defeats of 1919 to 1923 as much as it was manufactured in the polemics of 1924.

None of this rescues the textual case, and the article's discipline requires the two judgements to be kept apart. A doctrine may be circumstantially intelligible, even circumstantially necessary in some form, while being textually fraudulent in the form its author gave it. The steelman has a limit as well: the argument from circumstance justifies an orientation toward domestic construction, but it does not by itself justify converting that orientation into a dogma binding on every communist party, nor does it answer for the uses to which the dogma was put. Those uses are the subject of the objection, and the objection must now be given the same full strength that the defence has received.

## The opposition: who argued, and on what grounds

The doctrine was contested from the moment of its statement, and the contest had a structure that later summaries flatten. Trotsky's opposition rested on his theory of permanent revolution, and its relevant component held that the contradictions of socialist construction in a backward country, above all the contradiction between a workers' state and a peasant economy inside a capitalist world market, could not be resolved within national limits. The argument was economic before it was political: a single country building alone would face the world market's prices, technology and military competition with inferior resources, and would be forced either into concessions that restored capitalism by degrees or into a coercion that consumed the revolution's own social base. Zinoviev and Kamenev, who joined Trotsky in the United Opposition of 1926, came to the quarrel from a different direction, emphasising the danger of national narrowness and the party's degeneration, and their alliance with their former antagonist showed how far the doctrine had reorganised the party's internal map. The biographical and factional narrative is treated in the series article on [stalin and marxism leninism](/2019/03/15/stalin-and-marxism-leninism/); here the grounds matter more than the camps.

### Who led the opposition inside the party?

Trotsky led it, and his theory of permanent revolution supplied its theoretical core; from 1926 the United Opposition joined him with Zinoviev and Kamenev. Its grounds were that construction in isolation would collide with the peasant economy at home and the world market abroad, and that subordinating the world movement to a single state would corrupt movement and state alike.

The opposition also made the textual argument, and made it early. The codification's first edition was quoted against the doctrine in the polemics of the period, since it stated, in the author's own words, the position the doctrine now denounced as heresy. This gave the controversy its peculiar bitterness: the heresy being prosecuted had been the orthodoxy of the previous spring, printed in the movement's standard textbook. Participants noticed. The charge that the doctrine was an improvisation, assembled for a factional struggle and retrofitted with quotations, drew much of its credibility from the documentary record of the revision, and the leadership's refusal to acknowledge the change in print confirmed, for many waverers, that something other than open argument was taking place. A controversy conducted by a party that controlled the presses could be settled by organisational means, and it was: the opposition was expelled, exiled and eventually annihilated. The settlement by organisational means is itself part of the doctrine's history, because a proposition that wins by expulsion carries a different epistemic status from one that wins by persuasion, whatever its merits.

### What did the United Opposition argue about the peasantry?

It argued that building in isolation would force the state into an ever sharper collision with the peasantry, since an industry starved of foreign support would be financed from the village, and the resulting coercion would break the worker and peasant alliance on which the regime rested. The procurement crises and forced collectivisation that followed gave the prediction grim retrospective weight.

Beyond the party's borders, the doctrine was opposed by the surviving currents of European social democracy, by anarchist critics who saw in it the completion of the revolution's nationalisation, and by communists outside the Soviet Union who broke with the International over the subordination it implied. Those external oppositions matter to the article's later sections, because the objection that carries the greatest weight does not come from inside the factional struggle at all. It comes from observing the doctrine's effects on the movement that adopted it, across two decades and every continent where communist parties operated. Before reaching it, one clarification is owed to the record. The opposition's alternative was never tested in power, and its spokesmen bore responsibility for much of the regime's early coercive practice; the controversy is between flawed bearers of rival propositions, and a glossary article serves its readers by assessing the propositions, not by canonising their bearers.

## The strongest objection, stated at full strength

The objection that must be answered is not the textual one, damaging as that is. A doctrine can survive a weak pedigree. The objection at full strength, as its proponents framed it across the polemics of the period and the historiography that followed, runs as follows. The doctrine converted an emergency accommodation into a theory, and having done so made every international communist party an instrument of one state's security requirements, a cost so large that no argument from circumstance can carry it. Each clause of that sentence is load bearing. The conversion matters: an accommodation is provisional, judged by results, and abandoned when the emergency passes, whereas a theory claims truth, demands assent, and reproduces itself in every party that adopts the doctrine as its own. What began as a description of what the Soviet government would do while isolated became, by codification, a proposition about what socialism is, binding on communists in countries whose circumstances bore no resemblance to Russia's. The instrument clause then follows. If completing socialism within a single country is the movement's central task, then the defence of that country is every party's overriding duty, and the interests of foreign parties, their timing, their alliances, their very survival, are properly subordinated to the security requirements of the state where construction is under way. The objection holds that this is not a slippery slope but the doctrine's plain logic, stated by its proponents at the time without embarrassment.

### How did the doctrine change the International's reference point?

It made one state's interests the standard against which foreign parties' lines were set. An organisation founded to spread revolution came to exist, in the objection's account, to protect the homeland of the one successful revolution, and its sections were judged by their usefulness to that protection rather than by their progress toward power at home.

The historical illustrations the objection marshals are specific, and they are given here as its proponents give them. In China in 1927, the Communist Party was held inside an alliance with the nationalist government long after that government's intentions were plain, in deference to a Soviet diplomacy that valued the alliance, and the party was destroyed in the ensuing massacres. In Germany in the early nineteen thirties, the doctrine's domestic corollary, the theory that social democracy was the main enemy, divided the working class before the Nazi threat, while Soviet state interest pursued its own arrangements with German governments. In Spain, in France, in the popular front period, communist parties calibrated their ambitions to the alliance requirements of Soviet foreign policy, restraining revolutions that might frighten prospective partners. After the Second World War, parties in Greece, in France, in Italy and in China itself were at various moments counselled, pressured or abandoned in accordance with agreements between states. The objection does not need every illustration to stand; it needs the pattern, and the pattern's explanation, which it locates in the doctrine. A movement whose theory assigns world historical priority to one state's construction will reliably sacrifice peripheral opportunities to that state's security, and will experience the sacrifice not as betrayal but as duty.

The objection also has a domestic clause that its proponents pressed with equal force. A theory that makes one country's construction the axis of world politics supplies the ruling stratum of that country with an ideology of its own permanence. The bureaucracy governing the isolated state could present its privileges as the wages of the class that was building socialism, and could present every critic, at home or abroad, as an objective ally of the encircling enemy. On this account the doctrine did not merely permit the regime's degeneration; it furnished the concepts in which degeneration was described as progress. Trotsky's later writings developed this clause most fully, arguing that the doctrine was the ideology of a conservative stratum that had exchanged the international revolution for a national settlement, and that its triumph marked the point at which the revolution's own party became the administrator of its retreat. Even readers who reject that sociology must concede the objection's structural observation: a doctrine is also an instrument of rule for whoever rules in its name, and this one's domestic convenience to its rulers was not incidental to its career.

Finally, the objection addresses the argument from circumstance directly, and its answer is the clause with which it closes. Circumstances explain accommodations; they do not entitle anyone to found a theory on an emergency and impose the theory on a world movement. The isolated state might legitimately build what it could, trade where it must, and defend itself as states do. What it could not legitimately do, on the objection's terms, was to declare its necessity a universal truth, rewrite the record to make the truth look inherited, and discipline every foreign party that declined to subordinate itself. The cost, the subordination of an international movement to one state's security, was foreseeable and was in fact foreseen, in print, by the doctrine's opponents while the conversion was taking place. A cost foreseen and imposed anyway, the objection concludes, is not a tragedy of circumstance but a choice, and it must be judged as one.

## The answer, and the concession it must carry

The answer begins by returning the objection to the situation it abstracts from, and asking the question its proponents must be made to face: what was the alternative course, concretely, for a state that was isolated and invaded? The invasions were not rhetorical. Foreign armies had operated on Russian territory within living memory of every participant; the blockade had starved cities; the encirclement by hostile states was the organising fact of Soviet security planning because it was a fact. A government that declined to build, that treated its own economy as a provisional camp pending rescue, would not thereby have preserved the international movement's purity. It would have presided over stagnation, and stagnation in a besieged state is not neutrality but decline, ending either in collapse or in the restoration the doctrine's critics also claimed to fear. The parties abroad, moreover, were not sacrificed from a position of strength. The German party had failed in 1923 with every advantage of crisis; the Chinese catastrophe of 1927 had roots in the strategy of the International before the doctrine's full codification, in decisions taken when the old internationalism still formally reigned. Subordination, the answer insists, was the International's growing practice under the pressure of defeats, and the doctrine ratified a reality more than it created one. To lay the movement's whole subsequent history at the doctrine's door is to credit a formula with powers that belonged to the distribution of defeats.

### Can a doctrine be textually weak and circumstantially right?

Yes, and the possibility is why this article keeps the questions apart. Pedigree and truth are different properties: the two editions destroy the claim to faithful transmission, while the isolation of the Soviet state remains a fact any rival position also had to answer. Letting a verdict on either question impersonate a verdict on the other ruins assessment.

The answer also presses the objection on its counterfactual. The international movement the doctrine allegedly ruined was, by the later nineteen twenties, a movement whose independent road to power had closed in every advanced country. The objection speaks of parties made into instruments, but instruments of a state that existed, in place of a strategy that had failed everywhere it was tried. There is a real reply available to the objection here, and it is not cynical: between a movement oriented to a real construction, however deformed, and a movement oriented to an imminent world revolution that was not coming, the choice was not between purity and corruption but between two wagers, one of which at least had an object. The doctrine's defenders could add that the Soviet state's survival, which the doctrine organised, was later the material precondition of the defeat of Nazism, a contribution to the century's history that no ledger of the International's failures can simply omit. Weighing that contribution is beyond this article's scope, but the objection's accounting is incomplete without it, and fairness requires the entry to be made.

Then the concession, which the answer must carry without varnish, because the evidence for it is documentary. The international consequence is real, and it was foreseen by the doctrine's opponents. Communist parties were, repeatedly and as a matter of acknowledged principle, subordinated to the requirements of Soviet state security; the doctrine supplied the theoretical form in which that subordination was understood by its practitioners as loyalty rather than surrender; and the critics who predicted exactly this development, in the years when the doctrine was being codified, were right about the mechanism whatever else they were wrong about. A defence of the doctrine that denies the consequence is not a defence but a refusal to read the record of the International between the wars. The most that the answer establishes is that the consequence had causes deeper than the formula, in defeat and encirclement, and that the alternative courses carried costs of their own that the objection declines to price. That is a mitigation, not a rebuttal, and this article records it as such. The doctrine stands, at the end of the exchange, as a position that was textually unfounded, circumstantially intelligible, and internationally expensive, with the three judgements held apart because collapsing them is how the controversy became useless.

## Consequences for the international movement

Whatever the balance of the argument, the consequences followed a legible sequence, and a glossary article should trace it as institutional history rather than as polemic. The Communist International, founded in 1919 as the general staff of an expected world revolution, was progressively reorganised around defending the Soviet Union, as its member parties absorbed the doctrine. The turn is visible in the International's own congresses and resolutions: the language of imminent revolution gives way, across the later nineteen twenties, to the language of defending the homeland of socialism, and the criterion of a good party shifts from its revolutionary prospects to its reliability in that defence. The popular front strategy of the middle nineteen thirties, in which communist parties sought alliances with liberal and social democratic forces against fascism, was the doctrine's diplomatic expression: the Soviet state needed allies among the Western democracies, and the International's parties became advocates of the alliances their state required. The later doctrine of peaceful coexistence between states of different social systems extended the same logic into the postwar decades, presenting the avoidance of war between the blocs as the framework within which socialism would prevail by example and economic competition rather than by export of revolution.

### What was the popular front's relation to the doctrine?

The popular front translated the doctrine into strategy for parties abroad. If defending the state where the building was under way outranked every local ambition, then alliances serving that state's diplomacy followed as a matter of course, including alliances that required communist parties to restrain their own revolutionary aims. Critics called the strategy the doctrine applied; defenders called it tactics.

The dissolution of the International in 1943, carried out as a gesture to the Soviet Union's wartime allies, can be read as the sequence's logical terminus: an institution created to make revolutions was wound up because its existence inconvenienced the diplomacy of the one state where a revolution had succeeded. Defenders of the doctrine reply, correctly, that the International by then directed little and that its dissolution acknowledged a reality of national parties. The reading matters less than the fact that the gesture was available to be made, and was made without convulsion inside the movement, which shows how thoroughly the reordering of priorities had been absorbed. The factional afterlife of the opposition, organised from 1938 in a Fourth International founded on the rejection of the doctrine, is followed in the [fourth international legacy](/2014/12/01/fourth-international-legacy/) article, and the comparison of the two camps' doctrines is carried by [trotskyism vs stalinism](/2014/11/15/trotskyism-vs-stalinism/), which links back here for the definition.

A further consequence, quieter than the diplomatic ones, deserves record because it shaped how Marxism was studied for half a century. The doctrine turned the Soviet Union into the movement's reference society, the place where the theory's claims could be inspected in built form. Parties and intellectuals oriented their arguments around the Soviet example, defending or attacking a single case as though the theory stood or fell with it. That orientation survived the doctrine itself: long after the formula ceased to be repeated, habits of argument formed under it, in which one state's record served as the universal exhibit, persisted on all sides. The series' own discipline, separating a doctrine from the record of the states that claimed it, is a deliberate refusal of that habit, and the present article's separation of the textual, circumstantial and consequential questions is the refusal applied to a single case.

## Foreign policy: the state as reference point

A doctrine about where socialism can be completed becomes, in the hands of the state that adopts it, a doctrine about what foreign policy is for. The Soviet state's diplomacy had always mixed the interests of the state with the interests of the movement, since its first separate peace at Brest-Litovsk, defended at the time as a sacrifice of revolutionary principle to the survival of the one revolutionary state. The one country doctrine gave that mixture a settled hierarchy. The state's security came first in theory as it increasingly did in practice, and the movement's parties were expected to understand their own activity within the frame the state's diplomacy set. Trade agreements, non-aggression pacts, entry into the League of Nations, the search for collective security against Germany, and after the war the management of a sphere in Eastern Europe: each of these can be narrated as ordinary statecraft, and each was also defended inside the movement by reference to the needs of the country where socialism was being built. The formula did not invent Soviet statecraft, but it removed the theoretical embarrassment that had attended it, the suspicion that a revolutionary state pursuing its interests was betraying its mission. Under the doctrine, the mission and the interest had been defined into alignment.

### Did the doctrine invent the subordination of party to state?

No, and precision requires the qualification. The International's sections had been guided from Moscow since its founding, and defeat pushed toward subordination before the formula was codified. What the doctrine added was legitimacy in theoretical form: a reason, stated as doctrine, why one state's requirements should set the movement's priorities, where previously the practice had worn the dress of temporary discipline.

Peaceful coexistence, the foreign policy doctrine most associated with the postwar Soviet Union, is best understood in this line of descent. As a slogan it predates the controversy, reaching back to the breathing space diplomacy of the early nineteen twenties. As a doctrine, elaborated after Stalin's death and canonised at the twentieth party congress, it held that war between the systems was no longer inevitable and that competition would proceed by economic and ideological means. Its genealogy runs through socialism in one country: both propositions assign the existing socialist state a long historical life within a divided world, and both subordinate the timetable of revolution elsewhere to the preservation and flourishing of that state. Readers who meet peaceful coexistence as a novelty of the nineteen fifties miss the continuity, and with it the reason the formula was so easily absorbed by parties formed under the earlier doctrine. The tradition as a whole, from the codification to the postwar settlement, is surveyed in the series article on the [soviet marxism complete guide](/2019/03/01/soviet-marxism-complete-guide/), which this article supplies with its definition of the founding formula.

## The textual case assessed

The assessment can now be stated in the article's own terms, with the three questions kept apart as they have been throughout. On the textual question, the verdict is not close. The doctrine's founding work asserted the doctrine's negation in its first edition and the doctrine in its second, within a single year, without acknowledgement. The founder's passage offered as warrant denotes the seizure of power, a proposition no disputant denied, and is silent on the completion the doctrine asserts. A case built from an authority that contradicts itself and a quotation that refers elsewhere is not a weak case that might be strengthened by further research; it is a case that fails on the evidence its own side produced. The two editions name this failure, and the failure is permanent, because the documents are fixed and no future scholarship can unpublish the first edition or rewrite the 1915 article's context.

### Where does the honest defence part company with the textual one?

The honest defence argues from the situation of an isolated state and can be conducted in the open, since the situation is documented and the choices it imposed were real. The textual defence argues from authorities and collapses on inspection, since the codification contradicts itself and the founder's passage denotes another proposition. Competent defenders choose the first ground; the second survives mainly in popular summary.

On the circumstantial question, the verdict is genuinely divided, and this article's neutrality consists in refusing to force it. The argument from circumstance is a serious argument, grounded in defeats and scarcities that are not in dispute, and the alternative it rejected, governing as a garrison awaiting rescue, carried costs its critics rarely priced. On the consequential question, the record is heavier on one side: the subordination of the world movement to a single state's security is documented across decades, its opponents had predicted it in print at the time, and the doctrine supplied its theoretical legitimation. Whether that cost was the doctrine's fault or the era's is the question on which reasonable assessments divide, and the division maps, not accidentally, onto the oldest division in the historiography of the Soviet Union itself. A glossary article ends by marking the division clearly, not by pretending to have dissolved it.

There is a reason this article has been built as a glossary and precision piece rather than as a narrative of the factional struggle, and the reason is itself a finding. Narratives of the struggle are plentiful, and they reliably reproduce the loyalties of their narrators. The textual question, by contrast, is one of the few points in the entire controversy where the evidence is documentary, the documents are short, and the conclusion does not depend on trusting any participant's memory or motives. Starting there gives the reader a fixed point from which the larger, murkier questions can be approached with some independence. The doctrine's author reversed himself in print within a year: that much is bedrock. The founder's sentence denotes a seizure of power: that much is grammar and context. Everything else, the wisdom of building, the price of subordination, the counterfactuals of the opposition, is argument, and argument conducted downstream of two fixed points is at least argument that knows where its ground is solid and where it is marsh.

## Misreadings to correct

Certain errors recur in summaries of the doctrine often enough to deserve a catalogue. The first is that Lenin formulated the doctrine. He did not: the doctrine in its distinctive form, completion within one country as a positive thesis against permanent revolution, dates from 1924, after his death, and the passages of his cited in its support address other questions, as the textual analysis above shows. The second error is that the 1915 passage settles the matter. It settles the possibility of a revolution beginning and winning power in one country, which the whole movement accepted, and it is silent on completion. The third error is that the dispute was purely factional, a screen for a succession struggle. The succession struggle was real, and the doctrine served a faction; but the propositions in dispute were genuine propositions, argued with evidence, whose truth does not vary with the motives of those who argued them. Treating every theoretical controversy inside a ruling party as a mere mask for ambition is itself a failure of analysis, since it cannot explain why the participants bothered to argue, in print, at such length and with such documentary care.

### What should a student separate between an accommodation and a theory?

An accommodation is provisional, adopted under pressure and judged by whether it works. A theory claims truth, is taught as doctrine and is applied far beyond the circumstances that produced it. The controversy turns on the conversion of the first into the second, so the student's opening task is to notice which of the two a given statement is performing.

The fourth error is that the doctrine claimed socialism could be completed in a vacuum, indifferent to the rest of the world. Its careful formulations distinguished building socialism in one country, held to be possible, from final victory, the guarantee against restoration by intervention, held to require revolutions in several countries. One may find the distinction unstable, as its critics did, but an account that has not noticed it exists has not read the doctrine it purports to summarise. The fifth error is that the argument from circumstance was a rationalisation invented after the doctrine, a excuse stitched onto a decision taken for factional reasons. The circumstance preceded the doctrine: the defeats in Europe, the isolation, the invasions and the blockade are the documented setting of the early nineteen twenties, and the argument from them was available to, and partly made by, figures who later opposed the doctrine itself. Rationalisation is a claim about sequence, and the sequence here refutes it. The sixth error, mirror image of the fifth, is that because the circumstance was real the doctrine was innocent. Reality of circumstance does not launder consequences, and the international cost stands in the record regardless of how honestly the founding dilemma is stated.

## Sources, translations, and where the texts can be consulted

The primary texts for the textual question are short and accessible, which is part of the question's value as a teaching case. The revised text of Foundations of Leninism is widely available in English in the standard editions of Stalin's works and in many reprints, and the polemical article of December 1924 circulates with it. The first edition's superseded passage is the harder item for an English reader: the standard translation follows the revised text, so the original wording is usually consulted through the scholarly literature, where it is quoted with bibliographical references by historians of the period, and through research libraries holding the early Russian printings or the studies that reproduce them. Lenin's article on the European federation slogan is available in his collected works in any research library and in digital collections of his writings, and it should be read whole, since its argument about the slogan is the context that fixes the famous sentence's reference. Trotsky's critique, developed across his writings on the revolution and the International, is likewise available in English, as are the congress documents of the International for the years of the turn.

### Where can a reader consult the first edition's text?

In research libraries holding the early printings, and more conveniently in the scholarly literature, where historians quote the superseded passage with edition references. The standard English translation of the codifying work follows the revised text, so an English reader meets the original wording at second hand and should know that this asymmetry has shaped the debate in English.

The scholarly literature divides, predictably, by sympathy, and the reader is better served by knowing the division than by being protected from it. Deutscher's political biography of Stalin treats the revision as a symptom of a doctrine being improvised under pressure. Carr's volumes on the interregnum document the sequence with a bibliographer's patience. Robert Service's biography is severe on the doctrine's intellectual quality while conceding the circumstance. Stephen Kotkin's work situates the formula inside the regime's broader project of building a civilisation in one country, a framing that takes the doctrine seriously as a governing idea rather than a slogan. Trotsky's own writings remain the indispensable statement of the opposition's case, to be read as a participant's brief, powerful and interested in equal measure. For the International's side of the story, the documentary histories of the Comintern trace the subordination in institutional detail. A reader who samples across this shelf will notice that the two editions appear in nearly every serious treatment, whatever its sympathies, which is the surest sign that the fact belongs to the record and not to a faction.

## The doctrine's afterlife in later formulas

Doctrines outlive their controversies by becoming templates, and the one country form proved remarkably portable. Its structure, a socialist state declaring that construction within its borders is a complete task rather than a provisional one, reappears wherever a revolutionary government settles into a long national existence. The Chinese parallel is the most consequential: the doctrine of socialism with Chinese characteristics, examined in the series article on [socialism chinese characteristics](/2018/08/01/socialism-chinese-characteristics/), likewise asserts that a socialist society can be built within one country under its specific conditions, across a long primary stage, without the completion of the task depending on revolutions elsewhere. The parallel is structural rather than genealogical, since the Chinese doctrine arose from a different party's experience, but the family resemblance is exact, and it explains why the textual and circumstantial questions of 1924 keep being re-argued in new vocabularies. Yugoslav self-management, the various national roads proclaimed in Eastern Europe after the war, and the Eurocommunist parties' insistence on roads fitted to their own countries all drew, in different measures, on the permission the original doctrine had issued: the permission to treat a national construction as a subject in its own right, not as a falling away from an international script.

### How did later doctrines inherit the one country form?

By repeating its founding move: converting the circumstance of a single revolutionary state, obliged to build within its borders for an indefinite period, into a doctrine that such building is complete in itself. Each later version met the original's questions in a new idiom, about warrant, about dependence on events abroad, and about the price paid by movements outside the favoured state.

The afterlife also includes the doctrine's quiet abandonment by its own heirs. The Soviet leadership after Stalin retained the substance, a national construction treated as the movement's centre, while allowing the formula itself to fade from canonical repetition, and the later language of a developed socialist society described the built result rather than the contested claim. By the time the Soviet Union dissolved, the proposition that socialism had been built in one country had been official truth for two generations, taught in every school of the bloc, and its collapse posed the question the doctrine's first critics had posed in reverse: if the construction was complete, what exactly had been completed, and why could it be dismantled by decree and referendum within a few years? That question belongs to the historiography of the Soviet collapse rather than to a glossary article, but a reader of the doctrine should know that its career ends there, in the irony of a completion that proved reversible. The glossary's task is narrower and, in its way, more durable: to fix what the words claimed, what evidence was offered, and what the evidence shows, so that later arguments start from the record rather than from the slogans the record left behind.

## The polemic of the winter: how the doctrine was launched

The doctrine did not enter the world as a paragraph in a textbook revision. It entered as a weapon in a specific fight, and the fight's chronology explains the revision's timing better than any theory of the author's intellectual development. In the autumn of 1924 Trotsky published a preface to a volume of his writings on the year 1917, an essay on the lessons of October that drew pointed parallels between the waverings of party leaders during the revolution and the conduct of his rivals in the succession struggle then under way. The essay was read, correctly, as an attack on Zinoviev and Kamenev and by extension on the triumvirate that governed the party. The response was a campaign, conducted through the party press, in which the charge of Trotskyism was assembled as a heresy with a history, and in which the theory of permanent revolution was exhumed and arraigned as the heresy's core. Stalin's contribution to the campaign, the December article on the October Revolution and the tactics of the Russian communists, performed the decisive operation: it separated Leninism from Trotskyism by means of the one country question, asserting that the Russian party had always believed socialism could be built in Russia, and that permanent revolution's denial of this was the root deviation from which the others grew. The revised edition of the codification, appearing in the same season, aligned the textbook with the campaign. Read against this chronology, the two editions stop looking like a scholar's second thoughts. They look like a record being brought into conformity with a polemic, and that is how the opposition read them at the time, and how most historians have read them since.

### Why was permanent revolution made the doctrine's target?

Because the campaign against Trotsky required a theoretical charge graver than ambition, and permanent revolution was the position genuinely attached to his name. Defining Leninism as belief in building at home made the theory's insistence that construction could not be completed nationally into the defining heresy, and made loyalty to the construction the test of loyalty to the founder.

The polemic's method deserves attention in a precision article, because it established a template that Soviet theoretical controversy followed for decades. The method was retrospective systematisation: a position held in a new situation was projected backward as the party's timeless doctrine, an opposing position was projected backward as a timeless heresy, and the documentary record was then cited selectively to support both projections. The two editions were the method's material trace, the point where the projection had to overwrite a printed page. Participants who had read the first edition in the spring could watch the operation happen, and the opposition's polemics are full of the anger of men who had been present at the original. Later controversies inside the movement, over questions from genetics to the national question, reused the template: define the orthodox line, discover that it had always been held, and treat the archive as an embarrassment to be managed rather than a record to be consulted. Socialism in one country was thus not only a proposition but a demonstration, performed in public, of how propositions would henceforth be established in the tradition it governed.

There is also a question of audience. The campaign of the winter of 1924 and 1925 was conducted before a party swollen by the mass recruitment that followed Lenin's death, hundreds of thousands of new members whose knowledge of the old controversies came entirely from the codifications and the press. For that audience, the revised textbook was not a revision, since they had never seen the first edition; it was simply the doctrine, and the doctrine's claim to be the party's inheritance was, for them, uncheckable. Generational turnover is the silent partner of every successful retrospective systematisation, and the Soviet party of the middle decade offered it on an enormous scale. A glossary article written long afterward cannot restore the first audience's experience of watching a text change, but it can insist on the bibliographical fact, which is the form in which that experience survives.

## Bukharin and the doctrine's second voice

The doctrine is remembered as one man's formula, but its most elaborated defence came from Nikolai Bukharin, the party's leading theorist after Lenin, and the difference between the two defences is instructive. Where the codifier argued by assertion and quotation, Bukharin argued from the structure of the Soviet economy: a workers' state in a peasant country could grow toward socialism through the market, using cooperatives to draw the peasantry gradually into socialist relations, at a tempo set by the alliance between the classes rather than by administrative ambition. His notorious advice to the peasantry to enrich itself, offered in 1925 and later retracted under fire, belonged to this construction: a prosperous peasantry inside a framework of state industry and cooperative trade would, on his account, grow into socialism rather than needing to be expropriated into it. The formula was the international face of this domestic gradualism, since a construction expected to take decades at home required, abroad, not imminent revolutions but time, trade and the avoidance of war. Bukharin's fall, when the regime turned to forced collectivisation and breakneck industrialisation, did not refute the doctrine; it refuted his tempo. The formula survived its most serious theorist, which suggests how loosely it was bound to any particular economics, and how completely its function was the legitimation of the national construction as such, at whatever speed the leadership chose.

### What did Bukharin add to the doctrine?

He added an economics and a tempo. The codifier's formula asserted possibility; Bukharin's version described a mechanism, growth through the market and the cooperatives, and a pace, the pace the peasant alliance could bear. His version made the doctrine a programme rather than a slogan, and its defeat showed the formula could survive losing the programme that had made it plausible.

The Bukharin episode also complicates the objection considered earlier, in a way fairness requires. If the doctrine were nothing but the ideology of a conservative bureaucracy, its career would be expected to track the bureaucracy's comfort, yet its gradualist form was destroyed by the regime in a convulsion that consumed millions of lives, while the formula itself was retained and radicalised: socialism in one country, built at forced tempo, by coercion, against the peasantry the alliance had courted. The doctrine proved compatible with both the market road and its violent opposite, which means its content was thinner, and its function clearer, than either of its defences suggested. It named a location and a legitimacy, not a method. Methods could be reversed overnight; the location, one country, and the legitimacy, the national construction as the movement's centre, endured through every reversal. Precision about the doctrine therefore requires holding it at that level: it is a claim about where socialism can be completed and a warrant for the state completing it, and almost everything else associated with its era, tempos, alliances, terror and triumph, was variable scenery around that fixed assertion.

## The doctrine at home: from formula to proclamation

Inside the Soviet Union the doctrine's career passed through three stations, and each station shows the formula doing different work. At the first station, in the middle nineteen twenties, it was a permission: the country would build, the building would take time, and the population was asked to invest its effort in a future located at home rather than in a rescue expected from abroad. At the second station, from the end of the decade, it became a mobilisation order: the first five year plan, forced collectivisation and the liquidation of the kulaks as a class were all conducted under the sign of completing the construction the doctrine had declared possible, and the declaration's optimism was converted into a standard against which hesitation read as sabotage. At the third station, in 1936, it became a proclamation: the new constitution announced that socialism had in the main been built, that the exploiting classes no longer existed, and that the society consisted of friendly classes of workers, peasants and intelligentsia. The doctrine that began as a contested proposition about possibility ended, a dozen years later, as an official description of accomplished fact, and the distance between those two states of the claim is the measure of what codification does to a proposition once a state takes it up.

### When was the doctrine declared fulfilled?

In the middle nineteen thirties, when the constitution of 1936 declared the construction in the main accomplished. The proclamation converted the doctrine from a claim about what could be done into a description of what had been done, and it fixed the official answer to the one country question for the remaining life of the state that issued it.

The domestic career also generated the doctrine's most consequential silence. A theory of building socialism in one country had to say what socialism, once built, would look like, and on this the doctrine was nearly empty: it specified ownership forms and the abolition of exploiting classes, and was vague to the point of absence on political liberty, on the withering of the state that classical theory had predicted, and on the relations between the builders and the apparatus that directed them. The emptiness was functional. A doctrine that defined its goal by property forms could declare the goal achieved while every political question remained exactly as the regime wished it. Critics from the opposition had predicted this too, arguing that a national socialism defined without reference to democracy or to the international division of labour would define itself, in practice, as whatever the national state had built. The proclamation of 1936 is the strongest evidence for their prediction, and it belongs in any honest assessment alongside the argument from circumstance, since the circumstance explains the doctrine's birth but not the use made of its emptiness.

A second domestic consequence followed from the doctrine's national frame. A federation carrying out the building inside a single country had to decide whether the country was the Union or its constituent nations, and the doctrine's silence on the point allowed a steady gravitational pull toward the centre and toward the Russian language and cadres as the construction's default medium. The nationalities policy of the nineteen twenties, which had fostered local languages and elites, gave way across the following decade to centralisation dressed as the friendship of peoples. The doctrine did not cause that turn by itself, but its unit of analysis, the one country, supplied the frame in which the turn appeared as consolidation rather than as a change of regime character. Precision articles exist to notice such frames, because frames decide in advance which events count as the story and which count as noise at its edges.

## The International's congresses: the turn in the record

The subordination the objection describes was not a mood but a sequence of datable institutional acts, and tracing them gives the consequence argument its documentary spine. The Communist International's fifth congress, in 1924, still spoke the language of imminent European revolution, bolshevising the parties for an offensive expected soon. By the sixth congress, in 1928, the programme adopted there defined the movement's period as one of wars and revolutions in the long run but of capitalist stabilisation in the short, and it wrote the Soviet Union's defence into the record as the supreme duty of every section. The same congress blessed the third period line, whose designation of social democracy as social fascism split the German working class at the moment of its greatest danger, a line unintelligible except as the product of an International whose centre feared a social democratic foreign policy orientation in Moscow's neighbours more than it feared the parties of the radical right. The seventh congress, in 1935, reversed the line completely into the popular front, Dimitrov presiding, and the reversal was executed by the same machinery with the same unanimity. An institution that can turn from social fascism to popular front in seven years, at the signal of its centre, has demonstrated the objection's thesis about itself more eloquently than any critic: its parties' lines were functions of the centre's requirements, and the centre's requirements were those of the Soviet state.

### What did the sixth congress codify?

It codified defence of the Soviet Union as the overriding duty of the International's sections, and adopted the programme that framed world politics around the fate of the one state where construction was under way. The congress marks the point where the doctrine's priority ceased to be a tendency and became the constitution of the international movement in documentary form.

China supplies the objection's most harrowing exhibit, and it predates the full codification, which is precisely why the answer to the objection insists that subordination had roots older than the doctrine. The Chinese Communist Party, instructed by the International to enter and remain within the Kuomintang, was exposed when Chiang Kai-shek turned on his allies in Shanghai in April 1927 and massacred the party's urban base. The policy had served a Soviet diplomacy that prized the Chinese nationalist alliance as a blow against British influence in Asia. The doctrine, then in formation, did not author the policy; but the doctrine's triumph in the years after ensured that the lesson drawn was never the one the opposition proposed. The party that had been sacrificed was instructed to blame its own leaders' execution of the line, the line itself was absorbed into the centre's infallibility, and the theoretical justification for the whole arrangement, the priority of the Soviet state's alliances, was elevated into the movement's common sense. Institutions reveal their doctrines not in their programmes but in their post-mortems, and the post-mortem of 1927 is the doctrine's clearest early portrait.

Germany at the decade's opening is the second exhibit, and the gravest in its consequences. The German Communist Party, the International's largest section outside the Soviet Union, spent the decisive years before Hitler's appointment treating the Social Democrats as the principal enemy, occasionally cooperating with the Nazis themselves against the republic's defenders, in episodes its historians have never succeeded in explaining away. The line came from the centre and served the centre's reading of Soviet interests, in which a social democratic Germany oriented toward the Western powers appeared more dangerous than a nationalist Germany that might be kept in negotiation. The answer to the objection must concede the exhibit while noting its complications: the Social Democrats' own record, from 1919's bloodshed onward, had made unity genuinely difficult, and the German party's leaders believed their line with a sincerity that puppet imagery cannot capture. Sincere belief, however, is compatible with the objection's structural claim. A doctrine does its deepest work not by issuing orders but by forming the convictions of those who then act without needing them, and the German catastrophe is evidence about formation as much as about command.

## The doctrine and the war, and after

The Second World War tested the doctrine's hierarchy under maximum pressure, and the record of those years is the one the answer to the objection cites most hopefully and the objection cites most bitterly. The pact with Germany in 1939, defended inside the movement as a breathing space for the state where socialism was being built, required communist parties in France and Britain to oppose their own countries' war effort as an imperialist war, a reversal executed within weeks of those parties having been the loudest voices for resistance to fascism. The invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941 reversed the line again, and the parties became the most disciplined organisers of national resistance, supplying a disproportionate share of the partisan movements of occupied Europe. The objection reads the sequence as the doctrine's reductio: parties whose line could be inverted twice in two years by the diplomacy of one state had ceased, in any meaningful sense, to possess a politics of their own. The answer reads the same sequence and points to the outcome: the state whose preservation the doctrine organised survived the greatest invasion in history and destroyed the regime that launched it, at a cost to its own population that no other state approached. Both readings are of the same facts. The article's discipline is to keep both in view and to refuse the comfort of a ledger in which one cancels the other.

### What happened to the doctrine's hierarchy after the war?

It was extended, then broken by its own logic. Eastern Europe's states were organised as a protective zone and their parties subordinated as the doctrine prescribed. But Yugoslavia's expulsion from the bloc in 1948 showed a communist state applying the one country logic to itself against Moscow, and the later split with China generalised the fracture across the movement.

The Yugoslav break deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the doctrine's most elegant refutation in practice. Tito's partisans had taken power largely by their own efforts, and when the Soviet leadership attempted to subordinate Yugoslav policy to Soviet requirements, the Yugoslavs replied, in effect, with the doctrine itself: a party that had built its revolution in its own country would govern that country in its own interest, and the completion of its construction was its own affair. The heresy of Titoism, as Moscow denounced it, was socialism in one country practised by the wrong country. The episode exposed the doctrine's unspoken indexical: the one country whose construction outranked the movement was never any country, it was a specific one, and the universal form of the proposition concealed a proper name. The Chinese party learned the lesson thoroughly. Its eventual break with Moscow, fought partly over the postwar doctrine of peaceful coexistence and the subordination it implied, reproduced the Yugoslav structure at continental scale, and the Chinese doctrine of building socialism under national conditions, noted earlier in this article, is the Yugoslav and Soviet precedents generalised into a standing permission that Beijing has never since surrendered.

The doctrine's final decades inside the Soviet Union were quiet ones. No congress debated it; no textbook questioned it; it had passed from proposition to presupposition, which is the fate of successful doctrines and the reason historians must work to recover the moment when it was a choice. Its presuppositional status is also why its collapse, when the Soviet state dissolved, attracted so little theoretical attention from its former custodians: a presupposition does not get refuted, it gets abandoned, and the parties that had been formed by it discovered, in the early nineteen nineties, that the centre whose security had organised their existence no longer existed to be defended. The speed with which most of them dissolved, rebranded or dwindled is the last piece of evidence in the objection's file, since a movement whose parties had possessed independent reasons to exist might have been expected to survive the loss of its centre with more resilience than most of them showed.

## Lenin's other texts: the defence's second shelf

Fairness to the doctrine's defenders requires examining the texts they cited beyond the 1915 passage, because their case in print was broader than the single famous quotation, and a precision article should show why the broader case also fails to reach the doctrine's distinctive claim. The favourite supplementary texts came from Lenin's final writings of 1922 and 1923, above all the article on cooperation, in which he wrote that the growth of cooperative organisation under Soviet power was identical with the growth of socialism, and the notes on the revolution in which he answered the objection that Russia lacked the civilisation for socialism by asking why a people could not first take power and then acquire the prerequisites. These texts are genuine, late and moving, written by a dying man reconsidering the sequence his revolution had followed. Defenders read them as the founder's own turn toward the one country position: if cooperation under Soviet power is socialism growing, and if the prerequisites can be created after the seizure rather than before it, then construction in one country has Lenin's warrant in spirit whatever the 1915 passage denotes.

### Do the late writings supply the missing warrant?

They supply a warrant for beginning and for building, not for the claim about completion and self sufficiency. The article on cooperation presupposes the alliance with the peasantry and a long cultural transformation, and its author in the same months continued to tie the revolution's final fate to the international arena. The late texts deepen the founder's ambiguity; they do not resolve it in the doctrine's favour.

The honest verdict on the founder's corpus is that it underdetermines the controversy, because the controversy in its sharp form postdates him. Lenin expected the European revolution, organised his statecraft around its arrival, and died as its failure was becoming undeniable but before the party had to theorise the failure. His writings therefore contain the materials of both positions: the internationalist framework that the opposition systematised, and the improvisations of a governing revolutionary, building with whatever lay to hand, that the doctrine's defenders systematised. Each side's Lenin is a selection, and each selection is real. What distinguishes the doctrine's selection is not that it selected, which was unavoidable, but that it denied selecting, presenting its construction as the corpus's plain meaning while its own codifier's first edition was still in libraries stating the opposite. The two editions return at the end of every corridor of this controversy, because they are the point where the denial of selection became a documentary falsehood. That is why this article made them its centre, and why any future treatment of the doctrine that omits them should be read as advocacy rather than as reference.

## Uneven development: the law both sides claimed

Beneath the quarrel over a quotation lies a quarrel over a law, and the law deserves its own examination because both sides built on it and drew opposite conclusions. The law of uneven development, as Lenin formulated it during the First World War, holds that capitalism develops at radically different rates in different countries and branches, so that the chain of the world system breaks at its weakest link rather than at its most advanced point. From this law the 1915 passage drew its conclusion about sequence: revolution can come first where the contradictions concentrate, not where the textbooks of an earlier Marxism expected it, in the most industrialised countries. The doctrine of socialism in one country presented itself as the law's continuation. If development is uneven, its defenders argued, then the conditions for socialism ripen unevenly too, and a country where the working class holds power and commands great resources may complete its construction while others lag, the unevenness of the world being precisely what makes national completion possible rather than what forbids it. The opposition answered from the same law with the opposite inference. Uneven development explains why revolution breaks out in a backward country; it does not repeal the interdependence of the world economy, and the backwardness that made the outbreak possible is exactly what makes isolated completion impossible, since the country's productive forces remain entangled with a world market organised by its enemies. Two derivations from one premise, each internally consistent, which is why the controversy could not be settled by citing the law and had to be settled, insofar as it was settled at all, by power.

### Does uneven development support the doctrine?

It supports the premise about beginnings and is neutral, at best, on the claim about completion. The law describes how capitalism's contradictions distribute themselves across countries; the doctrine needs a further proposition, that a single country's resources suffice for a finished socialist society, and that proposition comes from the argument about scale and circumstance, not from the law itself.

The standoff over the law illustrates a recurring feature of doctrinal controversy that students of the tradition should learn to recognise. When both sides share a premise and divide over its implications, the controversy migrates to the premise's interpretation, and the side that controls the institutions controls the interpretation's official form. The law of uneven development survived the controversy in the codifications, but it survived in the doctrine's reading, so that generations of students in the Soviet sphere learned the law as a premise of the one country conclusion and never encountered the opposition's derivation. Recovering the lost derivation is part of what a reference article owes its readers, not because the lost side was necessarily right, but because a law with only one derivation on record is a law whose content has been decided by custody rather than by argument. The same custody problem afflicts the founder's corpus as a whole, and it is the deep reason the two editions matter: they are the visible seam where custody did its work on a text the custodians had themselves just written.

## Completion and guarantee: the distinction examined

The doctrine's qualification, its distinction between the building at home and the final victory, deserves a slower examination than it usually receives, because the whole fairness of the doctrine's statement depends on whether the distinction is a genuine analysis or a verbal escape hatch. As its author stated it from 1925 onward, the distinction ran as follows. The internal contradictions of the Soviet Union, between the working class and the peasantry, between socialist industry and the small commodity economy, could be resolved by the country's own efforts, and on that side complete construction was possible. The external contradiction, between the Soviet Union and the capitalist encirclement, could not be resolved by one country's efforts, since it concerned the danger of intervention and restoration, and on that side final victory required the triumph of revolution in several countries. Building, therefore, was a national task; guaranteeing the building against the world's hostility was an international one. Stated so, the distinction is coherent, and it maps onto a real difference between what a society can organise inside itself and what it cannot control outside itself. A reader who has not seen it stated cannot judge the doctrine, since the doctrine without the distinction is a cruder thing than its author, at his most careful, maintained.

### Is the building and guarantee distinction genuine?

It is genuine as an analysis of two different problems, internal organisation and external security. The critics' strongest point is not that the problems are identical but that the distinction worked asymmetrically: the strong claim about building did the public and polemical work, while the qualification about guarantee absorbed objections and then played no part in the movement's practical reasoning.

The critics' deeper objection to the distinction was that it misdescribed the external problem as merely military. On the opposition's analysis, the world market was not an army at the border but a set of prices, technologies and productivity levels pressing on the isolated economy every day, so that the external contradiction penetrated the internal one continuously. A country building alone does not face the world only at moments of invasion; it faces it in the cost of every imported machine, in the productivity gap between its factories and foreign factories, in the standard of comparison its own citizens apply. If that analysis is right, then the clean separation between an internal sphere resolvable nationally and an external sphere awaiting international resolution is an artefact of the definitions, and the doctrine's completion claim fails not on the guarantee clause but on the building clause itself, since the building is never purely internal. The doctrine's defenders replied that the state monopoly of foreign trade insulated the construction from the world market's daily pressure, converting external economic relations into a managed interface. That reply had real content in the early years, when trade volumes were small, and decreasing content as the construction's appetite for foreign technology grew, a tension the industrialisation drive of the nineteen thirties exposed in practice, when the building of socialism in one country proceeded by importing entire factories from the capitalist world and paying for them with grain extracted during a famine. The distinction, in short, survives as an analytical tool and fails as a description of the process it legitimised, and holding both halves of that sentence is the precision the subject requires.

## The economics underneath: accumulation and the village

Doctrines float above economics in most summaries, but the one country controversy had a hard economic core, and a reader who misses it will mistake the quarrel for a dispute about quotations all the way down. The Soviet Union of the middle nineteen twenties had recovered, under the New Economic Policy, to roughly its prewar levels of production, which meant it had recovered to the levels of a poor country. Industrialisation beyond that point required investment on a scale the economy did not generate spontaneously, and the question of where the surplus for investment would come from divided the party as deeply as any question of texts. Preobrazhensky, the opposition's economist, argued for primitive socialist accumulation: the state sector would accumulate by drawing resources from the private sector, above all from the peasantry, through unequal exchange administered by the state's monopoly positions. Bukharin answered that squeezing the village would destroy the alliance and the market that fed the towns, and that accumulation must come slowly, from growth itself, with the peasantry enriching itself inside a framework the state guided. The doctrine sat above this quarrel as its frame: both economists assumed a national construction, and divided over its financing and its tempo. When the leadership resolved the quarrel by force at the decade's end, adopting a tempo faster than the opposition had proposed and a squeeze harsher than either side had described, it did so entirely within the doctrine's frame, and the frame's survival through the reversal is again the evidence of what the doctrine was: not an economics, but the legitimation of the national arena in which any economics would be chosen.

### What was primitive socialist accumulation?

It was Preobrazhensky's term for financing state industry by transferring resources from the private economy, chiefly the peasantry, through the state's control of prices and trade. The proposal named honestly what rapid industrialisation in a poor country requires, a surplus extracted from agriculture, and the quarrel over it concerned the rate of extraction and its political price.

The grain procurement crisis of the winter of 1927 and 1928 converted these abstractions into the regime's defining emergency. The peasants, facing prices that made grain sales unattractive and a shortage of manufactured goods to buy with the proceeds, withheld their produce; the towns faced bread shortages; the state responded with emergency measures, forced requisitioning in all but name, and a campaign against the richer peasants blamed for the withholding. Within two years the emergency had become a transformation: wholesale collectivisation, the deportation of the kulaks, the destruction of the market framework within which Bukharin's gradual construction had been imagined. The doctrine did not dictate this sequence, and its author's own earlier statements had promised a long coexistence with the peasant economy. But the doctrine had established the premise that made the sequence governable as construction rather than as catastrophe: the country was building socialism, the building had a plan, and resistance to the plan was resistance to socialism itself, classifiable and punishable as such. Here the objection's domestic clause and the economic record meet. A theory that converts an emergency into a destiny will process emergencies as stages, and the processing of the collectivisation famine as a stage of construction, in the doctrine's own language, is the point at which many later readers, including readers sympathetic to the argument from circumstance, conclude that the conversion itself was the catastrophe's enabling condition.

It is equally part of the record that the industrialisation the squeeze financed built the heavy industry that later armed the state for its survival war, and that the doctrine's defenders have always rested their consequential case there. The tanks that met the invasion of 1941 came from factories raised in the decade of the doctrine's triumph, in cities built by the construction the doctrine legitimised. An assessment that omits this is as partial as one that omits the famine. The article's method, separating questions, is the only honest way to hold the whole record: the textual case fails; the circumstantial case is real; the economic process the doctrine framed was simultaneously a forced accumulation of world historical consequence and a crime against the population that bore it; and the doctrine's own categories are incapable of registering that simultaneity, which is perhaps the deepest criticism of it available, deeper than any quotation.

## The war scare and the military frame

No account of the doctrine's plausibility in its own time is complete without the military frame, because the men who adopted it believed, with reasons they could enumerate, that their state would be invaded again. The war scare of 1927, when a raid on the Soviet embassy in London, a rupture of diplomatic relations with Britain, and the assassination of a Soviet ambassador in Warsaw produced genuine panic in the leadership and the press, was not a propaganda invention, whatever use propaganda made of it. The memory of the intervention years, when British, French, American and Japanese forces had all operated on the territory of the former empire, was a decade old and personally carried by every senior figure in the regime. Defence required industry, industry required accumulation, accumulation required the village's surplus, and the whole chain required a doctrine that made the national effort meaningful rather than provisional. The case from circumstance, in its lived form, was not an abstract syllogism about isolation; it was a chain of necessities that began with the expectation of invasion. The doctrine's opponents shared the expectation and disputed the chain, arguing that a bled and coerced population would defend the state less reliably than an allied one, and that the only real security lay in the revolutions the doctrine postponed. Both sides were reasoning about survival. The difference was that one side's reasoning could be implemented by the state that existed, and the other's required events that had repeatedly failed to occur.

## The doctrine in the textbooks: codification completed

A doctrine's victory is measured by what the textbooks teach when the controversy is forgotten, and by that measure the doctrine's victory was total. The History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Bolsheviks): Short Course, published in 1938 and attributed in part to the doctrine's author himself, became the canonical narrative through which tens of millions of readers inside the bloc and sympathisers outside it learned the party's past. In its pages the one country doctrine appears not as a proposition adopted in a controversy but as the perennial position of Leninism, the opposition appears as a succession of deviations whose essence was disbelief in the Russian construction, and the two editions do not appear at all. The Short Course is the retrospective systematisation of 1924 perfected and industrialised: a past with no seams, in which the winning line was always held and the losing lines were always alien. Its chapter on dialectical materialism detached the philosophy from the controversy entirely, and its narrative chapters converted the controversy into a morality play whose outcome the reader was invited to admire rather than to examine. For a series concerned with how doctrines are transmitted, the Short Course matters as the doctrine's final form, the form in which it ceased to be arguable because it had become indistinguishable from the history of the party itself.

### What did the Short Course do to the controversy?

It erased the controversy as a controversy. The doctrine appears in its narrative as what Leninism had always taught, the opposition as a record of deviation and disbelief, and the documentary traces of the doctrine's contested birth, the revised textbook included, are absent. The codification of 1938 completed the operation the revision of 1924 began, at the scale of a movement's memory.

The pedagogical consequence lasted generations. Students in the Soviet sphere who encountered the phrase socialism in one country met it as a definition to be reproduced, one item in a catechism whose other items included the laws of dialectics and the stages of the party's history, and the idea that the definition had once been denied in print by its own author would have struck most of them as a slander rather than a bibliographical fact. Outside the bloc, the parties of the International taught their own members from the same narrative in translation, so that the erasure was international, matching the doctrine's own claim about the movement it organised. When, decades later, the Soviet leadership itself began to publish the record more honestly, during the glasnost years, the revelation of such elementary facts about the doctrine's birth landed on a public for whom the catechism had never admitted the possibility of seams. The shock of those years is unintelligible without grasping how completely the codification had done its work. A doctrine that began by overwriting one paragraph in one textbook ended by organising the historical memory of a civilisation, and the line between those points is the best available illustration of why textual questions, which look small, are not.

## Spain and Greece: the doctrine's logic in the field

Two episodes from the doctrine's mature period repay closer attention than the summary given earlier, because in each the mechanism the objection describes can be watched operating decision by decision. In Spain, after the military rising of 1936, the Soviet Union became the republic's principal foreign supplier, and its aid arrived with advisers, conditions and a political line. The Spanish Communist Party, previously small, grew into a major force by presenting itself as the most disciplined defender of the republic, the advocate of a conventional army, of property respected for the middle classes, of a war fought for democracy rather than for revolution. The line served the Soviet state's search for an alliance with France and Britain, which required a Spain that frightened no one, and it required the suppression of the revolutionary currents to the party's left, above all the POUM and the anarchists, whose defeat in the Barcelona fighting of May 1937 was accomplished with the participation of the security apparatus the Soviet advisers brought with them. The objection reads Spain as the doctrine's perfect field demonstration: a revolution restrained, and its most revolutionary elements destroyed, in the service of a diplomacy that failed anyway when the Western democracies chose non-intervention. The answer replies that a Spanish revolution in 1936 would have driven the middle classes to Franco and lost the war faster, and that the party's line was the only one compatible with holding the republic together. Both readings were available to contemporaries, and the participants argued them at the time in exactly these terms, which is why Spain remains the controversy's most instructive case: the doctrine's logic and its costs are visible there without any need for hindsight.

### Why is Spain the controversy's most instructive case?

Because every element of the dispute appears there in concrete form: a communist party grown powerful under the doctrine's discipline, a revolutionary situation restrained in deference to an alliance diplomacy conducted for the Soviet state's security, the suppression of rival revolutionaries by the movement's own apparatus, and a defence, sincerely held, that restraint was the only strategy compatible with survival.

Greece supplies the mirror episode, at the level of states rather than parties. In October 1944, in Moscow, Churchill and Stalin agreed the percentages by which the great powers would predominate in the Balkan countries, and Greece was assigned to the Western sphere. The Greek Communist Party, which by then controlled most of its country through the resistance, was not consulted, and when its forces clashed with British troops in Athens that December, the Soviet Union observed its agreement and offered no support. The Greek party's subsequent history, a civil war fought and lost without the patron its members had assumed stood behind them, is the doctrine's hierarchy drawn on a map: the security arrangements of the one state outranked the prospects of a fraternal party at the very moment its opportunity stood greatest, and the arrangement was not a betrayal of the doctrine but its application. Defenders note, again correctly, that a Soviet intervention in Greece risked a general war the Soviet Union, bled white, could not fight, and that the percentages agreement arguably spared Greece a worse fate. The objection's answer is by now familiar and remains unanswered in its own terms: a movement whose theory had not subordinated every party to one state's security would not have needed to choose between its Greek section and a general war, because it would not have taught the Greek section to expect, or the world to accept, that its fate was an entry in another state's ledger.

## Eurocommunism and the doctrine's long echo

The last act of the doctrine's career inside the international movement came in the nineteen seventies, when the communist parties of Italy, Spain and France, in different degrees, asserted their independence of Moscow and their commitment to roads shaped to their own national conditions and to democratic forms. Eurocommunism, as the tendency was named, was directed against the subordination the objection had denounced fifty years earlier, and its leaders said so, criticising the Soviet model and the discipline of the old International's heirs. Yet the episode carries an irony that completes the doctrine's story. The Eurocommunists justified their national roads by the same founding move the doctrine of 1924 had made: the circumstance of their countries, they argued, required a construction fitted to national conditions, pursued without waiting for a general European transformation, and legitimate in itself rather than as a deviation from a centre's script. The permission the doctrine had issued to one state was now claimed by parties against that state's successors. The Soviet leadership, denouncing Eurocommunism as a deviation, found itself in the position of the old opposition, insisting that socialism had an international content no national road could dissolve. Doctrines do not respect the intentions of their authors, and the one country form, having served a centre against its periphery, ended by serving every periphery against the centre. A glossary article can close its historical account there, with the form outliving every content that was poured into it, which is the usual fate of the formulas that organise a century.

## The comparison, stated as propositions

The brief for this article fixes its comparison basis, and the basis is worth honouring explicitly because most popular comparisons violate it. Where the doctrine is compared with the position it displaced, the comparison is between stated propositions about what can be completed in one country, not between the political outcomes of the factions that held them. Outcomes contaminate judgement: the doctrine's side won the state and the opposition's side was murdered, and a comparison run on outcomes will either credit the doctrine with everything the state did or blame it for everything, neither of which is an assessment of the proposition. Stated cleanly, the propositions are these. The doctrine asserts that the completion of socialist construction is possible within one country by that country's efforts, with the international revolution required only for the final guarantee against intervention. The displaced position asserts that completion is impossible within one country, because the productive forces, the culture and the division of labour required by a socialist society exceed any single country's endowment, so that construction begun nationally must either be completed internationally or degenerate under the pressure of its own incompleteness. Between these propositions, everything else in the famous controversy, the quotations, the editions, the expulsions, is evidence or noise. The textual evidence bears on whether the doctrine may claim the founder. The economic evidence bears on whether an isolated construction's contradictions are internal and manageable or external and compounding. The historical evidence bears on the fate each proposition brought to the movement that adopted it. A reader who keeps the three bodies of evidence assigned to the three questions they can actually answer possesses the controversy in its intelligible form, and possesses it in a form that survives the discovery of new facts about any participant's character.

### Why compare propositions rather than factions?

Because the factions' fates were decided by organisational power, and a verdict borrowed from their fates teaches nothing about the claims. The doctrine's proposition can be false though its author prevailed, and the opposition's can be true though its author was exiled and killed. Comparing stated claims about completion keeps the assessment answerable to evidence instead of outcomes.

There is one more proposition in the field that summaries usually omit, and its omission distorts the comparison into a duel when the reality was a triangle. Between the doctrine and permanent revolution stood the position associated at different moments with the party's right: that the Soviet Union should build gradually, integrate itself into the world economy, and treat the international movement's revolutionary prospects as a distant matter, a position whose adherents accepted the national frame as completely as the doctrine did while rejecting its mobilisation. The triangle matters because it shows that the national frame itself, the assumption that the Soviet construction was the arena, commanded a majority across the party's spectrum by the decade's midpoint, and the live dispute was over tempo, method and the movement's subordination, conducted inside a frame few still questioned. The opposition's distinctiveness was precisely its refusal of the frame, its insistence that the arena remained international. That refusal is what made it, in the end, unassimilable: a party can absorb disagreements about tempo, but a disagreement about the arena is a disagreement about what the party is. The expulsions followed from that logic with a grim consistency that the participants on the winning side understood perfectly well.

## How subordination worked: the machinery

Abstract claims about a movement's subordination gain credibility when the machinery is described, and the machinery was neither secret nor subtle. The Communist International's executive in Moscow stood above the national parties in its statutes, its congresses and executive plenums issued binding lines, and its emissaries travelled with authority to override local leaderships. Parties received subsidies from the centre, which gave the centre the leverage that money always gives, and their cadres were trained in Moscow schools where the doctrine, in its codified form, was the curriculum. Leadership changes in national parties were repeatedly made or unmade by the centre's intervention: the German party's leadership was replaced after its failed rising of 1923 in a settlement dictated from Moscow; the American party's factions were summoned and adjudicated by the International's commissions; the Polish party was dissolved outright in 1938 on fabricated charges of infiltration, its leaders shot, in the machinery's most extreme exercise. None of this required the doctrine to exist, and much of the machinery predated the doctrine's codification, which is the answer's strongest institutional point. What the doctrine supplied was the machinery's rationale in the movement's own theoretical language. Discipline imposed for the centre's convenience is resented as discipline; discipline understood as the defence of the one country where socialism is being built is internalised as virtue. The conversion of convenience into virtue is the doctrine's characteristic operation, visible in the two editions at the level of the text and in the machinery at the level of the institution, and recognising the operation in both places is the surest way to understand what the doctrine was for.

### Did the machinery need the doctrine?

The machinery of centralised discipline predated the doctrine and would have functioned without it. What it lacked was a theoretical warrant making subordination a virtue rather than an expedient. The doctrine supplied that warrant, teaching cadres everywhere that the centre's requirements expressed a law of the movement's development rather than the preferences of its strongest section.

The machinery's human texture should not be lost in the institutional description. The cadres who staffed the International's apparatus and the national parties were, in their great majority, sincere revolutionaries who had risked and often given their lives for the cause they served. The doctrine did not rule them by cynicism; it ruled them by giving their sacrifices a geography. To defend the Soviet Union was to defend the one place where the future had begun, and a cadre in Berlin, Shanghai or New York who subordinated a local judgement to that defence experienced the act as fidelity to the world revolution, not as its abandonment. This is why the objection's charge of instrumentalisation, though structurally accurate, has always failed to persuade those it describes: it mistakes the experience of the instrument. The sharper criticism, which the objection's best proponents also made, is that a doctrine should be judged by the experiences it makes available, and a doctrine that makes subordination feel like fidelity has disabled the faculty by which its victims might have corrected it. That criticism stands, and it is not answered by pointing out, truly, that the cadres were sincere. Sincerity is the medium in which the doctrine worked, not a defence against the charge.

## The national frame and the federation

One further precision belongs in a glossary treatment, concerning the doctrine's silent assumption about its own unit. Socialism in one country presupposes that the country is a natural container for a society, a bounded economy whose internal relations can be organised as a whole. The Soviet Union fitted that presupposition badly and interestingly. It was a federation of republics, themselves containing autonomous regions of other peoples, assembled from an empire's territory and held together by a party whose centralism did the work that the constitution's federalism described. The doctrine's singular noun, country, performed an act of unification every time it was uttered: it presented the Union as one society with one construction, where the reality was many societies at vastly different levels of development, from the industrial cities of the Russian heartland to the nomadic economies of Central Asia, bound in a single plan. Building socialism in one country therefore also meant building it across that internal unevenness, a task whose difficulties reproduced, inside the federation, the very problem the doctrine denied between the federation and the world. If completion in one country is possible despite the gap between its advanced and backward regions, the opposition asked, why is completion impossible despite the gap between countries, which is the same gap at a larger scale? The doctrine never answered the question in its own terms, and the federation's later history, in which the internal gaps persisted to the end and the union dissolved along the seams of its constituent nations, gives the question a retrospective force its first askers could not have anticipated.

## Successors and inversions: from peaceful coexistence to limited sovereignty

The doctrine's descendants include one that inverted it, and the inversion completes the conceptual history. In 1956 the twentieth congress of the Soviet party elaborated peaceful coexistence into a general line and acknowledged, in principle, that different countries might take different roads to socialism, parliamentary roads included. The concession was the one country logic extended outward: if construction is national, roads may be national too. The Hungarian rising of the same year, and its suppression by Soviet armour, marked the concession's limit in practice. In 1968, after the invasion of Czechoslovakia, the doctrine of limited sovereignty, associated with Brezhnev, stated the limit as a principle: the sovereignty of a socialist state ends where the gains of socialism, as judged by the community of socialist states and in practice by their strongest member, are threatened. Set the two doctrines side by side and the symmetry is exact. Socialism in one country had declared that a single state's construction outranked the international movement's independence. Limited sovereignty declared that the community of constructed states, meaning its centre, outranked any single member's independence, including the independence to define its own road. The periphery of 1924 had become the centre's subjects by the same logic, applied one level up. The Chinese and Albanian parties, denouncing the invasion, reached for the vocabulary of the old opposition, national independence against a hegemonic centre, and the Albanian leadership in particular preserved, in its own codifications, a museum version of the doctrines of the nineteen twenties, one country orthodoxy included, long after the country of its origin had ceased to repeat them.

### What is the relation between the two doctrines?

They state the same subordination at different levels. The earlier doctrine subordinated the international movement to the one state where building was under way; the later subordinated each built state to the community of such states as its centre defined it. The reformers of 1968 discovered that permission to build in one's own country had always been revocable by the country that issued it.

The Albanian coda is worth a sentence more, because it shows the doctrine's form surviving its content in the purest available specimen. Enver Hoxha's Albania broke first with Yugoslavia, then with the Soviet Union, then with China, each time in the name of an orthodoxy it alone preserved, and constructed, in conditions of extreme isolation and poverty, the most literal attempt in Europe to build socialism in one country as a self sufficient fortress. The experiment's results, a regimented poverty that collapsed with the regime, are cited by the doctrine's critics as its reductio and were defended by its last adherents as the consequence of encirclement, which is to say that the argument from circumstance outlived every other element of the controversy and was still being offered, unchanged in structure, seventy years after the defeats that first made it plausible. A glossary article notes the persistence without needing to resolve it: arguments from circumstance do not expire, because circumstances of isolation and encirclement recur, and each recurrence reopens the question this article has tried to state exactly.

## The critics outside the movement

The doctrine has also been read by historians who owed no allegiance to any party in the original quarrel, and their readings form a third literature beside the defence and the opposition. The liberal historiography of the Cold War period treated the doctrine as the moment the Soviet regime confessed its transformation into a national state like any other, its universal mission retained as decoration over an ordinary great power's interests; on this reading the doctrine's significance is diagnostic, revealing what the regime had become. The totalitarian school read it as one component of a monolithic ideology whose function was the legitimation of terror, and paid correspondingly little attention to its content as an argument. The revisionist social historians who followed shifted attention from the doctrine's text to its reception, asking what the formula meant to the workers, peasants and cadres who lived under it, and finding, in the construction campaigns of the nineteen thirties, a genuine popular investment in the building the doctrine named, especially among the young workers promoted into the new technical intelligentsia. Each literature captures something the others miss. The liberal reading sees the state's interest clearly and the argument's sincerity not at all. The totalitarian reading sees the legitimation and misses the dilemma. The revisionist reading recovers the experience and sometimes loses the text. The two editions discipline all three, since a fact about a printed page is available to every method, and the methods divide only over what the fact means. That is as it should be, and it is why this article has kept returning to the page.

### Why do the two editions matter to every school of interpretation?

Because they are the controversy's hardest evidence. Sympathisers must explain a codifier contradicting his doctrine within a year of printing its opposite; critics must explain why a mere opportunist bothered to argue; social historians must explain how a movement absorbed a reversal it mostly never noticed. Each interpretation is recognisable by how it steps around the fact.

A fourth literature, the memoir literature of the movement's own dissidents, deserves mention for the evidence it gives about the doctrine's lived meaning. Communists who broke with their parties over the episodes this article has traced, in 1939 over the pact, in 1956 over Hungary, in 1968 over Czechoslovakia, describe in their memoirs a remarkably consistent experience: the moment of break was the moment when the subordination, long accepted as theory, presented itself as a demand they could not perform, to defend an action of the Soviet state that their own judgement condemned. The doctrine had taught them that such defence was the movement's highest duty, and their breaks were therefore experienced not as changes of opinion but as excommunications from themselves, costly in friendships, in livelihoods and in identity. Reading these accounts alongside the congress resolutions gives the controversy its human scale. The doctrine was not only a proposition in a textbook. It was the load bearing belief of hundreds of thousands of organised lives, and its history is incomplete until the weight it bore, and the fractures where it failed, are part of the description.

## The counterfactual workshop: the garrison alternative

Serious assessment of the argument from circumstance requires constructing the alternative it rejected, not as a slogan but as a sequence of years. Suppose the Soviet leadership of 1925 had adopted the opposition's frame: the construction at home is provisional, the state's legitimacy rests on its role as the advance post of a European revolution, and policy is set accordingly. The first consequence is diplomatic: a state that defines itself as an advance post cannot pursue the trade agreements and recognitions that the actual Soviet government spent the decade obtaining, since its neighbours will treat its embassies as general staffs, which the frame instructs them to be. The second consequence is economic: investment and consumption are both subordinated to readiness, the peasantry is told that its sacrifices purchase a European upheaval, and when the upheaval does not come on schedule the legitimacy spent on the promise is not recoverable. The third consequence is internal to the movement: the foreign parties, told that their revolutions are the Soviet state's completion, are encouraged to attempt them in unfavourable conditions, as several did in the early nineteen twenties with catastrophic results, and each failure is then a failure of the Soviet state's own premise. The fourth consequence arrives with the decade's end: a world depression that the garrison state experiences without the industrial base the actual construction, at its terrible price, had begun to build, and a Germany rearmed under a regime whose first declared enemy is the advance post itself. The construction of this counterfactual does not refute the opposition, whose spokesmen had answers to each step and who would reply, above all, that the actual sequence ended in the same invasion with a population already bled by its own government. What the construction establishes is narrower and sufficient for this article's purpose: the alternative to the doctrine was not a costless fidelity to internationalism but a different distribution of costs, some of them falling due sooner and in crueller currency. The argument from circumstance survives the workshop as a serious argument. It does not emerge as a vindication, because the doctrine actually adopted was not merely the orientation the workshop examines; it was that orientation converted into dogma and imposed on a world movement, and the workshop prices the orientation, not the conversion. The conversion remains the objection's unrefuted charge.

## The formal adoption: congresses, expulsions, and the paper trail

The doctrine's passage from polemic to orthodoxy followed the party's constitutional forms, and the forms matter because they show a proposition becoming unfalsifiable inside its institution by procedural stages. At the fourteenth party conference in April 1925, a resolution on the party's tasks affirmed the possibility of building socialism in the Soviet Union and rebuked the contrary view as a deviation; the fourteenth congress that December, the first to call itself a congress of socialist construction, wrote the orientation into the party's programme of work. The opposition contested each step with documents of its own, culminating in the platform of the United Opposition in 1927, a comprehensive statement that included the international argument in its classical form. The fifteenth congress, at the end of 1927, declared adherence to the opposition's views incompatible with party membership, and the expulsions followed. Thereafter the doctrine was not a position within the party but a condition of belonging to it, and its status changed accordingly: a proposition that may not be denied may also not be examined, since examination requires the standing possibility of a negative result. The archives opened after the Soviet collapse added texture to this sequence without altering it. The protocols of the leading bodies show the controversy conducted with a documentary energy that belies any notion that the participants thought the questions trivial: long speeches on the meaning of the founder's sentences, on the statistics of the peasant economy, on the reports of foreign sections. The men who voted the expulsions had argued the texts for years. The tragedy the archives disclose is not ignorance but its opposite, a party that argued its way, with full knowledge of the evidence, into a settlement that forbade further argument.

### When did adherence to the doctrine become compulsory?

At the end of 1927, when the fifteenth congress declared the opposition's views incompatible with membership and the expulsions followed. Before that point the doctrine was a contested position winning a controversy; after it, the doctrine was a condition of belonging, and its status changed from a claim that had prevailed to a claim that could no longer be tested.

The paper trail of the revision itself also grew clearer with archival work. Bibliographers established the print history of the codification's editions, the dates of their appearance, and the exact passages altered, of which the one country passage was the most consequential but not the only one: the treatment of the peasantry, of the state, and of the party's role all shifted between printings as the polemical situation moved. The pattern of the alterations is itself evidence, since a revision undertaken for clarity would distribute its changes evenly across the text, whereas a revision undertaken for a controversy concentrates them at the contested points. The changes cluster where the campaign needed them. Scholars who have mapped the variants describe a text being steered, edition by edition, through the factional struggles of the decade, each printing a snapshot of the line at its moment. The codification that claimed to transmit a settled doctrine was, in its material history, the least settled text in the movement's library. There is no better emblem of the whole affair, and teachers who use the two editions in the classroom, as the persona section below recommends, usually find that the variant map teaches the lesson faster than any lecture: students who have seen a doctrine's paragraphs migrate between printings do not need to be told that codifications are made, not found.

## The Revolution Betrayed and the opposition's mature case

The opposition's case reached its mature form in exile, above all in Trotsky's book of 1936 on the Soviet Union, and its chapter on the doctrine remains the most concentrated statement of the objection this article has carried. There the doctrine is analysed as the ideology of the ruling stratum in its completed form: the bureaucracy, having despaired of the international revolution or having never believed in it, required a theory that pronounced its national rule a completed mission, and socialism in one country supplied the requirement so exactly that the fit itself is offered as evidence of the doctrine's origin. The book's famous formulation describes the doctrine as the theoretical expression of a stratum that wished to be liberated from the control of the world revolution it could not command. Around that core the mature case arranged the evidence this article has reviewed: the subordination of the International, the catastrophes of China and Germany, the doctrine's service in justifying each turn of the centre's diplomacy, and the textual record, which the exiled author cited with the satisfaction of a man whose quotations had been verified by his enemies' silence. The book also contains the opposition's concession, less often quoted: that the Soviet state, whatever its regime, rested on property forms created by the revolution and remained, against the capitalist world, a conquest to be defended. The concession complicates the objection's ledger, since the doctrine's critic-in-chief held simultaneously that the doctrine had corrupted the movement and that the state the doctrine legitimised embodied a gain worth defending against its enemies. Readers who flatten either side of that double judgement have not read the book they are citing.

### What did the mature opposition case add?

It added a sociology. The earlier polemics had argued texts and tactics; the exile writings explained the doctrine as the ideology of a ruling stratum whose material position required a national horizon, and predicted that a doctrine so rooted would outlast refutation because it served interests rather than errors. The prediction's accuracy is part of why the controversy is studied as more than a quarrel.

The mature case also fixed the controversy's vocabulary for everyone who came after. Terms that structure even neutral accounts, the degeneration of the revolution, the bureaucracy as a caste, the distinction between the state's property forms and its political regime, were minted in the exile literature and passed into general historiography, so that scholars who reject the opposition's conclusions routinely conduct their rejection in its language. The doctrine's own vocabulary had the opposite fate: its terms, construction, completion, final victory, survive mainly as objects of study rather than as tools of analysis. A controversy's outcome can be measured by whose words the future is obliged to use, and by that measure the organisational victor lost the peace. This is not a sentimental consolation for the defeated; it is a finding about how doctrines persist. A doctrine imposed as a condition of belonging dies with the institution that imposed it, while an argument addressed to readers survives wherever readers remain. The two editions, once more, are the emblem: the codification's authority was institutional and perished with the institution, while the bibliographical fact about it, available to any reader, is immortal.

## The colonial world and the doctrine's eastern front

The controversy's geography is usually drawn between Moscow and the European parties, but the doctrine had an eastern front where its logic operated with special clarity. The revolution's leaders had looked east from the beginning, convening the congress of the peoples of the East at Baku in 1920 and founding a university in Moscow for the cadres of the colonial world, on the theory that the liberation movements of Asia would strike the imperial system at its foundations. Under the doctrine's dispensation, that eastern strategy was progressively subordinated to the Soviet state's diplomacy with the colonial powers and with the nationalist governments of the East itself. The Chinese catastrophe of 1927 was the front's central disaster, but the pattern repeated in smaller theatres: parties and movements in Persia, in Turkey and in India found their strategies set by the requirements of a state that sought, at different moments, the friendship of the very governments those movements opposed. The Indian party's early history, examined elsewhere in this series in the cluster on Marxism in India, shows the pattern arriving by cable and emissary: lines reversed on instruction, alliances imposed and dissolved, each reversal explained by the needs of the centre. The objection's global claim, that the doctrine made one state's security the movement's compass, is nowhere easier to verify than in the record of the eastern departments, where the distance between the centre and the field made the compass's pull unmistakable and where the movements sacrificed were least able to answer back.

### How did the doctrine reach the colonial parties?

Through emissaries, subsidies and binding lines, and through the schooling of colonial cadres in Moscow, where the doctrine was the curriculum. A party in India or Persia met it as instructions about its own alliances, carrying the authority of the one successful revolution, rather than as a proposition about a distant construction.

There is a counterpoint that completeness requires. The doctrine's eastern record also includes the Soviet state's material support for liberation movements across the middle of the century, in Korea, in Vietnam, in the Portuguese colonies and elsewhere, support given when it aligned with the state's strategic interests and withheld when it did not. The alignment was the point. Under the old internationalism, support for a liberation movement was, in principle, owed to the movement's own merits; under the doctrine's dispensation, it was an instrument of policy, extended and withdrawn as policy required. The movements that received it were rarely ungrateful and rarely deceived about its terms, and several of them, Vietnam most successfully, learned to play the socialist states against one another in the space the doctrine's national logic had created. The eastern front thus ends in the same irony as the European one: the doctrine that subordinated movements to a state taught the movements, by example, to treat states as instruments of their own national constructions, and the pupils proved apt.

## Glossary work: the terms that must not blur

A glossary article earns its name in this section, where the controversy's working terms are fixed one by one, because most confusion about the doctrine is confusion about its vocabulary. Construction and completion name a process and its end state, and the doctrine's claim concerns the reachability of the end state within national borders; writers who use construction loosely, for any economic activity under a socialist government, dissolve the claim they mean to discuss. Final victory names the guarantee against restoration, the doctrine's reserved clause, and must not be confused with completion, since the doctrine's whole architecture rests on their separation. Codification names the genre of the founding text, a systematic exposition claiming to transmit an existing doctrine, and the word matters because the two editions are a fact about a codification specifically: a speculative essay may reverse itself without scandal, while a transmission that alters what it transmits raises the question this article has pursued. Breathing space, the term inherited from the regime's first years, names a pause granted by circumstance and is the vocabulary of accommodation, not of theory; the controversy's hinge can be heard in the language, since the doctrine's work was to redescribe a breathing space as a construction site. Encirclement names the hostile ring of states whose existence the argument from circumstance presupposes, and it should be used as the participants used it, for a strategic condition, not as a synonym for unpopularity. Deviation names the institutional verdict by which a position was expelled from the party's permissible range, and it is a term about procedure, not about truth, a distinction the participants themselves blurred at their convenience. Social fascism and popular front name the International's successive lines toward social democracy, and they belong in the glossary because they are the doctrine's consequences wearing tactical dress. A reader who holds these terms apart can follow any account of the controversy; a reader who lets them blur will find the accounts interchangeable and the controversy unintelligible, which is the condition in which slogans flourish.

### Why does the vocabulary decide so much here?

Because the doctrine's operations were verbal before they were institutional. It redescribed a pause as a construction, a completion as separable from its guarantee, and a state's interest as a movement's duty. Each redescription is invisible to a reader whose terms are loose, so the glossary is not an ornament on the analysis but the analysis itself, stated where the doctrine worked.

Translation adds a further layer of imprecision that the glossary should register. The doctrine's Russian formula speaks of building socialism in one country with a preposition that can carry the sense of within a single country taken separately, and the polemics in several languages exploited the shadings available in their own tongues. The German debate, conducted by the largest foreign party, worked with a phrase that emphasised the solitary country; the French and English versions settled into the forms this article uses. None of the shadings alters the proposition at stake, and the textual question is unaffected by them, since the two editions contradict each other in the original language before any translator intervenes. But the shadings mattered politically, because a doctrine transmitted to dozens of parties through translation acquired, in each language, a slightly different centre of gravity, and the centre in Moscow exploited the elasticity when it disciplined sections whose offence was partly lexical. Precision in a glossary therefore includes a warning: quote the doctrine in the language of the document being discussed, and be suspicious of any argument that depends on a shading introduced by a translator.

## The doctrine in the examination room of its own era

There is an unusual source for the doctrine's reception that historians use too rarely: the examination and study practices of the movement itself. Cadres in the Soviet Union and in the foreign parties were formed through circles, courses and examinations in which the codified doctrine was the syllabus, and the questions set in those settings reveal what the doctrine's custodians thought it was for. The examinations of the party schools tested the ability to identify deviations, to narrate the controversies as a sequence of errors overcome, and to apply the one country formula to the situation of the student's own party, which for a foreign cadre meant rehearsing the subordination as a personal conviction. The pedagogy was the doctrine's reproductive system. A proposition that is examined becomes, for the examined, a fact of the world rather than a claim upon it, and the millions who passed through the movement's schools carried the formula as part of the furniture of reality. When the persona section below addresses teachers, it is this pedagogy that it recommends inverting: the same materials, the two editions above all, examined with the opposite purpose, not to install the formula but to show how a formula gets installed. The contrast between the two pedagogies is the contrast between the doctrine's century and the purposes of this series, and it is fitting that an article in a study series should end its analytical work by acknowledging the rival series, the party schools, whose methods it exists to reverse.

### What did the movement's own examinations test?

They tested recognition and application: the ability to narrate the controversies as deviations overcome and to apply the doctrine's formula to one's own party's situation. For foreign cadres the application was the point, since rehearsing the priority of the Soviet construction as a personal answer converted an instruction into a conviction before it was ever issued as an order.

The study circle literature also preserves, in its questions and model answers, the doctrine's official self understanding at successive dates, a fossil record of the formula's hardening. In the middle nineteen twenties the study materials still present the one country thesis as a position being established against opponents, with the opponents' arguments summarised in order to be answered. By the middle nineteen thirties the opponents have vanished from the materials and the thesis appears as a definition among definitions. By the postwar editions the controversy survives only as a list of names in a chronology of deviations, the arguments gone entirely. A reader who lines up the three states of the study literature watches the exact process this article has described at the level of the textbook and the congress repeated at the level of the classroom: argument, then definition, then catechism. The fossil record matters to the assessment because it dates the doctrine's epistemic death precisely. A doctrine dies as an argument not when it is refuted but when its custodians stop being able to state the case against it, and by that test the doctrine was dead, as an argument, within a decade of its birth, however long its institutional afterlife continued.

## The programme of the world party: the doctrine goes global

The doctrine's export was formalised in 1928, when the Communist International adopted its programme at the sixth congress, and the episode deserves attention because it shows the conversion of a national position into a world party's constitution in documentary form. The draft programme, prepared under Bukharin's direction, presented the Soviet construction as the central fact of the epoch and the Soviet Union's defence as the international proletariat's supreme task, while the opposition in exile produced a book length critique of the draft, Trotsky's examination of the International after Lenin, which fastened precisely on the programme's theoretical core. The critique argued that a programme for a world party which organised its strategy around the completion of socialism in one of its national sections had inverted the relation between the movement and the state: the International existed, on the older understanding, to make revolutions, and the Soviet state was its first fruit and its base; on the draft's understanding, the International existed to protect the fruit, and its sections were valued as the base's outworks. The congress adopted the draft with the unanimity the machinery guaranteed. Thereafter every communist party's programme, catechism and examination syllabus carried the doctrine in its international form, and a cadre in any country who questioned the Soviet construction's priority questioned not a Russian opinion but the constitution of the world movement to which his party belonged. The step from textbook to programme is the step from influence to law, and it is the point in the doctrine's career where the objection's charge stops being a characterisation and becomes a description of a constitutional arrangement.

### What changed when the doctrine entered the world programme?

Its status changed. Inside the Soviet party it had been a ruling position enforced by discipline; inside the International's programme it became the stated purpose around which every section's strategy was to be organised. A national doctrine became a world constitution, and dissent from it anywhere became dissent from the movement's founding document rather than from a foreign leadership's preference.

The critique from exile also identified, with a precision the later record vindicated, the programme's internal tension. A world party whose programme subordinated all sections to one section's state had no principled answer when that state's diplomacy required a section's destruction, as it had in China the year before the congress met. The critique's author named the Chinese catastrophe the programme's refutation in advance, written before the programme that refused to learn from it. The congress's response was organisational rather than argumentative: the critic was already exiled, his text was banned inside the movement, and the programme was adopted without a recorded debate on its central proposition. Historians of institutions will recognise the pattern, and it is the same pattern the two editions exhibit at the scale of a single book. At every scale, the doctrine advanced by the same method: the proposition was stated, the contrary evidence was excluded from the record, and the exclusion was then cited as consensus. Naming the method is not a substitute for assessing the proposition, which this article has done on its merits throughout, but a reference work that failed to name it would leave its readers unable to explain the doctrine's career, since the career is unintelligible as a history of persuasion.

## The trials: orthodoxy in the courtroom

The doctrine's darkest institutional hour came in the Moscow trials of 1936 to 1938, where the controversies of the nineteen twenties were restaged as criminal conspiracies and the old opposition's leaders confessed to crimes constructed from their former positions. The connection to the doctrine is direct and is usually missed in accounts that treat the trials as pure theatre of terror. The indictments' logic required the defendants' theoretical positions to be presented as the origin of their alleged treason: disbelief in the possibility of building socialism in one country was narrated as the first step on a road that led, by an inner necessity the prosecutors spelled out, to wrecking, espionage and alliance with the encircling powers. The doctrine thus became the courtroom's measure of loyalty, the proposition whose denial defined the enemy. Bukharin, the doctrine's own most serious theorist, was tried in the last of the great trials and executed, having defended in his final plea a version of the construction he had theorised while confessing to the fantastical charges the script required. The episode completes the doctrine's institutional biography with a terrible symmetry. A proposition that began as one side of an argument among revolutionaries, was converted into a condition of party membership, then into a world movement's constitution, ended as the premise of a judicial machinery that consumed, among hundreds of thousands of others, the proposition's own best mind. No assessment of the doctrine is complete that treats its consequences as confined to foreign policy. The hardening of an accommodation into a theory, and of a theory into a loyalty test, has a domestic terminus, and the terminus has names and dates.

### How did the trials use the doctrine?

As the definition of the enemy's origin. The prosecutions narrated the defendants' old theoretical positions, above all disbelief in building at home, as the seed from which treason necessarily grew. The doctrine thereby completed its passage from proposition to loyalty test: to have doubted it a decade earlier, in print, was presented as a conspiracy's first act.

It should be recorded, against any temptation to make the doctrine bear the whole weight of the terror, that the machinery of the trials had many sources: the regime's security paranoia, the dictator's personal rule, the habits of a party formed by civil war, and the genuine encirclement that the argument from circumstance had always cited. Doctrines do not operate purge machinery; men do, through institutions. What the doctrine supplied to the trials was the script's plausibility structure, the story in which a debating opponent of one season becomes a traitor in the next, and the supply of scripts is not a minor function in a terror, since terror at scale requires its executors and its audience to believe, or to be able to perform belief in, a narrative connecting the victims to the catastrophe alleged. The opposition's oldest charge against the doctrine was that it disabled the movement's capacity for self correction. The trials are that charge's most extreme verification: a movement that had made one proposition unquestionable found, when the proposition's custodians demanded victims, that it possessed no remaining procedure by which the demand could be refused.

## The thaw that spared the doctrine, and the reassessment that did not

When the Soviet leadership turned on its own past at the twentieth congress in 1956, the indictment fell on the cult of the leader and on the terror's violations of legality, and it stopped, conspicuously, at the threshold of the doctrines in whose name the terror had been scripted. It was not examined, because it could not be: the state whose legitimacy rested on the completed construction could not reopen the question of whether the construction's founding theory had been an improvisation, without reopening the question of what, exactly, had been built and by what right its builders ruled. The secret speech's silence on the doctrine is therefore one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the objection's domestic clause. A doctrine that functions as a regime's title deed cannot be revised by the regime, since revision would cloud the title. For three more decades the formula slept in the textbooks, unexamined and unrepealed, while the society it described changed around it. The reassessment came only when the regime itself was dissolving. In the glasnost years the Soviet press and the historical profession reopened the party's buried controversies in public: the opposition's platform was published, the two editions were discussed in print for a mass readership, Bukharin was rehabilitated in 1988 and his gradualist alternative debated as a road not taken, and the doctrine was argued about, for the first time in sixty years, as a proposition that might have been wrong. The argument arrived too late to matter to the state it concerned, which had three years to live. But it matters to the record, because it confirms the article's central distinction from the other side: the moment the doctrine could again be treated as an argument, it was the textual evidence, the editions and the passages, that the reopeners reached for first, the fixed points having outlasted the institution that suppressed them.

### Why did the reassessment reach for the texts first?

Because the texts were the only evidence not mediated by the institution being reassessed. Memoirs were interested, statistics were compiled by the regime, and the narrative histories were its own. Two printed editions and a dated article by the founder stood outside the custodians' power of retrospective revision, which is why every honest reopening begins where this article began.

The rehabilitation debates of the late nineteen eighties also produced the doctrine's last serious defence, and it is worth recording because it restated the argument from circumstance in the conditions of the state's collapse. Defenders of the historical record argued that the alternative roads romanticised in the reassessment, Bukharin's gradualism above all, had been foreclosed by the same circumstances that produced the doctrine: a hostile world, a fragile alliance, a defence burden no gradual tempo could carry into the nineteen thirties. The argument's persistence across sixty years and a complete change of regime confirms what this article noted of its Albanian afterlife. The argument from circumstance is not a period piece; it is a standing temptation and a standing resource for any isolated revolutionary state, and it will be made again wherever the conditions recur. Which is the final reason for the precision this article has practised. The next makers of the argument will also produce codifications, and their codifications will also claim the founders, and the readers who must assess them will need the habit of asking, before anything else, what the first edition said.

## On method: reading a doctrinal controversy

The article closes its analytical work with the method it has used, stated generally enough to be carried to other doctrines in this series. A doctrinal controversy inside a movement that holds power should be read in four passes. The first pass is bibliographical: establish the texts, their dates, their editions and their variants, before believing any participant's account of what was said, because the material record is the only part of the evidence no participant could later adjust. The second pass is semantic: fix what the controversy's key terms denoted in each text, since doctrines travel between questions by exploiting the drift of words like victory, and most quotation arguments dissolve when the denotations are tabled. The third pass is circumstantial: reconstruct the situation the doctrine answered, at full strength and in the participants' own terms, because a doctrine whose circumstance is caricatured cannot be assessed, only mocked or worshipped. The fourth pass is consequential: trace what the doctrine did, institutionally and in the field, distinguishing what it caused from what it legitimised and what merely coincided with it, and recording the concessions that each side's evidence forces. The present article has executed the four passes in order, and its conclusions are exactly as strong as the passes: certain on the texts, because the texts survive; divided on the circumstance, because counterfactuals are not documents; heavy on the consequences, because the record of the International is long and legible. A reader who applies the same four passes to the other doctrines of the tradition, to the Chinese formula that inherited this one's form, to the guerrilla theories and the parliamentary roads examined elsewhere in the series, will find that the passes sort most controversies into the same pattern: a small core of checkable fact, a real dilemma honestly faced by somebody, and a large penumbra of legitimation in which the fact and the dilemma were both consumed. The sorting is the skill. The doctrines are only its occasions.

## The precondition: a party that had banned factions

Doctrines are settled by institutions before they are settled by arguments, and the institution that settled this one had been redesigned, three years before the doctrine was stated, in a way that predetermined how all its future controversies would end. At the tenth party congress in March 1921, meeting in the shadow of the Kronstadt rising and the peasant revolts that forced the adoption of the New Economic Policy, the party adopted a resolution on party unity that banned organised factions, authorising the expulsion of members who formed groups with their own platforms and discipline. The resolution was presented as an emergency measure for a besieged party, and its secret seventh point, allowing the expulsion even of central committee members, was not published at the time. Whatever its necessity in 1921, the ban transformed the party's epistemic constitution. A controversy in a party with factions ends when one side persuades a majority that can afford to let the minority persist; a controversy in a party without factions ends when the minority is defined out of the party, since its continued organised existence is itself the offence. Every subsequent doctrinal settlement in the Soviet party, beginning with the one country controversy, followed the second logic. The opposition of 1927 was not defeated in a vote it accepted; it was expelled under a rule that made its organisation, not its doctrine, the crime, and the doctrine it opposed was then recorded as having prevailed by acclamation. Readers assessing the doctrine's triumph should therefore discount the triumph's form entirely. A proposition adopted under a faction ban has been ratified by a procedure that cannot register dissent, and its unanimity is evidence about the procedure, not about the proposition. The point generalises grimly: the same congress that adopted the economic retreat of the New Economic Policy adopted the political instrument by which the retreat's meaning would later be dictated, and the two adoptions together, market flexibility below and monolithic discipline above, describe the regime within which the doctrine was the natural ideology, flexible in everything except the right to disagree.

### Why does the faction ban matter to a glossary article?

Because it explains the form of the doctrine's victory, which readers otherwise mistake for evidence of the doctrine's truth. Under the ban, a controversy ended by expulsion rather than persuasion, and the resulting unanimity records the procedure's operation, not the proposition's merits. Knowing the mechanism prevents the commonest misreading of the period, in which settled doctrine is taken for agreed doctrine.

Kronstadt itself belongs in the background of the argument from circumstance, since the rising of the Baltic sailors, the revolution's own praetorians of 1917, demanding soviet democracy in 1921, was the regime's first intimation that isolation would deform it from inside as well as from outside. The opposition of the middle decade argued that the deformation had a logic: a besieged state breeds a besieged party, a besieged party breeds a bureaucracy, and a bureaucracy breeds a doctrine that sanctifies its national confinement. The doctrine's defenders answered that the deformation argument proved too much, since it implied that no revolutionary government could survive isolation without corruption, which made the taking of power in a backward country a mistake from the start, a conclusion the opposition's leader, who had organised that seizure's military side, could not accept. The exchange has no resolution, and it marks the deepest level of the controversy, below the texts and below the economics: whether October itself was premature. The formula was, among its other functions, the regime's answer to that unbearable question. To deny the doctrine was, by a short chain, to doubt the founding act; to affirm it was to declare the founding act sufficient unto itself. A party does not adjudicate such a question by counting quotations, and it did not. It adjudicated it by expulsion, and then it taught the result as a definition.

## Kautsky and the older orthodoxy: the doctrine's prehistory

The doctrine's central claim had been denied in advance by the Marxist orthodoxy of the prewar world, and recovering that denial clarifies what was new in 1924. Karl Kautsky, the leading theorist of the Second International, had argued in his writings on the Russian Revolution that a socialist transformation required the productive level and the culture of the advanced countries, and that Russia, attempting it in isolation, would produce not socialism but a new despotism resting on a peasantry it would eventually have to coerce. The Mensheviks inside Russia, and Martov above all, held the same position in a Russian idiom: the revolution had outrun the country's development, and its socialist tasks would have to wait upon Europe. The Bolsheviks answered these arguments for years with the theory of the weakest link and the expectation of the European revolution, which is to say they answered them by conceding the premise and denying its application to the seizure of power. The doctrine of 1924 broke with that answering strategy. Where the party had previously said that Russia could begin because Europe would follow, it now said that Russia could complete whether or not Europe followed, and the older orthodoxy's premise, that completion required the advanced countries, was reclassified from a shared assumption into a heresy. The codification's first printing stands exactly at the hinge: written while the old answering strategy still held, it repeats the premise; revised when the new strategy was adopted, it denies it. The two editions are thus not merely one author's embarrassment. They are the material record of a tradition changing its mind about its own founding problem, and doing so, characteristically, by denying that any change had occurred.

### What had the older orthodoxy held?

It had held that socialist construction required the productive and cultural level of the advanced countries, so that a revolution in a less developed country could begin a transformation it could not finish alone. The Bolsheviks originally answered by expecting the European revolution to supply the missing level; the doctrine answered by denying the level was missing, which is where the tradition's mind changed.

Kautsky's late writings on the Soviet experiment, dismissed inside the movement as the renegade's spite, repay reading in this connection for one specific reason. He predicted, from the premise of isolation, the sequence that followed: the coercion of the peasantry, the substitution of the apparatus for the class, the conversion of the party into a state bureaucracy, and the eventual need for a doctrine that would call the result socialism. A prediction is not a refutation, and Kautsky's own political record gave his opponents ample material for discounting him. But the controversy's assessment is incomplete if it records only the predictions made by the participants inside the party. The doctrine was opposed from three directions: from the left, by the permanent revolution's economics; from the older orthodoxy, by the argument from development; and from the liberal world, by the argument from liberty. The three oppositions agreed on little except the destination they foresaw, and their convergence, reached from incompatible premises, is a datum that any final assessment must weigh alongside the argument from circumstance, which remains, after all the predictions are tabled, the doctrine's one argument that its opponents never managed to answer on its own ground: the ground of the morning after, in a capital city of a ruined and encircled country, with a population waiting to be told what its sacrifices were for.

## The provinces: how the controversy reached the membership

The disputes of the nineteen twenties are usually written from the stenograms of the central bodies, where the arguments were most sophisticated. But the doctrine triumphed, or failed to, in thousands of local meetings where its content arrived in simplified forms, and the simplified forms are historically significant because they are what the doctrine meant to most of the people who held it. In the provincial party press and in the records of local discussions, the controversy appears as a contrast between building and waiting, between confidence in the country's own strength and a disparagement of it that could be represented as disloyalty to the revolution's own people. The opposition's economics, the world market's pressure, the distinction between completion and guarantee, survived the journey to the provinces badly; the doctrine's emotional core, that the Russian working class and peasantry were sufficient for the task history had set them, survived it perfectly. Participants in local discussions who supported the opposition often found themselves arguing, in the eyes of their audiences, against the dignity of the country's own labour, a position no platform could make attractive. The doctrine's victory in the membership was therefore overdetermined: it had the machinery, it had the procedure, and it had the only version of the question that could be carried in a sentence. There is a lesson here for readers of doctrinal controversies generally. The proposition that wins inside a mass party is rarely the proposition with the best arguments; it is the proposition whose simplified form flatters the audience's self understanding while its rival's simplified form insults it. Precision work, of the kind this article practises, is the attempt to recover the unsimplified propositions from beneath their victorious and defeated slogans, not in order to reverse the verdict of the meetings, but in order to know what the meetings actually decided, which was less, and more, than the record of their resolutions suggests.

### What did the doctrine mean to the ordinary member?

In its travelling form it meant that the country's own working people were sufficient for the construction, and that doubting the construction slighted them. The theoretical content, the distinctions and the quarrel over a founder's sentence, thinned with distance from the centre, while the emotional content, confidence against disparagement, arrived intact and decided votes the machinery had not already decided.

The Lenin Levy, the mass recruitment of workers into the party after the founder's death, supplied the audience for this travelling form on a scale that decided the controversy's sociology before its arguments were heard. The new members of 1924 and 1925, hundreds of thousands of them, encountered the party's history entirely through the codifications and the campaign literature, and for them the doctrine was not a reversal of anything, since they had never held the earlier position it reversed. Generational replacement is the quiet mechanism by which institutions change their minds while denying it, and the party of those years executed it at a speed few institutions match. The old oppositionists, arguing from texts the new members had not read and a history they had not lived, appeared to the Levy's recruits as a faction of the educated disputing the party's faith in its own class. That appearance was manufactured, but it was manufactured from real materials: real class resentments inside a workers' party being led, on all sides, by intellectuals. The doctrine's author understood the sociology better than his opponents did, and the understanding, more than any single argument, is why the formula and not its rival became the party's common sense. A glossary article records this without cynicism. Doctrines that answer a membership's need to believe in its own sufficiency will always be available, and some of them will be true. The assessment of any particular one still has to be made on its evidence, which is why the article began with two printed pages and a dated sentence, evidence that no sociology can dissolve.

## Completion and the theory of stages

One philosophical thread of the controversy has been left implicit and should be drawn out, because it connects the doctrine to the oldest stratum of Marxist theory. The classical texts had imagined the transition to socialism as a sequence with internal stages: a lower phase, marked by the defects of the old society that bore it, distributing according to work; and a higher phase, in which the division of labour's enslaving subordination had vanished and distribution could follow need. The Gotha critique's famous two phases were phases of a society, not of a country, and the classical imagination located them in the advanced world as a whole. The 1924 doctrine silently nationalised the stage theory: the phases became stages of one state's development, to be traversed within its borders on its own timetable, and the state acquired a philosophy of history with itself as the subject. The later Soviet category of developed socialism, and the Chinese doctrine of a primary stage lasting a century, are both descendants of this nationalisation, stage theories whose stages are national biographies. The opposition's objection, at this depth, was that a stage theory needs a subject capable of completing the stages, and that the world economy, not the national state, was the subject the classical theory had in view, since the productive forces whose maturation the stages track are organised globally and mock national timetables. The rejoinder available to the doctrine was the one it always used: the subject that existed was the state, and a theory whose subject does not exist is a theory of nothing. Between a subject that exists and may be inadequate, and a subject that is adequate and does not exist, the controversy at its deepest was a choice, and choices of that kind are not settled by exegesis. They are settled, when they are settled, by the history that follows, and the history that followed gave each side its evidence: the national subject built, endured an invasion, industrialised a continent sized country, and then, within three generations, dissolved, while the global subject in whose name the opposition argued never acquired an address. A glossary article is not required to call that a verdict. It is required to notice that both sides claimed history would judge, and that history, characteristically, judged in a syntax neither side had prepared for.

## The sentence in its article: a full reading

Because so much turns on one sentence, the sentence's home deserves a full reading, and a full reading strengthens the article's central finding rather than merely repeating it. Lenin's article of August 1915 addressed a proposal circulating in the socialist movement, that the war's outcome should be met with the slogan of a United States of Europe, a federation of the continent's states as the framework for peace and progress. Lenin rejected the slogan on two grounds. As a political proposal it was either reactionary, imagining a cartel of European powers for the joint suppression of socialism and the joint defence of their colonies, or empty, since a federation of capitalist states would leave the system's laws untouched. As an economic proposition it misunderstood the epoch: the world had been divided among the great powers, and development under imperialism proceeded unevenly, by leaps and seizures, not by the harmonious federation the slogan imagined. The famous sentence appears as the article's constructive residue. Uneven development being, in his phrase, an absolute law of capitalism, victory could arrive first in several countries or in a single one, the rest lagging for a time at the old stage. The sentence's grammar is the grammar of sequence: first here, then there, the lagging countries following after an interval. Its imagined scene is a world in motion, revolutions succeeding one another across borders, the advanced guard of the process being whichever country's contradictions ripened soonest. Nothing in the scene resembles the doctrine's later picture, in which one country completes an entire social formation behind its borders while the rest of the world remains, indefinitely, at the old stage. The interval in Lenin's sentence is a lag in a common process; the doctrine converted the lag into a permanent condition and the first arrival into a finished building. That conversion, from sequence to completion, is the whole of the textual question, and it can be seen in the sentence itself by any reader who attends to its verbs.

### What does the sentence's own grammar show?

It shows a claim about order, not about completion. The adverbs of sequence, first and alone, distribute revolutions across time among countries at different stages of one process. The doctrine required the sentence to certify that the process could be finished inside its first country, a claim about a different matter, which the sentence's grammar, context and author all decline to supply.

The article's other famous passage, on the slogan's colonial dimension, reinforces the reading. Lenin objected that a European federation of capitalist states would function as an agreement for the common plundering of colonies, and the objection shows the author's mind running on the world system's unity: states in his analysis act as parts of an imperial whole, their policies unintelligible in isolation. A writer thinking in those terms in 1915, about the interdependence of the advanced countries themselves, is an unlikely author for a doctrine of one country's self sufficient completion, and the unlikelihood is not a biographical guess but a structural feature of the text. The doctrine's defenders never engaged the article as a whole; they engaged the sentence. The history of the controversy in miniature is contained in that difference of scale, between a text read entire and a sentence read as a token, and the difference is available to any student who performs the experiment of reading the article, which takes twenty minutes. Few episodes in the tradition offer so favourable a ratio between the effort of verification and the size of the conclusion it supports, which is why teachers reach for this case, and why this article has placed it at its centre alongside the two editions.

## Building on the internal frontier

The doctrine's unit, the one country, contained its own frontier of unevenness, and the construction there tested the doctrine's logic in ways the European controversy never examined. In Central Asia, the revolution inherited a colonial periphery of the old empire: cotton monoculture imposed by the metropole, nomadic and semi nomadic pastoralism across the steppe, oasis agriculture under traditional elites, and a population whose languages, faith and social organisation the new state proposed to transform as part of the same construction it was conducting in the Russian heartland. The national delimitation of the middle nineteen twenties drew republics where none had existed, the campaigns for women's unveiling and literacy proceeded alongside collectivisation and the forced sedentarisation of the nomads, and each campaign was justified in the doctrine's language as the building of socialism in the one country whose territory included Tashkent as well as Moscow. The results were the doctrine's domestic pattern at higher concentration: real transformations in literacy, health and women's legal status, achieved together with catastrophic violence against the pastoral economy and the cultures organised around it, the Kazakh famine of the early nineteen thirties being proportionally among the worst of the Soviet famines. For the glossary's purposes, the frontier matters because it exposes the doctrine's unit as an artefact. The one country was not a society but a jurisdiction, and the construction's claim to be one process was an administrative decision before it was a social fact. The opposition's unanswered question returns in this setting with documentary force: if the completion of socialism in the Union required the violent integration of economies at wildly different levels, the integration the doctrine declared impossible between countries was being performed, at bayonet point, within one. The doctrine's answer, that a single state can compel what separate states cannot, is honest as far as it goes, and it reveals the doctrine's final premise, which its texts never state: that the unit of completion is not an economy but a coercive jurisdiction. Stated plainly, the premise is a considerable weakening of the claim, since a socialism whose completion is defined by the reach of one state's compulsion has changed the meaning of completion from a social condition to an administrative one. The glossary records the premise because the record implies it, and because later doctrines in the one country line inherited it unexamined.

## The culture of the construction

A doctrine that organises a state's life acquires a culture, and that culture is part of the evidence for what the doctrine meant to those who lived inside it. The first five year plan generated a literature and an iconography of building: the great construction sites at Magnitogorsk, where a steel city rose in the steppe, and at the Dnieper dam, where engineers and poets collaborated in the spectacle of a backward country seizing modernity by force of will. Shock workers and, from 1935, the Stakhanovites who multiplied output norms became the construction's saints, their portraits carrying the message that the doctrine's abstract claim, completion by the country's own efforts, had faces and names. Foreign visitors were toured through the sites and wrote the era's most contested travel literature, dividing between those who saw a civilisation being born and those who saw a facade over famine, and the division itself was a front in the controversy, since the doctrine's prestige abroad was one of the assets the International existed to defend. The culture matters to the assessment for one precise reason. The argument from circumstance had promised that a positive task would rally a population as a garrison mentality could not, and the construction culture is the evidence that the promise was, for a significant stratum of the young and the promoted, kept. The workers who rose through the plan's crash industrialisation into the technical intelligentsia, the generation that staffed the state for the rest of the century, experienced the doctrine not as a formula in a textbook but as the story of their own ascent, and their loyalty to it was biographical before it was theoretical. Historians who treat the doctrine as a cynical device have never explained this stratum's sincerity. Historians who treat the sincerity as the whole story have never explained the famine in the construction's own countryside. The article's separation of questions holds here too: the doctrine as legitimation, the doctrine as lived meaning, and the doctrine as proposition are three objects, and the culture of the construction belongs to the second, where its evidence is genuine and its verdicts are local.

### What does the construction culture prove?

It proves that the argument from circumstance described something real about motivation: a population offered a building to raise responded differently from a population offered a siege to endure, and a generation's loyalty formed in the raising. It does not prove the proposition about completion, and the culture's own products depict the building, never the completion, in their frames.

The culture also had a terminus that mirrors the doctrine's own. The construction's literary hero gave way, after the war, to the manager and the veteran, and the society the doctrine had promised to complete settled into describing itself with a new formula, developed socialism, whose function was to declare the building finished while changing nothing in the architecture. The posters stopped showing cranes. A reader tracing the doctrine's career through its images watches the same arc the texts describe: proposition, mobilisation, proclamation, presupposition. The arc is the normal biography of a successful doctrine in a state that monopolises meaning, and recognising its shape in the visual record is a useful check on the textual record, since images are harder to revise retrospectively than books, though the Soviet practice of retouching photographs shows that the custodians of this doctrine attempted even that. The two editions, by contrast, defeated their custodians simply by existing in libraries beyond the reach of the retouchers, in the stubborn plurality of print. There is a media lesson in the controversy that the series has reason to underline: print runs are a distributed archive, and a doctrine that wishes to revise itself must reckon with every copy it ever shipped. The doctrine's author shipped the first edition to a movement of millions. The reckoning has been going on ever since.

## Weighing the whole: the article's assessment restated

The assessment can now be gathered without collapsing its parts. The doctrine claimed that socialist construction could be completed in one country. Its textual warrant fails twice over, in the codifier's self contradiction and in the founder's sentence denoting another proposition, and the failure is of the permanent kind, since the documents are fixed. Its circumstantial warrant is strong: the isolation was real, the defeats were real, the invasions had happened and were expected again, and the alternative orientations carried costs that the doctrine's critics systematically underpriced. Its consequential record is the heaviest part of the ledger: the reordering of a world movement around a single state's security, predicted in print by its opponents, executed through machinery the doctrine legitimised, and terminating domestically in a loyalty test that consumed the doctrine's own theorist. Its philosophical residue is the nationalisation of the stage theory, the conversion of a world process into a state biography, a conversion whose descendants organised the self understanding of every state in its line. And its cultural record shows a doctrine that genuinely motivated a generation's labour while licensing that generation's rulers. No single adjective covers such an object, and the controversy's century of polemic is largely the history of attempts to force one adjective onto it: heresy, necessity, betrayal, common sense. The glossary's refusal of the adjective is its contribution. What the doctrine was, stated with the precision the evidence allows, is a proposition textually unfounded and circumstantially intelligible, whose adoption reorganised a world movement around a state's security and whose costs were predicted, in writing, before they were paid. Each clause of that sentence can be defended from the record this article has assembled, and the sentence as a whole is offered as the controversy's portable residue, the thing a reader should carry away when the details have faded.

## A concordance of victory: the founder's usage across the years

The semantic argument at the controversy's centre can be tested against the founder's usage as a whole, and the test is worth performing in the open because it converts an assertion about one passage into a finding about a corpus. In the military programme essay of 1916, Lenin returned to the theme of victory in one country and drew out its implications for war: a victorious proletariat in one country, having expropriated its capitalists and organised its own production, would stand against the rest of the capitalist world, attracting the sympathy of other countries' workers and facing the hostility of their states, and war in such conditions could be a revolutionary war in the strict sense. The passage imagines victory as a condition of armed coexistence and continuing struggle, a regime born of revolution holding its ground under threat, not a society that has completed its internal transformation in tranquillity. In the writings of the revolution's first years the pattern persists. The final victory of socialism, in the formulations of 1918 and after, is repeatedly tied to the scale of the international movement, to the combined efforts of the workers of the advanced countries, to a process whose Russian chapter is described as a beginning whose ending lies elsewhere. In the report on the tax in kind of 1921, the emphasis falls on the retreat's necessity and the long period of transition the country faces, with the international framework still doing the analytical work. Even the late writings on cooperation, the defence's favourite shelf, frame their optimism inside the assumption of a long breathing space whose ultimate guarantee is not named as national. A concordance is not an argument by counting, and single sentences can be found that lean the doctrine's way, as single sentences can be found for almost any position in a corpus of fifty volumes. But the distribution matters. Across a decade of usage, victory in one country denotes, with a consistency no honest reader has succeeded in disturbing, the seizure and holding of power by a revolution whose completion is an international process. The doctrine needed the phrase to denote the opposite distribution of weight, a national completion with an international residue, and it found the phrase's owner unavailable for comment. The concordance is the textual question's final form: not one sentence misread, but a usage inverted.

### What does the wider corpus add to the single passage?

It removes the defence that the passage might be an isolated ambiguity. The founder used the vocabulary of victory across a decade, in essays, reports and programmes, and the usage is stable: victory names a revolution in power, completion names an international process. The doctrine's reading requires the corpus to have meant, at its most quoted point, what it nowhere else says.

There is a methodological moral here that students of any founder centred tradition should extract. Arguments from a founder's authority are usually conducted as duels of quotation, each side producing sentences, and the duel format flatters the side with the better anthology rather than the better reading. The concordance format is slower and less dramatic: it asks what a term does across the whole body of work, in what company it appears, what questions it is used to answer, and it treats a single famous sentence as one observation among many. Applied to the present controversy, the concordance dissolves the duel. The doctrine's sentence remains genuine, and its meaning remains what the corpus says it is, and the doctrine's claim must then be what the two editions already forced it to be: a new position, answerable to circumstance and consequence, wearing an inherited vocabulary whose owner used it otherwise. Traditions survive such findings routinely, by acknowledging development and defending the new position as development. The tragedy of this case, if the word is allowed, is that the acknowledgment was the one move the doctrine's institutional position forbade, since a codification that admits development admits that the codifier is an author, and an author's authority is exactly what the whole apparatus existed to deny.

## What the doctrine did to the idea of revolution

Beneath the controversy's explicit questions sits a conceptual transformation that the participants felt more clearly than they stated, and a glossary article should state it. In the tradition's classical vocabulary, a revolution was an event and a process located in society: the transfer of power and the transformation of relations that followed, judged by the movement of classes. Under the doctrine's dispensation, revolution acquired a new grammatical property: it became an attribute of a state, something a state had had, embodied and could export, defend or betray. The shift is visible in the period's language, where the revolution increasingly appears with a capital letter and a possessive, as the property of the country that housed it, and where defending the revolution and defending the state's territory become interchangeable phrases. The consequences of the shift ramify through the century's politics. A revolution that is a state's attribute can be defended by the state's ordinary instruments, its diplomacy, its army and its police, and opposition to the state's policy can be classified as opposition to the revolution itself, the classification the trials later weaponised. A revolution that is a social process, by contrast, retains the awkward property of being able to judge its own state, to find the state wanting by the revolution's own standards, which is precisely the judgement the opposition existed to pronounce. The doctrine's deepest work was to abolish that awkwardness by definition. Once the construction in one country is the revolution's continuation and completion, the state conducting the construction is the revolution in institutional form, and the space from which it could be criticised in the revolution's name has been legislated away. Later generations of dissidents inside the socialist world, reaching for a language in which to criticise their states from within the tradition, repeatedly had to rebuild that space from fragments, from the founder's late writings, from the suppressed platforms, from the two editions themselves. The fragments held. It is not the least of the reasons for preserving them in a reference article, catalogued and exact, against the next occasion on which a construction announces that it is beyond appeal.

## The doctrine judged by its own criterion: the productivity test

The opposition's economic argument contained a prediction that the century eventually tested, and a complete assessment should record the test's result with the same care given to the textual one. The prediction was that a construction in isolation would face the world market's productivity as a daily pressure, that the gap between the isolated economy's technique and the advanced countries' technique would reproduce itself inside the construction as a permanent lag, and that the lag would eventually present itself politically as the system's inability to satisfy the expectations its own ideology had taught its citizens to hold. The Soviet record across seven decades reads, in this light, as a long experiment on the proposition. The extensive growth of the first decades, achieved by mobilising labour and resources on a continental scale, was real and carried the economy to the rank of the second industrial power. The intensive growth of the later decades, which required innovation, quality and the efficient use of information, faltered in a system whose insulation from the world market's signals had been a founding principle. By the nineteen eighties the lag in the technologies that defined the era's productivity frontier was the regime's own acknowledged crisis, stated in its leaders' speeches as the reason for restructuring. The doctrine's defenders reply that the experiment was contaminated from the start by the arms burden the encirclement imposed, that no conclusion about isolated construction can be drawn from a case in which the isolation was enforced by a hostile coalition's military pressure, and that the counterfactual, a Soviet Union integrated into the world economy, was never on offer from that coalition. The reply is the argument from circumstance in its final form, and it has the same strength and the same limit as the original: it explains the outcome without rescuing the proposition. The proposition was that completion was possible in one country. The experiment's most defensible reading is that a formidable industrialisation was possible, that a completed socialism, in the doctrine's own sense of a society whose superiority would demonstrate itself, was not achieved, and that the distinction between those two results is the distinction the doctrine existed to deny. Building was possible. Completion was the word that failed.

### Did the Soviet record refute the doctrine?

It refuted the doctrine's strong word and vindicated its weak one, which is why the assessment keeps the two apart. Industrialisation within one country proved possible on a scale nobody in 1924 had imagined. Completion, a self sustaining socialism whose performance would vindicate the claim, did not arrive, and the system's own last leadership said so in the language of stagnation.

There is an irony in the test's administration that the historiography has savoured and that a glossary may record without relish. The doctrine's criterion of success was, in the end, comparative: the construction would vindicate itself against the capitalist world by overtaking it, a promise the doctrine's successors repeated in the famous undertakings of the postwar decades to catch up and surpass the advanced economies in production per head. By adopting the comparison, the doctrine's heirs accepted the opposition's premise, that the isolated construction would be judged, daily and in the end, by the world market's standards, the very pressure the doctrine had declared manageable behind the monopoly of foreign trade. The citizens of the construction made the comparison themselves, through the goods they could see and the goods they could not obtain, and their verdict, delivered in the system's final years by the simple act of ceasing to defend it, is the productivity test's human form. None of this was inevitable in 1924, and the article's discipline forbids reading the ending back into the founding dilemma as though the dilemma's participants should have known it. What can be said, and what the record supports, is that the opposition's economic mechanism, the world market as a daily pressure rather than a border army, described the construction's actual long run difficulty better than the doctrine's own account of an internal process with an external guarantee. On the controversy's economic question, as on its textual one, the losing side's analysis aged better than the winning side's. On the circumstantial question, the founding dilemma, the verdict remains what it was: the doctrine answered a real question, and its answer's costs were the ones its opponents itemised in advance.

## The phrase as a byword: the controversy's later career

Outside the historiography, the doctrine survives as a phrase in the language, and the phrase's career is a small study in how controversies end. In the polemics of the later twentieth century, socialism in one country became a portable accusation, applied to any project suspected of subordinating a universal cause to a national container: to the national roads of the Eurocommunists by their critics on the left, to the autarkic experiments of various states by their neighbours, to trade union movements accused of defending their own members at the expense of workers elsewhere, and, in a final migration, to political projects outside the Marxist tradition altogether, wherever a universalist rhetoric was suspected of serving a national interest. The accusation's portability depends on forgetting the original's precision, since the doctrine in its exact form, completion claimed, guarantee reserved, warrant fabricated, is a specific object that few of the phrase's later targets resemble. Yet the migration is not pure loss. The phrase carries, even in its loosest uses, the memory of a real discovery the controversy made: that a universal doctrine can be nationalised by redescription, that the redescription can be accomplished by editing a textbook, and that the costs of the operation fall on those furthest from the centre where the editing is done. A reader who has followed the controversy in its exact form can use the phrase responsibly, as a question rather than a verdict: what is being redescribed here, whose textbook is being revised, and who is paying for the revision. The controversy's last gift to its students is that set of questions, and they have not dated.

## Questions the controversy leaves open

A reference article should end its analysis by inventorying what remains genuinely unsettled, since the appearance of total settlement is the codifier's trick and the surest sign that a treatment has been captured. Four questions remain open in the scholarship, and they are worth stating because each marks a place where new evidence or new argument could still move the assessment. The first concerns the revision's authorship in the narrow sense: the codification was produced with collaborators and editors, as all such works are, and the division of responsibility for the altered passage among the named author and his apparatus is a question the archives have illuminated without closing. The finding of this article does not depend on it, since the work was published under one name and defended by its bearer, but the biographical question is real and its answer would refine the portrait of how doctrine was manufactured. The second open question concerns reception at the base: how many of the party's members in 1925 knew that the textbook had changed, and how the change was explained in the local discussions where it was noticed. The provincial record is fragmentary, and the fragmentariness is itself a finding about the controversy's social depth, but the question stands for any historian willing to work the local archives. The third question is comparative: whether the conversion of an accommodation into a theory, which this article has treated as the doctrine's characteristic operation, is a general law of revolutionary governments in isolation, visible in other traditions and other centuries, or a specific product of this party's institutions and this founder's posthumous authority. The comparative literature is thin, and the question is the one most likely to reward future work, since the conditions that produce it have recurred. The fourth question is the counterfactual in its hardest form: not whether the opposition's policy would have succeeded, which is unknowable, but whether any leadership of the Soviet party in 1924, facing the same defeats and the same encirclement, could have avoided producing some version of the national doctrine, given what the party's membership needed to believe and what the state's survival required it to say. If the answer is no, the doctrine belongs to the history of necessities, and its author's contribution was the form, the fabricated warrant and the conversion into dogma, rather than the orientation itself. That distribution of responsibility, between a situation that demanded an answer and a man who supplied a dishonest one, is where the most careful recent scholarship has tended to settle, and it is a distribution this article's evidence supports. The two editions remain the dishonesty's document. The circumstance remains the necessity's. The controversy endures because both are true, and because every generation that inherits an isolated revolution, or imagines one, must decide for itself how much of the doctrine it is prepared to repeat.

## The state as the subject of socialism

The doctrine carried an implication for the theory of the state that its participants sensed and its successors inherited without examining, and the glossary should make it explicit. Classical Marxism treated the state as an instrument and an epiphenomenon: the committee of a ruling class, destined to wither as classes disappeared, and never the subject of the historical process in its own right. In practice the doctrine reversed the polarity. In its construction, the state is the builder: it plans, accumulates, educates, defends, and its activity is the form the working class's historical mission takes for the whole period of the construction, which is to say indefinitely. The class recedes into the state's predicates, the workers and peasants whose friendly cooperation the constitution of 1936 celebrated, while the state acquires the biography, the stages and the destiny. Later theory inside the tradition registered the reversal uneasily. The debates over whether the Soviet formation was socialist, a degenerated workers' state, state capitalist or a new bureaucratic collectivism were, at bottom, attempts to classify a society in which the state had become the subject, using a vocabulary designed for societies in which it had not. Each classification was also a verdict on the doctrine, since the doctrine was the theory under which the state had assumed the role. The state capitalist reading held that the doctrine's completion claim concealed a new form of class rule administered by the state itself. The degenerated workers' state reading held that the property forms remained a conquest while the political regime betrayed them, a distinction impossible to state without the doctrine's own separation of the construction from its guarantee. The bureaucratic collectivist reading held that a new class society had arisen for which the old vocabulary, and the doctrine with it, was simply obsolete. The taxonomy wars consumed the energies of the doctrine's critics for two generations, and they are best understood as the controversy's continuation by other means: the question of 1924, what can be completed inside one state, returning as the question of what had in fact been completed there. A glossary article takes no side in the taxonomy, but it owes its readers the map, since the doctrine cannot be understood apart from the classificatory crisis it bequeathed. The series treats the rival classifications in their own articles; here it is enough to mark that all of them are footnotes to a formula announced in a revised textbook in 1924.

### Why did the doctrine make the state the subject?

Because a construction needs a builder, and within one country's borders the only agent capable of planning, accumulating and defending at the required scale was the state. The doctrine therefore transferred the historical mission, in practice, from the class to its state, and the transfer governed how the resulting society could be described, defended and criticised thereafter.

The transfer also explains the doctrine's otherwise puzzling longevity as a legitimation. A theory that makes the state the subject of socialism makes every question about the state a question about socialism's fate, and therefore makes the state's custodians the custodians of the meaning of socialism itself. To ask whether the construction was complete was to ask the state; to ask whether the state served the construction was to use criteria the state supplied. The circle is the doctrine's perfected form, and the opposition's insistence on an external standpoint, the international movement, the world market, the founder's texts read entire, was an insistence on breaking the circle at some point where an independent measurement could be taken. The two editions matter, one final time, because they are a measurement the circle could not absorb: a printed contradiction, outside the custodians' present control, against which the claim of faithful transmission can be checked by anyone. Doctrines that make a state their subject fear exactly such external points, and the energy this one spent on erasing its own first edition is the measure of how well it understood the danger.

## The controversy restaged: the postwar debates

The generation after the war restaged the controversy in a new idiom, and the restaging is worth a brief account because it shows the questions surviving their original personnel. Within the Trotskyist movement, the doctrine's assessment divided those who held that the Soviet Union remained a conquest to be defended, on the founder of the opposition's own double judgement, from those who concluded that the bureaucracy's rule, legitimised by the doctrine, had created a new exploitative formation requiring a new revolution. Ernest Mandel's writings gave the first position its most systematic postwar form, arguing the economics of the transitional epoch with the doctrine's categories turned against the doctrine's heirs. Isaac Deutscher, standing outside the movement's organisations, wrote the controversy's great narrative trilogy on its opposition leader and its single volume on the doctrine's author, and his balanced severity, his insistence that the doctrine answered a real dilemma with a ruinous formula, set the terms in which the educated public of several countries understood the dispute. The New Left of the nineteen sixties, encountering the controversy through these mediations, drew from it a general suspicion of codified doctrine that shaped its own allergy to programmes, an inheritance as ambiguous as any other in this history. Meanwhile the doctrine's state, in its postwar textbooks, continued to teach the controversy as a chronology of deviations, unaware or affecting unawareness that the argument it had buried was being conducted, in a dozen languages, by readers who had never belonged to it. The asymmetry is the controversy's final institutional lesson. A doctrine that wins by custody must be rewon in every generation by the same custody, while an argument that loses by custody needs only readers. The readers kept arriving.

## The evidence locker: the documents in order

The controversy's whole documentary basis can be held in a short shelf, and a glossary article should describe the shelf before closing, so that a reader who wishes to verify the argument knows exactly what to ask a library for. The shelf begins with the founder's article on the European slogan, printed in the party's émigré press in August 1915, a few pages long, containing the sentence on which the doctrine's warrant was later built and the argument about uneven development from which the sentence draws its sense. Beside it stands the military programme essay of the following year, where the founder works out what victory in one country would mean for the victorious state's relations with the world, in terms of war and defence rather than of completed construction. The third item is the codification's first edition, printed in the spring of 1924 from lectures delivered that April and May, containing the passage that denies the doctrine in the movement's own textbook voice. The fourth item is the same work revised, printed before the year ended, in which the passage asserts the doctrine and the denial has vanished. The fifth item is the December article of 1924, in which the doctrine is launched as a polemic against permanent revolution and the founder's sentence is set to its new work. The sixth item is the record of the party conference of April 1925, whose resolution converts the polemic into the party's official position. The seventh is the programme of the Communist International adopted in 1928, which writes the doctrine's priority into the world movement's constitution. The eighth is the constitution of 1936, which proclaims the doctrine fulfilled. The ninth is the Short Course of 1938, which teaches the whole sequence as a history without seams. Nine items, most of them short, all of them printed, dated and consultable, containing between them the doctrine's birth, warrant, triumph, fulfilment and canonisation. Few controversies of comparable consequence rest on so compact and so checkable a record, and the compactness is a standing rebuke to the literature's grander manner. A reader who has worked the shelf possesses the controversy in a way that no summary, including this article, can confer, and the article's final service is to insist that the shelf, and not the article, is the authority.

### Why insist on the documents over the summaries?

Because every summary in this controversy was written by a custodian with an interest in the record's shape. The documents are the only participants that cannot revise themselves, with the single instructive exception of the codification, whose self revision is the central exhibit. Verification against the shelf is the reader's one reliable defence against custody, reference articles included.

One property of the shelf deserves emphasis as the analysis closes. The documents do not merely support the article's findings; they explain the controversy's peculiar emotional register, which newcomers often find disproportionate to a dispute over definitions. The participants were not arguing about words in the abstract. They were arguing in a movement where the printed record was the constitution, where a textbook paragraph could define the loyalty of millions, and where the alteration of such a paragraph, silently, in the year of the founder's death, was an act of state in miniature. The anger in the polemics is the anger of men watching a constitution being amended by erasure. Recovering that register is part of understanding the doctrine, because the doctrine's own nature, a proposition that ruled by being printed as settled, is unintelligible in a culture that treats texts as opinions. In the world the doctrine governed, texts were law, editions were events, and a glossary that fixes what the texts said is not a pedantic exercise but a constitutional history. This article has tried to be worthy of that description, and it leaves the shelf, as it found it, open.

## The nationalities credential: why this author held the pen

A question the controversy rarely pauses over is why the party's codifier, of all its leaders, was positioned to pronounce on the relation between a revolution and a country. The answer lies a decade before the doctrine, in the author's one substantial theoretical work of the prewar years: the essay of 1913 on the national question, written under the founder's encouragement and published under the name the Georgian seminarian turned revolutionary had taken up not long before. That essay defined a nation by a checklist of attributes, territory, language, economic life and psychological make up among them, and it made its author's reputation inside the movement as the specialist on nationality. When the revolution came he became the first head of the commissariat charged with the nationalities of the former empire, and he presided over the federal architecture within which the one country of the later doctrine was assembled. The credential matters because the doctrine's plausibility inside the party rested partly on it. A proposition about what a single country could contain and complete carried further from the man who had defined what a nation was, administered the federation's peoples, and negotiated the union treaty, than it would have carried from a military organiser or an economist. His authority on the national form of the state was genuine, earned in a portfolio the other contenders for the succession had not held, and it silently underwrote the doctrine's central move, the treatment of the Union as one society with one biography. Readers who know the credential can see the doctrine's authorship as something other than an accident of the succession struggle. The party's expert on nations became, in the decade of the doctrine, the theorist of the nation's sufficiency, and the continuity between the two roles is one of the controversy's underappreciated threads.

### Why did the author's earlier career matter to the doctrine's reception?

Because he was the party's recognised specialist on nationality, the author of its standard prewar essay on the national question and the first administrator of its federation. A claim about what one country could hold and finish sounded different from the man who had defined the units and run the commissariat, and the credential lent the formula an authority its textual warrant could not supply.

The 1913 essay also contains, in embryo, a tension the doctrine would later have to suppress. Its definition of a nation is historicist and demanding: a nation is a historically constituted community, formed through a long process, and not every population qualifies. Applied strictly, the definition sits awkwardly beside the later treatment of the Union as a single country whose peoples formed one society for the purposes of construction, since the Union's peoples were, on the essay's own criteria, at wildly different points in the formation the definition describes. The doctrine never revisited the tension, and its author's two theoretical selves, the careful taxonomist of 1913 and the codifier of 1924, were never required to meet in print. The meeting can be staged by a reader, and staging it is instructive, because it shows the same pattern as the two editions at a longer wavelength: a position held when the question was theoretical, quietly abandoned when the question became governmental, with the abandonment nowhere acknowledged. The pattern, repeated across a career, is the strongest biographical evidence that the revision of 1924 was not an aberration but a method, the author's characteristic way of moving between positions while conserving the appearance of a single unbroken doctrine. Biographical evidence cannot settle a proposition's truth, and this article has not asked it to. It can explain a proposition's career, and the career of this one, from the nationalities portfolio to the codification to the proclamation of fulfilment, is unintelligible without the man who carried it and the method he brought to every text he touched.

## The publishing empire: how the revised text reached the world

The doctrine's victory was also a feat of distribution, and the distribution deserves a section because it explains how completely the revised version monopolised the movement's reading. The Communist International maintained a publishing apparatus without precedent among political movements: publishing houses in the major capitals, a press service supplying articles to the parties' newspapers, translation bureaux, and a network of bookshops and study circles through which approved texts reached members in dozens of languages. The codification of Leninism travelled this network as a set text. In language after language, the edition prepared for translation was the revised one, since the apparatus worked from the Moscow text of record, and the superseded spring printing existed only in the Russian copies that had reached readers before the revision and in the libraries that had filed them. A communist in Lyon, in Buenos Aires or in Calcutta who studied the foundations of the movement studied the doctrine in its second form, and the first form was not merely unpersuasive to him but physically absent from his world. The opposition's counter literature travelled the same world by opposite means, in suitcases and through small independent publishers, reaching a fraction of the readership at a multiple of the effort. Controversies are often described as though arguments meet in a common space and the stronger prevails. The material record of this one describes something else: two literatures with incomparable means of circulation, one of them revised at the source before export, meeting in a space the stronger apparatus had already furnished. None of this bears on the doctrine's truth. All of it bears on the doctrine's history, and on the naivety of any account that treats the outcome as the verdict of a debate freely held.

The apparatus also standardised the controversy's memory abroad. The parties' newspapers reported the Soviet discussions in the centre's summaries, the opposition's documents were characterised rather than printed, and the expulsions of 1927 were communicated to the sections as accomplished facts accompanied by the victors' explanations. A member expelled in Berlin or New York for adherence to the opposition typically encountered the charges against his tendency in his own party's press before he encountered the tendency's platform, if he ever did. The doctrine's international triumph was thus achieved substantially before it was argued, in the flow of paper from a centre that controlled both the texts and the news of the texts. Later historians, reconstructing the controversy from the centre's publications, have had to correct for this furnishing of the record at every step, and the correction is one reason the documentary exhibits this article has privileged, editions, dates, printings, matter so much: they are the residue that the apparatus could not retrospectively arrange, because it had already shipped them.

## The archive of the defeated

Against the publishing empire stands a quieter institution that decided what later generations could know: the archive of the defeated. The opposition's leader, exiled from the Soviet Union in 1929 and murdered in Mexico in 1940, sold his papers to Harvard University in the last year of his life, judging, correctly, that a university library in Massachusetts was the safest address available for the record of a movement being annihilated. The collection, opened to scholars after a long closure, contains the correspondence, drafts and internal documents of the opposition's last decade, and it transformed the controversy's historiography when it became available, because it allowed the losing side's arguments to be studied from its own files rather than from its enemies' summaries. Other collections travelled other roads: the papers gathered by the Menshevik historian Boris Nicolaevsky, assembled through a network of exiles and smuggled documents, came to rest at the Hoover Institution, and the archive of the Soviet party itself, captured in part at Smolensk by two armies in succession, gave Western scholars their first sight of the regime's internal paperwork. The first edition of the codification survived by the same dispersed logic, in the legal deposit copies of national libraries and the holdings of universities that had bought Soviet publications in the nineteen twenties as a matter of collection policy, indifferent to the use a later controversy would make of them. There is a general truth here that this series has met in other connections. Regimes that revise their texts depend on the centralisation of the record; scholarship depends on its dispersal. Every copy of a superseded edition lodged beyond the reviser's reach is a small defeat for custody, and the sum of those defeats is the reason a controversy conducted by expulsion can be reopened, generations later, as a controversy conducted by evidence.

### How did the losing side's record survive?

By emigration and by accident: an exile's papers sold to a distant university, an émigré historian's hoard carried across three capitals, a provincial party archive captured in war, and the deposit copies of superseded books resting in libraries that had collected them without polemical intent. The survival was nobody's plan, which is precisely why the custodians of the official record could not prevent it.

The archive's opening also changed the controversy's balance of genres. While the record was closed, the dispute was conducted as a duel of memoirs and official histories, each side's narrative answerable mainly to the other side's. With the files open, the dispute could be conducted as history, with claims checked against documents whose provenance neither side controlled. The two editions were the controversy's first instalment of that better genre, available all along to anyone with library access, and the profession's slowness to make them central says something about the profession as well as about the parties. Dramatic archives announce themselves; a variant reading in a textbook waits quietly for a reader who thinks to compare printings. The bibliographical turn in the controversy's study, when it came, came from scholars who had learned to treat the movement's printed output as a material record rather than as a transparent window on its beliefs. That lesson, learned on this doctrine, has since been applied across the tradition this series covers, to congress proceedings, to journal runs, to the successive editions of every codification that claimed merely to transmit. A reader who finishes this article with one habit should finish with that one: when a movement's text matters, ask for its printing history before asking for its meaning, because the printing history is frequently where the meaning was decided.

The dispersal that preserved the record had a geography worth noticing. The papers survived in Massachusetts and California, in the deposit libraries of London, Paris and Tokyo, in the catalogues of antiquarian dealers who handled émigré imprints without grasping their charge, and in the private shelves of old members who kept the pamphlets of their youth through every subsequent disillusion. No ministry planned the pattern and no conspiracy arranged it; it is simply what happens to printed matter in a world of many jurisdictions, where paper crosses borders in luggage and bequests and settles wherever a shelf receives it. The custodians in Moscow commanded an empire of print inside their own frontiers and commanded nothing outside them, and the controversy's afterlife was decided in the gap between those two sovereignties. Every later reassessment, from the thaw's hesitant disclosures to the open arguments of the Soviet collapse, drew on materials that had waited out the custodians in foreign stacks. The point is not antiquarian. It is the material basis of intellectual freedom in a documentary tradition: a text that exists in ten thousand copies in fifty countries cannot be unwritten, and a movement that prints its doctrines by the million has, by that very act, surrendered the power to recall them. The codifier of 1924 shipped his spring edition to a world that kept it. His winter revision has been answering for it ever since.

## The doctrine and the peasant majority: the alliance as the domestic stake

Every abstraction in this controversy had a domestic address, and the address was the village. Four fifths of the Soviet population were peasants, farming small plots with wooden ploughs in tens of thousands of communes, and any construction, at any tempo, under any formula, would be built substantially out of their grain, their labour and their patience. The doctrine's domestic meaning was therefore decided by a question it never directly posed: on what terms would the village consent to feed a construction whose benefits would reach it last? The alliance between the working class and the peasantry, the smychka of the period's vocabulary, was the name for the consent while it lasted, and the controversy's factions were, in one of their aspects, rival theories of the alliance's durability. The gradualist reading held that the alliance was the construction's foundation and its tempo's limit, that the village would sell its grain and join the cooperatives if the terms respected its interests, and that a construction paced by consent could proceed for decades. The opposition's reading held that the alliance concealed an extraction that industrialisation would force into the open, and that the state would face a choice between slowing the building to the village's pace and breaking the village to the building's pace. The doctrine, characteristically, declined to choose in advance. It affirmed the construction and the alliance in the same breath, as though the affirmation settled their compatibility, and the affirmation held the party together through the middle of the decade precisely because each faction could hear its own tempo in the formula. When the grain crisis forced the choice at the decade's end, the formula survived the alliance it had declined to price, and the building proceeded over the village's resistance. A glossary that omitted the village would leave the doctrine floating in the realm of pure text, and the text is only half its story. The other half is a hundred million smallholders discovering, across a decade, what the completion of socialism within their country's borders would be permitted to cost them.

### Why does the village decide the doctrine's domestic meaning?

Because the construction's resources were the village's surplus, and the terms on which that surplus moved, by consent through the market or by compulsion through requisition, determined what the building was as a social fact. The factions divided over the terms before they divided over anything else, and the formula's refusal to price the alliance is what allowed it to govern both the alliance's preservation and its destruction.

The village also supplies the controversy's most reliable clock. The texts can be dated by their printings, but the doctrine's phases are dated by the harvests: the recovery harvests that made gradualism plausible, the shortfall that made the emergency measures thinkable, the collectivised harvests gathered by a state at war with its own countryside. Participants felt the clock even when they argued about sentences. A debate over the founder's meaning conducted in a capital that bread queues were forming in was never only a debate over meaning, and the participants' urgency, their willingness to settle by expulsion what could not be settled by argument, reads differently once the queues are restored to the picture. The argument from circumstance, which this article has treated as the doctrine's serious core, was in its daily form an argument from the harvest, and its force rose and fell with the grain collections. When later readers ask why the party of 1927 could not afford the opposition's patience, the answer is partly in the polemics and partly in the procurement figures, and an assessment that consults only the polemics will mistake a famine's approach for a faction's manoeuvre. The doctrine fed on that confusion then, and feeds on it still wherever its history is told as a duel of quotations. Restoring the village to the account is the surest way to starve it.

## The vernacular translations: the doctrine in other parties' idioms

A doctrine exported to dozens of parties does not travel in its original wording. It is translated twice, once into the local language and once into the local political idiom, and the second translation is where its meaning multiplies. The American party of the nineteen thirties offers the purest specimen. Its leader, Earl Browder, popularised the formula that communism was twentieth century Americanism, presenting the movement as the heir of the country's own revolutionary and democratic traditions, of Jefferson and Lincoln as much as of the founders of Marxism. The slogan was the one country logic in vernacular dress: the universal doctrine was to be realised through a national inheritance, argued in the nation's own vocabulary, and legitimated by the nation's own history. Browder's party grew in those years as it never had before, and the growth was inseparable from the vernacular turn. The same pattern, with local variations, ran through the French party's embrace of the national tradition during the popular front, when its leader held out a hand to Catholic and nationalist France in the name of a communism rooted in French soil, and through the British party's wartime discovery of a people's war fought in a national key. In each case the party gained room to move inside its own society, and in each case the room was rented from the doctrine's central permission: that a national road was not a deviation. The rent came due in the currency the objection had named. When the centre's requirements reversed, as they did with the pact of 1939, the parties that had taught their members to think nationally had to teach them, overnight, that the national interest as they had defined it was an error, and the whiplash damaged their credibility in ways the more frankly internationalist rhetoric of an earlier period would have spared them. Browder's own end completes the specimen: expelled in 1946 for extending the vernacular logic beyond the centre's tolerance, he learned that the permission to nationalise the doctrine was, like the permission the centre later gave its satellite states, revocable by the issuer.

### What did the vernacular translations add to the doctrine?

They added proof of its portability and a new register of its costs. Rendered into national idioms, the formula let parties grow inside their own societies as heirs of national traditions. The same rendering left them exposed when the centre reversed its line, since a party that had taught its members to reason nationally could not easily explain why the national reasoning was suddenly impermissible.

The vernacular episode also refines the objection in a way its original proponents did not foresee. They had imagined the doctrine's international cost as subordination, parties reduced to instruments, and the image suggests passivity. The vernacular record shows something more active and more melancholy: parties eagerly translating the doctrine into their own traditions, investing it with their own histories, building real movements on its permission, and thereby binding themselves more thoroughly than any instruction could have bound them. An instrument does not translate its orders into poetry. The parties did, and the poetry was theirs, which is why the reversals hurt as they did and why the survivors of those years so often described their experience in the language of a faith betrayed rather than a discipline imposed. The doctrine's deepest international effect was not that it commanded the parties but that it taught them what to want: a socialism at home, in their own country's colours, under the protection of the one state where the building had begun. Wants so taught outlast the commands that taught them, and the national colourings of the communist tradition persisted in its successor movements long after the centre that issued the permission had ceased to exist. The glossary's interest in the episode is taxonomic as much as historical. It shows the doctrine completing a circuit its author never described: a formula devised to legitimate one state's construction became, by translation, the charter of every party's national self understanding, and the universalism it displaced survived mainly as the memory of a time when the movement's members had been asked to want something larger and harder than a country.

## A companion case in custody: the suppressed testament

The spring and winter printings of the codification were not the only documents of those years whose circulation was managed, and the parallel case sharpens the reader's sense of the period's textual politics. In the last weeks of his active life the founder dictated a letter to the forthcoming party congress, assessing the leading figures of the succession and recommending, among other things, the removal of the party's general secretary on grounds of rudeness and disloyalty of temper. The letter was read to the delegations of the thirteenth congress in closed session, under conditions that prevented its publication, and it then vanished from the movement's printed record for three decades, surfacing abroad through the opposition's channels and acknowledged at home only in the thaw of the nineteen fifties. Set beside the codification affair, the testament's fate shows the same machinery working in the opposite direction. There, a text was altered between printings so that a new position could wear an old authority. Here, a text was withheld from printing so that an old authority could not be heard against a new position. Alteration and suppression are the two hands of custody, and the party of the nineteen twenties used both within a single year, on the two documents that mattered most to the succession: what the founder had said about the future, and what his codifier had said about the past. A student who grasps the pairing grasps the period. The controversy's arguments were conducted in speeches and articles, but its outcomes were decided in the print shop and the archive, by decisions about which pages would exist for the membership and which would not. That is why a reference treatment of a doctrine from those years must be, among its other things, a history of paper: of editions and their variants, of letters and their seals, of the long shelf life of a paragraph and the short one of a testament. The ideas were real, and this article has weighed them as ideas throughout. But they travelled in objects, and the objects were guarded.

### What does the testament's fate add to the picture?

It shows the codification affair as one instance of a general practice rather than an isolated embarrassment. In the same season, one document was rewritten between printings to lend a new position an inherited authority, while another was sealed away to keep an inherited authority from condemning a new position. Custody of texts, exercised in both directions, was the period's decisive political technology.

The pairing has one more service to perform for the reader of this controversy. It explains the peculiar intensity with which the participants fought over bibliography, an intensity that puzzles newcomers who expect political struggles to be waged over budgets and armies. In a movement whose legitimacy was documentary, whose founder ruled from the grave through his collected works, and whose every faction legitimised itself by quotation, control of the record was not a preliminary to power but a form of it. The men who revised a textbook paragraph and sealed a testament understood their own system perfectly: a party that governs in a dead man's name will be governed, in turn, by whoever administers the dead man's papers. The doctrine examined in this article was the beneficiary of that administration, and its long reign in the textbooks was the administration's masterpiece. When the papers were finally opened, two generations later, the masterpiece was among the first casualties, since an argument that had depended on a managed record could not survive an unmanaged one. The lesson travels well beyond its decade, to any institution that keeps its founding texts under lock and its editions under revision, and it is the fitting close to a glossary article whose whole method has been to go back to the printings and read what they actually say.

## For students and exam candidates

Your task with this doctrine is separation. Keep three questions apart in every answer you write: the textual question, where the verdict is clear and documentary; the circumstantial question, where the argument from circumstance deserves full marks when stated at full strength; and the consequential question, where the International's record is the evidence. Answers that collapse the three into a single verdict, favourable or hostile, read to an examiner as allegiance rather than analysis. Lead with the two editions, since a checkable fact in the first paragraph tells the marker that the script knows where the ground is solid. State the strongest objection before your own judgement, and carry the concession the record forces: the international consequence was real and was foreseen. The series guide for the wider tradition will help you place the doctrine among its neighbours, and for timed practice on the examination questions this material attracts, the UPSC previous year question explorer at [ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) lets you rehearse against real papers rather than invented ones.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The two editions are the most efficient teaching object in this entire tradition. A single seminar can be built from them alone: distribute the two passages without attribution, ask the group to date and place them, and let the discovery that one author published both within a year do the work that lectures on codification rarely achieve. Follow it with the founder's short article read entire, all twenty minutes of it, and set the group to find the sentence the doctrine used and to state, in one line each, what the sentence is about. The exercise teaches the transferable skill, reading a warrant before honouring it, better than any methodological preamble. For seminar planning and for keeping the textual question, the table and the variant record organised across a course, the study notes companion at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) provides a structured place to hold the materials this article assembles. Close the sequence by staging the objection and the answer as a formal debate, with the concession assigned to the defence as a required move, since students remember the concession longer than the verdict.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The open questions inventoried in this article mark the live edges of the subject: the revision's division of labour inside the codifier's apparatus, the reception of the change in the provincial party, the comparative question of whether accommodation hardens into theory wherever revolutionary governments are isolated, and the counterfactual distribution of responsibility between situation and author. The provincial reception is the most tractable of the four and the least worked, and local discussion records, where they survive, would repay the attention that the central stenograms have monopolised. Journalists covering the afterlife of the doctrine in later national roads will find the discipline of the evidence locker useful: nine documents, dated and consultable, against which any new claim about the doctrine's meaning can be checked in an afternoon. The comparison with the displaced position belongs with the article on permanent revolution, and researchers pursuing the opposition's economics should begin there and with the exile literature's mature statement, read as a participant's brief whose quotations, unusually, can be verified against the shelf this article describes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the doctrine of socialism in one country claim?

The doctrine claims that the Soviet Union could finish building a socialist society by its own efforts, with social ownership established and the exploiting classes abolished, and that this finishing did not have to wait for revolutions in the advanced countries. Its careful statements added a reservation: final victory, meaning security against restoration by foreign intervention, would still depend on revolutions in several countries. The strong claim concerned the building; the reservation concerned the guarantee.

### Q: Did the doctrine's author change his position?

He did, and the evidence is bibliographical rather than testimonial. The lectures on the foundations of Leninism, published in the spring of 1924, told readers that a single country could not organise socialist production by its own efforts and that victory demanded several countries acting together. Before the year was out, a new edition of the same lectures told them the reverse, presenting the building of socialism within the Soviet Union as both possible and obligatory. No preface announced the change, and the superseded text survived only in the copies already in circulation.

### Q: Did Lenin support socialism in one country?

The claim that he did rests on one sentence from an article of 1915, written against the slogan of a United States of Europe, where he argued that under uneven development a socialist victory could come first in several countries or in one alone. In that setting victory meant a revolution taking power, which nobody in the later dispute denied. His writings across the following years repeatedly tied the revolution's fate to the movement abroad, and he died before the question was ever posed in the sharp form the doctrine later gave it.

### Q: What does victory mean in the disputed passage?

In the article where the sentence appears, victory means the success of a revolution: a working class that has taken power in its country and holds it while others follow. Lenin is distributing revolutions across time, arguing against the idea that they must arrive everywhere at once. The doctrine needed the word to certify something else, the finishing of an entire socialist construction inside the first country, and that is a meaning the sentence's setting, its grammar and its author's usage elsewhere will not support.

### Q: What is the argument from circumstance?

It is the doctrine's defence from the situation rather than from the texts. The revolutions expected in Europe had failed, the Soviet state was isolated, invaded within living memory and encircled by hostile powers, and its population needed a positive task. On this argument, a government in that position must either build what it can within its own borders, with the resources it commands, or organise its existence around an external event it cannot schedule, rotting in suspension while it waits.

### Q: Who opposed socialism in one country?

Inside the party, the opposition was built around Trotsky and the theory of permanent revolution, and from 1926 it took organised form as the United Opposition with Zinoviev and Kamenev. Its case was that an isolated construction would be ground between the world market and the peasant economy, and that a movement reordered around one state's needs would be corrupted together with the state. Outside the party, the older social democratic orthodoxy opposed the doctrine on developmental grounds, holding that a backward country lacked what completion required.

### Q: What were the consequences for the international movement?

The movement's centre of gravity moved from the revolutions it hoped to make to the state it felt bound to protect. Congress resolutions recast the defence of the Soviet Union as each section's overriding duty, and the sections' strategies, their alliances, their timing and at times their survival, were adjusted to the requirements of Soviet diplomacy. The sequence runs from the enforced alliance in China and its catastrophic end, through the popular fronts of the nineteen thirties, to the quiet winding up of the International itself in 1943, when its existence had become a diplomatic inconvenience to its own centre.

### Q: Is the textual case for the doctrine strong?

It fails at both of its supports. The work that codified the doctrine for the movement printed the contrary position first and the doctrine second, inside a single year, so its authority cancels itself. The passage of Lenin produced as the doctrine's warrant speaks of a revolution winning power in one country, which every participant in the dispute accepted, and offers no sentence about completing a socialist construction there. What remains after both supports are removed is not a damaged textual case but no textual case, which is why the argument from circumstance carries the whole weight of the defence.

### Q: Did the doctrine shape Soviet foreign policy?

It shaped the terms in which foreign policy was justified to the movement, and through those terms it shaped the movement's behaviour. A diplomacy of trade agreements, collective security and, later, peaceful coexistence could be presented to communist parties not as a state's ordinary pursuit of advantage but as service to the one homeland of the construction, whose safety outranked their local prospects. The state's interests would have been pursued in any event; the doctrine determined that the pursuit would be experienced by the movement as duty rather than as compromise.

### Q: Does the socialism in one country dispute still matter?

It matters wherever a universal doctrine is asked to live inside a national container, because the dispute isolated the operation by which the fitting is done: an emergency accommodation redescribed as a theory, a founder's sentence transferred to a question it did not address, and a textbook revised to make the transfer invisible. The same operation recurs in later national roads and in arguments far from the tradition that produced it. The dispute also preserves a rare asset, a controversy whose central facts are short, printed and checkable, which makes it a standing exercise in reading warrants before honouring them.

### Q: How do the two editions grade the doctrine's textual authority?

They grade it at zero, by the author's own hand. A work whose claim on readers is faithful transmission cannot certify a proposition it began by denying, and the grading is precise about what it does and does not show. It does not show the revised position to be false. It shows the revised position to be unauthorised, a new doctrine wearing an inheritance, which must then stand or fall on the argument from circumstance and on the record of its consequences.

### Q: What should a student separate between an emergency accommodation and a theory?

The student should separate a practice from a claim. A practice adopted under pressure is provisional, answerable to results, and expires with the emergency. A claim about what is true does not expire; it is taught, examined and enforced, and it travels to situations its authors never faced. The doctrine's history is the conversion of a practice into a claim, and nearly every confusion in the literature comes from grading the claim by the practice's excuses or the practice by the claim's ambitions.

### Q: Why did the doctrine's author reverse his position within a single year?

The sequence points to the polemic. In the spring of 1924 the printed position was the party's common property. In the winter, the campaign against Trotsky defined Leninism against permanent revolution, and the definition adopted was belief in building socialism at home. The textbook was revised to match the campaign, silently and at the contested passage. A genuine reconsideration forced by governing cannot be ruled out as a contributing cause, but the timing and the manner of the change identify it as a polemical act in the first instance.

### Q: How does the textual question table order the evidence?

The table arranges the four relevant texts in the order a reader meets the problem and interrogates each with the same three questions. It asks what the text says, what the text means by its decisive word, and what follows from it. Put through that interrogation, the codification's two editions contradict one another, the founder's passage answers a question about beginnings, and the doctrine stands at the end of the row with its warrant exhausted, which is the article's central finding in miniature.

### Q: What separates defending the doctrine from circumstance from defending it from the text?

The two defences differ in their evidence and in their fate. Defence from circumstance points to a matter of record, an isolated state ringed by enemies after the failure of the European revolutions, and argues that building at home was the only positive course available. Defence from the text points to authorities, and the authorities refuse the errand: one contradicts himself between editions, the other is speaking of something else. The first defence can be answered but not dissolved; the second dissolves on inspection.
