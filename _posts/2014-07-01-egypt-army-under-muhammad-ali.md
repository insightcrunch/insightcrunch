How did an Ottoman provincial governor build the strongest military machine in the Middle East out of peasant farmers? Muhammad Ali's army is the answer, and it is one of the most remarkable military creations of the nineteenth century. Between 1822 and 1840 the ruler of Egypt conscripted tens of thousands of fellahin, drilled them in European tactics under French officers, armed them with cannon cast in Egyptian foundries, and launched them on campaigns that conquered Sudan, Arabia, and Syria and twice shattered the armies of the Ottoman sultan himself. Then, at the peak of his power, the great European navies intervened, and the force that had humbled an empire was cut down to 18,000 men.

This article explains how Egypt fought under Muhammad Ali, what decided its battles, and why the army that made him great was also the reason Europe united to stop him. It traces the failure of the old mercenary system, the coercive machinery of peasant conscription, the French training that turned farmers into soldiers, the arsenals and the fleet at Alexandria, the campaigns in sequence, and the 1840 settlement that amputated the force in exchange for a hereditary dynasty. Readers coming from the broader story of [Muhammad Ali and the birth of modern Egypt](/2014/03/01/muhammad-ali-and-modern-egypt/) will find here the military engine behind that transformation, examined with the evidence stated plainly and the legends corrected.

## Why the Old Army Had to Die: Mercenaries, Mutiny, and the Lesson of 1815

When Muhammad Ali took effective power in Egypt in 1805, he commanded no army that could be called his own in any institutional sense. What he had was a coalition of armed bands: Albanian bashi-bazouks, the irregular infantry left over from the Ottoman expeditionary force that had helped expel the French, and the Mamluk beys with their household troops and retinues. These were the same categories of fighters who had been clawing at each other across Egypt since 1801. Their loyalty ran to a paymaster and a patron, not to a state, and certainly not to Egypt as a political community. A commander of such forces did not so much govern a country as rent it, season by season, by keeping the men fed and paid before they decided to feed themselves at the population's expense.

These irregulars did not simply fight for money; they ate the countryside as a matter of system. Ottoman practice in Egypt had long quartered soldiery on villages and towns, and commanders treated the right to exact supplies, lodging, and cash from the peasantry as part of the bargain of service. A band that went unpaid did not starve in its barracks; it levied itself on the nearest village at sword point. The result was a military system that punished the very population it was supposed to defend. The fellahin paid twice, once in the taxes that were supposed to fund the troops and again in the exactions of the troops when the taxes failed to arrive. Muhammad Ali understood this economy of plunder from the inside, because his own Albanians practiced it. The countryside of the Delta and Middle Egypt in the first years of his rule was not governed so much as grazed by competing armed bands, and every village headman learned to bargain with whichever armed men happened to be passing.

The pattern was established well before Muhammad Ali became wali. In 1803 the Albanian troops mutinied over unpaid wages, seized the Cairo Citadel, and forced their Ottoman paymasters out of the city; their leader Tahir Pasha was soon assassinated by other unpaid soldiery who then burned and plundered his residence. The lesson was plain to anyone watching: whichever faction could promise these men their arrears owned Cairo for the moment, and no longer. Muhammad Ali watched, waited, and positioned himself. He allied with the ulama and the merchant notables of Cairo, whose shops stood shuttered and whose streets were barricaded whenever the soldiery went unpaid and began looting. When the people of Cairo demanded his appointment as governor in 1805, it was not because he commanded a disciplined force but because he appeared, amid the competing bands of Albanians, Turks, and Mamluks, to be the one man who could restrain them. That appearance was a fiction, and he knew it. His own base was the Albanian irregulars. The very mutinies that had destroyed his rivals could destroy him just as easily the moment his treasury ran dry.

The famous Citadel massacre of March 1811 is usually told as the act that made Muhammad Ali master of Egypt. It destroyed the Mamluk leadership in a single night of slaughter: the beys were invited to a ceremonial departure at the Citadel and cut down as they filed through its gate, and their followers were hunted through Cairo in the days that followed. The survivors fled south to Dongola in the Sudan. The elite he destroyed that night is profiled in the article on [the Mamluk beys of Ottoman Egypt](/2013/09/01/mamluk-beys-of-ottoman-egypt/). As a political operation it was complete. Yet it solved only half of the problem, and arguably the easier half. The Mamluks had been a rival faction; they had not been the structural disease. The disease was that Egypt's armed force was composed of mercenaries whose obedience lasted exactly as long as the money held out. Removing the Mamluk beys merely removed one set of competing paymasters. The Albanian soldiers who now formed the bulk of Muhammad Ali's garrison were cut from the same cloth as the men he had just destroyed, and they would prove it within four years.

The test arrived from an unexpected direction. In 1811 Sultan Mahmud II ordered Muhammad Ali to crush the Wahhabi movement in Arabia, whose fighters had taken Mecca and Medina and thereby humiliated the sultan as custodian of the holy cities. The commission was a mark of confidence, but it was also a trap: obedience would drain the treasury and test the army, and disobedience would confirm the sultan's suspicions of his too-ambitious governor. Muhammad Ali sent an expeditionary force under his son Tusun Pasha, some 8,000 to 14,000 men by the varying accounts, which landed at Yanbu on the Red Sea coast in October 1811. The landing itself went smoothly enough. Then, in December, the column pushed inland toward Medina through the pass of Wadi al-Safra and walked into an ambush. Wahhabi cavalry tore the Ottoman-Egyptian force apart; thousands were killed, artillery was lost, and the survivors fled back to Yanbu. Medina was eventually taken after a long siege in November 1812, and Mecca and Jiddah followed in early 1813, but the pattern of the campaign had been set at al-Safra: an army of irregulars, brave in a raid and ferocious in plunder, broke down when asked to conduct a disciplined campaign far from its bases, across terrain its soldiers did not know, against an enemy that refused set-piece battle.

The campaign also taught a fiscal lesson. Moving an army across the Red Sea and supplying it in the Hijaz consumed money at a rate Egypt's irregular fiscal system could barely sustain, and the irregulars' habit of living off the country they occupied turned the local population against them and complicated every supply problem. Muhammad Ali was paying a premium for an army that fought badly at distance, and every piastre spent in Arabia was a piastre not available to keep his Cairo garrison quiet. The economics of mercenary warfare made distant campaigns ruinously expensive and domestic peace permanently hostage to the treasury. A standing army, paid regularly and supplied from depots, would have been cheaper in the long run than the irregulars he was forced to keep buying, and the pasha, who was nothing if not a calculating steward of revenue, could do the arithmetic.

Muhammad Ali went to Arabia himself in 1813 to repair the damage, and after grinding warfare and a negotiated settlement with the Wahhabi leadership in 1815 he returned to Egypt. He came home in a hurry. News had reached him that Napoleon had escaped from Elba, and he feared, with reason, that the sultan might use the European crisis as cover to strip him of Egypt while his best troops were still across the Red Sea. What he found on his return in June 1815 confirmed his deepest anxiety about the instrument he had been wielding. His Albanian troops, many of whom had followed him to Arabia, came home to discover that his new agricultural reforms had gutted the tax farms they had acquired, and they demanded long-delayed arrears of pay. Muhammad Ali decided that this was the moment to impose order by force. He gathered the Albanians in Maydan al-Rumayla, below the Citadel, put them through volley firing in the French manner, and announced his intention to place their pay and service under an organized principle, with European-style drill, uniforms, and discipline.

The experiment collapsed within days. The soldiers went through the motions for a single day, then conspired to assassinate him the following night. Warned in time, Muhammad Ali slipped away to the Citadel, and the foiled mutineers poured into the streets of Cairo, looting shops and homes for days until he pacified the merchants by paying compensation out of his own funds. The verdict of 1815 was sharper than the verdict of al-Safra. The Albanians could not be reformed because their grievance was not merely about drill or uniforms; it was about the very nature of their service. They were mercenaries who had signed on for pay, privilege, and the right to live off the country, and no amount of parade-ground training could convert that bargain into soldierly obedience. The man who had ridden their mutinies to power could not discipline them into an army. To try to drill them was to threaten the terms of their service, and to threaten those terms was to invite a knife in the dark.

Here is the causal chain that runs from 1805 to 1815. Muhammad Ali did not want to be a provincial governor subject to recall from Istanbul; his confiscation of tax farms, his monopolies, his Arabian campaigns, and his defiance of rival appointees all point in one direction, toward a ruler building a hereditary power base independent of the sultan's pleasure. A ruler with that ambition needed an instrument that his ambition could trust: troops who would obey orders on distant campaigns, who would not dissolve into looting mobs when pay was late, and who could not simply be bought away by a rival with deeper pockets. Everything he had learned in a decade pointed the other way. The Citadel massacre had removed his rivals but left the mercenary system untouched. The Arabian campaign had shown that irregulars could take a port and seize a holy city but could not be relied upon in hard, sustained warfare. The 1815 mutiny had shown that the Albanians would kill him rather than submit to discipline. The old army, in short, could keep him in Cairo and could conquer a port across the Red Sea, but it could not be the foundation of the durable, independent power he intended to build. The structural problem was not the men he had killed. It was the men he had kept.

The lesson took a decade to become inescapable, but by 1815 there was no other reading available. A mercenary army had three properties that no reform could remove: it could be bought, it could abandon its post the moment a better offer appeared, and it regarded discipline as an insult to its status as a free contractor of violence. The Citadel massacre had removed the Mamluk buyers. The Arabian war had exposed the operational limits of the Albanian sellers. The 1815 mutiny had proven that the bargain itself could not be rewritten. For Muhammad Ali, who meant to found a dynasty and not merely hold an office, there was now only one road open: to stop renting soldiers and start manufacturing them, which meant finding a body of men who could not negotiate the terms of their service at all. That search would take him first to the Sudan, and then, when the Sudan failed him, to the Egyptian peasantry.

## How Muhammad Ali's Army Was Built: The Conscription of the Fellahin

The decision to abandon the mercenary system did not immediately produce a plan to conscript Egyptian peasants. Muhammad Ali first tried a substitute that fit the logic of everything he knew: a slave-soldier army on the mamluk model, raised from captives, loyal to no patron but himself, and trained by European instructors to fight in the European manner. The conquest of the Sudan, launched in 1820 under his son Ismail, was undertaken partly with this purpose in mind. Thousands of captives were marched north to a training camp at Aswan, where a French officer of Napoleon's former army, Colonel Joseph Seve, later Suleiman Pasha, began drilling them in European tactics. For a brief moment this looked like the solution: an army with no local ties, no village to desert to, and no political price to pay for obedience.

It failed catastrophically, and the cause was biology as much as policy. The captives died in appalling numbers, first on the march north and then in the training camps, where unfamiliar climate and disease cut them down faster than drillmasters could replace them. Of the roughly 20,000 captives gathered for the army over four years, current scholarly consensus holds that only about 3,000 remained alive by 1824. The experiment consumed a fortune in lives and treasure and produced nothing that could take the field. Muhammad Ali had discovered that a slave army was no answer at all if the slaves could not survive to become soldiers.

The training camps at Aswan and Farshut, first built for those Sudanese captives, became the laboratory where the new army was manufactured. Seve and his fellow Frenchmen imposed European infantry drill: the manual of arms, volley firing, bayonet work, and parade-ground discipline, translated through Turkish-speaking officers and Mamluk sergeants into commands the Arabic-speaking recruits could barely understand. Punishment was immediate and severe, modeled on the discipline of the French revolutionary armies, with flogging for slackness, the bastinado for insubordination, and death for desertion. It was an unlikely alchemy, French drill manuals, Turkish commands, and Egyptian fellahin, yet it worked after a fashion. Within a few years the new regiments could march in column, fire by volley, and hold a line, which was precisely what the Albanian irregulars of 1815 had refused to learn and what the Wahhabi campaign had shown to be necessary.

It was this failure that drove him, in 1822 and 1823, to the Egyptian peasantry itself, a step no Egyptian ruler had taken for centuries. As the Sudan campaign unraveled, Muhammad Ali ordered the conscription of around 4,000 men from Upper Egypt. He justified the choice bluntly: the Turks, he wrote, were members of his own race and must be spared the trouble of being sent to remote and dangerous areas, so Egyptians would be taken in their place. The first conscripts were promised three years of service and stamped certificates of discharge at the end of it. They were sent to the training camps at Aswan and Farshut, where French officers drilled them while an officer corps of the pasha's own Mamluks, chief among them his son Ibrahim, learned the new system from above. From this nucleus the army grew with astonishing speed, estimated by scholars at roughly 100,000 to 130,000 men within a little more than a decade.

### Why did Muhammad Ali conscript Egyptian peasants instead of hiring mercenaries?

He had run out of alternatives. The Sudanese slave experiment collapsed, with most captives dead before they could serve, and the Albanian and Turkish irregulars had mutinied so often that reforming them was impossible. The peasantry was the only large, captive population available, so he seized it.

The machinery of the levy was coercion at every joint, and the Ottoman archives show how little it resembled any voluntary enlistment. Egypt had no census, no medical boards, and no rules about which men could be taken: the conscripting officers dispatched from Cairo carried quotas for each village and, on arriving, seized whatever men they could lay hands on, without any order, arrangement, inscription, or lot drawing. The taken men were tied together with ropes around their necks in groups of six or eight and marched to the training camps in files. The village headmen became the essential gears of the system: they were held responsible for producing the quota, which gave them enormous power to decide whose sons went and whose stayed, and to sell exemptions. Chained recruits shuffling through the countryside under guard became one of the signature sights of Muhammad Ali's Egypt, and the three-year terms promised in 1822 were routinely extended into years of service that amounted to a life sentence for many.

The peasantry answered with every form of resistance available to people with no political voice. Men fled to the towns or into the desert when the levy officers approached. Some blinded themselves or knocked out their own teeth to fail the physical examination, a practice of self-mutilation documented across the conscription system that testifies to the desperation it produced. Families paid bribes they could not afford; whole villages concealed their young men or watched the quota fall on the poorest households, those without protectors. When resistance turned collective, as it did in several peasant revolts, the response was military repression, and the rebellions were broken without changing the system. Desertion from the ranks became a permanent condition of the army, an endless war between the recruiting sergeants and the villages that consumed administrative energy for decades.

The levy did not only take men; it hollowed out the villages they left. A village that surrendered its quota lost its strongest field laborers in the very years when Muhammad Ali's state was demanding more labor than ever for canals, dams, and corvee. The burden fell back on women, children, and the old, and on the remaining men who had to cover the tax quota of a community now missing its producers. The pasha's administration answered the resulting shortfalls with collective punishment: villages that failed to produce their quota were fined, and headmen who could not deliver recruits were imprisoned or beaten. Service, meanwhile, proved nothing like the promised three years. Discharges were delayed, terms were extended by decree, and men seized as youths in the early 1820s were still in uniform when the Syrian campaigns ended. The stamped certificate became a document that most recruits never lived to carry home.

Above the conscripted rank and file rose an officer corps that had almost nothing in common with the men it commanded. The officers were largely Turkish-speaking, drawn from Muhammad Ali's own Mamluk household and from his family circle, with Ibrahim Pasha, his eldest surviving son, as the exemplary commander. The drill, the tactics, and the organization were European, supplied by French and other European officers left adrift by the Napoleonic wars, with Seve, now Suleiman Pasha, as the chief architect of the new model army. The result was a force divided by language and origin: Arabic-speaking fellahin in the ranks, Turkish-speaking officers above them, and European doctrine imposed on both. It fought like a modern army anyway. The new troops were blooded in the Morea in Greece from 1825, and in the 1830s they shattered Ottoman armies in Syria, first under Ibrahim in 1831 to 1833 and again in 1839, victories that made Muhammad Ali the terror of the sultan's ministers and the arbiter of the Eastern Question. None of this would have been conceivable with the Albanian irregulars of 1805.

This is the peasant-army paradox, and it sits at the center of Muhammad Ali's reign. He built the first modern, European-style standing army in the Arab Middle East out of the very population his other policies were systematically impoverishing. The same fellahin who were conscripted into the ranks were the ones crushed by his agricultural monopolies, which made him the sole buyer and seller of Egypt's cash crops at prices he dictated; they were the ones dragooned into corvee labor on his irrigation canals and factories; they were the ones taxed to pay for the army that seized their sons. The fellahin were the one large reservoir of bodies that could not sail home to Albania, could not appeal to the sultan, and could not melt into a foreign garrison. They could only run, and the machinery was built to catch them.

The paradox is that the army worked as an instrument of power and worked, simultaneously, as a grinding burden on the country that sustained it. Without the conscript army there is no independent Egypt under Muhammad Ali, no victory in Syria, no dynasty, and no modern Egyptian military tradition; the army became the nursery of the officer class that would dominate Egyptian politics for a century and more. Yet the cost was extracted from the peasantry in bodies, in labor, and in taxes, year after year, and the coercive apparatus built to feed the ranks, the quotas, the headmen, the ropes, the hunting of deserters, became a permanent feature of the Egyptian state's relationship with its rural population. Muhammad Ali had solved the problem of 1815. The mercenaries were gone, replaced by soldiers who drilled, marched, and obeyed. He had solved it by conscripting the poorest subjects of his realm into a machine that made him great and ground them down, and the greatness and the grinding were the same fact.

## Training the Fellahin: French Officers and the New Order

Conscription alone never won a battle. Muhammad Ali understood this from the start, and the proof arrived with his early experiments in recruiting Egyptian peasants. Men dragged from the fields of the Delta and the valley could fill ranks on paper, but a column of frightened farmers carrying mismatched muskets was not an army. It could not maneuver under fire, it could not hold a line, and it could not be trusted to remain on a battlefield once the artillery opened. The difference between a levy and a weapon was training, and Muhammad Ali set out to build that weapon with a thoroughness that no other Ottoman provincial ruler ever attempted. The institution he created was called the Nizam al-Jadid, the "new order," a name borrowed from the reforming vocabulary of the Ottoman Empire's own military experiments, but pursued in Egypt with a persistence that eventually outstripped the original.

The instrument of this transformation was foreign expertise. Muhammad Ali recruited European military instructors, and the most consequential among them was a French officer named Joseph Seve. Seve arrived in Egypt seeking employment after the Napoleonic Wars had left many French officers without a career. He entered the pasha's service, converted to Islam, and took the name Suleiman Pasha, by which history remembers him. He became the chief architect of the new Egyptian army. Around him gathered a collection of other European adventurers and officers: French, Italian, and other instructors who had served in the Napoleonic armies and now sold their knowledge to the highest bidder in the Mediterranean. Some were excellent soldiers. Others were mercenaries of questionable reputation. Muhammad Ali was not particular. He wanted men who could drill troops in the European fashion, and he was willing to tolerate the rogues among them so long as they produced soldiers.

The site chosen for the training effort reveals the pasha's understanding of the problem. The new army's principal camp and training ground was established at Aswan, far up the Nile, some nine hundred kilometers from Cairo. This was deliberate. The first Egyptian recruits were peasants who had been seized from their villages by press gangs, and their first instinct was to flee back to their families and their fields. At Aswan, surrounded by desert, hundreds of kilometers from home, desertion became far harder. A man who ran from the camp ran into empty country with no water, no food, and no hiding place. The location also kept the experiment at a remove from the political intrigues of the capital and from the hostile gaze of the established Turkish military establishment in Cairo, which watched the rise of the new force with suspicion. Aswan became, in effect, a sealed laboratory in which the fellahin could be remade.

What happened inside that laboratory was the systematic application of European military drill. The recruits were organized into battalions, regiments, and brigades on the French model. They were taught to march in step, to form line and column, to handle their muskets through the standardized manual of arms, to load and fire in volleys, and to execute the evolutions of the battlefield: advancing, wheeling, changing front, and forming square against cavalry. Commands were standardized. In the early years, with officers and instructors who spoke French, Italian, or Turkish, the language of command on the parade ground was a hybrid affair, and considerable effort went into fixing a stable Turkish terminology of drill that Egyptian soldiers could memorize and respond to automatically. The point of all this repetition was to produce men who would react to orders without thinking, because thought collapses under artillery fire and only drilled reflex survives it.

Uniforms were part of the same machinery. The new troops were dressed in standardized European-style uniforms, with trousers, jackets, caps, and eventually the tarboush that would become emblematic of the Egyptian army. This was not vanity. Uniform dress erased the visible distinctions between men from different villages and districts, marked the soldier as the property of the state rather than of any local notable, and accustomed him to cleanliness, order, and the outward signs of discipline. A man who must keep his uniform brushed and his equipment polished has begun to internalize the habit of obedience. The fellahin, who had arrived at Aswan in rags or in the loose galabeyas of the countryside, were cut, shaved, and redressed as soldiers, and the transformation of appearance preceded and reinforced the transformation of conduct.

Discipline was severe, and it is not possible to describe the Nizam al-Jadid honestly without saying so. The instructors and officers enforced obedience with the lash, with bastinado, and with executions for desertion and mutiny. The peasant conscript served because he had no choice, and the system made sure he understood the costs of defiance. Modern readers sometimes ask whether the European methods alone produced the army, and the answer is that they did not: they were applied through coercion of a kind that would have been familiar to any army of the period, including the European ones, but sharpened by the fact that these men had not volunteered. The discipline worked. Desertion remained a constant problem and a constant drain, but enough men were held in the ranks, drilled long enough, that battalions emerged which could stand and fight.

The officer question was the most politically charged part of the whole enterprise. In the early years of the Nizam al-Jadid, the officers who commanded Egyptian soldiers were overwhelmingly not Egyptian. They were Turkish and Circassian officers drawn from the pasha's own military household and from the Ottoman military tradition, together with the European instructors who had been commissioned into the new force. The rank and file were Egyptian fellahin; the men who gave them orders, promoted or punished them, and drew the higher pay and prestige were foreigners in their own country. There were reasons for this arrangement that went beyond prejudice. An army needed literate officers who could read manuals, keep rolls, and understand artillery mathematics, and the Egyptian peasantry had been deliberately kept illiterate for centuries. The Turkish officers supplied the administrative and linguistic continuity with the Ottoman world. The Europeans supplied the technical knowledge. But the arrangement stored up a grievance. Egyptian soldiers saw men of their own blood doing the dying while men of another language did the commanding, and Egyptian officers were admitted to command only slowly, reluctantly, and usually at junior levels. This hierarchy would matter enormously in later decades, when the sons of the fellahin who had been drilled at Aswan began to demand the commissions that the system had promised but withheld. The resentment planted in the 1820s would flower, a half century later, in the nationalist revolt of the Egyptian officer corps.

Supporting the army required institutions that Egypt had never possessed. A modern force needed literate officers, so Muhammad Ali founded military schools. The staff college and the artillery and infantry schools at Cairo and elsewhere taught mathematics, fortification, gunnery, and the reading of maps. Cadets were drawn increasingly from Egyptian families as the system matured, because the pasha understood that an army permanently commanded by foreigners was a political vulnerability. A modern force also needed surgeons, because an army that cannot treat its wounded cannot campaign far from home. The medical school founded at Abu Zaabal, under the French physician Antoine Clot, known as Clot Bey, trained Egyptian doctors and surgeons who followed the army into the field and staffed its hospitals. The school at Abu Zaabal was among the first modern medical institutions in the Arab world, and its graduates kept alive soldiers who in earlier Egyptian armies would have died of their wounds or of camp disease. Disease killed more soldiers than bullets in every army of this era, and the pasha's investment in military medicine was a cold calculation about manpower as much as it was a humanitarian gesture.

None of this could have worked without translation. The European manuals of drill, artillery, fortification, and administration existed in French, Italian, and English. Egyptian officers and cadets could not read them. Muhammad Ali therefore sponsored a sustained program of translation, centered on the schools and later on the government press at Bulaq, which rendered European military texts into Turkish and Arabic. Translators, many of them Syrians and Armenians and European converts in the pasha's service, produced versions of the French infantry regulations, artillery manuals, and works on engineering and military administration. This was unglamorous work, and it rarely appears in the heroic narratives of the period, but it was the channel through which two centuries of European military knowledge flowed into Egyptian heads. An army cannot drill from manuals it cannot read, and the translation program was the unglamorous precondition of everything done on the parade ground at Aswan.

The causal claim is straightforward and deserves to be stated plainly. Conscription gave Muhammad Ali men. Training gave him soldiers. Without the years of drill at Aswan, the schools, the translated manuals, and the enforced discipline, the Egyptian army would have remained what every previous Egyptian levy had been: a crowd with weapons, capable of plundering villages and intimidating civilians, but useless against a formed enemy. With that training, it became something the Ottoman Empire had not produced in generations: a provincial army that could take the field against Ottoman regulars and beat them. The victories of the 1830s, when Egyptian armies under Ibrahim Pasha shattered Ottoman forces in Syria and drove to the gates of Anatolia, were not the product of numbers alone. The Ottomans could raise numbers. They were the product of an army whose infantry could stand in line and deliver volley fire, whose artillery was served by trained gunners, and whose officers, whatever their origins, knew how to maneuver a division. That capability was manufactured at Aswan and in the schools of Cairo in the 1820s, out of peasant conscripts whom the military wisdom of the age had written off as unusable material. The fellahin, it turned out, learned drill as well as any French conscript. They only needed to be taught.

### What made the Egyptian infantry better than Ottoman infantry?

Years of continuous drill rather than any advantage in courage. Egyptian battalions had been trained to form line, fire by volley, and hold position under artillery for as long as it took; Ottoman units raised hastily after the Janissaries were destroyed had officers promoted by favour and conscripts with little training.


## Guns, Factories, and Ships: Arming the New Army

An army without weapons is a parade. Muhammad Ali knew that trained men were only half the problem, because every musket, every cannon, and every round of powder his soldiers used had to come from somewhere. In the early years it came from Europe, purchased at high prices from merchants and governments that were happy to sell arms to a rising power in the eastern Mediterranean but could cut off supply at any moment. Dependence on foreign arms dealers was a strategic vulnerability that the pasha found intolerable. His answer was characteristic: if Egypt needed weapons, Egypt would make them. He set out to build a military industry from nothing, and the arsenals, foundries, and workshops that rose around Cairo in the 1820s were among the most ambitious industrial projects attempted anywhere outside Europe at the time.

The center of this effort was the Cairo citadel complex and its surrounding workshops. Cannon foundries were established to cast artillery pieces for the field armies and the fortresses. Small-arms manufactories produced muskets for the infantry. Powder mills ground out gunpowder. Workshops turned out cartridges, shot, shell, uniforms, saddlery, and the thousand other articles an army consumes. European technicians supervised the work, because Egypt had no native tradition of modern arms manufacture, and skilled founders, gunsmiths, and machinists were imported at considerable expense. Egyptian workmen were trained under them, and over the years a class of Egyptian artisans learned the trades of the arsenal. The output was real: by the mid-1820s the Egyptian arsenals were supplying the army's campaigns in the Morea and could equip the tens of thousands of men under arms. But honest accounting requires the admission that Egyptian-made weapons rarely matched European quality. Cannon burst. Muskets misfired. Powder was inconsistent. The metallurgy, the precision of boring and fitting, and the quality control of a French or British arms works could not be reproduced overnight by an industry built in a decade, and Egyptian ordnance remained inferior to the best European products throughout Muhammad Ali's reign. It was good enough to beat Ottoman armies, which suffered from the same deficiencies and worse, but it was never good enough to face a European army on equal terms.

Paying for all of this required money on a scale that the old Egyptian fiscal system could not provide. Muhammad Ali's solution was the state monopoly system. He made the government the sole buyer of Egypt's agricultural produce, particularly cotton, and the sole seller on the export market, capturing the margin between the price paid to the peasant and the price realized abroad. He extended monopolies over other crops, over manufactures, and over urban trades, squeezing revenue out of every productive activity in the country. The fellahin who filled the ranks of the army were the same fellahin whose grain and cotton were bought at fixed government prices to pay for the guns they carried. There is a direct and brutal line from the peasant's field to the foundry's furnace: the army was fed, clothed, and armed by the systematic extraction of the agricultural surplus of Egypt. This was the fiscal engine of the whole military project, and it worked. By the late 1820s the pasha commanded revenues far beyond anything his Mamluk or Ottoman predecessors had disposed of, and he spent them overwhelmingly on the military. But the cost was the immiseration of the countryside. The monopolies depressed the peasant's income, the corvee labor that built the factories and the canals took him from his fields, and the conscription that filled the regiments took his sons. The military machine was built on the backs of the very people it claimed to defend, and the prosperity of the state was purchased with the poverty of the village.

The most spectacular product of this industrial and fiscal effort floated on water. Muhammad Ali understood that a land army, however well drilled, could not project Egyptian power across the Mediterranean. His ambitions ran to Arabia, where the Wahhabi movement defied Ottoman authority; to Greece, where the revolt against the sultan offered a chance to win territory and favor; and to Syria, which he coveted as a matter of long-standing strategic desire. None of these objectives could be reached, supplied, or held without sea power. So he built a navy. The naval arsenal at Alexandria became one of the great shipbuilding enterprises of the Mediterranean. Under European shipwrights and naval officers, Egyptian yards laid down ships of the line, frigates, corvettes, and brigs: a battle fleet on the European model, armed with Egyptian-cast cannon and manned by Egyptian sailors who had been trained, like the soldiers at Aswan, from raw material. By the late 1820s the Egyptian fleet numbered dozens of warships, including some of the largest vessels afloat in the Mediterranean, and it was supplemented by the pasha's merchant marine and transports. For the first time in centuries, an Egyptian ruler commanded a navy that could contest the eastern Mediterranean.

Why a Mediterranean fleet mattered is a question of geography and logistics. An army marching to Arabia had to be supplied across the Red Sea or overland through the Sinai, and the campaigns against the Wahhabis depended on control of Red Sea communications. An army sent to the Morea to suppress the Greek revolt had to cross the sea, and once there it lived or starved according to whether supply ships got through. An army invading Syria needed its seaward flank covered and its coastal supply line protected. In each case the fleet was the enabling instrument: it carried the troops, landed them, fed them, and evacuated them when things went wrong. Without the navy, Muhammad Ali was a provincial governor with a large army pinned to the Nile. With it, he was a Mediterranean power capable of intervening in three theaters at once. The European great powers understood this perfectly, which is why they watched the growth of the Alexandrian arsenal with mounting alarm. A strong Egyptian fleet altered the balance of the eastern Mediterranean, and it did so in the hands of a ruler whose ambitions were visibly expanding.

Honesty requires stating the limits of what had been achieved. Egyptian industry, for all its genuine accomplishments, could not match European output in either quality or scale. The foundries and workshops depended on imported expertise at every level: European founders to cast the guns, European shipwrights to design the hulls, European engineers to maintain the machinery. They depended on imported materials as well, above all timber for shipbuilding, which Egypt's treeless landscape could not supply, and high-grade iron and steel for gun founding. When European suppliers chose to withhold materials or technicians, Egyptian production faltered. The industrial base was broad but shallow: impressive in its extent, fragile in its foundations. It was an industry built by state command in a single generation, and it lacked the deep ecosystem of mines, forests, machine shops, and skilled labor pools that sustained European arms production. Muhammad Ali had created the appearance, and much of the reality, of a military-industrial complex, but it remained dependent on the very Europe it was meant to free him from.

The navy illustrated this dependence most cruelly. The fleet that the Alexandrian yards had built with such effort and expense would be shattered at Navarino in October 1827, when a combined British, French, and Russian squadron destroyed the Ottoman and Egyptian fleets in the bay of Navarino during the Greek war of independence. The full story of that battle, and of the campaigns that led to it, belongs to the campaigns section of this history. It is foreshadowed here only to make the essential point: the Egyptian navy, for all its size and modernity, was an instrument built to fight regional enemies, and it could not survive contact with the concentrated sea power of the great European navies. Navarino erased in an afternoon what a decade of shipbuilding had created. Muhammad Ali would rebuild, because rebuilding was his habit, but the lesson of Navarino was permanent. Egypt could become the strongest military power of the eastern Mediterranean. It could not become a power of the first rank so long as Britain, France, and Russia chose to deny it.

The verdict on the arming of the new army is therefore a mixed one, but the achievement remains extraordinary. By the late 1820s, Egypt possessed arsenals that cast its cannon, workshops that made its muskets and powder, a fiscal system that paid for them through the ruthless but effective monopoly over the country's agriculture and trade, and a navy built at Alexandria that could carry Egyptian power to Arabia, Greece, and Syria. No other state in the eastern Mediterranean outside the great European powers could match this combination of trained manpower, domestic arms production, and sea power. The Ottoman Empire itself, the sovereign to whom Muhammad Ali nominally owed allegiance, fielded armies less well drilled and a navy less modern than the pasha's. Egypt had become, in military terms, the most formidable power of its region. The limits were real: inferior quality, dependence on European technicians and materials, and a fleet that the great powers could destroy at will. But within the world that mattered to Muhammad Ali, the world of Ottoman provinces and Mediterranean rivals, he had built a military machine without peer. The guns, the factories, and the ships were the material foundation of everything his armies would accomplish in the decade that followed.

## The Commanders Who Made the Army Work: Ibrahim Pasha and Suleiman Pasha

Armies are not only systems; they are men, and Muhammad Ali's army worked because two men in particular made it work. The first was his son Ibrahim Pasha, the field commander who turned the drilled regiments into victories. The second was the Frenchman Suleiman Pasha, the organizer who turned peasant conscripts into drilled regiments in the first place. Between them, the warrior and the schoolmaster, they explain why this army fought so much better than anything Egypt had produced before.

Ibrahim Pasha was the eldest of Muhammad Ali's surviving sons and the heir to his military project in every sense that mattered. He learned soldiering the hard way, in the grinding Wahhabi war in Arabia, where he took over a stalled campaign and finished it by marching into the Najd and destroying Diriyah in 1818. He commanded the Egyptian expedition to the Morea in Greece from 1824, where his troops displayed a professionalism that startled European observers even as his scorched-earth methods horrified them. And he led the invasions of Syria in 1831 and the campaigns that twice destroyed Ottoman armies at Konya and Nezib. Ibrahim was not a distant palace general. He marched with his columns, drove them hard, punished failure, and understood the European tactics his father's instructors had taught. His energy supplied what the system could not manufacture on its own: a commander willing to push an army across the Taurus mountains in winter and to fight the battles that made Egypt, briefly, the arbiter of the Ottoman Empire's fate. The debate over whether Muhammad Ali deserves the title of reformer or tyrant, examined in [the article that weighs his whole record](/2014/12/01/muhammad-ali-reformer-or-ruler/), runs through Ibrahim's career as well, because his victories were won by an army built on conscription and his methods in Greece and Syria were merciless.

Suleiman Pasha, born Joseph Seve, was the opposite kind of soldier and the more indispensable of the two. A French officer left without a career after Napoleon's fall, he entered Muhammad Ali's service, converted to Islam, and devoted himself to the unglamorous work of organization: writing drill regulations, supervising the training camps, building the schools, and imposing the Nizam al-Jadid on raw material that European military wisdom had written off. Where Ibrahim won battles, Suleiman built the instrument that won them. He understood that an army is a school before it is a sword, and the staff college, the artillery schools, and the translated manuals were his doing as much as any parade-ground evolution. He stayed in Egyptian service for decades, rising to high command, and his career illustrates the bargain at the heart of the whole project: European knowledge purchased with Egyptian money and applied to Egyptian bodies, producing a force that belonged fully to neither world but served the pasha's purposes in both.

The pairing reveals the army's deepest structural fact. At the top stood a Turkish-speaking dynasty and its French instructors; in the middle, a largely Turkish and Circassian officer corps; at the base, Egyptian peasants who did the marching, the bleeding, and the dying. Ibrahim and Suleiman sat at the hinge of that hierarchy, one translating the pasha's ambition into campaigns, the other translating European method into Egyptian practice. The system worked brilliantly as long as both functions were performed. When the 1840 settlement cut the army down to 18,000 men, it destroyed the base while leaving the hierarchy intact, and the resentment of Egyptian soldiers commanded by foreigners would outlive the pasha himself, resurfacing a generation later when native officers demanded the commands their fathers had been denied. The commanders made the army; the army's composition would, in time, unmake the dynasty's military monopoly.

## The Campaigns in Sequence: Sudan, Arabia, and Greece

Muhammad Ali's new army earned its reputation not in Egypt but abroad, in three successive campaigns that the Ottoman sultan ordered him to fight. The Wahhabi war in Arabia tested whether his troops could survive a desert campaign far from home. The conquest of Sudan tested whether Egypt could turn itself into a self-sustaining military state, paying for its soldiers with African gold and captives. The intervention in Greece tested whether Egyptian power could be projected across the sea against a European-backed insurgency. The first two campaigns vindicated Muhammad Ali's reforms and gave him confidence, troops, and territory. The third ended in catastrophe at sea, and the catastrophe fixed the outer limit of what his army could achieve.

The Arabian campaign came first because the sultan needed it most urgently. The Wahhabi movement, centered in the Najd of central Arabia, had by the early 1800s captured Mecca and Medina, sacked Karbala, and humiliated the Ottoman claim to be the protector of Islam's holy cities. Sultan Mahmud II, whose own Janissary forces were unreliable, ordered Muhammad Ali to crush the movement, and the pasha dispatched an expedition under his son Tusun in 1811. The early campaigns went badly. Egyptian columns melted under heat, disease, and Wahhabi ambushes, and the logistical lines from Egypt across the Red Sea and through the Hijaz mountains proved brutally long. Tusun's forces took Medina late in 1812 and Mecca and Jiddah early in 1813, but the interior of the Najd remained beyond reach and progress stalled.

Muhammad Ali responded the way he responded to every failure of these years: by changing the command and tightening the system. He sent his eldest son Ibrahim Pasha to take over the campaign, and Ibrahim brought with him the improving discipline of the Nizam al-Jadid regiments and a willingness to fight the Wahhabis at their own game of desert mobility. The fighting dragged on for several years, and it consumed enormous quantities of money, grain, and Egyptian lives, but by 1818 Ibrahim had marched into the Najd itself and laid siege to the Wahhabi capital at Diriyah. The siege lasted months and ended with the city's destruction. The Wahhabi leader was sent to Istanbul in chains, and Mecca and Medina returned to Ottoman control, which in practice meant Egyptian control, since Egyptian troops garrisoned the Hijaz and Egyptian administrators ran the holy cities.

The Arabian war proved three things. First, Muhammad Ali's army could be deployed and supplied hundreds of miles from Egypt for years at a time, which no Egyptian force had attempted since the age of the Mamluk sultans. Second, the pasha could absorb catastrophic losses in men and money without his regime collapsing, because the monopoly system squeezed enough revenue from Egypt's peasants to keep the war machine fed. Third, success against the Wahhabis bought Muhammad Ali immense prestige in Istanbul and Cairo alike: he had done what the sultan could not do himself, and the sultan's gratitude translated into tolerance for the pasha's growing autonomy. The cost was also instructive, though Muhammad Ali drew the wrong lesson from it. The war had nearly bankrupted Egypt, and it showed that even a victorious campaign on this scale depended entirely on the pasha's personal energy and on the treasury he had built from the land and its cultivators.

The Sudan campaign of 1820 to 1822 grew directly out of Muhammad Ali's new ambitions and his new army's new needs. He wanted gold, which was rumored to lie in the hills of Sennar. He wanted a buffer zone to the south, beyond which no invasion of Egypt could easily come. And he wanted a supply of Black African soldiers for his army, men he could train without the local resistance that Egyptian conscripts were already beginning to show. The expedition was his own project rather than an imperial commission, undertaken with the sultan's acquiescence rather than at his order, and the motives were the pasha's throughout. In 1820 he sent an army south under his third son Ismail Kamil, with instructions to conquer the upper Nile valley and bring back whatever it could extract.

Ismail's force, which combined Ottoman-style cavalry with detachments of the new Nizam al-Jadid infantry and artillery, moved up the Nile in stages. The Funj kingdom of Sennar, already decaying, offered little serious resistance, and by 1821 the Egyptians had taken Sennar itself. That same year they laid out a military camp at the confluence of the Blue and White Niles, the camp that grew into the city of Khartoum. From this base Egyptian columns pushed further south and west, into Kordofan and toward the Nuba mountains, levying tribute, seizing captives, and prospecting for the gold that never materialized in the quantities Muhammad Ali had hoped for.

The Sudanese conquest mattered more for what it revealed about the Egyptian military state than for what it yielded. The gold proved a disappointment. The slave soldiers proved a far greater one: of the tens of thousands of Sudanese captives marched north to be trained as soldiers, a large proportion died of disease and climate on the way, and those who survived never formed the reliable corps Muhammad Ali had envisioned. The disaster of the Sudanese conscription experiment pushed him decisively toward the conscription of Egyptian peasants instead, the fellahin who would become the bulk of his infantry. But the campaign also demonstrated that his army could conquer and hold a vast territory, that it could found a city at the junction of two Niles a thousand miles from Cairo, and that the Ottoman Empire would ratify whatever he seized. Sudan became an Egyptian province, ruled by Egyptian governors and garrisoned by Egyptian troops, and it stayed one for the rest of the century.

The Greek campaign was a different kind of test, and it ended the way the first two had not. The Greek revolt against Ottoman rule, which began in 1821, had by 1824 reduced the sultan to desperation. His armies had failed repeatedly against the Greek insurgents, and once again he turned to Muhammad Ali, ordering the pasha to reconquer the Morea, the southern peninsula of Greece. Muhammad Ali saw the opportunity and took it: in exchange for putting down the revolt, he expected to receive Crete and to see his son Ibrahim installed as governor of the reconquered Morea. Ibrahim sailed from Alexandria in 1824 with a well-equipped expeditionary force of infantry, cavalry, and artillery, landed in the Peloponnese early in 1825, and set about the work of reconquest with systematic brutality.

Ibrahim's campaign in the Morea displayed the Egyptian army at its professional peak. His troops took fortress after fortress, drove the Greek forces into the mountains and onto the islands, and by 1826 had recaptured most of the peninsula. The siege and fall of Missolonghi in April 1826, carried out jointly with Ottoman forces, broke the back of organized Greek resistance on the mainland. Ibrahim's columns ravaged the countryside, burning villages and carrying off captives, a campaign of terror designed to make the Morea ungovernable for any future Greek state. By any purely military measure, the Egyptians had done what the sultan had asked: they had crushed the revolt on land.

What Ibrahim could not do was keep the sea lanes open, and what Muhammad Ali could not do was keep the European powers neutral. Britain, France, and Russia, each pursuing its own interest in the Eastern Mediterranean, had grown increasingly hostile to the reconquest, and in 1827 their governments agreed to impose an armistice on the combatants. When Ibrahim refused to comply, the allied squadrons entered the bay of Navarino on October 20, 1827, where the combined Ottoman-Egyptian fleet lay at anchor, and in a few hours annihilated it. The allied ships suffered few losses; the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet ceased to exist as a fighting force.

Navarino changed everything and changed nothing. It changed nothing about the quality of Ibrahim's infantry, which marched out of the Morea intact. But it changed everything about the strategic position, because it proved that Egyptian sea power, and therefore Egyptian power projection beyond the range of land supply, depended on the tolerance of the European navies. The Morea campaign was unwound by diplomacy after Navarino: under allied pressure Ibrahim evacuated the Peloponnese in 1828, and Greece moved toward independence. Muhammad Ali had spent a fortune, lost his fleet, and gained Crete as compensation, a poor return. The lesson was plain and he understood it: his army could beat any force in the Middle East, but it could not survive a confrontation with the great European powers at sea, and any future clash with the sultan would have to be won quickly, on land, before the European navies could intervene. The three campaigns also trace the arc of Muhammad Ali's own transformation. In Arabia he had fought as the sultan's appointed agent, grateful for the commission; in Sudan he had fought for himself, extending his own dominion with the sultan's blessing; in Greece he had fought as a contractor whose price was territory, and when the price was not paid, the resentment that followed pointed him toward Syria.

## Syria and the March Toward the Sultan

The invasion of Syria in 1831 was the moment when Muhammad Ali stopped being the sultan's servant and became his rival. The causes were personal, political, and strategic at once. The pasha believed the sultan had cheated him of his due reward for the Greek campaign, offering too little for too much Egyptian blood and treasure. He had his eye on Syria's timber, which his shipyards needed, and on its ports, which would complete Egyptian control of the eastern Mediterranean. And he had in Ibrahim Pasha a general at the height of his powers, commanding an army that had been hardened by Arabia, Sudan, and the Morea into the finest fighting force in the Middle East. Late in 1831 Ibrahim marched into Syria.

What followed shocked Istanbul, London, and Paris alike. Ibrahim's army moved through Palestine with a speed that Ottoman commanders could not match and could not explain. Fortress after fortress fell: Gaza, Jaffa, Haifa, Jerusalem. The great obstacle was Acre, the port city whose walls had famously defied Napoleon in 1799 and which the Ottomans had spent decades strengthening. Ibrahim laid siege to Acre and kept at it for months, through the winter of 1831 to 1832, hammering the fortifications with siege artillery and repelling relief attempts, until the city surrendered in May 1832. With Acre fallen, the road north lay open. Ibrahim took Damascus, pushed into the interior, and then did what no Egyptian commander had done in centuries: he marched over the Taurus mountains into Anatolia itself, into the Ottoman heartland.

The Ottoman response was frantic and futile. Sultan Mahmud II scraped together an army under his grand vizier and sent it to block Ibrahim's advance. The two armies met at Konya, in central Anatolia, in December 1832. The result was not a battle but a demolition. The Egyptian infantry, drilled in European tactics, supported by disciplined artillery and cavalry, tore the Ottoman host apart, captured the grand vizier himself, and left the road to Istanbul open. Ibrahim could have marched on the capital. He did not, because Muhammad Ali held him back: the pasha wanted concessions from the sultan, not the destruction of the empire, and he knew that a march on Istanbul would bring the European powers down on him as Navarino had done.

The settlement that followed, the Convention of Kutahya in 1833, ratified Muhammad Ali's victory while preserving the fiction of Ottoman sovereignty. The sultan granted Muhammad Ali the governorship of Syria, along with Adana and other territories, while the pasha remained nominally the sultan's vassal. It was a compromise that satisfied no one and settled nothing. Mahmud II never forgave the humiliation of Konya and spent the following years rebuilding his army for a war of revenge. Muhammad Ali used the breathing space to consolidate his hold on Syria, building roads, disarming the local population, conscripting Syrian peasants into his forces, and imposing the same monopoly system on the Syrian economy that had funded his power in Egypt. Both sides understood that Kutahya was a truce, not a peace.

The second crisis came in 1839, and it nearly ended the Ottoman Empire. Mahmud II, believing his rebuilt army was ready, ordered it across the Euphrates into Syria in the spring of 1839, seeking to retake what Ibrahim had won. Ibrahim met the Ottoman army at Nezib, in southeastern Anatolia, on June 24, 1839. Once again the Egyptian army destroyed its Ottoman counterpart, and this time the collapse in Istanbul was total. Within days of the battle, the Ottoman fleet defected wholesale to Alexandria and placed itself under Muhammad Ali's command. And then Sultan Mahmud II died, on July 1, 1839, leaving the throne to his sixteen-year-old son Abdulmecid I.

For a few weeks in the summer of 1839, Muhammad Ali stood closer than any provincial governor in Ottoman history to deciding the fate of the empire itself. His son's army had annihilated the sultan's last field force. The Ottoman navy rode at anchor in his harbor. The new sultan was a boy, and the regency in Istanbul was paralyzed. Had Muhammad Ali ordered Ibrahim to march on the capital then, there was arguably no force in the empire that could have stopped him. He hesitated, and the hesitation proved fatal to his ambitions. The European powers, alarmed at the prospect of an Egyptian strongman controlling Istanbul and the Straits, intervened. Britain, Austria, Russia, and Prussia signed the London Convention of 1840, stripping Muhammad Ali of Syria and confining him to Egypt, and when he refused, a British-Austrian fleet bombarded Beirut and Acre and landed troops in Syria. Ibrahim's army, unbeatable on land against the Ottomans, could do nothing against a European fleet and a European-backed campaign. Muhammad Ali accepted the settlement of 1841: he kept Egypt as a hereditary possession for his family, which was no small prize, but Syria was gone, his fleet was gone, and his army was cut down to a fraction of its strength. The march toward the sultan had ended where the Greek campaign had ended, at the limit drawn by European sea power.

### How did Ibrahim Pasha's army conquer Syria so quickly?

Ibrahim's army won through superior drill, artillery, and mobility: European-trained infantry that held formation under fire, concentrated guns that reduced fortresses, and a command system that moved columns faster than Ottoman forces could react, while Ottoman armies suffered from outdated tactics, divided command, and collapsing morale.

The deeper explanation begins with the two armies themselves. Ibrahim's force was the product of fifteen years of Muhammad Ali's reforms: infantry regiments drilled in European linear tactics by French instructors, artillery served by trained gunners rather than by the casual crews of the old Ottoman service, cavalry that could maneuver in coordination with the guns, and a staff system that kept columns supplied and moving on schedule. The Ottoman armies that faced him were the armies of a state that had tried and failed to reform itself. Mahmud II had destroyed the Janissaries in 1826, but the new Ottoman troops raised to replace them were raw, hastily trained, and led by officers who had learned nothing from the European methods that were beating them. At Konya and again at Nezib, the pattern repeated: Egyptian infantry stood its ground and poured in disciplined volleys, Egyptian artillery broke up Ottoman formations before they could close, and Ottoman cavalry charges dissolved against formed lines. Once the first Ottoman line broke, the rest of the army tended to follow, because the men had little faith in their officers and the officers had little faith in each other.

The siege of Acre illustrated both the strength and the limits of Ibrahim's method. Acre was the strongest fortress in Syria, a walled port with modernized defenses, and it held out for roughly six months, from late 1831 until May 1832. Ibrahim could not take it by storm without ruinous losses, so he settled into a formal siege: trenches, parallels, and a sustained artillery bombardment that gradually reduced the walls. The garrison fought well and the Ottoman navy made attempts to relieve the city by sea, but Ibrahim's control of the surrounding country meant the defenders could not be resupplied by land, and his guns meant the walls could not hold forever. When Acre fell, it fell completely, and its fall broke the psychological backbone of Ottoman resistance in Syria: if Acre could not stop Ibrahim, nothing could. The siege also showed how far Egyptian siege craft had advanced since the early bombardments of the Wahhabi war; Ibrahim's engineers, many of them French-trained, conducted the approaches methodically and kept the guns firing through months of counter-battery fire and sorties from the garrison.

Speed itself was a weapon, and Ibrahim wielded it deliberately. His columns moved fast because they were supplied by a system that Muhammad Ali had built for exactly this purpose, with magazines, transport, and requisitioning organized in advance. They moved fast because Ibrahim drove them hard and punished straggling. And they moved fast because the Syrian population, exhausted by Ottoman misrule and taxation, offered little resistance and in some places welcomed the Egyptians as deliverers, at least until the conscription gangs and the monopolies arrived. Ottoman commanders, receiving contradictory orders from a panicked Istanbul, could never concentrate their forces in time; Ibrahim beat them in detail, one isolated garrison and one confused field army at a time.

Anatolia raised the stakes beyond anything Ibrahim had attempted before. Crossing the Taurus mountains in winter with an army, artillery, and supply train was a logistical feat that impressed even European observers, and it put the Egyptians into the Ottoman heartland, among a Turkish population that had no reason to welcome them. That Ibrahim could do it at all showed how far the Egyptian military machine had come since the ragged columns that had stumbled through the Hijaz twenty years earlier. At Konya the Ottomans fielded a large army under the grand vizier, and the Egyptians destroyed it in a single day of fighting. European military observers who studied the campaign concluded that the difference was not courage, the Ottoman soldiers fought bravely enough when they stood, but organization: the Egyptian army functioned as a single instrument under a single will, while the Ottoman army functioned as a collection of contingents under competing commanders.

Why the Ottoman armies collapsed so completely, twice in seven years, has a further layer that goes beyond tactics. Mahmud II's reforms had created an army that was new in name and old in practice: the officers were often the same men, promoted by favor rather than competence, and the rank and file were conscripts with little training and less loyalty to a distant sultan. Ibrahim's soldiers, by contrast, were the product of a system that, however brutal its conscription methods, at least trained its men thoroughly and paid and fed them regularly. Discipline held under fire because it had been built up over years of drill, not decreed overnight. When the lines met, the army that believed in its officers and its methods beat the army that believed in neither.

There is a final irony in the Syrian conquest that Muhammad Ali himself came to understand. The very speed and completeness of Ibrahim's victories alarmed the European powers whose navies had already set the limit at Navarino. Each Ottoman collapse made British and French statesmen more determined that Muhammad Ali should not be allowed to replace the sultan, because an Egypt that controlled Istanbul and the Straits would upset the balance they were managing in the eastern Mediterranean. Ibrahim could beat any Ottoman army put in front of him, and he proved it at Konya and Nezib. What he could not beat was the diplomatic and naval coalition that his victories summoned into being, and that coalition, not any Ottoman general, was what finally stopped him.

## The 1840 Check: Why Europe Stopped Muhammad Ali's Army

By the summer of 1840, Muhammad Ali stood closer to dictating terms to the Ottoman sultan than any vassal in the empire's history. His son Ibrahim Pasha had destroyed Ottoman armies in Syria twice, first at Konya in December 1832 and again at Nezib in June 1839, the second victory landing within days of Sultan Mahmud II's death. When the new regime in Constantinople tried to strike back, the Ottoman grand admiral instead sailed the empire's fleet to Alexandria and handed it to Muhammad Ali, a defection that gave the Egyptian ruler the strongest navy in the eastern Mediterranean on top of the strongest land army. Egyptian garrisons held Syria, Palestine, the Hijaz, and Crete. From his palace in Cairo he was negotiating not as a governor asking favors but as a sovereign proposing a settlement: hereditary rule for his family and recognition of his conquests in exchange for loyalty he no longer felt. Nothing in Ottoman law or precedent offered a mechanism for stopping him, because the mechanism that had stopped every earlier overmighty governor, the sultan's army, lay shattered in the dust of Anatolia.

What stopped him was not Ottoman but European power, and the reasons had little to do with his justice or injustice as a ruler. Britain feared an Egyptian empire planted across the routes to India, astride the Levant, the Red Sea, and the approaches to the Persian Gulf. A strong Muhammad Ali controlling Syria and the Hijaz could close or complicate the overland and sea corridors on which British communications with India depended, and his well-organized state threatened to make Egypt an independent pole of power rather than a manageable dependency. Just as important, British statesmen feared the collapse of the Ottoman Empire that his success implied. If Muhammad Ali broke the sultan's authority, the partition of the Ottoman dominions would follow, and partition meant Russia. A Russian move toward Constantinople, the Straits, and the eastern Mediterranean was the nightmare that ordered British policy in the Near East, so London concluded that a vigorous Egyptian empire was more dangerous to British interests than a decrepit Ottoman one. Austria shared the logic from a different angle: Metternich's empire preferred the Ottoman status quo, however decayed, to the unpredictable borders a new Egyptian power would draw. Russia, paradoxically, joined the coalition against Muhammad Ali precisely because it wanted to keep its own leverage over a weak Constantinople rather than watch a new power scramble the board. Prussia followed the consensus. The four powers did not disagree about the desirability of his success. They disagreed about almost everything else, but on this they were unanimous.

### Why did the European powers intervene against Muhammad Ali in 1840?

They feared an Egyptian empire astride the route to India and a collapse of the Ottoman order that would hand Russia a pretext to partition Constantinople's dominions. Britain, Austria, Russia, and Prussia preferred a weak sultan they could manage over a strong Egyptian ruler they could not control.

Behind that plain answer lay a calculation that historians have traced through the diplomatic correspondence of Lord Palmerston and his counterparts. The powers had learned, from the Treaty of Unkiar-Skelessi in 1833, how a crisis in the Ottoman Empire could hand one rival an advantage; Russian warships had reached the Bosphorus during Ibrahim's first advance, and only diplomacy had extracted them. No European capital wanted a repeat. Muhammad Ali's ambassador in London found no sympathy, and the French court, which had flattered the pasha for a decade and sold him arms and advisers, discovered that sympathy did not extend to a general war. France alone defended him, and France alone was outvoted. The collective preference was cold and explicit: a weak sultan whom the powers could collectively manage was worth more to the European order than a strong Egyptian empire that answered to nobody. Muhammad Ali's genius as a state builder counted against him. His army's discipline, his fleet's size, and his administrative grip on Egypt proved not that he deserved independence but that independence in his hands would be real, and real independence was exactly what the system could not absorb.

The fragility of his Syrian position should have warned him. Egyptian rule in Syria had rested from the start on conscription and taxation that the local population resented, and the great Palestinian revolt of 1834, and the Hauran rising of 1838, when communities rose against the Egyptian levy, had required Ibrahim to divert his best troops to suppression. By 1840 the Syrian peasantry saw the Egyptian army not as a liberator from Ottoman misrule but as another extractor, and when the British fleet appeared, the revolts reignited behind it. France, meanwhile, proved a fair-weather patron. The French government made bellicose noises and fortified Paris against a possible war, but it would not fight Britain, Austria, Russia, and Prussia to keep Muhammad Ali's Syrian empire, and by October the war scare had collapsed into a change of ministry in Paris. The pasha's isolation was complete.

The instrument of the check was the Convention of London, signed in July 1840 by Britain, Austria, Prussia, Russia, and the Ottoman Empire. Its terms were an ultimatum disguised as an offer. If Muhammad Ali withdrew from northern Syria, the Hijaz, and Crete within ten days and from the rest of Syria within a further period, he would keep Egypt as a hereditary possession of his family and receive the district of Acre for his lifetime. If he refused the terms but submitted within twenty days, he would keep hereditary Egypt alone. If he rejected even that, the powers would take what they pleased. Muhammad Ali, counting on France and overestimating his leverage, let the deadlines pass. He believed, not unreasonably given the history of his career, that the powers would bargain and that his armies in Syria would make any settlement expensive. He was wrong about both. The British Mediterranean fleet under Admiral Stopford, with Austrian support, appeared off the Levantine coast in the autumn of 1840. Beirut was bombarded in September and fell in October, Syrian coastal towns followed, and the Egyptian garrisons, formidable in the field, could not hold fortified ports against naval guns they could not answer. On November 3, 1840, the allied fleet bombarded Acre, the fortress that had been the keystone of Egyptian power in Syria. A shell struck the main powder magazine, the explosion shattered the defenses, and the city fell the same day. Acre's fall broke the Egyptian position in Syria as Nezib had broken the Ottoman one. Ibrahim's army, still intact as a fighting force, had no ports to supply it and no political base left to defend, because the local populations that had resented Egyptian conscription and taxation rose in revolt behind the allied advance.

The settlement that followed was a deliberate amputation. Muhammad Ali withdrew his forces from Syria, Palestine, the Hijaz, and Crete, surrendering every conquest that had made him a Mediterranean power. The February 1841 firman from the sultan, confirmed under European supervision, granted his family the hereditary rule of Egypt, the prize he had sought for three decades. But the grant came fettered. His army was capped at 18,000 men, a fraction of the force that had humbled the sultan, and his navy was restricted, with the great fleet he had built to be reduced. Tribute was fixed, and the administration of Egypt was bound to the Ottoman order he had nearly overturned. The peasant regiments that had marched to Konya and Nezib were disbanded in droves, their men returned to the villages they had been dragged from, and the schools, arsenals, and military industries that had fed the war machine withered or were repurposed. Europe had not destroyed Muhammad Ali. It had cut him down to a size it could tolerate, leaving him a dynasty in exchange for his empire. The 18,000-man limit was the number the powers judged sufficient to keep order in Egypt and insufficient to threaten anyone beyond it, and that cold arithmetic was the final verdict on the army he had spent twenty-five years building.

The crisis left one more settlement in its wake. In July 1841, the powers signed the Straits Convention, closing the Bosphorus and Dardanelles to foreign warships in peacetime and thereby resolving the Russian question that had haunted British policy since Unkiar-Skelessi. The settlement of 1840 and 1841 thus redrew the rules of the eastern Mediterranean for a generation: the Ottoman Empire would survive under collective European guarantee, Russia would be kept from the Straits, and Egypt would remain a hereditary principality whose army could police the Nile Valley but never again march on Constantinople.

### Did the army survive the 1841 settlement in any form?

Not as a fighting instrument. Capped at 18,000 men and stripped of its garrisons, it became a force for internal order. What survived was the supporting apparatus: the military schools, the medical college, the arsenals in reduced form, and the officer class they had trained.


## The Honest Verdict: What the Army Proves About His Rule

The judgment on Muhammad Ali's army begins with what it was made of, because the composition answers the central question about his rule. This was not a force of imported mercenaries fighting for a foreign dynasty. The early regiments raised after 1815 drew on Albanian and Turkish soldiers left over from the old Ottoman order, but the decisive transformation came when the pasha's agents began conscripting Egyptian fellahin by the tens of thousands and drilling them in European methods. By the time Ibrahim Pasha crossed into Syria, the rank and file of the new order army were Egyptian peasants: men taken from the Nile villages, dressed in European-style uniforms, armed with modern muskets cast in Egyptian foundries, and commanded in a hierarchy copied from the French and British services. Officers of Turkish, Circassian, and Albanian origin held most senior posts, and European advisers, above all the French colonel known as Suleiman Pasha, supplied the technical training. But the muscle and the mass were Egyptian. The army that defeated the sultan's troops at Konya and Nezib was the Egyptian countryside in uniform, and that fact binds the story of the army to the story of Egypt itself in a way no account of mercenary warfare can.

The thesis that follows from the composition is the peasant-army thesis: Muhammad Ali's power rested on turning the Egyptian peasantry into a modern military machine, which means he built his strength from the very population his regime burdened most heavily. The fellahin who filled the regiments were the same fellahin who paid the land taxes, surrendered their cotton and grain to the state monopolies at fixed prices, and performed the corvee labor that dug the Mahmudiyya Canal and built the fortifications. Conscription added a further extraction to the fiscal one. Villagers hid their sons, bribed recruiting officers, and in some districts fled their lands to avoid the levy; the archives record desertion rates that in some years ran into the thousands, and the regime answered with branding, chaining, and collective punishment of villages that sheltered deserters. None of this should be softened. The army's discipline was real, and so was the coercion that produced it. Muhammad Ali did not discover a martial people waiting to be armed. He manufactured soldiers out of peasants who would have preferred to remain farmers, and he kept them in the ranks with a system of punishment that treated the body of the conscript as state property. Any honest account of the army must hold these two facts together: the regiments fought with a steadiness that impressed European observers, and the steadiness was produced by force.

Weighed against that cost, the achievement was extraordinary by any comparative standard. The Nizam al-Jadid was the first non-European army of the nineteenth century to master European organization, tactics, and military industry at scale and then to use that mastery to defeat a major established power repeatedly. The claim requires care, because other non-European states fielded formidable armies in the same period, but none combined the elements Muhammad Ali combined: a conscript rank and file in the hundreds of thousands at peak strength, a domestic arms industry producing muskets, cannon, and powder, a navy built in Egyptian shipyards, staff schools training officers in mathematics and engineering, and a logistics system that sustained campaigns hundreds of miles from Cairo. The victories in the Morea, in Arabia against the Wahhabis, and above all in Syria were not accidents of Ottoman decay. The Ottoman armies of 1832 and 1839 were serious forces, and Ibrahim's troops beat them in open battle through superior maneuver, artillery handling, and infantry fire discipline, the exact skills European drill was designed to teach. Scholars debate the precise peak strength of the army, with estimates clustering between roughly 100,000 and 150,000 men at the height of the Syrian campaigns, and the uncertainty itself testifies to the scale. Whatever the true figure, it was an order of magnitude larger than anything Egypt had fielded under the Mamluks or the early Ottoman governors, and it was built in barely two decades.

The counter-reading deserves a direct answer, because it has a surface plausibility that dissolves on inspection. The argument runs that the army was a foreign imposition: Turkish-speaking officers, French advisers, Albanian remnants, a pasha who was himself born in Kavala and spoke Turkish, as the article on [Muhammad Ali Pasha](/2014/04/01/muhammad-ali-pasha-of-egypt/) sets out, all commanding Egyptian peasants who had no stake in the enterprise. In this reading the army proves nothing about Egypt and everything about one man's ambition. The evidence points the other way. The fellahin did not remain inert instruments. They learned the drill, manned the guns, and in campaigns from Arabia to Anatolia developed the cohesion of veteran troops, which is why European observers who watched them in Syria described Egyptian infantry holding formation under fire that broke Ottoman lines. The army's very Egyptian character explains both its greatest strength and the regime's greatest vulnerability. Because the soldiers were peasants, the army transmitted the state's demands deep into village life, and because the state's demands fell on peasants, conscription bred the desertion and resistance that never fully stopped. A mercenary force could have been dismissed; a peasant army had to be fed, clothed, and constantly replenished from the same villages being taxed to pay for it. The suffering and the modernization were not two separate stories. They were the same story told from opposite ends, the conscript's empty village and the pasha's full arsenal.

One comparison sharpens the verdict. Sultan Selim III had attempted his own European-style army and Muhammad Ali had watched that experiment collapse under Janissary revolt in 1807, after which Mahmud II had to begin again from the wreckage. The pasha succeeded where his sovereign failed, partly because he had a peasantry to conscript and no entrenched Janissary corps to overthrow him, and partly because he built the supporting institutions the sultan never sustained: military schools teaching mathematics and engineering, arsenals casting cannon, powder mills, and a naval yard at Alexandria that launched warships. These institutions outlived the army's amputation in truncated form and trained the generation of Egyptian officers and technicians who staffed the state for decades afterward. The army was thus a school as well as a sword, and its curriculum of drill, engineering, medicine, and administration seeded the modern Egyptian state even after the regiments themselves were disbanded. The officers who would later lead Egypt's nationalist movements traced their professional lineage to these schools, which means the peasant army's longest legacy may have been political rather than military.

The 1840 settlement is the hinge on which the verdict turns, because it revealed what the army was and was not for. Capped at 18,000 men, stripped of its Syrian and Arabian garrisons, and reduced to a force for internal order, the army could no longer serve as the instrument of empire. The amputation was not a military defeat in the conventional sense. Ibrahim's field army was never destroyed in battle; it was stranded by the loss of its ports and the revolts its own exactions had provoked, then disbanded by diplomacy. That distinction matters for the judgment. The army did not fail as a fighting instrument. It failed as a political one, because the political order of Europe would not permit an Egyptian empire no matter how well its armies fought. Muhammad Ali had built the one thing that could challenge the sultan and discovered that challenging the sultan was forbidden to everyone. The 18,000-man limit froze Egypt's military development for a generation and ensured that the dynasty would survive as a client rather than rule as a conqueror. The hereditary grant of 1841 was the consolation prize for a career of conquest: his family would keep Egypt, but only an Egypt the powers could live with.

Tie the army to the reach and limits of his power and the picture is complete. The reach was unprecedented. From the conscription of Nile peasants in the 1820s to the capture of the Ottoman fleet in 1839, the army carried Muhammad Ali's authority from the Sudan to the Taurus Mountains, built the industries and schools that made Egypt the most administratively advanced state in the Middle East, and forced every European chancery to reckon with Cairo as a capital of consequence. The limits were drawn by the same instrument. An army powerful enough to humiliate the sultan was powerful enough to frighten London, Vienna, St. Petersburg, and Berlin into a coalition, and the coalition's answer was to cut the force down to a size that could threaten no one. The instrument that nearly made him master of the eastern Mediterranean was the reason Europe united to stop him. That is the honest verdict on Muhammad Ali's army: the most remarkable military achievement of any non-European ruler in the nineteenth century, built on the backs of the Egyptian peasantry, and dismantled by the powers precisely because it worked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali build a modern army?

He replaced a mercenary system that had failed him with a standing conscript force trained in the European manner. After the Albanian mutiny of 1815 proved his irregulars could not be disciplined, and after a Sudanese slave-soldier experiment collapsed with most captives dead, he turned in 1822 to conscripting Egyptian peasants. French officers under Suleiman Pasha drilled them at Aswan in infantry tactics, artillery, and maneuver, while new arsenals at Cairo cast their cannon and the Alexandria yard built their fleet. State monopolies over cotton and grain paid for it all. Within a decade Egypt fielded a force estimated at roughly 100,000 to 130,000 men, the first non-European army of the century to master European organization at scale.

### Q: How was Muhammad Ali's army trained?

At the isolated camp at Aswan, far up the Nile where desertion was nearly impossible, French instructors imposed European infantry drill: marching in step, forming line and column, the manual of arms, volley firing, bayonet work, and battlefield evolutions such as wheeling and forming square against cavalry. Commands were standardized in Turkish drill terminology that Arabic-speaking recruits memorized by repetition. Discipline was enforced with the lash, the bastinado, and execution for desertion. Behind the parade ground stood military schools teaching mathematics, fortification, and gunnery, a medical school at Abu Zaabal training surgeons, and a translation program rendering French manuals into Turkish and Arabic. Training, not just conscription, is what turned a peasant levy into an army that could beat Ottoman regulars.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali conscript Egyptian peasants?

Yes, and on a scale no Egyptian ruler had attempted for centuries. Beginning in 1822 and 1823, conscripting officers carried village quotas into the countryside and seized men without census, lot drawing, or medical boards, marching them to the training camps in chained files. Village headmen were held responsible for delivering the quota, which let them decide whose sons went and sell exemptions. Peasants resisted by flight, bribery, and self-mutilation, and desertion became a permanent condition of the army. Promised three-year terms were routinely extended into what amounted to life service. The fellahin were chosen precisely because they could not sail home to Albania or appeal to the sultan; they were the one large reservoir of manpower the pasha could seize and hold.

### Q: How powerful was Muhammad Ali's army?

At its height in the 1830s it was the most formidable military force in the eastern Mediterranean outside the great European powers. Scholars debate the exact peak strength, with estimates ranging from roughly 100,000 to well above 200,000 men, supported by domestic arsenals producing muskets, cannon, and powder, and by a battle fleet built at Alexandria. It conquered Sudan, destroyed the Wahhabi capital in Arabia, reconquered most of the Morea in Greece, and twice annihilated Ottoman field armies, at Konya in 1832 and Nezib in 1839, capturing the Ottoman grand vizier in the first battle and receiving the defected Ottoman fleet after the second. Its limit was not any regional rival but the European navies, which destroyed its fleet at Navarino in 1827 and dismantled its Syrian empire in 1840.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali challenge the Ottoman Empire militarily?

Directly and repeatedly, though he always stopped short of formally breaking with the sultan until events forced the issue. He fought the sultan's wars in Arabia and Greece as a loyal governor, then turned his army against the sultan's own forces: invading Syria in 1831, crushing the Ottoman army at Konya in December 1832, and destroying another Ottoman army at Nezib in June 1839, days before Sultan Mahmud II died. After Nezib the Ottoman fleet defected to Alexandria, leaving Muhammad Ali with the strongest land army and navy in the region. He hesitated to march on Istanbul, and that hesitation gave Britain, Austria, Russia, and Prussia time to intervene. The challenge ended not with an Ottoman victory but with a European coalition imposing the 1840 settlement.

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali's army conquer Syria?

Ibrahim Pasha invaded in late 1831 with European-trained infantry, disciplined artillery, and a supply system built for rapid movement. Fortress after fortress in Palestine fell, and Acre, which had defied Napoleon in 1799, surrendered in May 1832 after a six-month siege of trenches and bombardment. Ibrahim took Damascus, crossed the Taurus mountains into Anatolia, and destroyed the Ottoman army at Konya in December 1832, capturing the grand vizier. The 1833 settlement at Kutahya granted him Syria while preserving nominal Ottoman sovereignty. When the sultan tried to retake it in 1839, Ibrahim destroyed the new Ottoman army at Nezib that June. The conquest rested on superior drill, artillery, and organization against Ottoman armies that were new in name but poorly led, and it collapsed only when European sea power intervened.

### Q: Why did European powers stop Muhammad Ali?

Because his success threatened the order they managed in the eastern Mediterranean. Britain feared an Egyptian empire astride the routes to India, across the Levant, the Red Sea, and the approaches to the Persian Gulf, and feared even more that an Ottoman collapse would hand Russia a pretext to move on Constantinople and the Straits. Austria under Metternich preferred a weak sultan to unpredictable new borders. Russia joined the coalition to preserve its own leverage over a feeble Constantinople rather than face a vigorous new power. By the Convention of London in July 1840 the four powers issued an ultimatum, and when Muhammad Ali refused, a British-Austrian fleet retook the Syrian coast, Acre falling in November 1840. His victories had proven his army too strong for the system to tolerate.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali build a navy?

He built the first Egyptian battle fleet of the modern era at the naval arsenal of Alexandria. Under European shipwrights, Egyptian yards laid down ships of the line, frigates, corvettes, and brigs, armed with Egyptian-cast cannon and manned by Egyptian sailors trained from raw recruits like the soldiers at Aswan. By the late 1820s the fleet numbered dozens of warships, some among the largest in the Mediterranean, and it carried Egyptian power to Arabia, Greece, and Syria. Its limits were exposed at Navarino in October 1827, when a combined British, French, and Russian squadron annihilated the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet in hours. Muhammad Ali rebuilt the navy afterward, only to see the 1840 settlement restrict it sharply, ending Egypt's career as a Mediterranean naval power.

### Q: Who led Muhammad Ali's armies in the Syrian campaigns?

His son Ibrahim Pasha commanded in the field. Ibrahim had already proven himself in the Wahhabi war, where he took over a stalled campaign and destroyed Diriyah in 1818, and in the Morea in Greece from 1824 to 1827. In Syria he directed the siege of Acre, the march over the Taurus, and the victories at Konya in 1832 and Nezib in 1839, driving his columns at a speed Ottoman commanders could not match. Behind him stood the organizational work of Suleiman Pasha, the Frenchman who had built the training system, and a staff of Turkish, Circassian, and European officers. But the campaigns themselves were Ibrahim's: he was the executor of his father's ambition and the general whose victories made Egypt, briefly, the arbiter of the Ottoman Empire's fate.

### Q: What was the Nizam al-Jadid?

The Nizam al-Jadid, meaning the "new order," was the system of European-style military organization Muhammad Ali imposed on his conscript army from the 1820s. It meant infantry regiments drilled in French linear tactics, standardized uniforms and commands, disciplined artillery service, and an officer hierarchy copied from European models, all enforced through severe punishment. The name was borrowed from the Ottoman Empire's own reform vocabulary, but in Egypt it was pursued further than Istanbul ever managed. The Nizam al-Jadid was more than drill: it included the military schools, the translated manuals, the arsenals, and the medical service that made a standing army sustainable. It turned peasant conscripts into soldiers and made Egypt's the first non-European army of the nineteenth century to master European warfare at scale.

### Q: Where were Muhammad Ali's soldiers trained?

The principal training ground was the camp at Aswan, far up the Nile, roughly nine hundred kilometers from Cairo, chosen deliberately for its isolation. Peasant recruits seized by press gangs were marched there and drilled by French officers, and the surrounding desert made desertion nearly suicidal: a man who ran faced empty country with no water, food, or hiding place. The distance also kept the experiment away from the political intrigues of the capital and the hostile Turkish military establishment in Cairo. Supporting schools grew up around Cairo itself, including the staff college, artillery and infantry schools, and the medical school at Abu Zaabal. Aswan was the sealed laboratory where fellahin were remade into soldiers; Cairo's schools produced the officers, surgeons, and technicians the new army required.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali's army fight the Wahhabis in Arabia?

Yes, in a long and brutal campaign from 1811 to 1818 fought on the Ottoman sultan's orders. The Wahhabi movement had captured Mecca and Medina, humiliating the sultan as custodian of the holy cities, and Sultan Mahmud II commanded Muhammad Ali to crush it. The first expedition under his son Tusun went badly, with an Egyptian column ambushed at Wadi al-Safra and Medina changing hands. Ibrahim Pasha then took command, applied the improving discipline of the new regiments, and by 1818 had marched into the Najd and destroyed the Wahhabi capital at Diriyah after a months-long siege. Mecca and Medina returned to Ottoman control, in practice garrisoned and administered by Egyptians. The war proved the new army could campaign for years hundreds of miles from home, and it bought Muhammad Ali the prestige that funded his later ambitions.

### Q: What happened to Muhammad Ali's navy at Navarino?

It was annihilated on October 20, 1827, in the bay of Navarino off the southwestern Morea. Ibrahim Pasha's Egyptian forces had nearly reconquered the peninsula for the sultan, and Britain, France, and Russia, each pursuing its own interests, agreed to impose an armistice. When Ibrahim refused to comply, their combined squadrons sailed into the bay where the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet lay at anchor and destroyed it in a few hours of close-range cannonade, suffering few losses themselves. The Egyptian infantry marched out of the Morea intact, but Egyptian sea power ceased to exist. Under allied pressure Ibrahim evacuated Greece in 1828. Navarino fixed the permanent outer limit of Muhammad Ali's ambitions: his army could beat any force in the Middle East, but it could not survive contact with the concentrated navies of the great European powers.

### Q: How did the 1840 settlement limit Muhammad Ali's army?

The Convention of London of July 1840 and the settlement confirmed in 1841 amputated the force. Muhammad Ali withdrew from Syria, Palestine, the Hijaz, and Crete, surrendering every conquest that had made him a Mediterranean power. In exchange his family received the hereditary rule of Egypt, but the grant was fettered: his army was capped at 18,000 men, a fraction of the force that had humbled the sultan, and his navy was sharply restricted. The peasant regiments that had marched to Konya and Nezib were disbanded in droves, their men returned to the villages they had been dragged from, and the arsenals, schools, and military industries withered. The 18,000-man figure was the number the powers judged sufficient to keep order in Egypt and insufficient to threaten anyone beyond it, freezing Egyptian military development for a generation.

### Q: Where were the conscripts trained?

At camps deliberately placed far from the villages the men had been taken from, principally Aswan in the far south and Farshut in Upper Egypt. The distance was the point. The first Egyptian recruits were peasants seized by press gangs whose first instinct was to run home, and a man who fled a camp at Aswan ran into empty desert with no water and no hiding place. The remoteness also kept the experiment away from the intrigues of the capital and from the hostile gaze of the established Turkish military establishment in Cairo. The camps had been built first for the Sudanese captives whose deaths ended the slave-soldier experiment, and they became the laboratory in which the fellahin were remade.

### Q: Who was Suleiman Pasha?

Joseph Seve, a French officer left without a career after Napoleon's fall, who entered Muhammad Ali's service, converted to Islam, took the name Suleiman Pasha, and became the chief architect of the new Egyptian army. He was the opposite kind of soldier to Ibrahim Pasha and arguably the more indispensable of the two. Where Ibrahim won battles, Suleiman built the instrument that won them: writing drill regulations, supervising the training camps at Aswan and Farshut, establishing the schools, and imposing European method on raw material that European military wisdom had written off. He stayed in Egyptian service for decades and rose to high command, and his career illustrates the bargain at the heart of the project, European knowledge bought with Egyptian money and applied to Egyptian bodies.

### Q: How did villagers try to avoid the levy?

By every means available to people with no political voice. Men fled to the towns or into the desert when the conscripting officers approached. Families paid bribes they could not afford, and village headmen, who were held responsible for producing the quota, sold exemptions and decided whose sons went. Some men blinded themselves in one eye or knocked out the teeth needed to bite a cartridge, a documented practice of self-mutilation that testifies to the desperation the system produced. Whole villages concealed their young men or watched the quota fall on the households without protectors. When resistance turned collective it became revolt, and revolt was broken by troops without changing the system. Desertion from the ranks remained a permanent condition of the army.

### Q: What happened at the Battle of Konya?

Ibrahim Pasha destroyed an Ottoman field army in central Anatolia in December 1832 and captured the grand vizier commanding it. The engagement was less a battle than a demolition: Egyptian infantry drilled in European tactics stood and delivered disciplined volleys, Egyptian artillery broke up Ottoman formations before they could close, and once the first Ottoman line gave way the rest followed. The road to Istanbul lay open afterward and Ibrahim did not take it, because his father held him back, wanting concessions from the sultan rather than the destruction of the empire and knowing that a march on the capital would bring the European powers down on him. The settlement that followed, the Convention of Kutahya in 1833, granted him Syria and Adana.
