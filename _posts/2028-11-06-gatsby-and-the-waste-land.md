Three years separate the two most quoted American accounts of a spiritually dead modern world, and they are not two novels. One is a poem of four hundred and thirty-odd lines published in 1922. The other is a short novel published in April 1925. Reading Gatsby and T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land together is not a stunt assignment, because the connection is documented on both sides: Fitzgerald admired Eliot enough to inscribe a copy of his novel to him as the greatest living poet, and Eliot wrote back that he had read the book three times and considered it the first step American fiction had taken since Henry James. Two writers who did not share a country of residence, a form, or a temperament recognized each other immediately, and the reason is visible in the first paragraph of the novel's second chapter.

![Gatsby and T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land](/assets/images/blog/blog-88.webp)

What they share is a landscape. Both works place a ruined, sterile terrain at the center of a modern civilization and then refuse to redeem it. Eliot's is dry, stony, and thirsty. Fitzgerald's is grey, powdered, and industrial. Neither writer offers the reader a way out of it. But the two landscapes fail in opposite ways, and that opposition is where the comparison earns its keep. Eliot's world dies of drought. Fitzgerald's world is surrounded by water on every side and dies anyway, which is a bleaker proposition than the poem's, and one the novel arrives at by inversion rather than imitation.

Every reference to Eliot's poem in what follows is described rather than quoted. The poem's imagery is summarized in plain terms so the comparison can be built without reproducing the text, and every Fitzgerald passage is quoted directly, since the novel entered the public domain in 2021 and the sentences are the evidence.

## What Is Being Compared in Gatsby and T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land

The comparison runs between one poem and one novel, which is unusual enough to need justifying before it can be used.

The Waste Land appeared in 1922, first in the British magazine The Criterion in October, then in the American magazine The Dial in November, then as a book from Boni and Liveright in December with the author's notes attached. It runs to five titled sections and moves without transition between voices, languages, cities, and centuries. Ezra Pound cut it heavily from a much longer draft. Eliot's own notes point readers to Jessie Weston's study of Grail legend and to Frazer's comparative anthropology, which supply the poem's governing figure: a wounded king whose infertility has spread to his land, and a quest that might or might not restore it.

The Great Gatsby appeared from Scribner's on April 10, 1925. It runs about fifty thousand words across nine chapters, narrated by one man recalling a single summer. Its surface is orderly where the poem's is fractured, and its diction is uniform where the poem's is a collision of registers.

The justification for comparing them is not formal. It is that they render the same object. Both take the modern industrial West and describe it as a place where fertility, belief, and love have gone inert, and both use a physical ruin to say so rather than stating it. When two works of different genres produce the same central image within three years and their authors recognize each other's achievement, the pairing is not a coincidence to be explained away. It is a period speaking in two voices.

### Which came first, the poem or the novel?

The poem. Eliot published The Waste Land in 1922 and Fitzgerald published The Great Gatsby in April 1925, a gap of about two and a half years. The chronology matters because it makes influence possible in one direction only, and because the poem had already become the defining literary event for Fitzgerald's generation by the time he was drafting.

## The Documented Connection

Most cross-genre comparisons are built on resemblance alone. This one has a paper trail, and using it correctly is what separates a strong essay from a speculative one.

Fitzgerald sent Eliot a copy of the novel with an inscription naming him the greatest of living poets. The gesture is not casual admiration; it is a novelist identifying the poet as the standard. Eliot, then working in London and about to take up his position at the publishing house that became Faber and Faber, replied at the end of December 1925. His assessment, paraphrased, was that he had read the novel three times and that it represented the first advance American fiction had made since Henry James. Coming from a critic who was in the process of rearranging the entire English poetic canon, and who was not generous by disposition, that is an unusually strong verdict. The letter was later printed among the material Edmund Wilson gathered as The Crack-Up in 1945, which is how it reached general readers.

The second piece of evidence is internal to Fitzgerald's drafting. Among the titles he considered for the novel was Among Ash-Heaps and Millionaires. That title puts the ash heaps first, ahead of the wealth, and names the book after a landscape rather than after a man. He also considered Trimalchio in West Egg, Gold-Hatted Gatsby, The High-Bouncing Lover, and Under the Red, White, and Blue. The one he nearly used foregrounds the waste ground that occupies a single chapter of the finished book. A writer who considers naming his novel after an ash heap has already decided that the ash heap is the thesis.

The third piece is the timing. The poem appeared while Fitzgerald was between This Side of Paradise and the work that became Gatsby. It was, for writers of his cohort, the event against which contemporary seriousness was measured. Claiming that Fitzgerald sat down with the poem open and wrote his second chapter from it would overreach the evidence. Claiming that he wrote a wasteland into the center of his novel while the most discussed poem of the decade was a wasteland is not overreach at all. It is what the record supports, and it is enough.

### Is the kinship between the two works mere coincidence?

No. The chronology allows influence, the two authors exchanged documented admiration, and Fitzgerald considered naming the novel after its ash heaps. Independent invention of the same central image in the same three years, by two writers who recognized each other's work, is a shared response to a shared moment rather than an accident.

## The Genuine Points of Contact

Five points, each anchored in a specific Fitzgerald passage and a described feature of the poem.

The first is the ruined landscape as the moral center. Fitzgerald introduces the valley in the opening lines of Chapter Two and does not soften it. It is "a valley of ashes," "a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens," a place "where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke." The vocabulary is agricultural applied to sterility: a farm that grows nothing, gardens made of waste. Eliot's poem opens on a comparable inversion, a spring that stirs dead ground and is described as cruel for doing so, and returns repeatedly to stony, rootless terrain. Both writers use the language of growth to describe places where nothing grows, and both mean the reversal as the point.

The second is the anonymous crowd of the modern city. Fitzgerald's ash-grey men "swarm up with leaden spades" and "stir up an impenetrable cloud" that hides them. They are given no names, no dialogue, and no faces, and the cloud they raise erases them from the reader's view within a sentence of their appearance. Eliot's poem contains a comparable procession, a crowd of commuters moving across a bridge into a city he calls unreal, each with eyes fixed downward. Both are images of labor and transit that convert people into a mass. Neither writer individualizes them, and the refusal is deliberate.

The third is the presiding eye that does not intervene. Above the valley hang the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, "blue and gigantic," their "retinas are one yard high," and they "look out of no face." They belong to an oculist's advertisement whose owner has moved away or died, and as the paint fails they "brood on over the solemn dumping ground." Eliot's poem has a presiding consciousness of comparable strangeness: a blind prophet who has lived as both sexes and who sees everything in the poem without altering any of it. Neither figure is a god, both are treated as if they might be, and both watch a sterile scene without acting.

The fourth is the joyless sexual encounter rendered without heat. Fitzgerald gives us the apartment on 158th Street, where Tom keeps Myrtle, a room too small for its furniture, a dog bought on impulse from a man on the street, a party that gets drunk in the afternoon and ends in violence when Myrtle says Daisy's name. Eliot's poem contains its own scene of mechanical intimacy in a rented room, ending in indifference rather than in anything. Both writers make an encounter that should carry life carry nothing.

The fifth is the ending that refuses resolution. Eliot closes on a sequence of fragments in several languages and a repeated formula of peace borrowed from Sanskrit scripture, which is a resolution in form only, since the fragments do not assemble. Fitzgerald closes on a narrator alone on a beach, imagining a continent nobody alive has seen, and a current that carries everyone backward. Neither ending restores the land. One breaks apart; the other flows back.
## The Decisive Difference: The Dry Waste and the Drowned Waste

This is the article's central claim, and it is the reason the pairing produces an argument rather than a list of parallels.

Eliot's wasteland is defined by absence of water. The poem's governing figure is a wounded king whose land has gone infertile, and the cure, if there is one, is rain. Dryness organizes the imagery from the first section to the last: cracked earth, stone without water, a longing for a sound that does not come. When water does appear it is either the wrong kind, a drowning in the shortest section, or a river carrying the city's refuse. The poem's final movement waits under thunder for rain that is promised and not delivered on the page. Thirst is the condition, and relief is the thing withheld.

Fitzgerald's wasteland has the opposite problem. West Egg and East Egg sit on a bay. The Sound is visible from every important window. Gatsby's lawn runs down to the water and the green light burns across it. There is a swimming pool he does not use all summer, an ocean liner Nick imagines, a hydroplane, and a boat that anchors on Lake Superior and starts the whole story. Water is not scarce in this novel. It is the medium the entire plot floats on, and it produces nothing.

Consider what water actually does in Gatsby. It divides the lovers for five years and then, when the bay is finally crossed, the crossing is worthless. It provides the green light, which is an object of longing precisely because water lies between the looker and the thing looked at, and the moment Daisy stands beside him Gatsby's "count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." It arrives as rain on the afternoon of the reunion in Chapter Five, soaking Gatsby in his white flannel suit before the meeting and then clearing, and the rain does not renew anything; it only makes an awkward scene wetter. And at the end it kills him. He dies in his own pool in the first days of autumn, floating on a pneumatic mattress, in water he had not entered once all summer.

Set the two together and the finding is sharp. Eliot's land is barren because the water will not come. Fitzgerald's world is barren although the water is everywhere. That is a harder verdict. A drought can end. A drought is a condition with a cure, which is why the poem can spend its last section waiting for thunder and mean something by the waiting. Fitzgerald removes even the structure of waiting. There is nothing to arrive, because everything that might have arrived is already present and inert.

Call this the dry waste and the drowned waste. The framework is portable, and it does what a good comparative model should do: it explains features neither work announces. It explains why Eliot's poem has a quest structure and Fitzgerald's novel has none. A quest implies a cure exists somewhere; Fitzgerald's characters do not go looking for one, they go to the Plaza. It explains why Eliot ends in fragments and Fitzgerald ends in a current. Fragments are what a broken thing leaves; a current is what a saturated thing does. And it explains the shape of the two closing gestures. Eliot's speaker shores fragments against his own ruin, an act of salvage. Nick performs no salvage. He goes home to the Midwest and reports that the East remained distorted for him afterward.

### How do the two works treat water and drought?

Eliot builds his wasteland on the absence of water and organizes the poem around waiting for rain that never falls on the page. Fitzgerald surrounds his world with water, a bay, a pool, a rainstorm, and lets none of it renew anything. Gatsby dies in a swimming pool.

## Eckleburg and the Blind Witness

The figure that most often draws students to this comparison is Doctor Eckleburg, and the comparison is sound if it is made carefully.

Fitzgerald is precise about what the eyes are and are not. They are painted on a billboard by an oculist who wanted to build his practice in Queens and then "sank down himself into eternal blindness, or forgot them and moved away." They are enormous, blue, faceless, and abandoned. Their power in the novel comes from being an advertisement that has outlived its commercial purpose and acquired, without earning it, the appearance of a moral instrument. In Chapter Eight, George Wilson stares at them and says, "God sees everything." Michaelis, standing next to him, corrects him: "That's an advertisement." Fitzgerald puts both readings in the text within two lines of each other and declines to adjudicate. The eyes look like judgment and are made of paint.

Eliot's presiding figure is a blind prophet drawn from classical myth, who in the poem's own notes is identified as the most important personage and the one in whom the poem's separate figures meet. He sees, in the sense that he witnesses everything, and he is blind, in the sense that he has no eyes and cannot intervene. He suffers what he observes and changes nothing.

The pairing works because both writers solved the same problem the same way. A poem or a novel about a spiritually vacant world needs some position from which the vacancy is registered, or the reader has no purchase on it. Traditional fiction supplies a moral narrator; traditional poetry supplies a speaker with a stable position. Neither Eliot nor Fitzgerald wanted a moralist, because a moralist implies a standard still in force. So each installed a witness that has the shape of authority and none of its function: a blind seer, a faceless advertisement. Something is watching, and it can do nothing, and it may not exist.

Where the pairing must stop is on the question of divinity. Eliot's figure is mythological and the poem places it inside a religious framework it takes seriously, even in ruins. Fitzgerald's eyes are commercial, and the novel's joke, if that is the word, is that in a country where the sacred has been replaced by advertising, an oculist's signboard is the only thing left overhead. Eliot is describing a religion that has failed. Fitzgerald is describing a country that replaced religion with a product and did not notice the substitution. Those are neighboring positions, not identical ones, and an essay that flattens them loses the sharpest thing it has.

## The Unreal City and the View from the Bridge

Eliot's phrase for London, which recurs in the poem, presents the modern metropolis as a place with the appearance of solidity and no substance, crossed each morning by a crowd of the effectively dead. It is a vision of a city as a machine for processing people who are already finished.

Fitzgerald's corresponding passage is one of the few genuinely rapturous moments in the novel, and the contrast is instructive. Crossing into Manhattan in Chapter Four, Nick describes the approach: "Over the great bridge, with the sunlight through the girders making a constant flicker upon the moving cars, with the city rising up across the river in white heaps and sugar lumps all built with a wish out of nonolfactory money." Then the summary judgment: "the city seen from the Queensboro Bridge is always the city seen for the first time, in its first wild promise of all the mystery and the beauty in the world." Gatsby, in the same scene, has just told Nick his manufactured life story, and Nick has just been shown a medal from Montenegro and a photograph from Oxford. The chapter ends with the introduction of Meyer Wolfshiem and a set of human molars used as cuff links.

The move here is Fitzgerald's most characteristic and his most un-Eliotic. He grants the city its full promise and then walks the reader immediately into its underside, and he refuses to withdraw the promise. The city is "built with a wish out of nonolfactory money," which concedes the whole indictment in a clause and keeps the wonder anyway. Nick says, "Anything can happen now that we've slid over this bridge," and within two pages what happens is organized crime at lunch.

Eliot does not do this. His city is unreal from its first appearance and is never granted a moment of beauty that the poem then withdraws. He begins with the diagnosis. Fitzgerald begins with the enchantment and lets the diagnosis catch up to it, which is why the novel can be read, wrongly but persistently, as a celebration of the era it dismantles. The poem cannot be misread that way, and the difference is not accidental. Eliot wants the reader to see through the surface. Fitzgerald wants the reader to feel the pull of the surface and then to feel what it costs, a strategy examined throughout the series treatment of [dust and ash imagery in The Great Gatsby](/2025/10/06/dust-ash-imagery-gatsby/).

## The Valley as Real Place and as Figure

One further distinction protects the comparison from a common error.

Eliot's wasteland is nowhere. It is assembled from London, the Thames, a desert, a chapel, the Ganges, and a set of literary and anthropological sources, and it is deliberately unlocatable. That is the point of the method: a condition that belongs to no single place because it belongs to the civilization.

Fitzgerald's valley is somewhere. It sits between West Egg and Manhattan, it has a road and a railway and a drawbridge over "a small foul river," and it corresponds to an actual ash dump in Queens that the city was filling through the 1920s and later cleared. The series article on [the valley of ashes and Corona's dump](/2027/05/31/valley-of-ashes-corona-dump/) sets out the documentary background. Fitzgerald wrote a real municipal waste site into his novel and gave it a name that turns it into an emblem, and the emblem never fully detaches from the site.

That difference has consequences for how each work argues. Eliot's method is mythic: he claims a universal condition and supports it with fragments from many cultures. Fitzgerald's method is documentary and local: he claims a specific arrangement, in which one city's wealth is produced and disposed of in a corridor everyone drives through, and supports it with a place you could visit. Both arrive at barrenness. One reaches it by generalization, the other by particular. The full symbolic reading of the site is developed in the series analysis of [the valley of ashes as moral wasteland](/2025/05/12/valley-of-ashes-wasteland/).

An essay that treats Fitzgerald's valley as purely figurative loses this, and it loses the strongest thing the novel has that the poem does not: an address.
## Seasons, Rebirth, and the Refusal of Renewal

The seasonal structure is where the two works align most precisely and where the alignment is easiest to overlook.

Eliot opens by reversing the oldest convention in English poetry. Spring, which the tradition uses for renewal, is presented as an affliction: it disturbs ground that had settled into a comfortable deadness and forces growth on a land that would rather stay frozen. Winter is described as the kind season, because forgetting is easier than remembering. The reversal establishes the poem's whole position in a few lines. Renewal is not withheld from this world; renewal is unwanted, because what would be revived is unbearable.

Fitzgerald builds his novel on the same calendar and reaches the same conclusion by a different route. The story begins in the spring of 1922 with Nick buying books on banking and credit and registering "that familiar conviction that life was beginning over again with the summer". The parties run through July and August, the crisis comes in the hottest week the narrator can remember, and Gatsby dies in the first cool days of autumn. The seasonal arc looks conventional and is not. Nick says at one point that "Life starts all over again when it gets crisp in the fall," which is a Midwestern sentiment about school terms and new beginnings, and the novel disproves it within pages by killing its protagonist in exactly that weather.

Notice what neither writer allows. Eliot will not permit spring to renew; Fitzgerald will not permit autumn to restart. Both are dismantling the same assumption, that time is cyclical and therefore forgiving, and both replace it with something linear and one-directional. In the poem, the cycle turns and produces nothing but disturbance. In the novel, the cycle appears to turn and then simply stops: Gatsby's pool is drained the week he dies, the gardener has been waiting for the leaves, and the house goes dark.

The autumn material is the more precise inversion. Fitzgerald had available to him the ordinary consolation, that a death in September is followed by a spring. He refuses it. Nick's last chapter contains no next spring for anyone in the book. It contains a funeral almost nobody attends, a broken engagement, an encounter with Tom on Fifth Avenue, and a departure for the Midwest. Then the narrative leaves the calendar entirely and goes back three hundred years to Dutch sailors, which is a way of saying that the only renewal available in this world happened before any of it began and cannot recur.

## Voices: One Narrator Against Many

The formal difference between the two works is usually stated as a difference of genre, which explains nothing. The real difference is the number of positions from which the wasteland is reported.

Eliot uses many. The poem moves without warning between a Lithuanian aristocrat's memory, a fortune teller with a bad cold, a woman in an expensive room whose nerves are failing, two women in a pub at closing time, a typist, a clerk, a Thames maiden, and a blind prophet who contains them all. The voices do not agree, are not introduced, and are frequently unattributable on a first reading. This produces the poem's central effect: a wasteland has no single vantage, because everyone in it is equally inside it.

Fitzgerald uses one, and the choice costs him something Eliot gets for free. Nick Carraway is a single consciousness with a stated class position, a family stake, and a personal involvement with three of the principals. Everything the reader knows about the valley of ashes, the Wilsons, and the ash-grey men arrives through a Yale man from Minnesota who is passing through on a commuter train. He never speaks to a single ash worker. The valley is described from a railway carriage and a car, twice from a stopped train and once during a stop for gasoline, and it is never entered on foot except to reach a garage.

That limitation is not a flaw and is not accidental. Fitzgerald is showing the reader precisely how the wasteland is seen by the people whose lives depend on its existing: through glass, at speed, as landscape. The poem's method makes the reader inhabit the ruin from inside many lives. The novel's method makes the reader observe it from a train window with someone who finds it depressing and then goes to lunch. Which is more damning is a defensible question, and it is a much better essay topic than asking which work is sadder.

The one place Fitzgerald breaks the pattern is Chapter Eight, where the narration slides into Michaelis's night with George Wilson, hours Nick could not have witnessed. That chapter is the only sustained time the novel spends inside the valley rather than passing over it, and it is where the eyes on the billboard get their most explicit reading and their immediate deflation. Fitzgerald enters the wasteland exactly once, at the moment it produces the killing, and then leaves.

## What the Parties Are Doing

The poem has no equivalent of Gatsby's parties, and the absence is worth an essay paragraph on its own.

Eliot's world contains social life, but it is uniformly joyless: a pub conversation at closing time, a nervous exchange in an expensive room, an encounter that both parties want over. There is no glamour anywhere in the poem, and no scene a reader would want to attend. The wasteland is total.

Fitzgerald's is not total, and this is the source of both his popularity and his critical difficulty. His novel contains the most seductive parties in American fiction: the orchestra, the buffet tables with spiced baked hams, the lights, the cars arriving from the city, the girl who sings and cries with mascara running, the guest in the library with owl-eyed spectacles marveling that the books are real. Readers finish the book wanting to have been there, which no reader has ever finished The Waste Land wanting.

The parties are the mechanism by which the novel conceals its own wasteland from the reader for as long as possible, and the concealment mirrors the concealment inside the story. The valley sits between the houses and the city, and the party guests drive through it in both directions without registering it. Fitzgerald gives his reader the same experience: two chapters of ash, seven chapters of light, and the ash keeps producing the plot. A reader who reaches the end and says the book is about parties has been caught by exactly the trick the book is about.

Eliot could not have used this device, because his form does not permit a seductive surface. Fitzgerald's genre gave him one, and his decision to keep the surface intact while the fracture runs underneath is the single most consequential formal difference between the two works. It is also why one of them is assigned to sixteen-year-olds every year and the other is not.

## Three Misreadings to Retire

The pairing attracts three errors reliably enough to be worth pre-empting in the body of any essay.

The first is treating the resemblance as the finding. Establishing that both works depict a barren modern world takes one paragraph and is where a weak essay stops. The resemblance is the setup. The finding is what each writer does with the barrenness, and the fastest route to it is the water axis: Eliot's is caused by absence and Fitzgerald's persists amid abundance.

The second is quoting the poem at length and calling it analysis. A long block of Eliot dropped into a paragraph transfers the burden of interpretation to the reader and usually swamps whatever the student was arguing. Describe the poem's images in your own words and reserve quotation for the novel, whose text is public domain and whose sentences are built to be lifted.

The third is flattening Eckleburg into a straightforward God figure and then claiming Eliot's prophet is the same thing. Fitzgerald blocks this reading himself. When Wilson says God sees everything, Michaelis answers that it is an advertisement, and the novel prints both lines and adjudicates neither. The eyes are a commercial object that has accumulated the appearance of moral authority through abandonment, which is a much stranger and more modern proposition than divine surveillance. Eliot's figure sits inside a religious tradition the poem still takes seriously. Fitzgerald's sits inside a country that replaced the tradition with signage.

## Where the Comparison Should Stop

Every comparative framework has a limit, and naming it is a mark of control rather than a concession.

The two works do not share a theory of what went wrong. Eliot's poem is a religious document in the making, written by a man moving toward the Anglican church, and its wasteland is the condition of a civilization that lost a tradition. The Grail material in his sources is not decoration; it supplies a model in which the wound is spiritual and the cure would be sacramental. Fitzgerald has no equivalent framework and shows no interest in acquiring one. His ruin is produced by money, class, and the disposal of what industry leaves behind, and nothing in the novel suggests that a restored tradition would fix it.

They also do not share an audience or an ambition of scale. Eliot wrote for a readership prepared to chase citations across five languages and to consult notes. Fitzgerald wrote a novel that a general reader could finish in an evening and that has since become a standard classroom text partly for that reason. Comparing their difficulty as though it measured seriousness misreads both.

And they do not share a relationship to America. The Waste Land is a European poem by an American expatriate, with London at its center and the American material largely absent. Gatsby is a novel about the meaning of a continent, ending on a page about Dutch sailors and a green breast of the new world. Eliot's wasteland is Western civilization. Fitzgerald's is a specific national promise that turned out to be a light on somebody else's dock.
## Four Passages That Carry the Argument

A cross-genre comparison is only as strong as the sentences under it, and since the poem is being described rather than quoted, the entire evidentiary weight falls on four Fitzgerald passages. Learning them properly is most of the work.

### The valley, Chapter Two

The description arrives in the chapter's first sentence and is over within a paragraph, which is part of its force. Fitzgerald establishes the landscape as "a valley of ashes," then extends the figure: "a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens," a place "where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke." The ash is not merely present; it is imitating the built world, producing counterfeit houses and counterfeit weather.

Then the people. "Occasionally a line of grey cars crawls along an invisible track, gives out a ghastly creak and comes to rest," and the ash-grey men "swarm up with leaden spades" and "stir up an impenetrable cloud which screens their obscure operations from your sight." Every noun of labor is attached to a verb of concealment. The workers appear and are erased inside one sentence, and the erasure is performed by the material they are moving. Eliot's crowd on the bridge is faceless because it is a crowd; Fitzgerald's is faceless because the ash hides it, which is a more specific and more accusatory image.

The chapter then supplies a geographic fact that makes the valley structural rather than atmospheric: "The valley of ashes is bounded on one side by a small foul river, and, when the drawbridge is up to let barges through, the passengers on waiting trains can stare at the dismal scene for as long as half an hour." Everyone with money must pass this way, and sometimes the bridge goes up and they must look at it. The novel's entire class argument is compressed into a traffic condition.

### The eyes, Chapter Two and Chapter Eight

Fitzgerald introduces the billboard immediately after the valley: the eyes "are blue and gigantic," "their retinas are one yard high," and "they look out of no face, but, instead, from a pair of enormous yellow spectacles which pass over a nonexistent nose." He then gives the commercial history, that some oculist set them there to build a practice in Queens and afterward "sank down himself into eternal blindness, or forgot them and moved away," leaving the eyes to fade and "brood on over the solemn dumping ground."

The payoff arrives six chapters later. Wilson, having sat up all night after his wife's death, tells Michaelis he took Myrtle to the window and said God knew what she had been doing, and then says, "God sees everything." Michaelis, following his gaze, sees only the billboard emerging from the dissolving night and answers, "That's an advertisement." Fitzgerald prints both readings in consecutive lines and adjudicates neither, which is exactly the modernist strategy Eliot uses with his blind prophet: install something that occupies the position of judgment and give it no capacity to judge.

### The bridge, Chapter Four

The city passage supplies the enchantment Eliot never grants. "Over the great bridge, with the sunlight through the girders making a constant flicker upon the moving cars, with the city rising up across the river in white heaps and sugar lumps all built with a wish out of nonolfactory money." The phrase built with a wish concedes that the whole thing is imaginary, and the word nonolfactory concedes that the money has a smell it has been engineered not to have, and the sentence remains rapturous anyway. Then the general claim: "the city seen from the Queensboro Bridge is always the city seen for the first time, in its first wild promise of all the mystery and the beauty in the world."

Immediately afterward the reader meets Wolfshiem at lunch. Fitzgerald's method throughout is to grant and then invoice, and this passage is the clearest instance.

### The pool, Chapter Eight

The last passage is the one that closes the water argument, and it is domestic rather than lyrical. The gardener comes to the steps and says, "I'm going to drain the pool today, Mr. Gatsby. Leaves'll start falling pretty soon, and then there's always trouble with the pipes." Gatsby tells him not to, then turns to Nick and says, "You know, old sport, I've never used that pool all summer?"

Two pages later he is dead in it. Fitzgerald built the entire novel next to water, gave his protagonist a private body of it, had him leave it untouched for the whole season, and then killed him there on the day it was scheduled to be emptied. Eliot's wasteland is waiting for rain that does not come. Fitzgerald's central character owns his rain, never enters it, and drowns in it on a mattress. An essay that ends on this passage has ended on an argument rather than a summary.
## What Each Work Illuminates in the Other

The test of a comparison is whether it changes both readings. This one does.

### What the poem shows you about the novel

Reading Eliot first makes the valley of ashes structurally visible rather than atmospheric. Students routinely treat Chapter Two as a mood piece, a grim interlude before the parties resume. Set beside a poem whose entire architecture is a barren land, the valley stops being an interlude and becomes the novel's foundation. Fitzgerald returns to it in Chapter Two, Chapter Seven for the crucial stop at Wilson's garage, Chapter Seven again for the accident, and Chapter Eight for Wilson's vigil and the killing. Every consequential event in the last third of the book happens in or because of that corridor. The parties are the interlude.

The poem also clarifies why Fitzgerald's imagery of dust and grey extends far beyond Chapter Two. Nick's first paragraph announces that what closed out his interest was "what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams," which places the dust in the novel's opening frame, before any ash heap has appeared. Gatsby's abandoned house at the end wears "a thin beard of raw ivy" and the household has collapsed so that "the whole caravansary had fallen in like a card house." Reading with the poem in view, these stop looking like scattered dark images and start looking like a single sustained condition that the parties temporarily conceal.

### What the novel shows you about the poem

Running it the other way is less common and more valuable. Fitzgerald's local, documentary method exposes how much Eliot's poem gains and loses by refusing an address. The poem's power comes from its claim to universality: this is not London's condition, it is the condition. But universality has a cost, which is that no one is responsible. A land that has gone infertile through a mythic wound has no owner and no polluter. Fitzgerald's ash heap has both. Somebody dumps there, somebody profits from what the dumping serves, and a family lives at the edge of it running a garage that fails.

The novel therefore supplies what the poem systematically withholds: a distribution of responsibility. Tom and Daisy drive through the valley to reach their entertainment and drive back through it afterward, and Nick's verdict on them is that they "were careless people" who "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money." Eliot's poem contains no equivalent sentence, because it contains no equivalent structure. Reading Gatsby against the poem clarifies that Eliot wrote a diagnosis and Fitzgerald wrote an indictment, and that the two are not the same intellectual act.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict this article defends: Gatsby is not an imitation of The Waste Land, nor a coincidence beside it. It is the same diagnosis delivered by inversion, and the inversion makes Fitzgerald's version darker in one specific respect while making Eliot's larger in another.

Darker in Fitzgerald: a wasteland caused by absence contains, structurally, the idea of its own remedy. Eliot's poem is organized around a wound that could in principle be healed and a rain that could in principle fall, which is why its last section can be built out of waiting and why its closing formula, however fragmented, is a benediction. Fitzgerald removes the remedy from the architecture. His characters are not deprived of anything. They have water, money, houses, cars, music, and each other, and the barrenness is produced by the having. Nothing is withheld from them, which means nothing can be granted.

Larger in Eliot: the poem's claim is civilizational and multilingual and reaches across centuries, where Fitzgerald's stays inside one summer on one coast of one country. The scale difference is real and should not be argued away. Eliot is describing the condition of a culture; Fitzgerald is describing the condition of a class in a decade.

The deciding factor between the two, and the thing to build an essay on, is what each writer thinks a wasteland is for. For Eliot it is a diagnosis of a spiritual condition, offered by a poet who would shortly enter the Anglican church and who was already looking for the tradition that might answer it. For Fitzgerald it is an indictment of an economic arrangement, offered by a novelist who names the people who drive through the ruin without stopping. Eliot's wasteland asks what has been lost. Fitzgerald's asks who is dumping. Both questions are serious, and neither answers the other, but the second one has an address on it and the first does not.

That is why the comparison holds and why it produces argument rather than a checklist. Two writers in the same three years reached for the same image and used it for different intellectual work, and the difference in the work is legible in the difference in the water.
## Two Reputations, One Recovery

The afterlives of the poem and the novel diverged sharply and then converged, and the convergence is part of why the pairing became standard.

The Waste Land was recognized immediately. It provoked hostility and bafflement in some quarters, but nobody mistook it for a minor work, and within a decade it had become the reference point against which serious poetry in English was measured. Eliot's critical prose reinforced the position, and his editorial role at Faber gave him institutional power over what the next generation read. His reputation never required rescue.

Gatsby's did. The novel sold modestly against expectations in 1925, well below the numbers Fitzgerald's earlier books had reached, and reviews were mixed. By the time Fitzgerald died in December 1940 the book had effectively left circulation and he regarded himself as a failure. The reversal came in the 1940s and 1950s, driven partly by wartime distribution that put the novel into the hands of a very large number of readers who had no prior relationship to Fitzgerald's celebrity, and partly by a generation of academic critics who arrived with modernist standards and found that the novel satisfied them.

Those standards were, in large part, Eliot's. The critical apparatus that made Gatsby look like a major work, close attention to imagery, an interest in the unreliable or limited perspective, a taste for symbolic structure over plot, a willingness to treat a novel the way one treats a poem, was assembled by a criticism that had learned its habits from modernist poetry. Eliot's own verdict in 1925 anticipated the reading by twenty years. He saw the novel as an advance in fiction because he was reading it with the equipment he had built for reading poems, and once the profession adopted that equipment, everyone else saw what he had seen.

This is worth stating plainly in an essay because it corrects a common assumption. The pairing of Gatsby with The Waste Land is not a modern classroom convenience. It reflects the historical fact that the novel's reputation was manufactured by a critical culture Eliot helped create, and that Eliot himself was the first significant reader to place the book where the culture eventually put it.

## Reading Order and Practical Advice

For a student meeting both texts, the sequence matters more than it usually does.

Read the novel first if the assignment is comparative and you have limited time. Gatsby is short, its surface is transparent, and it will give you a stable set of images, the ash heaps, the eyes, the pool, the green light, to hold up against the poem. Approaching the poem without any anchor produces the standard experience of reading four hundred lines and retaining nothing.

Read the poem's five sections in order and do not chase every citation on a first pass. Eliot's notes are useful and were also, by his own later admission, partly a device to fill out a thin book. A first reading that follows the imagery of dryness, water, and the city, and ignores the source-hunting, will produce a working understanding sufficient for a comparative essay. The Grail material matters for one thing only in this context: it establishes that the poem's barrenness is a wound with a theoretical cure, which is the exact feature Fitzgerald removes.

When you write, keep the two texts in a fixed relation on the page. The strongest structure alternates within paragraphs rather than between them: state the axis, describe the poem's version in a sentence or two, quote the novel's version, then say what the difference buys. A paragraph that spends four sentences on Eliot and then four on Fitzgerald has produced two summaries adjacent to each other, which is the most common failure mode in cross-genre work and the easiest to avoid once it has been named.
## How to Write a Comparative Essay on the Poem and the Novel

Cross-genre comparisons are graded harder than same-genre ones, because the temptation to compare loosely is stronger. Four rules keep the essay tight.

Do not quote the poem. This is partly a practical matter of permissions and partly a strategic one. A described image you control beats a block quotation that sits inert in your paragraph and invites the grader to ask what you did with it. Write that the poem opens on a spring that stirs dead ground and is called cruel for it, and then move immediately to what Fitzgerald does with the same reversal. Quote the novel freely, since its text is public domain and its sentences excerpt cleanly.

Build the thesis on the inversion, not the resemblance. A thesis that says both works depict a spiritually barren modern world has stated the premise, not the argument. A thesis that says Fitzgerald inverts Eliot's governing condition, replacing drought with saturation, and thereby removes the possibility of remedy that the poem preserves, has an engine in it. The inversion is the finding; the resemblance is the setup.

Anchor every claim in a Fitzgerald passage. Four carry the weight. The valley description in Chapter Two supplies the landscape. The Eckleburg passage supplies the witness, especially the pairing of Wilson's claim that God sees everything with Michaelis's correction that it is an advertisement. The Queensboro Bridge passage in Chapter Four supplies the city and the enchantment Fitzgerald grants that Eliot never does. The final page supplies the ending. A five-paragraph essay uses three of these; a longer paper uses all four and the table's six axes.

Address the coincidence objection early. Some readers will say the parallel is imposed by critics rather than present in the works. Answer with the record: the chronology permits influence, Fitzgerald inscribed a copy of the novel to Eliot as the greatest living poet, Eliot replied that he had read it three times and thought it the first advance in American fiction since James, and Fitzgerald considered titling the novel after its ash heaps. That is four pieces of evidence, and it converts the objection into a paragraph you have already won. Readers who want to work through the novel's wasteland sentences directly can [read and annotate The Great Gatsby free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), where the Chapter Two description and the Eckleburg passage sit beside the notes and can be marked up as evidence.

One further move separates a strong paper from a competent one. Extend the framework to a third case. If the dry waste and the drowned waste are real categories, they should sort works you were not assigned. Postwar novels of suburban affluence generally belong to the drowned waste, where the barrenness comes from abundance. Novels of scarcity and deprivation belong to the dry waste, where the barrenness has a cause that could be removed. A framework that sorts unfamiliar cases is a contribution; one that only relabels the two texts on your syllabus is a summary with a name attached.


A final structural note on length. Comparative essays on this pairing tend to run short because students feel they have said the main thing once the inversion is stated. The remedy is to work the table's six axes in sequence rather than restating the water argument in new words. The witness figures support a full paragraph on their own, since the difference between a blind prophet inside a religious tradition and an abandoned advertisement inside a commercial one is genuinely large. The city passages support another, since Fitzgerald grants an enchantment Eliot refuses. The endings support a third. Six axes, three of them developed properly, will carry a paper further than one axis restated six times.

## The Modernist Frame Around Both

The pairing also has a placement value, which is worth taking seriously because it explains why the two works keep appearing on the same syllabus.

Both belong to the moment when English-language literature decided that a work about a fractured world should itself be fractured, or at least should refuse to console. Both refuse continuous chronology. Both distribute meaning across recurring objects rather than stating it. Both use a witness figure whose authority is compromised. Both end without restoration. These are the shared technical commitments of the period, examined at length in the series overview of [Gatsby's place among modernist American fiction](/2028/08/21/gatsby-modernist-fiction/).

Where they diverge is on how much surface order to keep. Eliot broke the surface, and the breakage is the argument: a world in pieces produces a poem in pieces. Fitzgerald kept the surface intact, which is why his novel can be handed to a sixteen-year-old, and put the fracture underneath, in the withheld information, the retroactive revisions, and the narrator's own unstable position. Two solutions to the same problem, one visible and one buried.

That divergence is the most useful thing the comparison teaches a student about modernism generally. Difficulty is not the definition of the movement. It is one available technique within it, and Gatsby is the standing proof that a modernist argument about a broken world can be made in transparent prose across nine chapters. A student who has internalized that will read the period more accurately than one who has learned to equate seriousness with obscurity.

## Closing Verdict

Set the two works side by side and the 1920s stop looking like a decade of parties with a hangover attached. They look like a decade in which two writers, working in different genres on different continents, independently concluded that the modern West had become a ruin with entertainment on top of it, and each built a landscape to say so.

Eliot's ruin is dry, mythic, unlocatable, and organized around a rain that might come. Fitzgerald's is wet, documented, situated in Queens, and organized around the discovery that having everything is its own kind of drought. The poem asks what was lost. The novel asks who is dumping. Read them in order, poem first for the diagnosis and novel second for the indictment, and the closing page of Gatsby will land differently. The current that carries everyone backward is not the poem's withheld rain arriving late. It is water that was always there, doing nothing, and finally taking a man who never learned to use his own pool.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does The Great Gatsby compare to The Waste Land?

Both place a ruined, sterile landscape at the center of a modern civilization and refuse to redeem it, and both were produced within three years of each other by writers who recognized each other's achievement. The valley of ashes and Eliot's barren terrain do the same structural work: they carry the argument the text declines to state. The decisive difference is the cause of the barrenness. Eliot's land is dying of drought and the poem is organized around waiting for rain. Fitzgerald's world is surrounded by water, a bay, a rainstorm, a swimming pool, and is barren anyway. A wasteland caused by absence contains the idea of its own cure. A wasteland that persists amid abundance does not, which makes the novel's verdict harder than the poem's even though the poem's scale is larger.

### Q: How does the valley of ashes evoke Eliot's wasteland?

Through an agricultural vocabulary applied to sterility. Fitzgerald calls it a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens, and where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke. Every word of growth is attached to something that does not grow. Eliot performs the same reversal from his opening lines, describing a spring that stirs dead ground and calling it cruel for doing so, and returning throughout to stony, rootless terrain. Both writers also strip the people in the landscape of individuality: Fitzgerald's ash-grey men swarm up with leaden spades and stir up a cloud that hides them within a sentence, and Eliot's commuters cross a bridge as an undifferentiated mass. The technique is identical even though the landscapes differ.

### Q: What modernist vision of emptiness do the works share?

Four commitments. Neither work states its theme; both attach it to recurring objects and let repetition do the arguing. Neither preserves continuous chronology, so meaning is assigned retroactively rather than accumulated forward. Both install a witness figure whose authority is compromised, a blind prophet in one and an abandoned advertisement in the other, so that something appears to be watching and nothing intervenes. And neither ends in restoration; the poem breaks into fragments and the novel flows backward on a current. Where they part is on surface order. Eliot fractured the surface so the form would match the subject. Fitzgerald kept the surface intact and buried the fracture in withheld information and an unstable narrator.

### Q: What wasteland imagery do they share?

Grey, dust, ash, and the erasure of faces. Fitzgerald's dust is not confined to Chapter Two; Nick's opening frame already names what foul dust floated in the wake of Gatsby's dreams, before any ash heap has appeared, and the abandoned mansion at the end wears a thin beard of raw ivy while the household has fallen in like a card house. Eliot's poem works the same register through stone, dryness, and rubbish. Both also share the image of a river carrying waste: Fitzgerald's valley is bounded by a small foul river with a drawbridge, and Eliot's Thames section handles the city's refuse directly. The shared vocabulary is industrial residue used as a spiritual report.

### Q: How did Eliot regard Gatsby?

Highly, and the record is explicit. Fitzgerald sent him a copy inscribed to the greatest of living poets. Eliot replied at the end of December 1925, and his assessment, paraphrased, was that he had read the novel three times and that it represented the first step American fiction had taken since Henry James. The letter later reached general readers through the material Edmund Wilson collected as The Crack-Up in 1945. The weight of that verdict comes from its source. Eliot was in the middle of rearranging the English poetic canon through his criticism and was not a generous reader by temperament. Placing a young American novelist in the line of James was a substantial claim, and it is the strongest external evidence that the two works belong together.

### Q: Is the kinship mere coincidence?

No, and four pieces of evidence retire the objection. The chronology allows influence, since the poem appeared in 1922 and the novel in April 1925. Fitzgerald inscribed a copy of the novel to Eliot naming him the greatest living poet, which establishes that he was reading Eliot with attention. Eliot's reply shows the recognition ran both ways. And among the titles Fitzgerald considered for the book was Among Ash-Heaps and Millionaires, which puts the waste ground ahead of the wealth and names the novel after a landscape. A writer who nearly titles his book after an ash heap has already decided the ash heap is the argument.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald read Eliot's poem before writing the novel?

The honest answer is that his admiration for Eliot is documented and his exposure to the poem is overwhelmingly likely, but a specific claim that he drafted Chapter Two with the poem in front of him would exceed the evidence. What can be stated safely is this: the poem was the defining literary event for writers of Fitzgerald's cohort between 1922 and 1925, Fitzgerald identified Eliot as the standard in his own inscription, and he wrote a wasteland into the center of his novel during exactly that window. For an essay, phrase it as a shared response to a shared moment rather than as direct borrowing. The weaker claim is both defensible and sufficient.

### Q: Which came first, the poem or the novel?

The poem, by about two and a half years. The Waste Land appeared in October 1922 in The Criterion, in November 1922 in The Dial, and in book form from Boni and Liveright that December with Eliot's notes attached. The Great Gatsby was published by Scribner's on April 10, 1925. The order matters for two reasons. It makes influence possible in one direction only, which constrains what an essay may claim. And it means the poem had already reorganized what serious literary ambition looked like by the time Fitzgerald was drafting, so the novel was written into a landscape the poem had partly created.

### Q: How do the two works treat water and drought?

This is the comparison's decisive axis. Eliot builds his wasteland on absence of water: cracked ground, stone without water, a longing for a sound that does not come, and a final movement waiting under thunder for rain the poem never delivers on the page. Thirst is the condition and relief is withheld. Fitzgerald surrounds his world with water and lets none of it work. The bay divides the lovers for five years and the crossing turns out to be worthless. Rain falls on the afternoon of the reunion and renews nothing. The green light matters only because water lies between Gatsby and it, and the moment Daisy stands beside him his count of enchanted objects has diminished by one. Then the pool kills him.

### Q: What role does the city play in each work?

Eliot's city is unreal from its first appearance and is never granted a moment of beauty the poem then withdraws. It is a machine processing people who are already finished, and the diagnosis comes first. Fitzgerald grants Manhattan its full enchantment before dismantling it. Crossing the Queensboro Bridge in Chapter Four, Nick sees the city rising in white heaps and sugar lumps all built with a wish out of nonolfactory money, and says the city seen from that bridge is always the city seen for the first time. Then the chapter delivers Meyer Wolfshiem and a set of human molars used as cuff links. Eliot asks the reader to see through the surface. Fitzgerald makes the reader feel the pull first.

### Q: Are Eckleburg's eyes comparable to Eliot's Tiresias?

Comparable in function, distinct in kind. Both are witnesses with the shape of authority and none of its power. Eliot's blind prophet sees everything in the poem, suffers it, and alters nothing. Fitzgerald's eyes are blue and gigantic with retinas a yard high, they look out of no face, and they brood over the dumping ground for an oculist who sank into eternal blindness or simply moved away. Both writers needed a position from which vacancy could be registered without importing a moralist, since a moralist implies a standard still in force. Where they part is on divinity. Eliot's figure sits inside a religious framework the poem takes seriously in ruins. Fitzgerald's is an advertisement, and Michaelis says so out loud.

### Q: How does each work end, and what does the ending offer?

Eliot closes on fragments in several languages and a repeated formula of peace borrowed from Sanskrit scripture. It has the shape of a benediction and the substance of salvage: the speaker is shoring pieces against his own ruin, and the pieces do not assemble. Fitzgerald closes on Nick alone on a beach, imagining an island as Dutch sailors first saw it, then on the future receding year by year, then on the current that carries everyone backward. Neither restores the land. But Eliot's ending is an act, however futile, while Fitzgerald's is a submission to a force. Breakage against undertow, salvage against elegy.

### Q: Can a student write an essay pairing the poem and the novel?

Yes, and it is a strong choice provided four rules are followed. Do not quote the poem; describe its images in your own words, which reads as control rather than as padding, and quote the novel freely since its text is public domain and its sentences excerpt cleanly. Build the thesis on the inversion rather than the resemblance, since both works depicting barrenness is the premise and not the argument. Anchor every claim in a specific Fitzgerald passage, using the valley description, the Eckleburg paragraph, the Queensboro Bridge passage, and the final page. And handle the coincidence objection early using the documented record rather than defensively at the end.

### Q: What do the two works say about love and sterility?

Both render intimacy as something that produces nothing. Eliot's poem includes an encounter in a rented room that is mechanical on one side and indifferent on the other, and a marital exchange in which neither party reaches the other. Fitzgerald's equivalent is the apartment on 158th Street where Tom keeps Myrtle: a room crowded with furniture too large for it, a dog bought on impulse from a man in the street, an afternoon of drinking that ends when Myrtle says Daisy's name and Tom answers with his hand. The difference is that Fitzgerald's sterility ends in named violence against a specific woman who dies later in the book, while Eliot's ends in indifference. The novel assigns a victim; the poem describes a condition.

### Q: Why is a poem a fair comparison for a novel?

Because the comparison is being made on the object rendered, not on the form. Genre comparison fails when a student tries to match plot against stanza or narrator against speaker. It works when both texts are asked the same question: what does this work claim about the modern world, and what image does it use to claim it. On that question the poem and the novel are directly comparable, and their formal difference becomes evidence rather than an obstacle, since it shows two solutions to the shared modernist problem of how much surface order to preserve. Eliot fractured the surface. Fitzgerald kept it and buried the fracture underneath.

### Q: Which of the two works is bleaker about modern life?

Fitzgerald, on one specific measure, and Eliot on scale. A wasteland caused by absence contains the structure of its own remedy, which is why the poem can organize a whole section around waiting for thunder and mean something by the waiting. Fitzgerald's characters are deprived of nothing. They have water, money, houses, cars, music, and each other, and the barrenness is produced by the having, which leaves nothing to arrive. That is the harder verdict. Against it, Eliot's claim is civilizational, multilingual, and reaches across centuries, while Fitzgerald's stays inside one summer on one coast. The poem is larger. The novel is more airless.

### Q: Does the ash figure do work Eliot's imagery does not?

Yes, and it is the novel's structural advantage. Fitzgerald's valley has an address. It sits between West Egg and Manhattan with a road, a railway, and a drawbridge over a small foul river, and it corresponds to an actual municipal ash dump in Queens. Eliot's wasteland is deliberately nowhere, assembled from London, a desert, a chapel, and the Ganges, because he is claiming a universal condition. The cost of universality is that no one is responsible: a mythic wound has no polluter. Fitzgerald's ruin has both a dumper and beneficiaries, and Nick names them, saying Tom and Daisy smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money. Eliot wrote a diagnosis. Fitzgerald wrote an indictment.

### Q: What did Fitzgerald and Eliot exchange in writing?

A book and a letter, and both survive in the record. Fitzgerald sent Eliot a copy of the novel inscribed to him as the greatest of living poets, which is an unusually direct act of deference from a novelist already famous in his own country. Eliot answered at the end of December 1925 with the assessment that he had read it three times and regarded it as the first advance American fiction had made since Henry James. There was no sustained correspondence beyond this, no collaboration, and no shared circle. The exchange is brief, which is exactly why it is useful in an essay: two documents, mutually corroborating, that establish recognition without requiring any speculative claim about influence.
