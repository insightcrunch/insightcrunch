## What Happens When a Century-Old Novel Belongs to Everyone?

What does it mean for a book to be rediscovered not by critics or classrooms but by the calendar itself, when the law quietly hands a famous story to anyone with a printing press, a camera, or a laptop? Gatsby adaptations after 2021 present exactly that puzzle. A novel published in 1925, taught in most American high schools and sold by the tens of millions, slipped out of copyright protection in the United States on the first morning of 2021, and within months the shelves and screens began filling with fresh versions: new editions, a prequel about Nick Carraway, a fantasy retelling narrated by Jordan Baker, a young-adult remix, an announced television series, a Broadway musical. The stakes are bigger than one book's afterlife. This flood tests a reader's judgment in real time, because the wave ranges from careful, inventive work to opportunistic filler, and learning to tell the difference sharpens how you read everything, including the original novel itself.

![A reader considering the flood of new Gatsby editions, prequels, and retellings - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The question underneath the boom is an interpretive one, not a legal one. Anyone can explain the copyright mechanism in a paragraph; the harder question asks what the sudden freedom did to the story's meaning. When dozens of creators can simultaneously rewrite Fitzgerald's novel without permission, which parts of the book prove durable, which parts get discarded, and what does the pattern of those choices reveal about the source? The wave is an experiment run in public. Each new version is a reading of the original disguised as a new product, and taken together they form the largest collective annotation project the novel has ever attracted, except that nobody coordinated it and nobody agreed on the rules. Some versions treat the book as sacred text to be preserved in a new package. Others treat it as raw material for arguments Fitzgerald never intended. The interesting failures outnumber the successes, and the successes are genuinely illuminating, because a century-old novel suddenly everywhere again shows which of its gears still turn under modern hands.

To make sense of the surge you need categories, not just titles. The wave divides naturally into editions, prequels, retellings and reimaginings, and screen and stage projects, and each category behaves differently, answers to different incentives, and teaches a different lesson. Editions multiply because the barrier to printing a public-domain text is nearly zero. Prequels multiply because the original's silences are so precisely shaped that they read like invitations. Retellings multiply because the novel's social architecture, old money against new, insider against outsider, maps cleanly onto other eras and identities. Screen and stage projects multiply because producers had been circling the property for years and the legal opening removed the last obstacle. Understanding the boom means understanding these four engines separately, then watching how they interact, because the same freedom that let a celebrated novelist write a serious Jordan Baker fantasy also let opportunists ship slapdash reprints, and both facts matter equally to the full picture.

This article owns the analysis of that wave. The legal cause, the copyright expiry and the mechanics of the public domain, belongs to the companion piece on [the novel's entry into the public domain in 2021](/2029/11/19/gatsby-public-domain-2021/), which is linked rather than repeated here. The individual works get close readings elsewhere in this series, in [our survey of sequels and retellings](/2030/03/04/gatsby-sequels-retellings/) and [our guide to modern retellings](/2030/03/11/gatsby-modern-retellings/). What follows is the wave itself as an object of study: what arrived, how it was received, how wide the quality range runs, and what the pattern says about the book at the center. The namable claim will be stated plainly near the end, but here is its shape in advance. The post-2021 boom is the legal shift made visible, a flood of work from faithful to wildly free that put Gatsby back at the center of the culture and that has to be judged piece by piece, never as a bloc.

## Gatsby Adaptations After 2021: The Surge, Stated Plainly

Start with the facts, because the wave invites exaggeration in both directions. On January 1, 2021, the United States copyright in F. Scott Fitzgerald's 1925 novel expired, and the book entered the public domain along with every other work published in 1925. Before that date, anyone who wanted to publish the text, quote it at length, or build a derivative work on it needed permission from the rights holders, and the estate controlled which adaptations went forward. After that date, anyone could reprint the novel, illustrate it, translate it, abridge it, continue it, or rewrite it, without asking anyone and without paying anyone. That is the whole legal story, and the companion article linked above carries the full mechanism. What matters here is the consequence, which was immediate and visible in bookstores within weeks.

### What changed for The Great Gatsby when it entered the public domain?

The expiry removed the permission barrier, so publishers could issue new editions and creators could publish prequels, retellings, and adaptations without licensing the text. The result was a rapid, visible multiplication of Gatsby products across books, stage, and announced screen projects.

The first and largest effect was the edition flood. Within the first year, the market filled with new printings of the novel from publishers large and small, ranging from handsome annotated and illustrated volumes to bare-bones budget reprints. The Wikipedia overview of the novel's publication history notes that since the expiry, numerous altered and incomplete reprints have flooded the market, and that single sentence captures the commercial logic precisely. A public-domain classic with guaranteed classroom adoption and steady annual sales is the closest thing publishing has to free inventory. Some editions earned their existence with new introductions, scholarly apparatus, or design; others existed only to capture search traffic and shelf space. The range was the point, and it was visible to anyone browsing a bookstore in 2021, where the familiar Scribner paperback suddenly shared the shelf with a crowd of strangers wearing the same title.

The second effect was the derivative wave, and it arrived just as fast. Michael Farris Smith's Nick, a prequel centered on Nick Carraway, was published on January 5, 2021, by Little, Brown, four days after the expiry, which made it the symbolic first-born of the new era. Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful, a fantasy retelling narrated by Jordan Baker, followed in June 2021 from Tordotcom. The graphic-novel category, covered in depth by the sibling article on that form and mentioned here only as one channel among many, added new illustrated versions in the same window, including K. Woodman-Maynard's watercolor adaptation from Candlewick dated January 2021. Screen and stage developers, who had been developing projects under license or in quiet anticipation, announced their plans openly: a big-budget limited television series from A+E Studios and ITV Studios America with Vikings creator Michael Hirst writing, revealed in January 2021, and eventually a full Broadway musical that opened in the spring of 2024. Each of these will get its own section below. The pattern to register now is the speed. The wave did not build gradually over a decade. It broke in the first eighteen months and then kept rolling, with new retellings such as Anna-Marie McLemore's Self-Made Boys arriving in 2022 and stage productions following through the middle of the decade.

The third effect is harder to see but just as real: the surge changed the novel's position in the culture from a school-assigned classic to a live property. Before 2021, the book's afterlife was managed; after 2021, it became unmanaged in the productive sense that folk material is unmanaged. Fitzgerald's novel had, of course, been adapted before, five film versions across the twentieth century, stage productions, an opera, the Baz Luhrmann spectacle of 2013, but every one of those required a deal. The post-2021 versions required only an idea and a printer. That difference in kind is what the word wave is trying to capture. It is not that more adaptations happened; it is that the gatekeeping structure around them dissolved, and the resulting variety, faithful watercolors next to queer fantasy next to budget reprints next to Broadway, is the signature of the period. No single institution could have planned this assortment, and no single taste could have approved all of it.

Keeping the categories distinct matters because the incentives differ. Edition publishers chase margin and discoverability. Prequel and retelling authors chase interpretation, filling gaps or shifting centers. Screen and stage producers chase scale and event status. When critics and readers argue about whether the wave is good or bad, they are usually judging one category's output by another category's standards, praising a prequel for its edition-quality fidelity or damning a reprint for lacking a retelling's imagination. The honest assessment keeps the lanes separate and then asks, lane by lane, what the work gets right about the source, what it gets wrong, and what the attempt reveals. That assessment is the business of the next several sections.

One more fact belongs in the record before the analysis begins: the wave was not only American. The novel had already been translated into more than forty languages before the expiry, and the post-2021 years added new translations, new illustrated editions for foreign markets, and new stage productions abroad, each shaped by its own copyright calendar and its own reading traditions. The American legal date gets the headlines because the American market is the largest, but the boom's logic is international wherever the text went free. A full accounting would need a comparatist's library. What follows centers the English-language wave, where the evidence is thickest, and treats the international dimension as the frontier the next analyst should map.
## Prequels: Filling the Silences the Novel Leaves on Purpose

Of all the categories, the prequel is the most revealing about the original's construction, because a prequel can only exist where the source leaves a gap, and Fitzgerald's novel leaves its gaps in exactly the places a lesser writer would have filled. Nick Carraway tells you almost nothing about himself. In the opening pages he mentions the Middle West, Yale, the war, and the bond business, and then he spends nine chapters watching other people. Gatsby's past arrives as rumor, conflicting report, and one compressed autobiographical monologue. Daisy's interior life stays sealed. These are not accidents of a short book; they are load-bearing silences, and the prequel wave is a record of what happens when writers decide the silences are invitations rather than architecture.

### Is Nick by Michael Farris Smith a prequel worth reading?

Smith's Nick imagines Nick Carraway's war service in France, a doomed Paris romance, and a redemptive detour through New Orleans before West Egg. Reviews split sharply, with the New York Times praising its classic American sound and the Washington Post calling its second half lurid.

That split is the honest answer, and it is more useful than a verdict, because the disagreement maps the fault line that runs through every prequel to a masterpiece. Nick was published on January 5, 2021, by Little, Brown, and its timing was deliberate: Kirkus had reported the project in July 2020 as likely the first of many to exploit the novel's move into the public domain. Smith, known for gritty Southern novels like Desperation Road and Blackwood, took the narrator Fitzgerald kept in soft focus and gave him the full hard-focus treatment: trenches and tunnels in France, a whirlwind Paris romance that ends badly, then New Orleans, where Nick becomes entangled with a brothel owner and her estranged husband, a mustard-gas-scarred veteran. The publisher's own copy framed the journey as beginning on a foggy wartime Paris street corner and ending across the harbor from a Long Island estate, with mystery and revelation as the two poles.

The reception tells you exactly what the prequel form risks. Kirkus called it a compelling character study and a thoroughly unconventional prequel, and its review lingered on the nightmarish worlds Smith builds, places far from the staid tension and off-stage deaths of Fitzgerald's novel. Ben Fountain, reviewing for the New York Times, went further, calling it an exemplary novel with a classic American sound and praising Smith's rendering of Nick. But Publishers Weekly, while admiring the striking imagery of the war chapters, concluded that the book ultimately did not deepen the reader's understanding of the narrator. The Los Angeles Times criticized it as devolving into melodrama, reprocessing Nick rather than clarifying him. And Ron Charles of the Washington Post, who had praised the ambition of creating a worthy home for Fitzgerald's novel, judged the execution harshly, writing that the second half withdrew Nick's perspective too far and left readers with noir caricatures and their lurid spat.

Read those judgments together and a pattern emerges. The parts of Nick that critics praised, the war chapters, the atmosphere, the prose rhythms, are the parts where Smith wrote his own book. The parts they attacked, the New Orleans intrigue, the melodramatic machinery, are the parts where the prequel logic forced him to manufacture plot to connect the dots Fitzgerald left unconnected. That is the structural hazard of the form. A prequel must arrive at a fixed destination, the first page of the original, and every invented episode has to be aimed at that target, which is why prequels so often feel both overstuffed and thin. Smith's Nick is valuable less as a definitive backstory than as a demonstration: it shows how much of Fitzgerald's Nick depends on restraint, on being the man who watches, and how quickly the character changes when the camera turns around to face him.

There is a deeper interpretive question underneath, which the novel's most careful readers have always debated. Fitzgerald gives Nick the famous closing moral authority, the judgment on Tom and Daisy as careless people who smashed up things and creatures and retreated back into their money, and that authority depends on Nick remaining somewhat unformed, a lens rather than a subject. The moment you give the lens a lurid past of its own, the moral geometry shifts: is this still the judgment of a decent Midwestern observer, or the projection of a damaged man? Smith's version leans toward the second answer, and whether that enriches or distorts the original depends on what you believe Fitzgerald intended. The prequel cannot settle that debate, but it forces you to have it, which is the best thing any adaptation can do for a reader willing to argue back.

The prequel category also includes quieter entries, short fiction and small-press projects that gave Gatsby a childhood or Daisy a first marriage or Meyer Wolfshiem an origin, and their quality varies exactly as you would expect from a form with no gatekeeper. The honest generalization is that prequels to this novel succeed in inverse proportion to how much they explain. The ones that gesture, that suggest a past without cataloguing it, tend to harmonize with the source. The ones that document, that turn mystery into timeline, tend to fight it. Nick sits in the middle of that spectrum, which is why it drew both genuine admiration and genuine irritation, and why it remains the prequel against which the category's later entries get measured. For the longer lineage of continuations and the works that preceded the legal opening, [our survey of sequels and retellings](/2030/03/04/gatsby-sequels-retellings/) places Nick in its full historical context.
## Retellings: The Same Story Wearing Different Skin

If prequels fill the novel's silences, retellings move its furniture. The retelling keeps the plot's skeleton, the parties, the triangle, the accident, the pool, and rehouses it: new narrator, new century, new magic system, new set of bodies. The post-2021 retellings are the wave's most critically decorated category, and they are also its most intellectually ambitious, because a retelling is always an argument about what the original is really about. Change the center and see what holds. That is the experiment, and the two landmark entries of the wave ran it in opposite directions, one through fantasy and one through identity, and both found that the book's architecture is sturdier than its period detail.

### What is The Chosen and the Beautiful about?

Nghi Vo's 2021 fantasy retelling gives the narration to Jordan Baker, reimagined as a queer Vietnamese adoptee with suppressed magical powers. It follows Fitzgerald's plot closely while filling the Jazz Age with demons, paper magic, and cutting commentary on race and belonging.

The Chosen and the Beautiful was Vo's debut novel, published June 1, 2021, by Tordotcom, and its premise is the wave's single most elegant interpretive move. Jordan Baker in Fitzgerald's novel is a minor planet: the dishonest golfer, Daisy's friend, Nick's brief romance, the woman who tells Nick she thinks he is the only honest person she knows. Vo takes that marginal figure and makes her the consciousness of the book, and the specific reinvention matters. This Jordan was adopted from Tonkin, in what was then French Indochina, by a wealthy white Louisville family; she moves through the Jazz Age elite as both insider and exotic attraction, queer, Asian, magical, her ability to cut paper into living things a literalization of the social artifice everyone else practices. The plot beats land where Fitzgerald put them, the floating dresses, the parties, the confrontation in the Plaza suite, the road death, but every beat now passes through a narrator who can see the machinery because the machinery was never built for her.

The reception recognized the achievement without pretending it was flawless. Ron Charles, reviewing for the Washington Post, suggested Vo's demonic adaptation might be, and then caught himself with a gasp, jazzier than the original. NPR's review called it a vibrant and queer reinvention and praised its pull from the first sentence. It was nominated for the Locus Award for Best First Novel and for the Goodreads Choice Award in fantasy, and it landed on best-of-year lists for LGBTQ and Asian American writing. The dissenting notes are worth hearing too: some critics found that it flirts with theme without committing, that it reproduces Fitzgerald's plot so faithfully that it never quite breaks free to become its own book. That criticism is actually the retelling's central tension stated as a complaint. The closer a retelling hews to the source's events, the more its changes read as commentary; the further it strays, the more it risks becoming a different story wearing a borrowed title. Vo chose closeness, and the payoff is that every deviation, Jordan's magic, her alienation, Gatsby's possibly literal deal with the devil, lands as an interpretation of the original rather than a replacement of it.

Anna-Marie McLemore's Self-Made Boys: A Great Gatsby Remix, published September 6, 2022, by Feiwel and Friends as part of the Remixed Classics series, ran the experiment in the other direction, through identity rather than genre. The setup: Nicolás Caraveo, a seventeen-year-old Latinx transgender boy from Minnesota, rents a small house in West Egg from his eighteen-year-old cousin Daisy Fabrega, who has remade herself as Daisy Fay and now passes seamlessly as white. The neighbor throwing the parties is Jay Gatsby, and in McLemore's version Gatsby is also transgender, a self-made boy in the most literal sense. The novel draws on Nella Larsen's Passing as an influence and centers the questions Fitzgerald's novel circles without naming: who gets to invent themselves in America, what the invention costs, and which bodies the era's freedom was actually for.

Its reception was the warmest of any wave retelling in the young-adult space. It was selected for the National Book Award longlist, and Booklist's starred review declared that McLemore captures the spirit of the original while adding nuance and depth, setting a new bar for what a great retelling can be. The praise matters because it names the standard the best retellings meet: they do not merely borrow the plot, they find the pressure points in the original and press on them. Fitzgerald's Gatsby is, after all, already a story about self-invention, about a boy from North Dakota who rewrites his name and his past. McLemore's version asks what that myth looks like when the reinvention is embodied rather than merely social, and the answer turns out to be a genuine reading of the source, not a departure from it. The green light and the orgastic future speech at the novel's end read differently when the dreamer is a trans boy in 1922, and that difference is the retelling doing critical work.

Put the two landmark retellings side by side and the category's logic comes into focus. Both keep Fitzgerald's plot. Both change the narrator's position in the social order. Both use the changed position to make visible something the original keeps at the edge of the frame: race, empire, queerness, the labor of passing. Neither could have been published as a Gatsby book before 2021 without a license, and both are unimaginable without the legal opening, which is the wave's thesis in miniature. The freedom did not just multiply products; it multiplied perspectives, and the perspectives turned out to be where the interpretive gold was. Several of these works receive individual close readings in [our guide to modern retellings](/2030/03/11/gatsby-modern-retellings/), which treats each as a critical essay in fictional form.

The retelling category also absorbed the wave's share of weaker work, and honesty requires saying so. For every Vo or McLemore there were competent but forgettable re-skins, mysteries, romances, and thrillers that borrowed the names and the parties and little else. The pattern is instructive: the retellings that last are the ones with a thesis about the original, and the ones that fade are the ones that treated the source as set dressing. That is the quality-range argument in its purest form. The legal freedom is neutral. It enables the brilliant and the bland with equal efficiency, and the reader's job is to sort them, which is why reception, the record of which versions earned their keep, belongs at the center of any wave analysis rather than as an appendix to it.
## Editions: The Flood on the Shelf

The least glamorous category is the most commercially significant. When a text enters the public domain, the first movers are not artists but publishers, because a canonical novel with guaranteed classroom sales is inventory that suddenly costs nothing to acquire. The edition wave that followed the 2021 expiry had two faces, and confusing them is one of the wave's common misreadings. On one side stood genuine editorial contributions: new annotated editions, fresh translations building on the novel's existing forty-two language history, illustrated volumes, and design-forward reissues with introductions by living writers. On the other side stood the flood proper, the altered and incomplete reprints that the novel's publication history now records as having swamped the market. Both faces are real. Only one of them is interesting as interpretation, but both of them are the wave.

The serious editions did real work. Annotations that gloss the novel's period slang, its geography, and its classical allusions serve readers who meet the book without a teacher, and the best of the post-2021 annotated volumes treated the public-domain moment as an occasion to re-edit Fitzgerald's text with fresh eyes rather than simply reprint the old plates. Illustrated editions, a category the series treats in its own article and which is mentioned here only as one channel of the larger surge, found new visual languages for scenes every reader already knows, testing whether the green light and the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg can survive being drawn. New introductions reframed the novel for new cohorts, some through the lens of the American dream's latest crisis, some through the book's strange publishing history, the 1925 commercial disappointment that became the mid-century classic. The edition is the wave's most conservative form, and that is its virtue: at its best, it returns you to the text with better tools.

The flood is the wave's cautionary tale. The economics are brutally simple. Typeset the public-domain text, or scrape it, wrap it in a cover generated to catch search results, price it a dollar under the competition, and collect the margin that used to belong to the rights holder. Some of these reprints were merely cheap; others were actively defective, with the altered and incomplete versions drawing the sharpest criticism from booksellers and librarians who found themselves fielding complaints from students assigned corrupted texts. This is the part of the wave that tempts blanket condemnation, and it deserves criticism, but it also deserves proportion. The flood is a market phenomenon, not a literary one. It tells you about incentives, not about the novel. Judging the wave's artistic achievement by its worst reprints is like judging a film festival by its popcorn.

There is, however, a subtler lesson in the edition category, and it concerns what readers actually buy. The handsome editions sold well, which suggests that even in an age of free text, readers will pay for curation, design, and authority. The novel's sales history supports this: nearly thirty million copies in print since the initial disappointment of fewer than twenty-five thousand, with roughly half a million more selling every year even before the expiry. The public domain did not kill the market for the book; it segmented it. The official Scribner lineage kept its prestige lane, the scholarly editions kept their classroom lane, and the flood fought over the remainder bin. That segmentation is worth understanding because it predicts how every future classic's entry will play out. The text becomes free. The packaging becomes the product. And the reader who cannot tell a sound text from a corrupted one pays the price, which is why the boring work of textual stewardship, the kind Matthew J. Bruccoli devoted his career to for Fitzgerald, matters more after the legal opening, not less.

## Screens and Stages: From Announcement to Opening Night

If editions are the wave's commerce and retellings its criticism, screen and stage projects are its spectacle, the category where the most money moves and where the gap between announcement and achievement yawns widest. The pattern here predates the legal opening: producers had been circling the property for years, and the 2021 expiry functioned less as a starting gun than as the removal of the last toll booth. What followed divides into two very different stories, one about a project announced with fanfare that illustrates the development limbo of prestige television, and one about a musical that actually opened and thereby joined the small club of Gatsby adaptations audiences can judge as finished work.

### Has a new Great Gatsby television series been produced?

A limited series from A+E Studios and ITV Studios America, with Vikings creator Michael Hirst attached to write, was announced in January 2021 with estate cooperation and a focus on 1920s Black New York. It was presented as in development with no network attached.

The announcement, reported through the Hollywood Reporter in late January 2021, carried all the markers of a serious prestige play. Hirst, the writer behind Elizabeth, The Tudors, and Vikings, would write and executive produce alongside Groundswell Productions' Michael London. Blake Hazard, Fitzgerald's great-granddaughter and a trustee of the estate, signed on as a consulting producer. Columbia scholar Farah Jasmine Griffin was attached as a consultant on the 1920s Black community and musical subculture the reimagining planned to explore. The project had reportedly been in the works for at least three years, had quietly been in development at Apple before the company changed course, and drew on rights A+E had held since its 2000 television film starring Paul Rudd, Toby Stephens, and Mira Sorvino. Hirst's statement framed the moment explicitly: as America sought to reinvent itself, the time was right to look with new eyes at the story and explore its characters through the modern lens of gender, race, and sexual orientation, while capturing the majesty of Fitzgerald's vision.

And then, as with so many announced adaptations, the trail goes quiet. The series was announced as in development with no network attached, the producers planning to shop it to premium cable and streaming outlets, and the public record since has been thin. This is not unusual; it is the normal condition of development. But it makes the project useful as a different kind of evidence. The announcement itself was part of the wave's first crest, the flurry of claims staked in the months after the expiry, and its subsequent quiet illustrates the difference between the legal freedom to adapt and the industrial machinery required to produce. Anyone can option an idea; few can finance a period epic. The wave's screen category is full of announcements at various stages of reality, and the honest analyst reports the announcement, reports the principals, and declines to review a show that does not exist. The Hirst project remains, on the public record, a serious intention rather than a finished work, and intentions are data about appetite, not about art.

The musical is the opposite case: a finished work with an opening night, reviews, and a box office. The Great Gatsby, initially developed under the single-word title Gatsby, began Broadway previews on March 29, 2024, at the Broadway Theatre and officially opened on April 25, 2024, with music and lyrics by Florence Welch of Florence and the Machine and producer Thomas Bartlett, a book by Pulitzer Prize winner Martyna Majok, direction by Rachel Chavkin, and choreography by Sonya Tayeh. The production then crossed the Atlantic for a European debut at the London Coliseum, running from April into September 2025. Welch's connection to the material ran deep; she had contributed the song Over the Love to the soundtrack of Baz Luhrmann's 2013 film, and her statement on the project spoke of the book haunting her for much of her life and of Fitzgerald's broken romanticism.

A Broadway musical is the wave's most mainstream artifact, and its existence answers a question the announcements only pose: what does the story become when it must fill a theater eight times a week? The stage demands legibility, romance, and spectacle, which pulls any Gatsby adaptation toward the love story and away from the novel's corrosive social observation. That pull is neither virtue nor vice; it is the medium's gravity. The interesting critical question is how much of Fitzgerald's bite survives the translation, and the answer varies by production and by number. But the musical's real significance for the wave narrative is structural. It proves the pipeline works end to end: legal opening, development, financing, opening night, transfer. Most wave projects will never complete that journey. One did, at the largest commercial scale available to the stage, and that single completed journey tells you more about the wave's industrial reality than a dozen announcements.

Stage history also reminds you that the wave did not invent Gatsby performance; it accelerated it. The novel had been staged before, in straight plays and an opera and the immersive productions that multiplied in the 2010s. What changed after 2021 was the permission structure around new stagings and the confidence of producers who no longer needed to negotiate. The result was a thicker theatrical calendar, more regional productions, more experimental takes, more chances for the story to meet audiences who will never read the novel. Some of those productions were excellent. Some were tourist-trap jazz with a famous title. The range, again, is the story, and the stage is where the range is most visible to the naked eye, because a bad production cannot hide behind a cover design.

The economics of the stage category deserve one more note, because they explain the particular texture of the theatrical wave. A regional theater or immersive company can mount a Gatsby with a cast of ten and a rented mansion, which puts stage adaptation within reach of the same modest budgets that fueled the book wave. That is why the theatrical calendar thickened so fast: the permission barrier fell at the same moment that immersive and site-specific theater was already booming as a form, and Fitzgerald's parties are natively immersive. The audience at a Gatsby party scene is already, in the novel, part of the spectacle; Nick's first party chapter is essentially a man wandering through an installation. Directors recognized the fit immediately. The result was a tier of productions that no film studio would ever finance and no publisher would ever print: one-night speakeasy stagings, promenade performances, jazz-club readings. Most were ephemeral by design, and ephemerality is the enemy of the critical record, which is why this tier is the wave's least documented and possibly its most inventive. The historians of the boom will undercount it, because you cannot review what closed after a weekend. But the audiences who wandered through those rooms carry the most direct version of the wave's lesson: the story works when you are inside it, and the law finally let everyone build the room.
## What the Wave Gets Right and Wrong About the Novel

Every adaptation is a reading, and readings can be judged. The wave's versions, taken as a body of criticism in fictional form, get several large things right about Fitzgerald's novel, and they get a few large things wrong in ways that are themselves instructive. Sorting the two is the core intellectual work of this article, because the temptation is to treat the wave as a referendum, thumbs up or thumbs down on the whole enterprise, when the truth is granular. Some versions understand the book better than most classrooms do. Some misunderstand it in ways that reveal exactly which of its difficulties are load-bearing.

What the best of the wave gets right, first, is the novel's social geometry. Fitzgerald's book is ruthlessly precise about who is allowed where: East Egg against West Egg, the Buchanans' colonial mansion against Gatsby's imitation chateau, the valley of ashes between the city and the money, the Plaza suite where the confrontation must happen because neutral ground does not exist. The retellings that work, Vo's and McLemore's above all, preserve that geometry while changing who stands in it, and the geometry holds, which proves it was the load-bearing element all along. A lesser reading of the novel treats it as a love story with period costumes; the wave's best work treats it as a machine for sorting people, and then asks what the machine does to different people. That is not a distortion of Fitzgerald. It is the book's own logic extended to the readers Fitzgerald could not or would not imagine.

Second, the best versions get right the novel's interest in performance. Everyone in Fitzgerald's Long Island is performing a self: Gatsby performing old money, Daisy performing carelessness, Tom performing the gentleman, Nick performing the honest observer. The wave's retellings literalize this, Vo with actual magic, McLemore with the labor of passing, and the literalization works because the original was already about the gap between the performed self and the true one. When Nick says of Gatsby's parties that they are about the spectacle, he is describing a book in which spectacle is the currency and authenticity the rumor. The versions that understand this, that treat reinvention as the novel's true subject rather than its backstory, read Fitzgerald more faithfully than the adaptations that treat the plot as a romance to be prettified.

Third, the wave gets right the novel's darkness, or at least the best of it does. The Baz Luhrmann film of 2013 was criticized in some quarters for turning the book into a champagne commercial, all glitter and no rot. The wave's serious entries tend to restore the rot. Smith's Nick is soaked in war trauma and moral compromise. Vo's Jordan moves through a Jazz Age haunted by literal demons and the casual xenophobia of the elite. McLemore's lovers navigate a world where self-invention can get you killed. This is closer to Fitzgerald than the glitter, because the novel's famous last pages are not a celebration. They are an elegy for a dream that curdled. Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us, and the sentence is beautiful the way a headstone is beautiful. The wave versions that remember the headstone under the flowers are the ones that earned their license.

Fourth, and least remarked, the best of the wave restored the novel's ugliness, the parts that polite adaptation had spent decades sanding down. Fitzgerald's book contains a white supremacist rant in its first chapter, Tom Buchanan enthusing over a racist tract about the white race being submerged, and it contains Meyer Wolfshiem, a Jewish gangster drawn with the era's casual antisemitic caricature, and it contains the valley of ashes, a dumping ground the rich drive through without seeing. Prestige adaptations tended to trim or soften these elements, treating them as embarrassments to be managed. Several wave versions refused the trim. The retellings that centered race and empire could not avoid Tom's racism because their entire project was about who the Jazz Age excluded, so the rant stayed in and did work. The versions that took the underworld seriously could not avoid Wolfshiem because the bootlegging economy is the plot's engine, so the caricature had to be confronted rather than cut. This is the wave at its most honest: not flattering the source but facing it. A classic that cannot survive its own ugliness is not a classic. The versions that tested Fitzgerald's worst passages against modern readers, and found the book sturdy enough to hold them, did the novel a greater service than the versions that prettified it. The green light shines, in the original, over ashes. The wave's best work remembered the ashes.

What the wave gets wrong is equally patterned, and the errors cluster around three misunderstandings. The first is the confusion of backstory with depth. The novel's minor characters are minor on purpose; their thinness is a compositional choice, the way a portraitist leaves the background unfinished to keep your eye on the face. Prequels that fill every gap, that give you Gatsby's entire adolescence or Daisy's diary, mistake the background for an oversight. Fitzgerald scholar Matthew J. Bruccoli, who spent his career establishing the novel's texts and documenting Fitzgerald's craft, emphasized again and again how deliberate the book's construction is, how little in it is accidental. The wave versions that pile incident onto the silences are arguing, implicitly, that Fitzgerald left money on the table. Sometimes he did. More often the silence was the point, and the versions that fail are the ones that never considered that possibility.

The second misunderstanding is the confusion of the narrator with the author. A persistent thread in weaker wave work treats Nick Carraway as Fitzgerald's mouthpiece and then either sanctifies him or debunks him, when the novel's actual achievement is the unstable distance between the two. Nick is within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled by the inexhaustible variety of life, as the first chapter puts it, and that doubleness is the book's moral engine. Versions that flatten Nick into a hero or a villain, a reliable witness or a fraud, lose the engine. Smith's Nick is interesting precisely because it wobbles on this point, giving Nick a past dark enough to complicate his judgments without quite deciding whether the complication validates or undermines them. The weaker prequels pick a side and the book goes flat.

The third misunderstanding is the prettification of the ending. Fitzgerald's novel ends with death, flight, and a narrator retreating to the Middle West, and then the famous coda about boats against the current. Some wave versions, particularly in the romance and young-adult lanes, soften the landing: the lovers survive, the dream gets a second chance, the green light turns out to be reachable. This is the one error that is almost always fatal to the adaptation's claim on the original, because the novel's meaning lives in its refusal of consolation. A Gatsby that ends happily is not a retelling; it is a different book that borrowed the furniture. The wave's best entries understand that the tragedy is the thesis. The rest are fan fiction with a marketing budget, and there is nothing wrong with fan fiction except when it claims the authority of interpretation.

## What the Range of Choices Reveals About the Source

Step back from individual verdicts and a larger pattern appears, which is the real payoff of studying the wave as a wave. When dozens of uncoordinated creators all grab the same book, the choices they converge on tell you what the book is made of, the way a crowd's footprints reveal the shape of the floor. Three convergences stand out, and each one is a piece of literary intelligence about Fitzgerald's novel that no single adaptation could have delivered alone.

The first convergence is on the minor characters. Again and again, wave creators reached past Gatsby and Daisy to the figures at the edge of the frame: Nick, Jordan, and in some projects the peripheral presences like Owl Eyes or the party guests. This is not random. It is a collective recognition that the novel's center is deliberately hollow, that Jay Gatsby is a beautiful void around which other people's desires orbit, and that the most interesting consciousnesses in the book are the watchers. Fitzgerald built his novel as a study in mediated vision; everything arrives through Nick, and Nick himself arrives half-formed. The wave's instinct to re-center the watchers is therefore a genuine critical insight, arrived at independently by commercial writers following their noses. It suggests that the book's durability lies less in its hero than in its narrative architecture, the machine that lets a marginal figure see the center clearly. Sarah Churchwell, in her cultural history Careless People, traced how the novel's afterlife kept finding new centers of gravity; the wave is the largest demonstration of that restlessness, and it votes decisively for the edges over the middle.

The second convergence is on the novel's gaps as invitations. The war, the three missing years of Gatsby's rise, Daisy's months in Louisville, the precise nature of the bond business, the contents of the phone calls: wave creators treated every one of these as a door. What is striking is how rarely they converged on the same answers, which tells you the gaps are genuinely open rather than merely underwritten. A closed gap produces consensus; an open gap produces variety. The variety of wave backstories, Gatsby as gangster, as veteran, as striver, as fraud, as romantic, is evidence that Fitzgerald's omissions function as designed negative space, holding multiple projections at once. Maureen Corrigan, in So We Read On, her memoir of a lifetime with the novel, describes how the book rewards rereading precisely because it withholds; the wave is that rereading impulse industrialized, dozens of readers filling the same blanks differently, and the differences are the data. The source is not a fixed text with missing pieces. It is a field of structured indeterminacy, and the wave mapped the field.

The third convergence is on the American dream as the portable part. Strip away the period detail and nearly every wave version keeps the same engine: a self-invented striver, an exclusive world, a green light that recedes. Editions sell it as nostalgia. Prequels sell it as psychology. Retellings sell it as critique. The musical sells it as romance. That every category independently preserved the dream machinery while discarding or transforming almost everything else is the strongest possible evidence for what the novel is actually about, at least in its cultural function. Lionel Trilling's old judgment, that the book was still as fresh as when it first appeared and had even gained in weight and relevance, gets a new kind of confirmation here: not from critics but from the market, from the revealed preference of creators who could have taken anything and kept taking the same thing. The dream is the durable good. The parties are packaging.

There is a fourth, quieter revelation, and it concerns the novel's sentences. For all the plot-level reinvention, almost no wave version successfully imitated Fitzgerald's prose, and the few that tried produced the wave's most embarrassing pastiches. The market, left to itself, reinvented the story endlessly and left the style alone, which is a backhanded tribute of the highest order. It suggests that what is inimitable about the book is not its events, which turn out to be highly portable, but its voice, the particular pressure of its sentences. The wave proved the plot is public property in the deepest sense; it also proved, by universal tacit agreement, that the prose is not.
## The Business of the Boom: Who Profited and What the Money Shaped

Interpretation does not happen outside economics, and the wave's artistic record cannot be separated from the money that funded it. Each category of the boom answered to a different set of incentives, and those incentives shaped the art in predictable ways. Understanding who profited, and how, is not cynicism; it is the rest of the explanation. The legal opening created the possibility. The market decided what the possibility became.

Start with the edition publishers, because their math is the cleanest. A public-domain classic with guaranteed classroom adoption is as close to risk-free inventory as trade publishing offers. The novel was already selling roughly half a million copies a year before the expiry, which meant any new edition entered a market with proven demand and zero acquisition cost. The serious houses invested in the only differentiators left: editorial apparatus, design, and introductions by writers whose names could move copies. The opportunists invested in nothing but search optimization, pricing a scraped text a dollar below the competition and collecting margin. Both strategies worked, which is why the shelf filled so fast. The important insight is that the edition market segmented rather than collapsed. The Scribner lineage kept the prestige lane, the scholarly editions kept the classroom lane, and the flood fought over the price-sensitive remainder. Nobody's Gatsby outsold everybody else's Gatsby into oblivion, because the text being free meant the competition moved entirely to packaging. That is the permanent economic lesson of the public-domain transition: when the words cost nothing, the wrapper is the product.

The prequel and retelling economy runs on different fuel: advances, reviews, and awards. A trade publisher paying for a Gatsby prequel is buying a marketing hook, the famous title on the cover, and betting that the hook plus the author's talent clears the advance. Michael Farris Smith's Nick was that bet at the literary-fiction level, and its divided reception shows the gamble's shape: strong enough reviews to justify the outlay, not strong enough to become a franchise. Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful was the bet at the genre level, where a debut novelist could leverage the public-domain premise into award attention, and the Locus nomination suggests the bet paid. Anna-Marie McLemore's Self-Made Boys was the bet at the young-adult level, where the Remixed Classics series provided the institutional frame and the National Book Award longlist provided the prestige. In each case the publisher's incentive aligned, imperfectly but genuinely, with artistic ambition, because in trade fiction the only durable marketing is reputation. The weaker retellings, the competent re-skins that borrowed names and parties, reveal the same incentive structure from below: when the hook alone is deemed sufficient, the writing coasts. The market funded both the thesis-driven retelling and the cash-in; readers and reviewers sorted them afterward.

Screen and stage economics explain why that category looks so different. A novel can be written on speculation; a period film cannot be shot on speculation. The announced projects of the wave's first year, the Hirst limited series above all, were claims staked on expensive territory, and staking the claim cost little while developing it costs millions. That asymmetry is why the announcement-to-production ratio in the screen category is so lopsided. Producers announce to attract financing, attach talent to attract producers, and develop scripts to attract networks, and most of these chains break somewhere along the way. The Broadway musical survived the chain because theater economics, while brutal, are simpler: capitalize the production, sell the tickets, and let the reviews decide. Its opening in April 2024 proved the pipeline could complete, but it also showed what completion costs in interpretive terms. A stage musical must please a room, which pulls every adaptation toward the love story and away from the novel's colder judgments. The money did not corrupt the art; it selected which art could exist at scale.

There is a fourth economy, quieter but culturally decisive: the classroom. The novel's place in American high school curricula is the demand floor under the entire wave. Roughly half a million copies a year were selling before the expiry largely because teachers assign the book, and the edition flood was, at bottom, a fight over that assigned market. This matters for interpretation because the classroom is where most readers meet the novel first, and the version they meet shapes the reading for life. The defective reprints were therefore not just a commercial nuisance but a pedagogical hazard, which is why the stewardship argument in the editions section is not academic preciousness. When the text is free, the teacher's choice of edition becomes a critical act. The wave's serious annotated volumes understood this; they were built for the classroom lane and priced for it. The flood's operators understood it too, and aimed at the same buyers with worse goods.

Step back and the industrial picture clarifies the artistic one. The wave's quality range, the fact this article keeps insisting on, is not a mystery and not an accident. It is what you get when a single legal change opens four different markets simultaneously, each with its own cost structure, its own audience, and its own definition of success. The edition market rewarded packaging. The trade-fiction market rewarded reputation. The screen market rewarded scale. The classroom market rewarded reliability. Each market got roughly the Gatsby it paid for. The brilliant retellings and the slapdash reprints are not contradictions; they are the outputs of different machines running on the same newly free input. Anyone who judges the wave as a single artistic gesture is making a category error. It was never one gesture. It was four industries reacting to the same event, and the reaction tells you as much about the industries as about the book.

That is also why the wave is a preview, not just a history. Every beloved text that enters the public domain will now pass through the same four machines, and the Gatsby record is the baseline against which future waves will be measured. The next classic's expiry will produce its own edition flood, its own prequels, its own retellings, its own announcements, and the same arguments about whether the whole thing is wonderful or terrible. The Gatsby evidence suggests the argument will be miscast again, because the wave will again be four different things. The readers who learned to sort this one, category by category, work by work, will be ready for the next. The ones who settled for a bloc judgment will be surprised again, in exactly the same way.

## What a Reader of the Book Can Take From All This

The wave is not just a publishing story; it is a reading aid, if you use it correctly. The mistake is to treat the new versions as substitutes for the novel, shortcuts for students who would rather read the retelling than the source. The productive use is comparative: read a wave version against Fitzgerald's text and let the differences teach you what the original is doing. This is the oldest trick in literary study, the parallel-text method, and the wave has supplied an abundance of parallel texts, more than any teacher could have commissioned. The reader who takes advantage of them will understand the novel better than the reader who ignores them, not because the adaptations are better but because comparison is a sharper instrument than admiration.

Start with a single scene and two versions. Take the Plaza Hotel confrontation in Chapter 7, the scene where Gatsby demands that Daisy say she never loved Tom, and Tom dismantles Gatsby with the casual cruelty of a man who has never lost anything. Read Fitzgerald's version first, slowly, noting how little dialogue is direct and how much arrives filtered through Nick's appalled observation. Then read any wave retelling's version of the same scene, Vo's or McLemore's or the stage adaptation's, and ask what changed. The changes will cluster around exactly the points this article has been mapping: who gets interiority, who gets sympathy, whether the scene's outcome feels inevitable or contingent. Each change is a hypothesis about the original. Test the hypothesis against the text. That is the whole method, and it works with any scene, the first party, the drive into the city, the final phone calls that never come.

The wave also teaches a practical skill: judging editions. The flood of reprints means the reader can no longer assume that any copy with the right title contains the right text. The altered and incomplete versions documented in the novel's publication history are a real hazard for students, and the defense is simple but requires discipline. Prefer editions from established publishers with named editors. Check that the text includes the full nine chapters and the standard front matter. Be suspicious of covers that look generated and prices that look impossible. The boring version of this advice is the important one: the public domain gives you the text for free, but it does not give you a reliable text for free, and reliability is what you are actually shopping for. Readers who want to verify every claim in this article, and every wave version's departures, against the source itself can work through the fully annotated text at VaultBook, [the annotated edition of the novel](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), which is the fastest way to check what Fitzgerald actually wrote before judging what anyone else did with it.

Finally, the wave teaches something about cultural attention. The novel's path from 1925 flop to classroom staple to public-domain free-for-all is itself a story about how reputations are made, and this boom is only its latest chapter. Readers who follow the wave's reception, which versions get reviewed, which get taught, which get forgotten, are watching canon formation happen in real time, the usually invisible process by which a culture decides what to keep. That process used to take decades and happened in seminar rooms. The wave compressed it into years and moved it into bookstores, streaming queues, and theaters. Watching it is an education in how literature lives after its author dies, and Fitzgerald, who died believing himself a failure, would have found the spectacle instructive. The pattern of rediscovery is not new; [each generation finds its own Gatsby](/2030/03/18/gatsby-each-generation/), and the post-2021 generation simply found more of them at once, because the law finally let everyone look at the same time.

## The Namable Claim: The Legal Shift Made Visible

Here is the claim this article has been building toward, stated without hedging. A century-old novel suddenly everywhere again: the post-2021 wave of editions, prequels, and retellings shows what public domain unleashes, so the boom is the legal shift made visible, a flood of work that ranges from faithful to wildly free and that has put Gatsby back at the center of the culture.

Every clause of that claim is doing work. A century-old novel suddenly everywhere again names the phenomenon's strangeness: this is not a new book being discovered but an old book being re-released by the calendar into a world that had already decided it was a classic, and the everywhere is literal, shelves, screens, stages. Shows what public domain unleashes names the cause without mystifying it: the mechanism is the removal of permission, and everything else follows from that single change. The boom is the legal shift made visible insists on the connection that the wave's own marketing often obscures, because publishers sell each version as an individual creative event, and the truth is that the events cluster because the law moved. A flood of work that ranges from faithful to wildly free states the quality thesis plainly: the range is not a bug in the wave but its definition, and any account that pretends the wave was uniformly brilliant or uniformly worthless is selling a simpler story than the evidence supports. Put Gatsby back at the center of the culture names the outcome: whatever you think of individual versions, the aggregate effect was to return the novel to live cultural conversation, argued over in reviews and classrooms and theaters rather than merely assigned.

The claim also carries its own limitation, which honesty requires stating. The wave did not make the novel better, and it did not make it worse. It made it more available, more argued over, more variously embodied, and availability is not the same as understanding. Some readers met Fitzgerald for the first time through a retelling and then went back to the source; others met only the retelling and mistook it for the thing itself. Both outcomes are real, and the wave contains both, which is why the claim is about the culture rather than about individual readers. Cultures can be judged by what they keep in circulation. The post-2021 years kept Gatsby in circulation at a volume no estate, no matter how diligent, could have managed alone, and that volume is the legal shift rendered as a fact on the ground.

There is a final turn to the claim, and it concerns the future. Every classic that enters the public domain will now enter a version of this weather, because the Gatsby wave is the template: the edition flood, the prequels, the retellings, the announcements, the one or two finished spectacles, the long tail of forgettable product. Future waves will differ in their particulars, but the structure will rhyme, because the incentives are structural. The Gatsby case is therefore worth studying not just as literary history but as a preview. It is the first great public-domain wave of the streaming era, the first to play out across all media simultaneously, and the record of what the freedom enabled, the brilliant and the bland alike, is the document future readers will consult when the next classic's clock runs out. Reception theory has always argued that a book's meaning includes its afterlife; the wave is that argument with a balance sheet attached, and the balance sheet favors the readers who sort carefully over the ones who cheer or jeer on principle.

## Verdict: Judge the Wave Piece by Piece, and Let It Sharpen Your Reading

The verdict is not a grade. Grades belong on individual works, and this article has given several. The verdict is a posture, a recommendation for how to stand in relation to the flood, and it has three parts.

First, refuse the bloc judgments. The wave was neither a renaissance nor a desecration. It was a market and artistic event with the normal distribution of quality, a few works that will last, a larger number that were competent, and a long tail that was forgettable or worse. Anyone who tells you the whole wave was wonderful is selling you something, probably a book. Anyone who tells you the whole wave was trash is performing a taste, probably for an audience. The evidence supports neither. It supports the range, and the range is the finding.

Second, use the wave as an instrument. The best thing the post-2021 years produced, besides the two or three retellings that will endure, is a vast comparative apparatus for reading Fitzgerald. Every version is a hypothesis about the original. Test the hypotheses. The reader who does this work will know the novel better than before, not because the adaptations taught them facts but because disagreement taught them attention. That is the series' standard for reception as illumination: the afterlife of a book is not a distraction from the book but a lens for it, and this particular afterlife, being so large and so various, is an unusually powerful lens.

Third, remember what the wave proved about the source. It proved the plot is portable and the prose is not. It proved the minor characters are the load-bearing witnesses. It proved the gaps are designed. It proved the dream machinery is the durable good. None of these findings required the wave; careful readers had them all before 2021. But the wave confirmed them at scale, by experiment rather than by assertion, and confirmation at scale is worth something. A century-old novel suddenly everywhere again turned out to be a stress test, and the book passed, not because every version honored it but because the versions that honored it found so much to honor, and the versions that didn't revealed exactly where the honor was due.

So read the novel, then read around it. Pick one prequel, one retelling, one edition with real apparatus, and if you get the chance, one stage production. Compare them against the text and against each other. Notice what survives every translation and what survives none. And when you find a version you admire, ask what it understood that the others missed; when you find one you dislike, ask whether the failure is in the version or in your own attachment to the original. Both questions are productive, and both are forms of the same discipline the wave demands everywhere: judgment, exercised in public, about work made in freedom. You will be doing, in miniature, what the whole culture did at scale after 2021, and you will understand both the book and the boom better for it. The wave is over as an event; as a reading method, it is just getting started.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did the 2021 copyright expiry produce so many new Gatsby books so quickly?

The speed came from preparation meeting permission. Publishers and authors had been waiting for the January 1, 2021 expiry for years, and several projects were timed to land in the first weeks. Michael Farris Smith's Nick was published on January 5, 2021, four days after the opening, and K. Woodman-Maynard's graphic adaptation carried a January 2021 date as well. For editions, the lag was even shorter, because reprinting a public-domain text requires only typesetting and a cover. The deeper cause is economic: a canonical novel with steady classroom sales is valuable inventory, and the moment the licensing cost dropped to zero, the margin on every new edition belonged entirely to the publisher. Derivative works took slightly longer to write, which is why the retellings clustered across 2021 and 2022, but the pipeline had been filling during the final years of copyright. The wave looked sudden because the dam had been visibly straining.

### Q: Which post-2021 Gatsby prequel drew the most critical attention?

Michael Farris Smith's Nick is the prequel every later entry gets measured against, because it arrived first and drew the heaviest critical fire. Published by Little, Brown in January 2021, it imagines Nick Carraway's First World War service, a doomed Paris romance, and a violent New Orleans detour before West Egg. The reception split down the middle. Kirkus called it a compelling character study, and Ben Fountain in the New York Times praised its classic American sound. But Publishers Weekly found it added no deeper understanding of the narrator, the Los Angeles Times saw melodrama, and Ron Charles in the Washington Post dismissed the second half as lurid noir. That division is the point of interest. The praise clustered around Smith's own strengths, atmosphere and prose, while the attacks clustered around the prequel machinery, the forced plotting needed to connect Fitzgerald's silences. No later prequel attracted the same volume of serious review attention, which makes Nick the category's reference case.

### Q: What makes Nghi Vo's Jordan-centered retelling different from a standard adaptation?

Standard adaptations change the medium; Vo changed the consciousness. The Chosen and the Beautiful, published June 2021 by Tordotcom, keeps Fitzgerald's plot nearly beat for beat but hands the narration to Jordan Baker, reimagined as a queer Vietnamese adoptee with suppressed magical powers. The difference is interpretive rather than cosmetic. A standard adaptation asks how the story looks on screen or stage. Vo asks how the story feels from the social position Fitzgerald's novel keeps at its edge, the insider who is never quite inside. The fantasy elements, paper magic, demonic bargains, function as literalizations of the original's themes: reinvention, artifice, the cost of belonging. Reception recognized the ambition, with the Washington Post suggesting it might be jazzier than the original and nominations for the Locus Award for Best First Novel. The dissenting view, that it hews so close to the source it never fully becomes its own book, is really a description of the retelling's method pushed to its limit.

### Q: Why did so many wave creators center Nick Carraway or Jordan Baker instead of Gatsby?

Because the novel's center is deliberately hollow. Jay Gatsby is a beautiful void, a projection surface for other people's desires, and Fitzgerald gives him almost no interiority on purpose. The watchers, Nick and Jordan, are where the consciousness lives. Wave creators followed that structural hint independently and repeatedly. Nick offers the war, the Midwest, and the moral-judgment machinery; a prequel can plausibly fill his silences. Jordan offers the golf, the dishonesty, and the female insider's view of a male story; a retelling can plausibly promote her. Both choices are critical readings disguised as creative ones: they assert that the book's meaning lives in its margins. The convergence is evidence about the source, not coincidence among writers. When dozens of uncoordinated adapters all reach past the hero to the witnesses, they are collectively reporting that the witnesses are the load-bearing characters.

### Q: Are the inexpensive new Gatsby editions reliable texts for classroom use?

Some are, and some are actively defective, which is the practical hazard of the edition flood. The novel's publication history now records that numerous altered and incomplete reprints swamped the market after the 2021 expiry, and teachers and librarians reported fielding complaints about corrupted student copies. The safe rule is to prefer editions from established publishers with named editors, and to check that the text runs the full nine chapters with standard front matter. Be wary of covers that look generated, publishers you cannot trace, and prices that undercut the market implausibly. The public domain gives away the text but not textual reliability, and reliability is the actual product when you buy a classic. The serious annotated and illustrated editions from reputable houses are excellent; the bargain reprints are a lottery. Students assigned the novel should confirm their edition against a trusted copy before annotating it.

### Q: What did reviewers single out for praise in Self-Made Boys?

Reviewers praised the novel for finding a genuine thesis inside Fitzgerald's plot rather than merely borrowing it. Anna-Marie McLemore's Self-Made Boys: A Great Gatsby Remix, published September 2022 by Feiwel and Friends in the Remixed Classics series, reimagines Nick as Nicolás Caraveo, a seventeen-year-old Latinx transgender boy, and reveals Gatsby as transgender as well, while cousin Daisy Fabrega passes as white under the name Daisy Fay. Booklist's starred review said McLemore captures the spirit of the original while adding nuance and depth, setting a new bar for what a great retelling can be, and the book made the National Book Award longlist. The praise centered on how the identity framework illuminates the original's own obsession with self-invention: Fitzgerald's Gatsby already rewrote his name and past, so a version about embodied reinvention reads as criticism, not departure. Reviewers also noted the influence of Nella Larsen's Passing and the careful handling of 1920s queer and Latine history.

### Q: How does the Broadway musical's tone compare with the novel's?

The stage pulls toward romance and spectacle, which is the medium's gravity rather than a failure of the production. The Great Gatsby musical, which opened April 25, 2024, at the Broadway Theatre with music and lyrics by Florence Welch and Thomas Bartlett and a book by Martyna Majok, must fill a theater eight times a week, and that commercial demand favors legibility, big numbers, and the love story. Fitzgerald's novel is a corrosive social autopsy wearing evening clothes; a musical is evening clothes with the autopsy softened. The interesting question is how much bite survives, and that varies by number and staging. What the production proves structurally matters more than any single review: it completed the full pipeline from the legal opening through development and financing to opening night and a London transfer. Most wave screen and stage announcements never reached that point. As an adaptation it is necessarily a partial reading; as wave evidence it is the category's finished-work benchmark.

### Q: Did the announced Michael Hirst Gatsby television series ever get made?

On the public record, it remains a serious announced intention rather than a finished work. The limited series from A+E Studios and ITV Studios America, with Vikings creator Michael Hirst attached to write and executive produce, was announced in January 2021 with unusual prestige credentials: Fitzgerald's great-granddaughter Blake Hazard as consulting producer, Columbia scholar Farah Jasmine Griffin consulting on the 1920s Black community and musical subculture the reimagining planned to explore, and a reported three years of prior development including a quiet stint at Apple. The producers said they would shop it to premium cable and streaming outlets, with no network attached. Since then the public trail has been thin, which is the normal condition of television development rather than evidence of collapse. The honest account reports the announcement, the principals, and the plan, and declines to review a show that does not exist. Its value as wave evidence is about appetite: the industry's eagerness to stake a claim in the first weeks after the expiry.

### Q: Why do several wave retellings introduce fantasy or supernatural elements?

Because magic literalizes what Fitzgerald left as social metaphor. In the original, characters perform selves, reinvent pasts, and trade in illusion; a fantasy retelling simply makes the machinery visible. Nghi Vo's version gives Jordan Baker paper magic and fills the Jazz Age with demonic bargains, which turns the novel's theme of self-invention into plot. The choice also solves a retelling problem: how to make a familiar story feel new without abandoning its skeleton. A genre shift lets the writer keep Fitzgerald's events, the parties, the confrontation, the death, while changing their meaning, so every deviation reads as interpretation. There is a commercial logic too; fantasy and the retelling market overlap heavily in readership. But the best justification is critical. Fitzgerald's world already runs on enchantment, the green light, the mansion as stage set, the past as spell. The fantasy versions are not adding something foreign; they are developing a negative that was already in the camera.

### Q: What distinguishes a prequel from a retelling in the Gatsby wave?

A prequel extends the timeline backward; a retelling replays the timeline differently. Nick by Michael Farris Smith is a prequel: it ends where Fitzgerald's novel begins, filling Nick Carraway's war years and New Orleans detour, and its success depends on how convincingly it docks with the original's first page. The Chosen and the Beautiful and Self-Made Boys are retellings: they cover the same ground as Fitzgerald's plot, the summer of 1922, the parties, the triangle, the death, but from a new center or with a new framework, and their success depends on what the changes reveal about the source. The distinction matters because the two forms fail differently. Prequels fail by over-explaining, turning designed silences into timelines. Retellings fail by drifting, keeping the names while losing the novel's social geometry. The wave's best prequel is debated; its best retellings are not, which itself says something about which form suits this particular source.
### Q: Which wave works are most useful for teaching the novel?

The retellings with genuine theses, used comparatively, teach the most. Pair a chapter of Fitzgerald with the corresponding passage in Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful or Anna-Marie McLemore's Self-Made Boys, and ask students what changed and why. The differences are hypotheses about the original, and testing them is close reading with training wheels. Michael Farris Smith's Nick works for teaching narrative voice: have students compare Smith's fully realized Nick with Fitzgerald's deliberately thin one, and the lesson about the narrator as lens rather than subject teaches itself. For editions, a reputable annotated volume beats any wave reprint; the flood's defective copies are a hazard, not a resource. The wave's real classroom value is not as a substitute text but as a parallel text. Students who argue with an adaptation are doing literary criticism, often without realizing it, which is the best kind.

### Q: How can a reader tell a serious new edition from a slapdash reprint?

Check the publisher, the editor, and the apparatus. A serious edition names its publisher, names an editor or introducer, and shows its work: notes, a chronology, an introduction with a byline, clean typesetting. A slapdash reprint hides all of that, often behind a generic imprint, a generated-looking cover, and a price that undercuts the market. Open the book and check the chapter count; Fitzgerald's novel has nine chapters, and the altered and incomplete versions documented after the 2021 expiry sometimes rearranged or truncated the text. Compare a famous passage against a trusted copy if you have any doubt; the defective reprints tend to betray themselves in the details. Libraries are a useful filter, since collection librarians weed out the worst of the flood. The underlying principle is simple: the public domain gives you the words for free, but a reliable text is a manufactured product, and you should buy it from someone who manufactures carefully.

### Q: Why did the wave produce far more books than finished screen projects?

Books are cheap to make and screens are expensive. A novel requires one writer and a publisher willing to gamble a modest advance; the post-2021 retellings and prequels could move from manuscript to shelf in about a year. A film or prestige series requires financing, casting, period production design, and distribution, a machine with dozens of veto points. That is why the screen category is heavy with announcements and light with finished work: the Michael Hirst limited series was announced in January 2021 and remained in development, while the Broadway musical, which did open in April 2024, needed years of capitalization. The legal opening removed the permission barrier for everyone equally, but the capital barrier stayed exactly where it was. The wave's shape therefore reflects economics more than enthusiasm. Where the means of production were light, the flood was immediate; where they were heavy, the wave is still arriving.

### Q: What do the wave's weakest efforts reveal about Fitzgerald's prose?

They reveal that the plot is portable and the sentences are not. Across hundreds of wave versions, creators reinvented the story endlessly: new narrators, new centuries, new genres, new endings. Almost nobody successfully imitated the prose, and the attempts that tried produced the wave's most embarrassing pastiches, all glitter and no pressure. The market's revealed preference is striking: left completely free, adapters took the events, the geometry, the dream machinery, and left the voice alone. That is a backhanded tribute of the highest order. It suggests the inimitable element of the novel is not what happens but how it is told, the particular compression of Nick's narration, the similes that land like verdicts. A century of imitators failing at the same target is data. The wave confirmed by experiment what careful readers knew by attention: Fitzgerald's style is the part of the book that cannot be open-sourced.

### Q: Should you read the original novel before trying the wave's retellings?

Yes, and the retellings themselves make the case. A retelling is an argument about its source; without the source, you are hearing one side of a debate and mistaking it for the whole. The Chosen and the Beautiful lands its effects by deviating from scenes you recognize, Jordan's narration gaining force against Nick's. Self-Made Boys gains its charge by pressing on pressure points you have felt in Fitzgerald's text, the self-invention myth, the passing, the cost. Read the retelling first and you get a good fantasy novel or a good YA novel; read the original first and you get that plus a critical education. There is also a practical reason: the wave contains versions that misunderstand the book, and the unprepared reader cannot tell the misreading from the reading. The novel is short, under two hundred pages in most editions. The investment is small and the payoff is the ability to judge everything built on top of it.

### Q: How did the wave affect classroom teaching of the novel?

It gave teachers parallel texts and a cautionary tale in equal measure. The parallel texts are the gift: comparing Fitzgerald's Plaza Hotel confrontation with a retelling's version turns students into critics, because every change is a thesis to test. Several educators reported building units around the wave, asking students to evaluate which adaptations understand the novel's social geometry and which merely borrow its costumes. The cautionary tale is the edition flood: teachers learned to specify reputable editions after defective reprints reached students, a practical lesson in textual stewardship. More broadly, the wave made the novel's afterlife visible as a subject, not just background. Students could watch canon formation happening, see which versions got reviewed and taught and which vanished, and understand that classic status is maintained by argument, not granted once. The book did not change; the visible conversation around it got louder and more various.

### Q: What role did the Fitzgerald estate play in post-2021 projects?

A smaller one than before, by definition, and a continuing one by choice. Before the expiry, the estate controlled permissions; after it, anyone could adapt without asking. But the estate did not withdraw. Blake Hazard, Fitzgerald's great-granddaughter and a trustee, signed on as a consulting producer for the announced Michael Hirst limited series, lending the project continuity with the family's stewardship. Estate cooperation became a signal of prestige rather than a legal requirement, a way for serious productions to distinguish themselves from the flood. The Scribner lineage of the text also kept its market position; the official editions retained the prestige lane while the reprint flood fought over the remainder. The lesson is that the end of copyright does not end authority. It changes authority's basis from legal control to cultural credibility, and the estate's post-2021 role has been to spend that credibility selectively on projects it considers worthy.

### Q: Why do wave versions keep the parties and the green light while changing almost everything else?

Because those are the novel's portable icons, and the dream machinery is its durable good. Across every category, editions, prequels, retellings, stage, the creators who could take anything kept taking the same things: the glittering parties as spectacle, the green light as longing, the self-made striver against the exclusive world. The convergence is the strongest evidence for what the book is culturally about. The parties are the packaging everyone recognizes; the green light is the thesis everyone needs. What gets discarded, the period slang, the specific geography, the 1922 manners, turns out to have been the disposable part all along, at least for adaptation purposes. Fitzgerald's own final pages confirm the hierarchy: the novel ends not with a party but with the meditation on the orgastic future that recedes before us. The wave's collective editorial judgment, reached without coordination, ratified Fitzgerald's. Keep the dream, vary the dreamer.

### Q: Which wave category earned the strongest critical reception overall?

The retellings, by a clear margin. The Chosen and the Beautiful drew warm major-paper reviews and award nominations, including the Locus Award for Best First Novel and a Goodreads Choice nomination in fantasy. Self-Made Boys earned a National Book Award longlist selection and a starred Booklist review declaring it a new bar for retellings. No other category matched that hit rate. Prequels split critics, with Nick admired and attacked in equal measure. Editions divided into praised scholarly volumes and a condemned flood of defective reprints. Screen and stage produced one finished prestige success in the Broadway musical and a field of announcements. The pattern makes sense: retellings are the form where a strong thesis about the original pays off most directly, and the wave's best retellings had the strongest theses, about race, empire, queerness, and self-invention. The legal freedom mattered most where interpretation mattered most.

### Q: How should future public-domain waves be judged, using Gatsby as the template?

Judge them piece by piece, by category, and on the evidence of reception rather than announcement. The Gatsby wave established the template: an edition flood with a reliable core and a defective fringe, prequels that succeed in inverse proportion to how much they explain, retellings that last when they carry a thesis about the source, and a screen category heavy with development announcements and light with finished work. Future waves will rhyme because the incentives are structural. The useful questions transfer directly: which versions treat the source's silences as architecture rather than oversight, which find the portable machinery, which mistake packaging for meaning. And the meta-lesson transfers too: the wave is the legal shift made visible, so credit and blame belong partly to the law that enabled it and mostly to the creators who used the freedom well or badly. Gatsby was the first great public-domain wave of the streaming era. It will not be the last, and its record is the manual.
