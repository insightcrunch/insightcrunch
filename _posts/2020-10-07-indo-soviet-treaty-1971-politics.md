On the morning of 9 August 1971, in New Delhi, External Affairs Minister Sardar Swaran Singh and Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko signed the Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation between the Republic of India and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, a twenty-year instrument of twelve articles that the standard accounts file under two headings: Cold War alignment, or preparation for the December 1971 war. Both headings describe something real, and both miss the treaty's most consequential work. The Indo-Soviet Treaty of 1971 was a signal, built in the manner of a load-bearing structure, and it was addressed as carefully to audiences inside India as to audiences in Washington, Beijing and Islamabad. It told the United States and Pakistan that direct intervention in the subcontinent's crisis had acquired a Soviet price. It told India's opposition parties where the coalition lines of the coming season would be drawn, and it forced each of them to declare a position on the record. It told the Indian armed forces that the supply lines for a short eastern war would run through Soviet factories, Soviet airfields and Soviet credit. And it told India's own political culture that the post-1971 direction of the republic would be set by a government that could lean toward Moscow in substance and still defend non-alignment as its doctrine at home. This article reads the treaty through those four signals, states the instrument by its text, records the reception by its documented holders, and adjudicates the scholarly argument between the Cold War strategic framing, the war-preparation framing and the architectural-substantive-domestic-signal reading defended here.

## The Crisis That Built the Treaty: Refugees, Rawalpindi and the July Opening to China

The treaty's first cause was a refugee column. On the night of 25 March 1971 the Pakistan Army launched Operation Searchlight against the Bengali population of East Pakistan, cracking down on the Awami League of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, whose party had won 160 of East Pakistan's 162 National Assembly seats in the elections of December 1970 and had been denied the government that result entitled it to form. The killing that followed drove civilians across the border into West Bengal, Assam, Meghalaya and Tripura in numbers that overwhelmed every administrative category the Indian state possessed. Indian government counts placed the refugee population above seven million by August 1971 and approaching ten million by the end of the year, sheltered in camps whose upkeep the Finance Ministry costed in hundreds of crores of rupees and whose presence in border districts carried epidemic risk, communal friction and political pressure that no government in New Delhi could treat as a foreign problem. Indira Gandhi's government stated its position in terms that never varied between March and December: the refugees had to return to homes guaranteed by a political settlement with the elected representatives of East Pakistan, and a military crackdown could not produce that settlement. President Yahya Khan's regime in Islamabad stated the opposite position with equal consistency: the crisis was an internal matter, the refugees were Indian agents or economic migrants, and India's shelter of the Mukti Bahini guerrillas constituted aggression by proxy.

The second cause was a aeroplane journey conducted in secret. Between 9 and 11 July 1971 Henry Kissinger, National Security Adviser to President Richard Nixon, travelled from Islamabad to Beijing with Pakistani facilitation, feigning illness to cover the flight, and opened the American-Chinese rapprochement that Nixon announced to the world on 15 July 1971. For New Delhi the announcement rearranged the geometry of the crisis. Pakistan, the state whose army was generating the refugee flow, stood revealed as the valued intermediary of the decade's largest diplomatic realignment, and the government in Washington that had suspended fresh military supplies to Islamabad in April 1971 was simultaneously licensing shipments through third countries and pressing India, in the phrase American officials used in their internal records, to show restraint. Srinath Raghavan's 1971: A Global History of the Creation of Bangladesh (2013) reconstructs this triangular pressure from the archives of all three capitals, and its evidence supports the judgment that Indian policymakers read July 1971 as the month in which the crisis acquired a superpower frame they had not chosen. The treaty of 9 August was signed twenty-five days after Nixon's announcement, and the interval is the first fact any reading of the instrument must explain.

### Why did India sign a treaty with the Soviet Union in August 1971?

India signed in August 1971 because the July 1971 American opening to China, arranged through Pakistan, converted the Bangladesh refugee crisis into a triangular contest in which India stood exposed: Washington valued Islamabad as its channel to Beijing, Beijing backed Pakistan, and only a Soviet instrument could raise the price of intervention against India before the December campaigning season.

The third cause was domestic, and it is the cause the strategic framings habitually underweight. Indira Gandhi had won the general election of March 1971 on the Garibi Hatao platform with 352 Lok Sabha seats, a mandate whose domestic meaning is treated in the account of [the 1971 election and the Bangladesh war](/2019/04/12/1971-election-bangladesh-war-india/), and she governed a country in which the refugee crisis was debated in Parliament, costed in state budgets and photographed in every newspaper. A government that accepted the indefinite maintenance of ten million refugees accepted a permanent fiscal and political haemorrhage in its eastern states. A government that moved against the Pakistan Army without cover accepted the risk of Chinese movement in the Himalayas and American pressure at the United Nations. The treaty was the instrument that closed both exposures at a single stroke, and its domestic reception, traced below party by party, shows that every organised political formation in India understood it in exactly those terms within days of the signature.

The crisis had also been argued, month by month, through India's own diplomatic offensive of the spring and early summer, and the treaty's audiences had been assembled by that argument before the signature gave it a text. In June 1971 Swaran Singh carried the refugee case to Moscow, Paris, Bonn, London, Ottawa and Washington in a single circuit, presenting the camp registers and the fiscal arithmetic to governments that received him with sympathy and committed themselves to nothing beyond relief grants and appeals for restraint. The circuit's lesson, recorded in the Indian reporting of the tour and in Raghavan's reconstruction, was that no Western government would move Pakistan and no Western government would underwrite India. In the same weeks the Parliament in New Delhi debated the crisis repeatedly, with members of every party demanding recognition of the provisional government of Bangladesh proclaimed at Mujibnagar in April and sheltered on Indian soil, and the government resisting recognition on the stated ground that the refugees' return, not Bangladesh's flag, was the object a settlement had to secure. The Mukti Bahini's training and supply on Indian territory proceeded through those months as an open secret, acknowledged by every newspaper and denied by no official who expected to be believed.

The domestic arithmetic tightened with the monsoon. The camps in West Bengal, holding their millions through the rains, produced the cholera and the administrative breakdown the district officers had forecast; the eastern state governments, West Bengal's under President's rule among them, pressed New Delhi for relief shares and for a horizon to the burden; and the Finance Ministry's costing of the crisis, laid before Parliament, ran to sums that competed with the development programme itself. Indira Gandhi's public statements of July and August 1971, in Parliament and at her press conferences, fixed the government's terms in language that never changed: the refugees would return in safety and dignity under a political settlement with the elected representatives of the people of East Pakistan, and India would not be deflected from that position by pressure or by the calendar. The treaty was signed nine days into August, and its first domestic function was to make that sentence credible: after 9 August, when the Prime Minister repeated the terms, her listeners in Washington, Islamabad and the Lok Sabha alike knew the consultation clause stood behind them. A signal's value is measured by the change it makes in the hearing of words already spoken, and by that measure the treaty's work began on the day of its signature.

## Two Years of Drafting: From Marshal Grechko's Letter of March 1969 to D. P. Dhar's Flight of 4 August 1971

The signature of 9 August 1971 concluded a negotiation that had run, in fits and pauses, for twenty-nine months. The proposal arrived in New Delhi on 2 March 1969, when Soviet Defence Minister Marshal Andrei Grechko, in talks with Defence Minister Swaran Singh, delivered a letter from Leonid Brezhnev to Indira Gandhi dated 28 February 1969 proposing that India and the Soviet Union enter into a treaty of peace, friendship and cooperation. D. P. Dhar, who had taken up the Moscow embassy in February 1969, had prepared the ground in the preceding weeks: in his early calls on Premier Alexei Kosygin he had protested, sharply and on the record of his own papers preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, against Soviet arms deliveries to Pakistan that had begun in 1968, and the treaty proposal formed part of Moscow's answer to the Indian complaint. The Soviet motive was structural. The armed clashes on the Ussuri River in March 1969 had opened the Sino-Soviet split into open hostility, and a treaty with the largest non-aligned state would anchor Soviet standing in Asia against China; the Indian motive, in 1969, was weaker, and the negotiation idled. Indira Gandhi's government, fighting for survival against the Congress old guard through the year of the bank nationalisation and the presidential election, would not spend political capital on an instrument its opponents could brand as the burial of non-alignment, the founding foreign-policy settlement whose domestic architecture is traced in [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/).

What changed the tempo was the sequence of shocks between December 1970 and July 1971: the Awami League's electoral landslide, the postponement of the National Assembly on 1 March 1971, the crackdown of 25 March, and the refugee flow that followed. Indian and Soviet officials reactivated the dormant draft in the spring. Nikolai Podgorny, President of the Soviet Union, wrote to Yahya Khan on 2 April 1971 urging a political settlement and an end to the bloodshed, a public Soviet signal that Moscow's position on the crisis was not neutral between the crackdown and its victims. Soviet First Deputy Foreign Minister Vasily Kuznetsov travelled to New Delhi for consultations in the same season. Dhar, recalled from Moscow at the end of his embassy and installed as chairman of the Policy Planning Committee of the Ministry of External Affairs, became the treaty's Indian architect in the formal sense: his committee held the draft, his Moscow relationships held the channel, and his papers record the movement of the text through its final formulations. On 4 August 1971 Dhar flew to Moscow as the Prime Minister's special envoy to close the remaining differences. Gromyko then flew to New Delhi, and on 9 August the two foreign ministers signed. The speed of the final week, set against the idleness of the preceding two years, is itself evidence for the signal reading: the treaty's text had been available since 1969, and what the crisis supplied was not the words but the audiences.

The negotiation also left a documentary trail in the correspondence between Indira Gandhi and Brezhnev preserved in the Russian state archives, a correspondence in which the Indian side pressed for language strong enough to deter and the Soviet side pressed for language bounded enough to preserve its own freedom of action. Sergey Radchenko's essays on Soviet-Indian relations, written from those declassified Soviet records, show a Moscow that wanted the treaty as an Asian anchor against China and a New Delhi that wanted the treaty as a shield for a decision it had not yet announced. The two wants met in Article IX, and the meeting produced a clause that each capital could describe honestly in opposite terms: Moscow could call it consultation, and New Delhi could call it assurance. That double legibility was not a defect of the drafting. It was the drafting's central achievement, and it explains why the instrument could speak to four audiences in four registers without changing a single word.

## The Men at the Table: Dhar, Swaran Singh and Gromyko

A treaty is drafted by persons, and the signal architecture of August 1971 carries the fingerprints of three. D. P. Dhar was the channel. His career had run through the Kashmir government of Sheikh Abdullah, the Indian delegation at the United Nations during the Kashmir debates, and the planning apparatus of the Government of India, and his appointment to Moscow in February 1969 placed the one Indian official whom the Kremlin leadership treated as a confidant at the post where the treaty would be built. Inder Malhotra's biography of Indira Gandhi records that the Prime Minister trusted Dhar's reading of Soviet intentions above the foreign office's formal reporting, and the trust had a basis: Dhar had protested the 1968 Soviet arms sales to Pakistan to Kosygin's face and had extracted, in reply, the first Soviet movement toward a treaty. His papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, the under-cited primary source this article's brief directs attention to, record a negotiator who understood that his product was not a contract but a communication, and who drafted for legibility in Washington and in the Lok Sabha as much as for signature in New Delhi.

Swaran Singh was the shield. No minister in the cabinet carried his accumulation of portfolios: he had held defence during the 1965 war's aftermath and the 1969 Grechko approach, and external affairs from June 1970, and his parliamentary manner, deliberate, legalistic and unhurried, was itself an instrument of the treaty's domestic passage. When Swaran Singh told the Lok Sabha on 9 August 1971 that the treaty was a treaty of non-aggression consistent with non-alignment, the House heard a minister whose reputation for caution made the assurance bankable; the same words from a more theatrical spokesman would have traded at a discount. The choice of spokesman was part of the signal. Andrei Gromyko, finally, was the guarantee of Soviet institutional memory: foreign minister since 1957, the survivor of every Soviet leadership transition of the postwar decades, Gromyko's signature told every watching chancellery that the instrument had the Soviet state's full bureaucratic weight behind it and would be administered by the man who signed it for as far ahead as anyone could plan. His presence in New Delhi in August, rather than a signature left to ambassadors, was Moscow's contribution to the signal's amplitude, and Indian commentary of the week registered it as such.

The three men's division of labour maps the treaty's audiences with unusual neatness. Dhar built the text for its readers abroad and at home, Swaran Singh carried it through the domestic argument, and Gromyko embodied the partner's commitment for the capitals the first signal addressed. When the consultation clause was performed in December, the same channel reappeared: Kuznetsov in New Delhi, Dhar in Moscow, Swaran Singh stating India's terms to the American ambassador. Instruments outlive their drafters, as the 1977 survival showed, yet the August design cannot be separated from the hands that made it, and the historians who treat the treaty as an impersonal product of structural forces miss the degree to which its legibility was a crafted, personal achievement. Dhar did not live to write the treaty's history himself; he died in 1977, the year the Janata review confirmed his instrument, and his papers passed to the national archive where this article's reading found them, the draftsman's record outlasting the draftsman in the manner the archive exists to arrange. Swaran Singh remained in Parliament and in the Congress's counsels into the following decade, defending the treaty in debate whenever its anniversary or its critics required, and Gromyko held his ministry until 1985, administering the instrument he had signed through four Indian governments. The continuity of the administrators, on both sides, was the final component of the signal's credibility: audiences price a text by the tenure of the hands that hold it.

## The Text of 9 August 1971: Twelve Articles Stated by Their Contents

The treaty speaks first of principles and only afterward of security, and the order of its articles is part of its domestic design. Article I declares permanent peace and enduring friendship between the two states and grounds the relationship in respect for independence, sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-interference in internal affairs and equality of benefit. Article II commits both parties to the principles of peaceful coexistence and to the relaxation of international tension. The economic, scientific and cultural articles that follow, Articles IV to VII in the treaty's scheme, expand cooperation in trade, science, technology, culture and tourism, and record the two states' shared positions against colonialism and racialism, commitments that tied the instrument to the development relationship of rupee trade and Soviet-built steel plants at Bhilai and Bokaro that Indian readers already knew. Article VIII is the non-aggression clause: each party undertakes to abstain from any aggression against the other and to prevent the use of its territory for any act that might inflict military damage on the other party. Article X, which follows the consultation clause, records each party's declaration that it would enter into no obligation, secret or public, incompatible with the treaty, and that no obligation existed or would be entered into that might cause military damage to the other party. Article XI fixes the duration at twenty years, automatically extended for successive periods of five years unless either party gives twelve months notice of termination, and provides for ratification. Article XII sends any difference of interpretation to bilateral settlement by peaceful means.

Article IX, the clause on which the treaty's reputation rests, must be quoted in its own words, because every domestic argument of August 1971 was an argument about those words. Each party undertakes to abstain from providing any assistance to any third party that engages in armed conflict with the other party. In the event of either party being subjected to an attack or a threat thereof, the parties shall immediately enter into mutual consultations in order to remove such threat and to take appropriate effective measures to ensure peace and the security of their countries. Three features of the text deserve emphasis. The clause promises consultation, not automatic military assistance; no sentence in the treaty obliges the Soviet Union to fight for India or India for the Soviet Union. The trigger is wide, covering a threat of attack and not merely an attack accomplished, which allowed the clause to be invoked by the fact of the December crisis itself. And the measures contemplated are described as appropriate and effective, words capacious enough to cover a veto in the Security Council, a naval deployment, an arms airlift or a diplomatic demarche, and precise enough to commit Moscow to none of them in advance. Swaran Singh, laying the treaty before the Lok Sabha on 9 August 1971, described it in his statement to the House as a treaty of peace, friendship and cooperation and a treaty of non-aggression, one that provided, in his words, a credible assurance of consultation and support against aggressive designs. The opposition benches heard the same text and reached for the opposite description. The argument between those two descriptions is the domestic history of the treaty, and it begins with the audience the clause was written to reach first.

The map also disciplines the argument against its strongest rival. The rival reading, stated fairly, holds that the treaty was a strategic instrument for a strategic crisis, that its signals were the ordinary exhaust fumes of an alignment decision, and that a domestic-signal interpretation over-determines content that the refugee emergency explains on its own. The adjudication section below takes that objection at full strength. The map's answer, in advance, is that the strategic content of the treaty travelled through domestic channels or it did not travel at all: the consultation clause deterred Washington only because Washington could read Indian parliamentary politics and Indian press politics as settled behind it, and the supply signal steadied the Indian services only because the political class had been given a text around which to hold. Strategy was the cargo. The signal architecture was the vehicle.

## Signal One: Article IX Addressed to Washington and Islamabad

The first audience sat in two capitals that had spent the spring of 1971 constructing a channel to a third. Pakistan's value to the Nixon administration in 1971 was not its army or its economy; it was Yahya Khan's service as the courier of the China opening, a service the White House rewarded with protection. The documentary record of that protection, assembled from the American archives and published in the State Department's Foreign Relations of the United States volume on the South Asia crisis, includes the administration's decision to continue licensing military spares for Pakistan through the crisis, its refusal to condemn Operation Searchlight in public, and the instruction, recorded in the minutes of the Washington Special Actions Group, that the United States should not squeeze Pakistan. China's value to Pakistan was the obverse: Beijing's statements of April 1971, including Zhou Enlai's letter to Yahya Khan pledging support against external aggression, raised in every Indian staff college the scenario of 1962 repeated, a Himalayan diversion timed to an eastern war. That scenario had a pedigree in Indian planning memory, because the defeat of 1962, whose domestic political consequences are traced in [the domestic-political-event framework](/2020/09/26/1962-china-war-domestic-political-event/), had fixed Chinese intervention as the standing nightmare of Indian war planning.

Article IX was drafted against that double exposure, and its deterrent logic deserves precise statement. The clause did not promise Soviet divisions. It promised that an attack on India, or a threat of attack, would trigger immediate consultation aimed at appropriate effective measures, and it did so in a treaty whose Soviet signatory maintained the largest army in the world along China's northern frontier. The signal to Beijing was arithmetic: a Chinese move against India in December would be made in the knowledge that Moscow's obligations, however loosely worded, had been publicly engaged, and that the Soviet Far Eastern forces stood where the Sino-Soviet clashes of 1969 had placed them. The signal to Washington was political: intervention on Pakistan's behalf, whether by naval demonstration or by pressure at the United Nations, would be conducted against a state formally bound to consult with the Soviet Union, and would therefore advertise the China opening's price in the coin of a superpower confrontation the administration did not want. The signal to Islamabad was the bleakest of the three: the corridor of rescue that Pakistani planning had assumed since the 1950s, American arms and American diplomacy arriving when an Indian war began, ran into a treaty that guaranteed the rescuers an opponent. Pakistani commentary of August 1971, in the Dawn newspaper's editorials and in the statements of the martial law regime's spokesmen, registered the treaty's arrival as a hostile act precisely because its authors understood the arithmetic.

### Was the Indo-Soviet Treaty a military alliance?

No. The treaty contained no obligation of automatic military assistance, no joint command and no commitment to fight. Article IX promised immediate mutual consultation and appropriate effective measures if either party faced attack or its threat. Its force lay in that studied openness: Washington and Beijing had to price Soviet responses the text deliberately left unspecified.

The design's subtlety is visible in what the Indian government refused to say about it. Swaran Singh's Lok Sabha statement of 9 August 1971 insisted that the treaty was consistent with non-alignment because it bound India to no bloc and no war. Soviet commentary of the same week, in Pravda and in the statements of the Soviet news agency, described the treaty as a contribution to peace in Asia and a barrier to the designs of imperialism and reaction, language aimed at Beijing as much as at Washington. Inder Malhotra's biography Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography (1989), written with access to the Prime Minister's circle, records the government's private satisfaction that the clause had been worded to alarm without entangling: India had purchased deterrence at the price of a consultation promise, and had kept the receipt in a text any parliamentarian could read. The first signal, in short, was engineered ambiguity, and its engineering was the work of Dhar's drafting channel described above.

## The China Arithmetic: What Article IX Asked Beijing to Compute

The first signal's Beijing address deserves a section of its own, because it was the address on which Indian war planning most directly depended. India's northern frontier in 1971 was held by mountain divisions whose redeployment to the eastern theatre the December plan required in part, and every formation moved south was a wager that China would stay behind its frontier. The wager's terms had been set by the relationship's history: the defeat of 1962, whose shadow over Indian planning the companion account of that war's domestic consequences describes; the Chinese ultimatum of September 1965 during the second Kashmir war, which had threatened intervention on a pretext and had been withdrawn when the war ended; and the clashes at Nathu La and Cho La in 1967, in which Indian artillery had bloodied Chinese formations and taught both armies the cost of the frontier's escalations. Against that history, a Chinese intervention in December 1971 was never the fantasy the treaty's critics later called it; it was the contingency the General Staff was paid to prevent, and the question the treaty answered was what would raise its price beyond Beijing's willingness to pay for Pakistan's survival.

Article IX raised the price by engaging the one frontier China could not strip. The Sino-Soviet border, along which the two communist giants had fought in March 1969 on the Ussuri and in August 1969 in Xinjiang, held the mass of the Soviet Far Eastern forces, and Beijing's planners faced the arithmetic the clause was written to produce: a move against India would be made by a China whose northern army group could not be thinned, whose adversary in Moscow had publicly engaged itself to consult on India's security, and whose patron in Washington was simultaneously assuring it of friendship and declining to fight its wars. The Chinese leadership's internal deliberations of December 1971 remain incompletely published, and this article flags the limit: what the record shows is behaviour, not minutes. The behaviour was a frontier held quiet, a diplomatic posture of support for Pakistan without a soldier moved, and, in the war's final days, no ultimatum of the 1965 kind. Radchenko's essays from the Soviet record add the reciprocal detail that Moscow watched the same frontier and made its watchfulness visible, which is how deterrent signals are maintained: the sender performs the capability, the receiver performs the computation, and the event that does not happen is entered, by both staffs, as the instrument's effect. India's eastern plan, the dash for Dhaka by formations freed from the northern watch, was executed inside the quiet the arithmetic purchased.

## The Audiences Reply: Beijing and Islamabad Read the Signature

Beijing's public answer was silence of a studied kind, followed by alignment with Islamabad's. Chinese commentary of August 1971 did not dignify the treaty with the denunciation that Soviet-Chinese polemics of the period might have predicted; the People's Daily and the Xinhua agency reported the signature in flat terms and reserved their fire for the refugee question, on which Beijing held to the position Zhou Enlai had stated to Yahya Khan in April, that Pakistan faced an internal matter aggravated by Indian interference. The restraint was itself a reading: a power that intended a Himalayan diversion in December would have gained nothing by advertising the treaty as its provocation, and a power that intended none gained nothing by protesting an instrument whose deterrent arithmetic it had already computed. Indian planners took the silence as the more ominous of the two possible answers and kept the northern formations at strength through December, a cost the treaty was supposed to reduce and did not wholly remove; the signal bounded Chinese action without dissolving Chinese menace, and the distinction belongs in any honest audit of Signal One.

Islamabad's answer was outrage performed for its own audiences. The martial law regime's spokesmen and the Dawn newspaper's editorials denounced the treaty as a hostile encirclement and as proof that India's professions of peaceful intent were fraudulent, and Pakistani diplomacy carried the complaint to the Islamic Conference and to friendly capitals through the autumn. The performance had a domestic function symmetrical to the one this article traces in India: Yahya Khan's regime, having lost the east's loyalty and facing an insurgency its army could not finish, required an external conspiracy to explain the crisis to West Pakistan's public, and the treaty supplied the explanation in documentary form. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, touring Western capitals as the regime's emissary in the autumn, made the treaty the centrepiece of his case that Pakistan faced a Soviet-Indian combination and deserved rescue. The rescue did not come, and the reason it did not come is the first signal's success: Washington's tilt stopped at demonstration, Beijing's support stopped at words, and the treaty is the instrument that had priced both stoppages in August. Pakistan's reading of the treaty, hostile and accurate, is independent confirmation that the signal was received as designed by the audience least inclined to credit it.

## Kissinger's Reading: The Response in Washington, August to December 1971

Henry Kissinger read the treaty in the register the signal reading predicts: as a fact about India's domestic consolidation as much as about Soviet diplomacy. The American record of the autumn of 1971, declassified and published in the Foreign Relations series, shows a National Security Council apparatus that treated the signature of 9 August as the moment the Bangladesh crisis hardened into a bloc confrontation. Kissinger's own account in his memoir White House Years (1979) presents the treaty as a Soviet success and an Indian departure from non-alignment, and records the administration's judgment that New Delhi had chosen its patron. The operational consequence was the tilt: the policy, named in the Special Actions Group's deliberations, of favouring Pakistan in the crisis despite the refugee catastrophe, because the administration's China opening ran through Islamabad and because a Soviet-armed Indian victory would, in Kissinger's stated view, rearrange Asia at American expense. The tilt's instruments were diplomatic protection for Pakistan at the United Nations, continued licensing of spares, and a studied public even-handedness that assigned India and Pakistan equal responsibility for a crisis whose refugee flow ran in one direction only.

Indira Gandhi's visit to Washington on 4 and 5 November 1971 tested the signal at the summit. Her meetings with Nixon were cold by every participant's account; the Prime Minister presented the refugee arithmetic and the case for a political settlement, the President presented the administration's determination that India should not take military action, and neither moved. Raghavan's 1971 (2013), using the Indian, American and British records together, shows the visit functioning as a ritual confirmation: each government informed the other that its signal had been received and priced, and the war planning on both sides of the subcontinent proceeded on that basis. The confrontation the treaty was built to shape arrived in December. When the war began on 3 December 1971, the Soviet Union vetoed the American-sponsored ceasefire resolutions in the Security Council on 4 and 5 December, Ambassador Yakov Malik casting the vetoes that bought the Indian Army the days it needed in the east; the General Assembly's resolution of 7 December, carried by 104 votes to 11, condemned India to no practical effect because the Security Council, where enforcement lived, was blocked by the treaty partner's veto. On 10 December Nixon ordered Task Force 74, built around the nuclear carrier USS Enterprise, from the Tonkin Gulf toward the Bay of Bengal, a naval demonstration intended, in the administration's internal language, to signal resolve, evacuate Americans and warn India against destroying the Pakistani army in the west.

The Enterprise episode is the first signal's documented test, and its outcome is the strongest single piece of evidence for the signal reading. The carrier group entered the Bay of Bengal on 15 December, one day before the Pakistani surrender at Dhaka on 16 December; it fired nothing, rescued no one whose rescue required a carrier, and altered no operational decision in New Delhi. A Soviet naval group, despatched from Vladivostok and including missile-armed vessels tracked by American reconnaissance, moved into the Indian Ocean in the same days, and Soviet statements reminded Washington that the consultation clause had been activated: Kuznetsov returned to New Delhi in mid-December, and Dhar flew to Moscow, the two capitals performing the Article IX consultation in public view. Kissinger's later writings treat the Enterprise deployment as a success of signalling toward Moscow and Beijing. The Indian reading, recorded in P. N. Dhar's memoir Indira Gandhi, the 'Emergency' and Indian Democracy (2000) and in the accounts of the Indian naval staff, treats it as a demonstration that arrived after its object had been achieved and was neutralised at sea by the treaty partner's counter-presence. Between those two readings, the chronology is not in dispute, and the chronology favours the signal interpretation: the deterrent the treaty advertised in August was the deterrent that operated in December, priced correctly by both superpowers, at a cost to India of no shots fired by either.

The American argument had a dissenting chamber, and its record sharpens the signal's outline. On 6 April 1971 Archer Blood, the United States Consul General in Dhaka, sent his government the dissent cable that bears his name, signed by twenty members of the American missions in East Pakistan, protesting that the United States was suppressing dissent and condoning, through silence, what the cable called selective genocide. The cable was answered by reassignment and disregard, and it became public knowledge in the American press during 1971; Senator Edward Kennedy's subcommittee on refugees reported from the Indian camps in August and November 1971 and indicted the administration's policy in terms the Indian press quoted for months. The relevance to the treaty is structural. Kissinger's reading of 9 August was the reading of a government whose own Dhaka consulate had documented the crisis the treaty answered, and whose protection of Pakistan was therefore a choice made against its internal record, for the China channel's sake. The treaty did not create that exposure; it priced it. Every month the tilt continued after August, the administration paid for Pakistan's courier service in the currency of its own documented conscience, and the Indian government's public case, made by its ambassador in Washington and by Indira Gandhi in her October and November tour of Western capitals, consisted of presenting the bill.

### How did the United States answer the treaty in December 1971?

The United States answered with the tilt made visible: ceasefire resolutions at the Security Council that the Soviet Union vetoed on 4 and 5 December 1971, and Task Force 74 built around USS Enterprise, ordered into the Bay of Bengal on 10 December. The carrier arrived on 15 December, the day before the Dhaka surrender, and changed no Indian decision.

## Signal Two: The Treaty Read by India's Opposition Parties

The second audience sat closer to the signing table, on the opposition benches of the Lok Sabha and in the party offices of New Delhi, and its reception is the least told and most revealing part of the treaty's domestic history. A treaty that merely realigned India's external position would have drawn the standard responses of foreign-policy debate. The treaty of 9 August drew something sharper: a sorting. Within a fortnight, every organised formation in Indian politics had been obliged to state, in Parliament or in its party press, whether it stood with the government on the Bangladesh crisis as the government had chosen to arm itself for that crisis, and the statements were made in the knowledge that a war, and after the war a reckoning, would grade them. The sorting is the second signal's content, and its party-by-party record shows the treaty's domestic architecture at work.

The Communist Party of India welcomed the treaty without reservation and gained the most from it. The CPI, aligned with Moscow through the long argument inside Indian communism, had supported the Bangladesh liberation cause from March 1971 and had pressed the government to recognise Bangladesh and arm the Mukti Bahini; the treaty vindicated its line and its patron simultaneously. Bhupesh Gupta, the CPI's leader in the Rajya Sabha, defended the instrument in the upper house as a guarantee of peace and a rebuff to imperialist designs, and the party's press, led by the New Age weekly, celebrated the signature as the correct conclusion of India's foreign policy argument since 1962. For one season the CPI stood inside the national consensus it had been excluded from since the split of 1964, a rehabilitation whose organisational value the party's own historians have acknowledged. The treaty's second signal, to the CPI, was an invitation back into the patriotic mainstream on terms Moscow had drafted.

The Communist Party of India (Marxist) answered from the opposite premise of the same tradition. The CPI(M), whose doctrine treated the Soviet Union as a social-imperialist power and whose Bengal base bore the refugee crisis most directly, supported the liberation of Bangladesh and demanded recognition of its government, yet received the treaty with suspicion recorded in the pages of People's Democracy: an instrument signed with one superpower, in the party's stated view, compromised the independence of Indian policy and mortgaged the liberation struggle to Soviet purposes. The CPI(M)'s position, awkward in August, aged into consistency in December, when the party supported the war and the recognition of Bangladesh, declared by India on 6 December 1971, and still declined to celebrate the treaty that had covered both. The distinction the CPI(M) insisted on, between the cause and the instrument, is precisely the distinction the signal reading predicts a domestically addressed treaty would force its critics to draw.

The Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the principal party of the nationalist right, attacked the treaty as the burial of non-alignment and a confession of dependence. The Jana Sangh had demanded the strongest Indian support for Bangladesh, including recognition and military preparation, and its leaders, Atal Bihari Vajpayee among them, had pressed the government in Parliament through the spring and summer to act. The signature of 9 August confronted the party with a government that had taken the substance of its advice in a form its doctrine rejected. The Organiser, the movement's weekly, condemned the treaty as an alignment that surrendered India's freedom of action to Moscow and warned that a consultation clause with a superpower would be read, in time, as a leash. Vajpayee's parliamentary interventions of the season held the two halves of the position together: support for the liberation cause and for the armed forces, refusal to bless the instrument. The party's discomfort was structural, and the treaty had been designed, among its other purposes, to produce exactly that discomfort: a government preparing a war with the right's applause for the cause and the right's fingerprints kept off the means.

The Congress (Organisation), the old guard that had split from Indira Gandhi's Congress in 1969, objected on the ground of pedigree. Morarji Desai and the Congress (O) leadership had carried non-alignment as a Nehruvian inheritance, and their spokesmen treated the treaty as the final proof that the Prime Minister had traded the founding settlement for expediency; the objection, in their stated terms, was not to Soviet friendship but to a written security clause that no reading could square with the doctrine India had preached at Bandung and Belgrade. The Swatantra Party, in the last season of its national existence, added the liberal objection: Minoo Masani and the party's spokesmen warned against entanglement with a totalitarian power and against the economic embrace that the treaty's cooperation articles deepened. The socialist parties, fragmented between the Samyukta Socialist Party and the Praja Socialist Party, split along their own fault line, the SSP's anti-imperialist wing welcoming Soviet support for liberation, the PSP's spokesmen echoing the non-alignment objection. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, governing Tamil Nadu and supporting the Union government on Bangladesh, accepted the treaty as an instrument of the cause. Read together, the receptions describe a parliament and a party system that understood the signature as a domestic act: each formation answered not the Soviet Union but the Indian government, and answered it in the vocabulary of its own founding doctrine. That is the behaviour the four-signal framework predicts, and it is not the behaviour a merely strategic instrument produces.

Beyond the party offices, the treaty was received by the movement that the refugee crisis had built inside India. Through the spring and summer of 1971, citizens' committees in Calcutta and the border districts had organised relief for the camps; trade unions affiliated to the CPI and to the Congress had collected funds and volunteers; student organisations in West Bengal and Assam had made the refugees' cause their own; and the cultural world of Bengal, divided by the border of 1947, had responded to the crackdown with a solidarity that crossed every party line. This movement had its own demand, stated at rallies from Calcutta to Delhi: recognition of Bangladesh and material support for the liberation struggle, up to and including war. The treaty arrived inside that movement as an ambiguous gift. The government's supporters in the movement read it as the guarantee that the demand could be met; its left critics, inside and outside the CPI(M), read it as the price the demand would pay to a superpower's purposes. The argument inside the movement replicated the argument in Parliament at street scale, and it had the same result: the government's course held, because the treaty gave the movement's demand a plausible road to fulfilment and left its critics arguing about the means to an end they shared. When recognition came on 6 December 1971 and the war ended ten days later, the movement's demand stood fulfilled under the treaty's roof, and the August sorting of the parties was ratified by the December outcome in the public mind. Political instruments are remembered by the company their outcomes keep; the treaty kept the company of a liberation, and that company, more than any editorial, fixed its place in the republic's memory.

The state governments that carried the crisis received the treaty through their own arithmetic. West Bengal, governed under President's rule through the emergency's first phase and holding the largest camp population, measured the signature in the only units its administrators possessed: a shortened horizon for the camps and a plausible end to the fiscal haemorrhage. Assam and Tripura, where the refugee presence altered district demographies in ratios no other Indian region approached, Tripura's refugee count approaching the scale of its own population, received the treaty as the first document of the crisis that promised an ending rather than an administration. Tamil Nadu's DMK government, which had made the Bengali cause a platform cause through the summer, endorsed the instrument as the government's necessary step. The regional receptions complete the second signal's map: the treaty sorted not only the parties of the Lok Sabha but the governments of the states that bore the crisis, and it sorted them, as the map predicts, by their exposure to the emergency the signature answered. A formation's distance from the refugee camps predicted its warmth toward the treaty with a regularity that no theory of the instrument as pure diplomacy can explain, and that the domestic-signal reading explains at once: the audiences nearest the burden heard the signal loudest.

## Parliament and the Press: The August Reckoning in Public

The treaty's formal domestic passage ran through the Lok Sabha on the day of signature. Swaran Singh laid the text on the table of the House on 9 August 1971 and made the statement quoted above, framing the instrument as continuity: a treaty of peace and non-aggression, fully consistent with the policy of non-alignment, directed against no country. The debate that followed across the August session rehearsed the party positions set out in the preceding section, and its record, printed in the Lok Sabha Debates for the monsoon session of 1971, preserves the exact terms in which the political class argued the signal. Government speakers stressed the refugee emergency and the American tilt. Jana Sangh speakers stressed the surrender of doctrine. CPI speakers stressed the identity of Indian and Soviet purposes. Congress (O) speakers stressed the Nehruvian precedent the treaty broke. No speaker of any party treated the treaty as a technicality. The House was not asked to ratify the instrument, treaties in the Indian system being an executive act, and that constitutional fact sharpened the politics: the debate was the ratification, conducted in public, for the record, before the audiences the signal addressed.

The press performed the same office in larger type. The Times of India welcomed the treaty in its editorials as a necessary response to the triangular pressure of July, a judgment its columns had been preparing since the Kissinger journey became known. The Statesman counselled caution, recording the government's case and the doctrinal cost in the same breath, in the manner its Calcutta and Delhi editions had made their institutional voice. The Hindustan Times supported the government. The Indian Express, under Frank Moraes's editorship, examined the consultation clause with a lawyer's suspicion and a patriot's acceptance. The Patriot, close to the CPI, celebrated. The Organiser attacked. Party and metropolitan papers divided, that is, along the coalition lines the second signal describes, and foreign correspondents in New Delhi reported the division to Washington and London as evidence of how far the Indian consensus behind the government extended. American diplomatic reporting from the embassy in New Delhi in August 1971, preserved in the Foreign Relations volume, treated the domestic reception as a material fact of the crisis: a government whose parliament and press had absorbed the treaty without fracture was a government whose threats and assurances alike carried weight. The second signal had reached its first audience as well, by the domestic route. The architecture held because its beams were visible from abroad.

The September consolidation completed the passage. Indira Gandhi travelled to Moscow from 27 to 29 September 1971, held talks with Brezhnev, Podgorny and Kosygin in the Kremlin, and returned with a joint statement that recorded the closeness of the two governments' positions and the Soviet Union's support for a political settlement of the Bangladesh question on the basis of the will of its people. Press reports of the visit, including the Hindustan Standard's Moscow dispatch of 29 September 1971, noted the Indian side's warning that the crisis might escalate after the monsoon and the Soviet side's public warmth. The sequence of August and September, signature, parliamentary debate, summit, reads in retrospect as a single sustained transmission: the treaty proposed, the domestic system ratified, the partner confirmed. By October, when Indira Gandhi carried the refugee case to the Western capitals, she travelled as the head of a government whose eastern policy had survived its domestic argument, and the Western chancelleries priced her accordingly.

## Signal Three: The Supply Architecture the Armed Forces Read

The third audience wore uniform. The Indian armed forces of 1971 were an institution with a procurement memory: the post-1962 expansion had been built substantially on Soviet equipment purchased on rupee credit, beginning with the MiG-21 agreement of 1962 and the licence production of the aircraft at Hindustan Aeronautics, and the services planned their readiness around Soviet supply habits, generous with platforms, slower with spares, priced in a currency India earned by exporting tea, jute and textiles rather than in the dollars it lacked. What the services required in the autumn of 1971 was not a new relationship but a guarantee that the existing one would run at wartime speed: aircraft delivered complete rather than in tranches, armour to equip the eastern formations, missile boats for the Karachi strike the Navy was planning, spares and ammunition at consumption rates no peacetime contract contemplated. The treaty was the political roof under which that acceleration could be demanded and granted. Service officers did not read treaties for doctrine; they read the signature of 9 August as the Kremlin's public commitment of its logistics to the Indian timetable, and the autumn's deliveries justified the reading.

The documented content of the acceleration is a matter of operational record. The Indian Navy took delivery of the Osa-class missile boats whose Styx missiles, fired by the missile boat squadron on the night of 4 December 1971 in Operation Trident, burned Karachi's oil farms and sank Pakistani warships in the war's most celebrated single action. Petya-class frigates and Soviet-built landing and patrol vessels filled the eastern fleet's order of battle. The Air Force received additional Sukhoi Su-7 strike aircraft and MiG-21 interceptors, with air defence radars and surface-to-air missile units thickening the cover under which the eastern campaign was fought in thirteen days. The Army's eastern armour, T-55 tanks and PT-76 amphibious light tanks of Soviet manufacture, crossed the riverine terrain of East Pakistan in the war's decisive manoeuvre, and Soviet transport aircraft supplemented the lift that moved men and stores between sectors. Histories of the war from the Indian side, including the official history drafted by the Ministry of Defence's History Division, record the supply relationship as the campaign's material precondition; Raghavan's 1971 adds the Soviet side, showing a Moscow that expedited deliveries through the autumn precisely because the treaty had converted supply from commerce into commitment.

### What changed for India's armed forces after the August 1971 treaty?

The treaty converted Soviet supply from a commercial relationship into a political commitment running at wartime speed. In the autumn of 1971 the services received missile boats, strike aircraft, interceptors, armour and spares on accelerated schedules and rupee credit, and planned the thirteen-day eastern campaign on the assurance that consumption would be replaced.

The signal ran in the opposite direction as well, and the return path matters to the domestic reading. A military that had been promised its material by a treaty the political class had publicly debated became, for the season, a stakeholder in the treaty's defence: the services' confidence in December, visible in the Eastern Command's audacious plan for Dhaka, rested on a domestic settlement that Parliament had argued and the press had absorbed. The third signal therefore closes a loop the strategic framing leaves open. War preparation, the rival reading's category, was not an alternative to the domestic signal; it was one of its four addresses, delivered to the one audience that would have to execute the policy if deterrence failed.

## The December Test: Consultation Performed in Public

The war of 3 to 16 December 1971 ran the treaty's machinery through every station the August text had specified. Pakistan's air strikes on Indian western airfields on the evening of 3 December opened the formal war; India recognised Bangladesh on 6 December; the Security Council met and was blocked, as described above, by the Soviet vetoes of 4 and 5 December. The consultation clause then operated in its literal form. Vasily Kuznetsov, the Soviet First Deputy Foreign Minister, flew into New Delhi at the head of a five-man delegation in the war's second week, and D. P. Dhar flew to Moscow, the two movements billed by both governments, in the contemporary reporting, as made under the consultation provision of the treaty. The substance of the mid-December consultations, as the declassified American record summarised it for Washington, was coordination of the endgame: Swaran Singh's insistence to the American ambassador that India had no territorial aims in West Pakistan, the Soviet interest in a war that ended with Bangladesh liberated and West Pakistan intact, and the shared interest in closing the conflict before the Enterprise group's arrival could internationalise it. The surrender at Dhaka on 16 December, whose ceremony and consequences belong to the companion account of [the domestic-consolidation-architecture framework](/2020/10/02/1971-bangladesh-war-indira-moment/), ended the eastern war on the thirteenth day, within the window the vetoes had purchased and the supply architecture had provisioned.

The December record also contains the honest limit of the first signal, and the signal reading gains credibility by stating it. The treaty did not deter the war itself, because the war's authors in Islamabad either discounted the Soviet commitment or valued the destruction of the Awami League's mandate above its price; Yahya Khan's regime attacked on 3 December in full knowledge of Article IX. Nor did the treaty deter the United States from the Enterprise demonstration, which sailed as a counter-signal precisely because signals are answered in kind. What the treaty deterred was the specific outcomes its drafters targeted: Chinese intervention, which Beijing's leadership debated and declined; an imposed ceasefire in the war's first days, which the vetoes prevented; and American escalation beyond demonstration, which the Soviet naval counter-presence and the consultation performance discouraged. Deterrence theory distinguishes the threat that fails from the threat that succeeds on the target it names. Article IX named intervention, imposition and escalation, and on those three targets the December record shows success purchased by a clause that promised only to consult.

The western endgame displayed the consultation architecture in its restraining register, a register the treaty's critics rarely credit. India's war aims, stated by Swaran Singh to the American ambassador in the consultations of mid-December and recorded in the American minutes, excluded territorial gain in West Pakistan and the destruction of the Pakistani state; the unilateral ceasefire India declared on the western front on 17 December, the day after the Dhaka surrender, converted the statement into fact. Soviet diplomacy in the same days pressed for the same closure, Moscow's interest in a short war and an intact Pakistan coinciding with New Delhi's declared aims, and the third Soviet veto of the Security Council season, cast as the Council arranged the ceasefire's formal terms, belonged to the management of the ending rather than to the protection of the offensive. The restraint matters to the adjudication below, because a war-preparation instrument pure and simple would have had no use for it: the treaty had been built to make a bounded war possible and a general war unnecessary, and its December career, veto and deployment and consultation and closure in thirteen days, followed the design with a fidelity that the design's critics and defenders can dispute only at the level of motive. The record of effects is not in dispute, and the record is the article's ground.

## Signal Four: Non-Alignment Argued at Home, 1971 to 1977

The fourth audience was the republic's own political culture, and its signal was the slowest and the most durable. The treaty forced the question that Indian foreign policy had deferred since 1947 into a single argument: was non-alignment a doctrine of equidistance, which a treaty with a superpower violated by definition, or a doctrine of independent judgment, which a government practised by choosing its instruments case by case? The founding settlement, whose terms are set out in [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/), had deliberately left the question ambiguous, because Nehru's India had needed both readings at different hours. The argument of 1971 settled the ambiguity for a generation. The government, through Swaran Singh's parliamentary statements and Indira Gandhi's public defences of the treaty, argued the independence reading at full strength: India had signed no alliance, joined no bloc, accepted no bases and surrendered no vote, and a treaty that promised consultation under attack was the instrument of a state judging its own interest in a crisis not of its making. The critics, from the Jana Sangh's Organiser to the Congress (O)'s spokesmen to the CPI(M)'s People's Democracy, argued the equidistance reading: whatever the text said, a twenty-year security-flavoured instrument with one superpower, signed in a crisis, ended the posture the world had recognised as non-alignment, and India's voice in the councils of the non-aligned would carry a Soviet accent thereafter.

The test of the fourth signal came after the emergency of the crisis had passed, and it came in two acts. The first act was the non-aligned movement's own behaviour: India attended the movement's forums through the 1970s as a leading member, hosted no Soviet bases, supplied no vote on command, and in 1973 received Brezhnev in New Delhi, from 26 to 30 November, for a visit whose joint declaration spoke of peace in Asia in language New Delhi shaped as carefully as Moscow. The doctrine bent toward Moscow in those years, as critics charged, on Afghanistan's later agony among other questions; yet the form of independent judgment survived, invoked by every subsequent government as the licence for its own choices. The second act was sharper. The Janata government that took office in March 1977, led by Morarji Desai, the treaty's Congress (O) critic of 1971, announced a policy of genuine non-alignment and reviewed India's foreign commitments in that light. The treaty survived the review intact. Desai travelled to Moscow in October 1977 and again in June 1979, the instrument was administered by the government of its former opponents without amendment, and the consultation architecture outlasted the change of regime that had been expected to dismantle it. An instrument that a hostile successor government examines and keeps has passed the severest domestic test a treaty can face, and the fourth signal's content stands confirmed by 1979: the post-1971 direction was not one prime minister's alignment but a renegotiated national settlement, available to her successors of every party, under which India would lean where its interests required and call the leaning independence.

The long tail confirms the reading. The treaty was prolonged by agreement as its twenty-year term ran out in 1991, and when the Soviet Union itself dissolved in December of that year, the architecture was rebuilt with the Russian Federation in the Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation signed at New Delhi on 28 January 1993 during President Boris Yeltsin's visit, an instrument that kept the friendship and cooperation frame and dropped the consultation clause's security edge. Readers of the archived instruments, often consulted as scanned official documents through [an office file viewer for archived treaty texts](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), can watch the 1971 text's phrases pass into the 1993 text with the signal machinery removed: what remained was the relationship, what was retired was the crisis architecture that had made the relationship legible to four audiences at once.

## Adjudication: Cold War Alignment, War Preparation, or Domestic Signal?

The scholarly disagreement the brief names has three positions, and each deserves its strongest statement before judgment. The Cold War strategic framing, carried by the standard international histories of the Bangladesh war and by the treaty's own Wikipedia treatment, reads 9 August 1971 as a realignment: India, pressed by the American-Chinese opening, moved into the Soviet camp for the duration of the crisis, and the treaty is filed beside the era's other alignment instruments. The evidence for the framing is genuine: the timing after the Kissinger journey, the Soviet vetoes, the naval counter-deployment, the supply flood. The war-preparation framing, carried by the operational histories, reads the treaty as the diplomatic roof of a campaign plan: the government that signed it intended the December war, and the instrument's function was to insure that war against interruption. Its evidence is also genuine: the deliveries of the autumn, the consultation performance of December, the surrender on the thirteenth day. The third reading, the one this article defends, does not dispute either body of evidence. It disputes the sufficiency of the categories, on a ground the other two cannot reach: neither framing explains the treaty's domestic behaviour, the sorting of the party system in August, the parliamentary debate conducted as a public ratification, the survival of the instrument under the Janata review, or the quarter-century argument over non-alignment's meaning that the signature organised. Those are not decorations on a strategic act. They are effects the instrument was built to produce, visible in the drafting history from Grechko's letter to Dhar's final flight, and a reading that cannot explain them explains the lesser half of its object.

The complication the brief instructs this article to face is the strongest form of the objection: that the domestic content is over-determined, that any treaty signed by any government in August 1971 would have sorted parties and filled newspapers, and that the crisis explains the reception without residue. The objection fails on the treaty's own chronology. The text had existed since 1969, when its reception would have been the reception of a choice; the government declined to sign it for twenty-nine months because the domestic cost, the burial-of-non-alignment charge, exceeded the strategic gain in peacetime. The crisis did not create the treaty's domestic meaning; it repriced it. When the signature came, the government spent its August managing the domestic argument with the same care it spent on the diplomatic one, Swaran Singh's statement to the House preceding any comparable public exposition abroad, and the opposition's answers, party by party, were addressed to Indian voters and Indian doctrines rather than to Moscow or Washington. The strategic content operated, as the complication concedes it did; the article's claim is that it operated through the domestic signal, the consultation clause deterring Washington partly because Washington could read a parliament and a press already settled behind it. That is the framework of the pillar this article serves, the same framework by which [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) organises the whole series, and it is the claim the Four-Signal Map makes citable.

A closing comparison sharpens the adjudication. The constitutional-political reading of decisive instruments is familiar elsewhere in this series: a text is judged by the architecture it builds and the audiences it instructs, the standard applied in [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/). Judged by that standard, the treaty of 9 August 1971 belongs with the republic's architectural acts rather than with its diplomatic episodes. It rearranged the party system's foreign-policy positions for a decade, it armed a war and bounded it, it redefined the founding doctrine of non-alignment in the act of claiming to preserve it, and it gave the Indian state a template, part consultation clause and part domestic argument, that its successors would reuse whenever a foreign instrument had to be sold at home as independence rather than dependence. The mandate that made the signature possible, the 352 seats of March 1971, is the subject of [the bank nationalisation settlement's companion history](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/) in its organisational dimension; the treaty is what that mandate purchased abroad and defended at home, and the four signals are the receipts.

The counterfactual test, applied with the discipline the charter requires, confirms the judgment. Suppose the instrument of August 1971 had been a private assurance, a Brezhnev letter held in the Prime Minister's safe rather than a treaty laid on the table of the Lok Sabha. The first signal would have failed at birth, because deterrence that cannot be shown cannot deter; Kissinger's Washington would have discounted a commitment it could not read, and Beijing would have priced a rumour. Suppose, alternatively, that the treaty had been signed and never debated, an executive act absorbed without a parliamentary season. The second signal would have failed: the parties would have sorted themselves later, at leisure, in the war's aftermath, and the government would have carried a divided political class into December, a division the American embassy's reporting would have transmitted to Washington as a discount on Indian resolve. Suppose the cooperation articles had been omitted and the instrument reduced to its security clause. The third and fourth signals would have thinned to a war contract, deniable by the Janata reviewers of 1977 as one government's expedient, and the twenty-year career would have had no economic ballast when the crisis generation passed. Each subtraction removes an effect the historical instrument demonstrably produced, and the subtractions converge on the same conclusion: the treaty's domestic and international effects were not by-products of a strategic core. They were the product, engineered in the text's publicity, its parliamentary passage, its economic articles and its doctrinal framing, and the strategic effect rode on them. That is the adjudication this article enters, and the Four-Signal Map above is the form in which it can be cited, checked and, where the record demands, corrected.

## The Economic Architecture Beneath the Signal

The treaty's cooperation articles are the part of the text the strategic framings skip, and the domestic reading cannot afford to, because they carried the relationship's everyday weight for the two decades the instrument ran. India and the Soviet Union had built, since the 1950s, a trade and aid architecture unusual in the Cold War world: Soviet-assisted steel plants at Bhilai and Bokaro, heavy machine building at Ranchi, oil exploration and refining capacity, and a bilateral trade conducted in rupees through annual protocols, under which Indian tea, jute manufactures, tobacco and light engineering goods paid for Soviet machinery, oil products and defence equipment without drawing on India's scarce convertible currency. The treaty's Articles IV to VII, committing the parties to expand economic, scientific and cultural cooperation, were not boilerplate laid over a security core; they were the security relationship's fiscal foundation, the reason the supply signal of the autumn of 1971 could run at wartime speed on credit terms a dollar-denominated relationship would not have sustained. Dhar's group in the September 1971 Moscow talks took the economic agenda as its formal assignment, and the protocols that followed the visit expanded the trade targets for the years ahead.

The domestic politics of that architecture were as sorted as the politics of Article IX. The treaty's supporters in the planning establishment and the public-sector unions read the cooperation articles as the guarantee of the development programme's Soviet pillar: Bokaro's completion, the expansion of heavy industry, the oil relationship that would matter acutely when prices convulsed in 1973. Its critics on the liberal right read the same articles as the deepening of a dependence that tied India's industrial choices to a single supplier's catalogue and a single market's appetite. Both readings were available in August 1971 and both were argued in the press of the season; the treaty's text, characteristically, supported each in part. What the signal framework adds is the observation that the economic articles addressed the fourth audience in its material register: the political culture was being told not only how India would stand in a crisis, but how it would trade, build and borrow for twenty years, and the post-1971 direction the fourth signal announced was written as much in the trade protocols as in the consultation clause. The trajectory bore the announcement out. Across the 1970s the bilateral trade expanded several-fold under successive protocols, the Soviet Union rose to stand among India's largest trading partners and its principal source of crude oil and petroleum products on rupee terms during the price convulsions that began in 1973, and the long-term programme of economic, trade and technical cooperation concluded in 1979 projected the architecture forward into the following decade. Indian exporters of tea, tobacco, jute goods and, increasingly, engineering products acquired in the Soviet market a buyer whose demand was planned rather than cyclical, and the planning establishment in New Delhi acquired a corresponding argument, deployed in every parliamentary debate of the period, that the relationship paid its way in the currency India could earn. The dependence critics answered with the mirror image of the same figures, and the argument between the two readings of the trade ledger is the economic form of the fourth signal's long debate: whether the architecture emancipated India's choices from the dollar constraint or mortgaged them to a single partner. The treaty's framers, characteristically, had written a text under which both claims could be documented, and had left the verdict to the political process the signal was built to organise.

## What the Treaty Did Not Do: The Independence Record Inside the Instrument

The adjudication between the framings gains from a negative inventory, the list of things a genuine alignment instrument would have contained or produced and this one did not. The treaty granted the Soviet Union no bases, no facilities and no port rights in India; Soviet naval visits to Indian ports remained visits, arranged case by case, a point Indian ministers repeated in Parliament whenever the Jana Sangh pressed the charge of a Soviet naval presence. It bound India to no vote: India's positions at the United Nations through the 1970s were its own, warm toward Moscow on the questions where interests converged and uncommanded on the rest, and the Indian delegation's independence on matters from disarmament language to the law of the sea was a matter of record in the same years. It created no joint command, no integrated planning and no obligation that any Indian government cited as a constraint on a decision it wished to take. When India conducted its peaceful nuclear explosion at Pokhran on 18 May 1974, the decision was New Delhi's alone, taken in the teeth of the displeasure of both superpowers, and whatever Moscow's private reservations, the treaty supplied no lever by which they could be enforced in public.

The negative inventory matters because it locates the treaty's true nature between the two polemical descriptions of August 1971. The government's description, a treaty of peace and non-aggression, understated the consultation clause's engineered deterrent content. The opposition's description, an alignment and a leash, overstated a text that bound India to consult and to nothing else. The signal reading accommodates the inventory without strain: a signal is precisely an instrument whose effects run through the expectations of its audiences rather than through obligations enforced on its signatories, and the treaty's twenty-year career, in which it deterred once, reassured often and constrained almost never, is the career of a signal that its signatories administered as one. The critics who called it a leash and the ministers who called it a formality were both describing the same text from the ends of the domestic argument the text had organised; the map in this article is offered as the description from the middle, where the drafting was done.

## The Historians' Bench: Raghavan, Radchenko and Malhotra Weighed

The three bodies of scholarship the brief mandates each hold a piece of the treaty's truth, and the signal reading is built by weighing them against one another. Srinath Raghavan's 1971 (2013), the product of research in the archives of India, the United States, Britain and the former Soviet world, restored the crisis to its global frame and demonstrated, from the American and Soviet records together, that the treaty functioned in the calculations of every capital as a fact about Indian resolve as much as about Soviet commitment; Raghavan's own emphases fall on the war's international history, and his treatment of the domestic reception is thinner than his treatment of the chancelleries, a gap the present reading exists to fill. Sergey Radchenko's essays on Soviet-Indian relations, written from the declassified Soviet files, supply the Moscow end of the drafting channel: a Soviet leadership courting India against China, divided about the treaty's value, and surprised by the speed with which the instrument it had proposed in 1969 became, in 1971, the hinge of a war. Radchenko's evidence disciplines any Indian-centred account that treats the treaty as New Delhi's unilateral design; the signal had two senders, and its drafting history is a negotiation between a capital that wanted an anchor and a capital that wanted a shield.

Inder Malhotra's Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography (1989) supplies the third vantage, the view from inside the Indian governing circle, written by the editor who had covered the Prime Minister's career from its beginnings. Malhotra's account of the treaty's reception inside the government, its author's access to the participants giving it a weight that later reconstructions must argue with rather than around, supports the domestic half of the signal reading directly: the government of August 1971 thought about its parliament, its parties and its press as audiences of the signature in the same deliberations in which it thought about Washington. Where the three benches agree, the article has followed them: the chronology, the drafting channel, the December performance. Where they divide, on the weight of the domestic against the strategic, the article has adjudicated for the reading that explains the most evidence with the fewest exceptions, and has stated the strongest form of the contrary case in the complication addressed above. That is the discipline the stance charter of this series prescribes: a claim defended, the disagreement named, the evidence weighed in the open, and the reader left in possession of the map by which the judgment can be checked.

## Ratification and the First Year: The Instrument at Work, 1971 to 1972

The treaty's formal life began within the month its text prescribed. Article XI provided for ratification, with the instruments to be exchanged in Moscow, and the governments completed the exchange in September 1971, bringing the twenty-year term into force on the schedule the drafters had set. The speed was part of the transmission: a treaty ratified within weeks of signature, by both parties, in the middle of the crisis it answered, told its audiences that neither capital intended the instrument as a gesture. Gromyko's ministry and Swaran Singh's exchanged the ratifications while Indira Gandhi's Moscow visit of 27 to 29 September was being prepared, and the visit then functioned as the treaty's first working session, its agenda divided between the crisis diplomacy Dhar's political group handled and the economic cooperation his second group negotiated.

### When did the Indo-Soviet Treaty come into force?

The treaty came into force in September 1971, on the exchange of the instruments of ratification in Moscow, as Article XI prescribed, within a month of the 9 August signature. The twenty-year term and the consultation clause were fully operative before the Moscow visit of 27 to 29 September, and long before the December war tested them.

The first year tested the architecture in peace as December had tested it in war. The Soviet Union recognised Bangladesh in January 1972, among the first states to do so, and Soviet minesweepers and salvage vessels worked in 1972 to clear the mined and wreck-strewn approaches to Chittagong harbour, a practical act of the friendship the treaty's cooperation articles described. When India and Pakistan negotiated the Simla Agreement of 2 July 1972, the bilateral frame it established, two states settling their questions without outside mediation, suited Moscow's interest in a stabilised subcontinent and New Delhi's interest in excluding the superpowers from the Kashmir file alike; the treaty partner's satisfaction with an outcome it had not negotiated was recorded in the Soviet commentary of that summer. Brezhnev's Asian collective security proposal, which Moscow had floated since 1969 and pressed upon Asian capitals through these years, received from India the courteous non-committal hearing that the negative inventory above would predict: the treaty had bound India to consult with the Soviet Union, and India construed that obligation narrowly enough to leave the Soviet Union's largest Asian project without an Indian endorsement. The first year thus displayed the instrument's settled character, warm in the relationship it crowned, exact in the limits its Indian signatory drew around it, and legible to every audience that had learned to read it in August 1971.

## The Long Argument: Arms Dependence and the Treaty Debated, 1972 to 1991

Across the treaty's twenty years, its domestic argument settled into a durable form, renewed at each anniversary of the relationship and each arms negotiation. The dependence critique, first framed by the Jana Sangh in August 1971, matured into the standard opposition brief of the 1970s and 1980s: by the middle of that second decade, Soviet-origin equipment constituted the large majority of the Indian Air Force's combat fleet and of the Navy's principal vessels, and opposition speakers in Parliament, from the Janata benches of 1977 to 1979 and from the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Janata Dal thereafter, charged that a single-source dependence on Soviet platforms, spares and doctrine had mortgaged India's freedom of action more thoroughly than any clause. The government's answer matured in parallel and drew on the treaty's own history: the dependence was commercial and technological, priced in rupees and diversified where diversification was affordable, as the Jaguar purchase from Britain and France in 1978 and the Mirage 2000 purchase from France in 1982 demonstrated, and it had never once been converted, in 1971 or after, into a political instruction India obeyed. Brezhnev's visit to New Delhi in December 1980, weeks before his death's long approach and months after the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan, staged the argument's hardest test: India received the Soviet leader with full honours, signed the agreements his visit carried, and declined, in the joint documents and in Indira Gandhi's public words, to endorse the Afghan intervention, a refusal delivered inside the friendship's own ceremonies.

The argument's resolution, insofar as Indian politics resolves its arguments, came with the term's end. The treaty was prolonged by agreement in 1991 as its twenty years expired, an act of the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao in the first weeks of its economic reorientation, and within five months the Soviet Union had dissolved, leaving the instrument in force with a successor state whose relationship to it required the rebuilding completed by the 1993 treaty with Russia described above. The dependence the critics had feared did materialise in one narrow, documented form: the spares and support crisis that struck the Soviet-equipped services in 1992 and 1993, when the supply system behind the platforms fragmented among the Soviet successor states and the Indian services scrambled for contracts with factories in Ukraine, Russia and Central Asia. That crisis is the long argument's final exhibit, and it cuts both ways, as the best exhibits do: it proved the dependence real, and it proved, by the services' recovery through contracts India negotiated as a customer rather than a client, that the dependence had never become the political subordination the August 1971 critics predicted. The treaty outlived its term, its signatory state and its founding argument, and it is remembered, in the republic's political memory, by the season in which it was signed: the season a foreign instrument sorted a party system, armed a liberation and taught a doctrine to bend without breaking.

## Reading the Treaty from Its Records: The Archive Behind This Account

The signal reading stands or falls on documents, and the brief for this article names the three bodies of record on which it rests; a reader is entitled to know what each can and cannot prove. The treaty text itself, published by the Ministry of External Affairs and laid before the Lok Sabha on 9 August 1971, is the primary artifact: every claim in this article about the consultation clause, the incompatible-obligations clause and the twenty-year term can be checked against its twelve articles in an afternoon. The second body is D. P. Dhar's papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, the negotiator's own record of the drafting channel, the Moscow soundings of 1969 and the final week's work; the papers carry the bias of a participant who believed in his product, and the article has used them for the channel's mechanics rather than for its vindication. The third body is the Soviet record, the Indira and Brezhnev correspondence and the internal Soviet deliberations preserved in the Russian state archives and opened to scholarship after 1991, on which Radchenko's essays rest; that record shows the treaty's Soviet drafting purposes and its authors' private estimates of what Article IX would be made to mean, and it corrects, at several points, the Indian memoir tradition's tendency to make the instrument an Indian invention. The American record, published in the Foreign Relations of the United States volumes on the South Asia crisis and supplemented by the Nixon tapes and Kissinger's memoirs, completes the circuit from the receiving end: it is the archive in which the first signal's arrival can be timed, priced and watched as it altered decisions.

The archive also teaches the reading's limits, and stating them is part of the article's obligation under its stance charter. No document in any of the four records says, in so many words, that the treaty was designed to sort the Indian party system; the second signal is an inference from behaviour, from the speed and the doctrinal precision with which every formation declared itself in August 1971, and from the government's management of the parliamentary passage. Inferences of that kind are the ordinary currency of political history, and this article has marked its central inference by building the Four-Signal Map as a checkable artifact rather than a flourish. But the distinction between the documented and the inferred should be kept where the article has placed it: the text, the chronology, the vetoes, the deliveries and the 1977 survival are record; the fourfold address is the reading that organises the record with the fewest leftovers. A rival reading that organises the same record more economically is invited, by the map's construction, to name its rows and be tested against them.

One further record deserves naming because the domestic argument ran through it: the Lok Sabha Debates of the monsoon session of 1971, in which Swaran Singh's statement of 9 August and the opposition's answers stand printed verbatim. Parliamentary debates are the least mined of the treaty's sources and the most direct evidence of the second signal's content, because they preserve the exact vocabularies in which the political class priced the instrument before the war graded its predictions. The Jana Sangh's leash, the Congress (O)'s pedigree, the CPI's vindication and the government's continuity are all there in the holders' own words, unmediated by memoir. This article's practice throughout has been to prefer that record, and the treaty text itself, to any later reconstruction, and to flag the points, the September ratification among them, at which the public record runs thinner than the argument would like. The flag is the charter's price, and it is paid here in the open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When was the 1971 Indo-Soviet Treaty signed?

The Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation between the Republic of India and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was signed at New Delhi on 9 August 1971. External Affairs Minister Sardar Swaran Singh signed for India and Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko for the Soviet Union, concluding a negotiation first opened by Marshal Grechko's delivery of Brezhnev's letter in March 1969 and closed in a final week of drafting after D. P. Dhar's flight to Moscow on 4 August 1971. Swaran Singh laid the text before the Lok Sabha on the day of signature itself.

### Q: What was Article IX of the Indo-Soviet Treaty?

Article IX was the treaty's consultation clause. Each party undertook to abstain from providing any assistance to any third party that engaged in armed conflict with the other party. If either party was subjected to an attack or a threat of attack, the two parties were to enter immediately into mutual consultations in order to remove the threat and to take appropriate effective measures to ensure peace and the security of their countries. The clause promised consultation, not automatic military assistance, and its open wording covered responses from a Security Council veto to a naval deployment.

### Q: Who was D.P. Dhar?

Durga Prasad Dhar was the treaty's principal Indian negotiator and the official Indian accounts credit as its architect. A Kashmiri official who had served in Sheikh Abdullah's government and in the Indian delegation at the United Nations, he took up the Moscow embassy in February 1969, protested to Premier Kosygin against Soviet arms sales to Pakistan, and carried the treaty negotiation through its two dormant years. Recalled to New Delhi as chairman of the Policy Planning Committee of the Ministry of External Affairs, he flew to Moscow on 4 August 1971 to close the final text and flew again in December as the consultation channel. His papers are preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library.

### Q: How did the treaty respond to the refugee crisis?

The treaty answered the refugee crisis by changing the prices around it. By August 1971 more than seven million Bengali refugees had crossed into India's eastern states, and the government in New Delhi held that only a political settlement in East Pakistan could send them home. The treaty told Pakistan that an attack on India, or a widening of the crisis, would trigger Soviet consultation under Article IX, told the United States that intervention for Pakistan had acquired a Soviet cost, and told China that a Himalayan diversion would be made against a Soviet-engaged India. The deterrent frame let India sustain the camps, shelter the Mukti Bahini and prepare its December decision without facing the crisis alone.

### Q: Who signed the Indo-Soviet Treaty for India?

Sardar Swaran Singh, the External Affairs Minister, signed for the Republic of India. Swaran Singh had held the defence portfolio when Marshal Grechko first delivered the treaty proposal in March 1969, and he had moved to external affairs in June 1970, so the same minister received the proposal and concluded it. Andrei Gromyko signed for the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. After the signature Swaran Singh laid the treaty on the table of the Lok Sabha and defended it in the House as a treaty of peace, friendship and cooperation consistent with non-alignment.

### Q: How did Henry Kissinger respond to the treaty?

Henry Kissinger, President Nixon's National Security Adviser, read the treaty as a Soviet success and an Indian departure from non-alignment, and he answered it with the policy the American record calls the tilt toward Pakistan. The administration he directed protected Pakistan diplomatically through the autumn of 1971, continued licensing military spares, pressed India against military action at the frosty Nixon and Indira Gandhi meetings of 4 and 5 November, sponsored the ceasefire resolutions the Soviet Union vetoed on 4 and 5 December, and ordered the Enterprise carrier group into the Bay of Bengal on 10 December. In his memoir White House Years he presented the deployment as a signal to Moscow; in New Delhi it was recorded as a demonstration that arrived too late to change any decision.

### Q: What domestic political signal did the treaty send?

The treaty told India's political parties that the coming season's coalition lines would be drawn around a Soviet-signed instrument, and it forced each formation to declare itself on the record. The Communist Party of India was invited back into the patriotic mainstream, the Jana Sangh was handed applause for the Bangladesh cause with its fingerprints kept off the means, the Congress (Organisation) was given a doctrinal grievance against the Prime Minister, and the CPI(M) was pressed to separate the liberation cause, which it supported, from the instrument, which it distrusted. The sorting, conducted in Parliament and the party press in August 1971, converted a foreign signature into a domestic settlement that foreign capitals then priced into their own calculations.

### Q: How long was the Indo-Soviet Treaty meant to remain in force?

Article XI concluded the treaty for a duration of twenty years from its entry into force after ratification, and provided that it would be automatically extended for each successive period of five years unless either party declared its desire to terminate it by giving the other party twelve months notice before expiration. The twenty-year term ran out in 1991, the year the Soviet Union dissolved; the instrument was prolonged by agreement as the term expired, and the relationship was then rebuilt with the Russian Federation in the Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation signed at New Delhi on 28 January 1993.

### Q: What did Article X of the Indo-Soviet Treaty declare?

Article X recorded a declaration by each party that it would not enter into any obligation, secret or public, with one or more states, which was incompatible with the treaty. Each party further declared that no obligation existed, and that no obligation would be entered into, between itself and any other state or states which might cause military damage to the other party. The clause fenced the consultation promise of Article IX: neither signatory could quietly sign away the treaty's effect in a conflicting instrument, and neither could plead an older commitment as an excuse for inaction in the crisis the treaty contemplated.

### Q: Why did the treaty negotiations accelerate in the summer of 1971?

Three shocks compressed two dormant years of drafting into one final week. The Pakistan Army's crackdown of 25 March 1971 drove millions of refugees into India and made the crisis a question of war or indefinite burden. The American opening to China, opened by Henry Kissinger's secret journey of 9 to 11 July through Pakistan and announced on 15 July, showed New Delhi that Pakistan's protection in Washington had been purchased with the decade's largest diplomatic service. The monsoon calendar fixed the campaigning season. D. P. Dhar's special mission to Moscow on 4 August 1971 closed the text, and Gromyko's arrival in New Delhi completed the signature on 9 August.

### Q: Which Indian political party welcomed the Indo-Soviet Treaty most strongly?

The Communist Party of India welcomed the treaty most strongly. Aligned with Moscow through the long division inside Indian communism, the CPI had supported the Bangladesh liberation cause from March 1971 and had demanded recognition of Bangladesh and support for the Mukti Bahini. The treaty vindicated the party's line and its patron at the same moment. Bhupesh Gupta defended the instrument in the Rajya Sabha as a guarantee of peace, the party's weekly New Age celebrated the signature, and for one season the CPI stood inside a national consensus from which it had been excluded since the communist split of 1964.

### Q: How did the Bharatiya Jana Sangh attack the Indo-Soviet Treaty in 1971?

The Jana Sangh attacked the treaty as the burial of non-alignment and a surrender of India's freedom of action to Moscow. The party had demanded the strongest Indian support for Bangladesh, including recognition and military preparation, and its leaders pressed that case in Parliament through the spring and summer. The signature confronted it with a government that had taken the substance of its advice in a form its doctrine rejected. The Organiser, the movement's weekly, condemned the consultation clause as a leash, and the party's parliamentary spokesmen supported the liberation cause and the armed forces and refused to bless the instrument that covered both.

### Q: What objection did the Congress (Organisation) raise to the treaty?

The Congress (Organisation), the old guard that had split from Indira Gandhi's Congress in 1969, objected on the ground of pedigree. Its leaders, Morarji Desai among them, carried non-alignment as a Nehruvian inheritance, and their spokesmen treated a written security-flavoured clause with one superpower as the final proof that the Prime Minister had traded the founding settlement for expediency. The objection was not to friendship with the Soviet Union, which the old Congress had itself cultivated, but to a twenty-year instrument whose consultation promise no reading could square, in their stated view, with the doctrine India had preached at Bandung and Belgrade.

### Q: What Soviet arms supplies followed the treaty in 1971?

The autumn of 1971 saw the Soviet supply relationship run at wartime speed under the treaty's political roof. The Navy received the Osa-class missile boats whose Styx missiles struck Karachi on the night of 4 December in Operation Trident, with Petya-class frigates and other Soviet-built vessels filling the eastern fleet. The Air Force received additional Sukhoi Su-7 strike aircraft and MiG-21 interceptors with air defence equipment. The Army's eastern formations fielded Soviet T-55 tanks and PT-76 amphibious light tanks, and Soviet transport aircraft supplemented the campaign's lift. Deliveries ran on rupee credit, in the established pattern of Soviet-Indian defence trade.

### Q: How many times did the Soviet Union veto ceasefire resolutions in December 1971?

Twice. In the Security Council debates that opened with the war on 3 December 1971, the Soviet ambassador Yakov Malik vetoed the American-sponsored resolutions calling for an immediate ceasefire and withdrawal on 4 December and again on 5 December. The vetoes bought the Indian Army the days it required in the east. The United States then carried the question to the General Assembly, whose resolution of 7 December passed by 104 votes to 11 without enforcement effect, because action lived in the Council the treaty partner had blocked. A third Soviet veto followed later in December on a further draft as the western ceasefire was arranged.

### Q: What did the treaty's opening articles say about peaceful coexistence?

The treaty's opening articles placed the instrument inside the language of peaceful coexistence that the non-aligned world recognised as its own. Article I declared permanent peace and enduring friendship between the two states and grounded the relationship in respect for independence, sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-interference in each other's internal affairs and equality of benefit. Article II committed both parties to the principles of peaceful coexistence and to the relaxation of international tension. The framing was deliberate drafting: a treaty whose first articles spoke of coexistence and non-interference could be defended in the Lok Sabha as non-alignment practised, whatever its consultation clause promised.

### Q: How did the Indian press respond to the signing of the treaty?

The metropolitan press divided along the coalition lines the treaty itself had drawn. The Times of India welcomed the instrument in its editorials as a necessary answer to the triangular pressure created by the American opening to China. The Hindustan Times supported the government. The Statesman counselled caution, recording the government's case and the doctrinal cost in the same breath. The Indian Express examined the consultation clause with suspicion and acceptance together. The Patriot, close to the Communist Party of India, celebrated the signature, and the Organiser, the Jana Sangh's weekly, attacked it as an alignment. Foreign correspondents reported the division to their capitals as a map of the consensus behind the government.

### Q: Did the Indo-Soviet Treaty end India's non-alignment?

The question organised Indian argument for a generation, and the honest answer is that the treaty redefined non-alignment rather than ending it. The instrument contained no alliance obligation, no bases and no commitment to fight, and the government defended it in Parliament as independent judgment exercised in a crisis. The critics answered that a twenty-year security-flavoured treaty with one superpower ended equidistance whatever the text said. Practice settled the argument in the government's favour on form and the critics' favour on leanings: India kept its non-aligned membership and its freedom of vote through the 1970s, and leaned toward Moscow in those years on questions from Bangladesh to Afghanistan.

### Q: When did India sign its replacement treaty with Russia?

India signed the Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation with the Russian Federation at New Delhi on 28 January 1993, during President Boris Yeltsin's visit. The 1993 instrument kept the friendship and cooperation architecture of the 1971 treaty and carried forward the relationship between the two states into the post-Soviet settlement, and it dropped the consultation clause's security edge that had made the 1971 text famous. The replacement closed the 1971 treaty's career: signed for twenty years in a crisis, prolonged as its term expired in 1991, and retired when the state that had signed it ceased to exist.

### Q: What did Indira Gandhi's September 1971 Moscow visit settle?

Indira Gandhi's visit to Moscow from 27 to 29 September 1971 converted the August signature into an operating partnership for the crisis's final season. She held talks with Leonid Brezhnev, Nikolai Podgorny and Alexei Kosygin in the Kremlin, gave the Soviet leadership the Indian analysis of the Bangladesh crisis in detail, and warned, as the Indian side briefed the press, that the crisis might escalate after the monsoon if no political settlement came. The joint statement recorded the closeness of the two governments' positions and Soviet support for a settlement based on the will of the people of East Pakistan. D. P. Dhar led the Indian group on economic cooperation in the parallel talks.
