Did Marxism neglect gender? The accusation is usually delivered as a settled fact, something a reader is expected to absorb before any evidence is shown. This article refuses that delivery. It treats the accusation as what it actually is: a serious criticism with a theoretical component and a historical component, pressed hardest not by Marxism's opponents but by Marxist feminists working inside the tradition, and it insists that the two components be assessed separately, because they have different evidence, different defendants, and different outcomes. A verdict that lumps them together will convict the wrong party or acquit the guilty one, and most published verdicts on this question do exactly that.

Consider who is making the charge. The political movements that defined themselves against Marxism rarely bothered with this particular criticism, since their own records on women's emancipation were seldom better and often worse. The charge was developed instead by women who had joined socialist organisations, read the canonical texts closely, and found that a theory which promised to explain the whole of social life had surprisingly little to say about the half of humanity they belonged to. Its classic statements came from the women's liberation movement of the nineteen seventies, written by authors who described themselves as Marxists or socialist feminists and who wanted the framework repaired rather than discarded. That origin matters for how the charge should be read: it is an internal audit, conducted with the tradition's own standards of evidence, and it deserves an internal audit in reply.

What follows is organised as an audit rather than a polemic. The charge is first separated into five components, each dated and each answerable on its own evidence: a claim about what Marx wrote, a claim about how the central category of Capital is constructed, a claim about what Marxist parties did with women's demands, a claim about what states ruling in Marxism's name delivered and then withdrew, and a claim about the logical structure of the categories themselves. The replies are then given in kind, component by component, because a reply that answers the historical charge with textual evidence, or the textual charge with historical evidence, answers nothing. Only after both columns are complete does the article deliver its verdict, and the verdict is in three parts, because the evidence genuinely falls into three heaps: a part where the charge lands decisively, a part where it lands with qualifications, and a part where it fails. Two pieces of apparatus carry the argument. The first is the charge and verdict ledger, a table that grades each component separately. The second is a discipline borrowed from the series treatment of Leninism: charges against a body of thought and charges against the organisations that invoked it require separate evidence and yield separate verdicts. Three technical terms do heavy work throughout and are used here in their established senses: labour power, the capacity to work that the worker sells; the value of labour power, the cost of keeping that worker alive and working; and social reproduction, the labour, mostly unpaid and mostly performed by women, that produces and restores labour power itself. Each has its own full treatment elsewhere in the series, and each is linked rather than re-derived where it first appears.

## A criticism from within the tradition

Origins shape arguments, and this argument's origins lie in disappointment experienced at close quarters. The women who built the charge had typically arrived in Marxist organisations through campaigns where the theory seemed to work: against low pay, against slum housing, against wars. Inside those organisations they encountered a recurring pattern. Women's demands were welcomed as recruits and postponed as politics. Meetings about the woman question, as the older movement called it, were scheduled and then displaced by what speakers called the main business. When the women's liberation movement formed at the end of the nineteen sixties, many of its founding activists were veterans of socialist groups who carried both the theory and the grievance out with them. Juliet Mitchell, writing from within the New Left in 1966, had already argued that women's condition required its own analysis of structures that orthodox Marxism left unexamined, and her essay is often counted the opening shot of the Marxist feminist literature. The charge that followed was therefore never a demand that Marxism be abandoned for some rival creed. It was a demand, issued by its own adherents, that the framework explain the world it claimed to explain, including the kitchen and the nursery.

That provenance gives the charge a distinctive evidentiary character. External critics of Marxism tend to argue from outcomes they dislike to theories they have not read. The Marxist feminist critics argued in the opposite direction, from passages they could cite to silences they could locate. When Heidi Hartmann published the best known statement of the charge in 1979, she did not accuse Marx of personal prejudice, a claim that would have been both unprovable and beside the point. She accused the categories. Her article's title announced an unhappy marriage, and its argument was that the union of Marxism and feminism had subordinated the junior partner so completely that the partnership had to be renegotiated or dissolved. Whatever one concludes about that argument, it is not the work of an enemy. It appeared in a journal of Marxist political economy, addressed an audience of Marxists, and proposed as its remedy a more complete Marxism, one in which patriarchy stood beside capitalism as an object of analysis. Reading the charge as an attack from outside therefore misreads both its intent and its force. Its force comes precisely from loyalty: these were the criticisms of people who had staked their politics on the framework and found it wanting on the question closest to their own lives.

There is a further reason to take the internal origin seriously. A tradition's own dissidents usually know where the bodies are buried. The Marxist feminists could point to the specific chapter where reproduction is set aside, the specific congress where a suffrage demand was traded away, the specific year when a women's department was dissolved and its work declared complete. Vagueness is the refuge of the hostile summary; this literature is not vague. Any adequate answer has to match its specificity, which is why the components below are kept separate and why each is given its evidence before any verdict is reached. An answer that begins by conceding everything concedes too much, because some components of the charge are weaker than others and one of them, the claim that the framework is constitutionally incapable of addressing gender, is contradicted by work the critics themselves inspired. An answer that begins by conceding nothing concedes the argument, because other components, above all the record of parties and states, are documented beyond serious dispute.

### Who pressed the neglect charge first, and from what position?

Marxist and socialist feminists pressed it first, from inside the movement they criticised. Mitchell's essay of 1966 opened the discussion within the New Left, and the women's liberation activists of the following decade, many of them former members of Marxist organisations, sharpened a private grievance into a published theoretical charge with named texts and dates.

The chronology deserves a moment, because it explains the charge's tone. For roughly a century after the founding texts appeared, the woman question had an official answer inside Marxism, supplied chiefly by Engels's book of 1884 and repeated in party schools as settled doctrine. A criticism that contradicts settled doctrine does not develop in the open; it accumulates in experience first, in the gap between what the doctrine promised and what organisations delivered, and it surfaces only when a generation arrives with both the experience and the means of publication. The women's liberation movement supplied both. Its publishing houses, journals and conferences created a space in which Marxist women could address Marxist shortcomings without asking permission from the organisations being criticised, and the resulting literature has the unmistakable flavour of an internal reckoning long deferred. Sheila Rowbotham's histories recovered a buried past of socialist women's activism even as her theoretical work asked why the recovery had been necessary. Zillah Eisenstein's collection of 1979 gathered socialist feminist essays under the paired rubric of capitalist patriarchy, a label that itself embodied the charge: if the existing theory had sufficed, the pairing would have been redundant. The critics were building, not burning. But what they built began from the premise that something in the inherited framework had failed to see them.

## The charge separated into five components

Bundled charges cannot be answered, only applauded or resented. Separated, this one resolves into five distinct claims, each with its own evidence and its own strength. The first is a claim about the corpus: Marx wrote nothing systematic on gender, and the family appears in his work chiefly as an instance of ideology, a transmitter of property, or a supplier of cheap factory labour. The second is a claim about the central construction of Capital: the category of labour is built around the waged worker, and the reproduction of that worker, the daily and generational labour that returns him to the factory gate, is explicitly set aside rather than analysed. The third is a claim about parties: Marxist organisations deferred women's specific demands to a post-revolutionary future, treating them as divisive in the present and automatic in the sequel. The fourth is a claim about states: governments ruling in Marxism's name produced an early transformation of women's legal position, then reversed much of it, while a double burden of waged work and unpaid domestic work persisted throughout and women remained nearly absent from the rooms where decisions were taken. The fifth is the theoretically sharpest claim: the categories of Marxism are sex-blind, constructed without reference to sex, so that when they are applied to a division of labour that is in fact sexed, they absorb gender into class instead of explaining it. Hartmann's marriage formulation belongs to this fifth component, and it is the component on which the whole charge ultimately stands or falls, since the first four could all be true of a theory that merely happened to be practised badly, while the fifth alleges a defect in the instrument itself.

Two clarifications before the evidence. The components are not equally strong, and treating them as a single indictment conceals that. The party and state components rest on archives: congress resolutions, legal codes, the founding and dissolution dates of institutions. The corpus component rests on a reading of texts that anyone can check. The construction component rests on a genuine feature of Capital's method, openly declared by its author, and the argument there concerns the consequences of a declared choice. The sex-blindness component rests on a logical claim about what sex-free categories can and cannot explain, and it is the hardest to settle because it turns on what counts as an explanation. Readers should also notice what the charge, even in its strongest form, does not claim. It does not claim that Marx was unusually prejudiced for a man of his century, a biographical question of limited interest. It does not claim that rival traditions of the same period did better, since liberalism's record on women's legal personhood and conservatism's on everything else are hardly exhibits for the defence of either. And it does not claim that nothing in the tradition addressed women, since the same critics wrote extensively about the texts and organisations that did. The charge is narrower and therefore harder: that at the centre of the theory and at the summit of the movement, gender was systematically subordinated, bracketed or presumed away.

A word about the standard of proof used in what follows. Where the charge alleges an absence, the reply cannot simply exhibit a presence; a paragraph about the family in a work of three thousand pages does not refute the claim that the treatment was unsystematic. Where the charge alleges a pattern, a single heroic counterexample does not refute it either; one woman commissar does not answer a claim about leaderships across decades and continents. The honest contest is over proportions, mechanisms and counterfactuals: how much treatment, of what depth, with what consequences, and whether the mechanisms the theory supplies can generate the phenomena it is accused of missing. That is the contest the ledger at the end of this article scores. Readers who want the compressed version of the argument can turn to the ledger now and return; readers who want the verdicts earned should take the components in order, beginning with the oldest and most easily checked: the claim about the books themselves.

## The first component: what the corpus actually says about gender

The corpus component is the most readily checked of the five, and checking it requires distinguishing three things that polemics routinely blur: absence, incidental presence, and systematic treatment. Marx and Engels were not silent about women. Their work is studded with remarks about the family, about women in industry, about the relation between the sexes, and about the position of women in bourgeois and in proletarian households. What the critics allege is different: that none of this amounts to a sustained analysis, that the remarks are scattered, inconsistent in their assumptions, and subordinate to questions the authors considered more important. That allegation is substantially true, and the honest reply begins by conceding the measure of it, before asking how much it proves.

The earliest evidence comes from the manuscripts of 1844, where the young Marx made the relation between the sexes a test of human development. In that early sketch, the direct relation of man to woman is presented as the most natural relation of human being to human being, and the degree of emancipation in a society is said to be measurable by the position women hold in it. The passage is striking, and Marxist feminists themselves have quoted it approvingly, which is part of its interest: the founder had, at the outset, an instinct for the topic that his mature system did not develop. The manuscripts were unpublished in his lifetime, and their argument about the sexes sits inside a philosophical treatment of alienation that has no place for empirical questions about the household economy. When the mature Marx returned to the question of the family, he did so in a different register, treating it as a form of ideology and as an institution that transmits property and reproduces the class relation. The German Ideology, written with Engels in the mid eighteen forties, describes the family as the first social relation and the earliest form of the division of labour, with the wife and children placed under the authority of the husband as slaves, and notes that a latent slavery exists in the family as the first property. These are remarks of real bite, but they are remarks: a sociology of the family in a few paragraphs, undeveloped, and followed in the subsequent work by silence.

The Manifesto of 1848 gives the family its most famous treatment in the corpus, and it is a treatment that illustrates the charge rather than answering it. Its argument is that the bourgeois family is an institution of property, that bourgeois marriage is a system of wives in common for the owning class, and that talk of family values is the ideological cover of a property relation. The passage is rhetorical, polemical and aimed at a specific opponent, the bourgeois moralist who accuses communists of wanting to abolish the family. What it does not do is examine the family as an organisation of labour. It says nothing about who cooks, who cleans, who bears and rears children, and how that work is coordinated with wage labour. The working-class family appears in the Manifesto chiefly as a victim of industry, its members drawn separately into factories, its bonds dissolved by the wage system. The dissolution is treated as an incidental effect of capitalist development, and the text does not pause to ask whether the family might have its own logic of exploitation, its own internal hierarchy, its own history separable from the history of class. A defender might reply that a political pamphlet is not the place for a sociology of the household, which is fair. A critic can fairly reply that the same text found room for a sociology of the bourgeoisie, of the petty bourgeoisie, of the lumpenproletariat, of German philosophy, and of reactionary socialism, while the household received a paragraph. Space is a measure of priority, and the Manifesto's priorities are legible.

Capital itself contains the most substantial material, and it is concentrated in the chapters on machinery and the working day. The famous passage on machinery describes how the machine, by making muscular power superfluous, draws women and children into factory labour, and how the value of labour power is then calculated across the family rather than the individual man, with the result that the wage that once maintained a man and his family is now spread across all its working members, depreciating the value of each. This is a genuine analysis of a mechanism, and it is often cited by defenders as evidence that Marx understood the relation between domestic life and the wage system. But the mechanism operates entirely on the employer's side of the equation. Women enter the argument as a supply of cheap labour power whose entry depresses wages. Their work at home, the labour that keeps the household running and the worker returning to the factory, appears only as an absence: the household is the place where the worker consumes what his wage buys, and the transformation of purchased goods into meals, clean clothes, rest and care is assumed rather than examined. Critics have pointed out that the passage's most revealing feature is its silence about what happens after the factory whistle. The worker's children are born, fed and clothed by a process the text treats as given, and the process is described nowhere. A theory that set out to follow labour power from its production to its consumption would have had to analyse it. Capital follows labour power from the market to the workshop and stops.

### Did Marx write about women's oppression?

Not systematically. He addressed the bourgeois family in the Manifesto, women's and children's factory labour in Capital, and the relation between the sexes in the early manuscripts, but produced no sustained analysis. Engels's book of 1884 was the movement's standard treatment for a century.

Engels's book, The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, supplied what Marx had not, and the movement treated it as the authoritative Marxist statement on women for a hundred years. Its account is worth summarising because so much of the later debate is a debate about it. Drawing on the anthropological work of Lewis Henry Morgan, Engels argued that the subordination of women was a historical product, not a natural condition, tied to the emergence of private property and the need to transmit wealth through identifiable male heirs. He described the shift from an earlier world of group marriage and matrilineal descent to monogamous marriage as the world historical defeat of the female sex, a formulation that gives the book its dramatic centre. He argued that the bourgeois family was a property relation and that women's emancipation required their return to public industry, and he predicted that the abolition of private property would dissolve the material basis of male domination. The book is genuinely radical in its premises and genuinely influential in its consequences; the socialist women's movement drew its doctrine from it for decades. Yet its limits are precisely the limits the critics identify. Its anthropology, borrowed from a single source, has not survived scrutiny by later scholars of kinship, who have shown that the sequence it assumes from promiscuity to monogamy cannot be sustained and that matrilineal descent did not carry the political meaning Engels assigned to it. More importantly for this article, its analysis stops at the threshold of the household. Once women enter public industry, Engels assumes, the problem of their subordination is substantially solved, because its material base has been removed. The question of who does the domestic labour after emancipation, and on what terms, is never seriously posed. The book predicts that the family will dissolve into free association, a prediction that has the form of a promissory note rather than an analysis.

The unevenness of the corpus is itself a datum. A reader who opens the collected works looking for a theory of gender finds a handful of brilliant fragments: the measure of development in the manuscripts, the property analysis in the Manifesto, the machinery chapter, Engels's book. A reader who opens the same works looking for a theory of class finds a system: concepts defined, mechanisms traced, histories periodised, objections answered across decades of revision. The comparison is not flattering, and Marxist feminists have been right to press it. Yet the comparison also suggests a limit on what the corpus component can prove. A thinker can neglect a question without his framework being incapable of addressing it. Adam Smith neglected the economics of the household too, and nobody supposes that economics was therefore incapable of studying it; the neglect reflected a choice about what to foreground. The corpus component establishes that Marx treated gender as secondary, episodic, and largely derivative of class. It does not establish that his categories could not have been extended, and the existence of a century of Marxist feminist work that does extend them is evidence against that stronger claim. The proper verdict on this component, recorded in the ledger below, is that it lands partly: the absence is real and it is a defect, but it is a defect of omission rather than of incapacity, and an omission can be repaired without abandoning the framework that committed it.

### What did the Manifesto say about the bourgeois family?

Its argument treats bourgeois marriage as a property relation disguised as morality. The family of the owning class exists to transmit wealth, and its defenders' moral language hides that function. No analysis of household labour follows, and the working-class family appears mainly as a casualty of industry.

Two further observations complete the corpus component. The first concerns Bebel, whose Woman and Socialism of 1879 became the socialist movement's most widely read book on the woman question and the text through which millions of workers first encountered the idea that women's emancipation and socialism belonged together. Bebel's argument was ambitious: he traced women's subordination through history, condemned the double standard, and insisted that socialism without women's liberation was a contradiction. Yet Bebel's book is not a work of theory in the sense Capital is. It is a synthesis of existing arguments, moral as much as analytical, and its economic account of domestic labour never advances beyond the claim that the household would be reorganised by collective services under socialism. Its popularity is evidence of a demand the movement felt, not of a theory the movement possessed. The second observation concerns the movement's own reception of Engels. The Origin of the Family was reprinted, translated and taught as doctrine, and party schools taught it as the answer to the woman question in the same way they taught the Manifesto as the answer to the class question. Doctrinal status has a freezing effect: a text that is taught as the answer stops being read as a starting point. When later anthropologists demolished Morgan's sequence and when later feminists pointed out the book's silence about domestic labour, party orthodoxy was slow to notice, because orthodoxy had already filed the question as solved. The corpus component therefore ends where the movement component begins: the neglect in the books was compounded by the institutional decision to treat the most imperfect of the books as the final word.

### Where do women appear in the factory chapters of Capital?

They appear as a supply of cheaper labour power drawn into production by machinery, whose entry spreads the family wage across more workers and depresses its value. The domestic work that returns those workers to the factory each morning is assumed, never examined.

Before leaving the corpus, one more distinction is worth insisting on, because it shapes the verdict. There is a difference between a theory that has nothing to say about a phenomenon and a theory that says the wrong thing about it. Marxism's corpus does not say the wrong thing about women in any systematic way. Its scattered statements are, by the standards of their century, often progressive: the insistence that the family is a historical institution rather than a natural order, the contempt for bourgeois moralising about marriage, the recognition that factory labour transformed women's lives. The fault is thinner and harder to dismiss: the corpus treats gender as an epiphenomenon, something that follows from class relations and will resolve itself when class relations are transformed. That is a substantive theoretical position, not a mere oversight, and it is the position the later components test against the record. If gender were in fact derivative of class, then parties could reasonably defer it and states could reasonably expect it to dissolve. The historical evidence, examined next in the party and state components, suggests that it did not dissolve, and that deferral had costs the theory did not anticipate. The corpus component alone cannot settle that, but it can state the assumption that made the deferral seem rational to those who practised it.

The tradition's women were present from its first generation, and the presence has a biographical emblem worth a paragraph because it corrects the picture of a movement that discovered women in 1970. Eleanor Marx, the founder's youngest daughter, was a socialist organiser of independent standing: she worked among the unskilled workers of the new unions, helped organise women in the gas workers' and other strikes of the late eighteen eighties, wrote and lectured on the labour movement in two languages, and treated the organisation of women workers as central business rather than as an auxiliary. Her career belongs to the record of the movement rather than to her father's biography, and it shows the pattern of the whole subject in miniature at the very origin: the work among women was done, energetically and well, by women; the theory of the movement, written by the men who edited its journals and its classics, registered the work as activity and not as knowledge. Eleanor's organising generated no theoretical text that the canon preserved, and her example was commemorated as devotion rather than studied as method. Multiply the miniature by a century and the charge's shape is complete: a tradition rich in women organisers and poor in the theory of what they were organising about, because the organising was assigned to the movement's hands while theory remained the business of its head, and the head had classified the hands' knowledge as experience rather than thought.

The founder's practical record deserves the same two-sided treatment as his texts, because the defence sometimes exhibits it as though activity answered the charge of theoretical neglect, which it does not, though it does complicate the portrait of indifference. Marx supported the admission of women to the International Workingmen's Association and to its affiliated societies at a time when the question was contested inside the labour movement itself, and the General Council he influenced included Harriet Law, a secularist organiser who sat in its meetings as a full member in the late eighteen sixties. His account of the Paris Commune gave the women of Paris a conspicuous place in the heroism of the episode, noticing what many of the Commune's own chroniclers had also noticed, that working women had organised, agitated and fought as political actors in their own right, through bodies like the women's union founded during the siege to organise women's work and defence. The pattern across these exhibits is consistent with the pattern in the texts: recognition without theorisation. Marx saw women as political actors when events put them in front of him, praised their action in the warmest terms his prose affords, and drew no theoretical conclusion from what he had seen. The Commune's women did not prompt a reconsideration of the family's place in his system, any more than the women of the Manchester mills prompted an analysis of the household that supplied them. A thinker is not obliged to theorise everything he witnesses. But a charge of neglect is precisely a charge about the relation between witness and theory, and the practical record, fairly exhibited, shows the relation at its most favourable and still finds the gap: present in the politics, absent in the system, honoured in the journalism and bracketed in the science.

One exhibit complicates the corpus picture in a way both sides should welcome, because it cuts against simple stories in both directions. In the last years of his life Marx filled notebooks with excerpts and commentary on the anthropological literature of his day, above all on Lewis Henry Morgan's account of kinship and the gens. These notebooks, unpublished until scholars issued them in the nineteen seventies, show the founder, at the end of his career, working directly on the history of the family, annotating Morgan's sequence with his own sharp marginal questions and reordering the argument in ways Engels later followed when he wrote the movement's standard book. For the defence, the notebooks prove the topic was alive for Marx to the end, not a youthful interest discarded. For the prosecution, they prove the opposite side of the same coin: the work remained notes. The founder excerpted, questioned and arranged the materials for a study of kinship he never wrote, and the movement waited for Engels's synthesis, drawn partly from those very notebooks, instead of receiving one from Marx himself. A projected analysis that dies in excerpt books is a peculiarly literal form of neglect, neglect as unfinished business, and it also helps explain a timing puzzle the article returns to below: the notebooks reached print in the same decade in which the charge was pressed in print, so the critics and the corpus were, in a sense, published together, and the critics could read the founder's own hesitations in his own hand.

## The second component: labour power and the bracketed household

The second component is the most intellectually serious of the textual charges, because it concerns not what Marx said but how his central construction is arranged. The argument starts from a passage every student of Capital meets early. When Marx defines the value of labour power, he defines it as the value of the means of subsistence necessary for the worker's maintenance and reproduction: the food, clothing, fuel and housing that keep the worker working, together with the costs of raising the generation that will replace him. He adds that this value contains a historical and moral element, varying with the habits and expectations of the society in question. Then he proceeds. The worker's means of subsistence are assumed to be available in the market, purchasable with the wage, and the analysis moves on to the workplace, where labour power is consumed in production and surplus value is created.

The critics' point is that something enormous disappears in the word assumed. Means of subsistence do not walk from the shop to the worker's body. They are bought, carried, cooked, cleaned up after, and converted into a fed, rested, clothed and emotionally repaired labourer by work that is itself labour, labour performed overwhelmingly by women, unpaid, and invisible in the accounts of the system Marx analyses. The child who will replace the worker is not a line item either: she is carried, born, nursed, taught to speak, socialised into discipline, and delivered to the labour market by years of the same unpaid work. Marx's system knows the cost of reproducing labour power in the sense of a price. It does not ask who performs the reproduction, under what relations, with what compulsion, and to whose benefit. The bracketing is explicit. In the chapters on reproduction in the second volume, the maintenance of labour power is treated as a precondition that the capitalist takes as given in the individual case, a matter falling outside the capital relation proper. Marx knew the boundary existed and drew it deliberately, as a statement about the level of abstraction at which Capital operates. The critics accept that the choice was deliberate. Their charge is that deliberate choices have consequences, and that this one structured a century of Marxist analysis in which the household figured as a zone of consumption rather than of production, and the women who worked there figured, when they figured at all, as dependents of the class analysis rather than subjects of it.

The domestic labour debate of the nineteen seventies was the tradition's attempt to repair the omission from inside, and its fate is the strongest evidence that the omission was structural rather than trivial. Beginning with Margaret Benston's insistence in 1969 that women's work in the home was production for use, of goods and services that capitalism required but did not pay for, a generation of Marxist feminists tried to insert domestic labour into the categories of Capital: to determine whether it produced value, whether it produced surplus value, whether the housewife was productive or unproductive labour in the technical sense, whether the wage concealed two workers' production. The debate generated exact arguments of real brilliance, and it also generated the famous mockery that greets scholasticism: positions multiplied, distinctions refined themselves into parody, and readers outside the debate could not tell what practical or theoretical difference the outcomes made. Both responses to the debate deserve equal weight, as the charge of scholasticism was partly deserved and the settlement it worked toward was real. What the debate established, through exhaustion as much as through proof, was that the categories of Capital could not absorb domestic labour without strain, because those categories had been built for the analysis of commodity production and the household produces use values outside the market. Something had to give: either the categories would be stretched until they lost their precision, or the household would be assigned to a different theoretical register. The first path produced the dead ends the mockery remembers. The second path produced, eventually, a theory of the whole process of reproduction that took the household seriously on its own terms. That second path is examined in the replies below. For now the point to register is diagnostic: the difficulty the debaters encountered is itself evidence for the second component. A framework in which the repair requires a decade of contorted argument is a framework in which something was, in fact, bracketed.

The boundary the bracket drew has never stood still, and following its movement supplies a test of the framework that both sides of this argument should accept. Across the capitalist centuries, portions of reproductive labour have repeatedly crossed the line from the household into the market and back: laundry left the home for the commercial washhouse and returned with the washing machine; food preparation moved toward the restaurant, the canteen and the prepared meal without ever surrendering the kitchen; childcare oscillates between the family, the market and the state according to policy and price. A theory of reproduction worth having must be able to describe this traffic, and the interesting question is whether the inherited framework can. Its defenders point out, correctly, that the categories handle the crossings with ease: work that enters the market becomes labour producing commodities and falls under the theory of value; work withdrawn into the household leaves the value relation and must be analysed as part of the reproduction of labour power. The critics' rejoinder is subtler and has been given memorable form by historians of household technology, Ruth Schwartz Cowan most famously, who showed that the industrialisation of the home, the arrival of appliances, running water and commercial services, did not reduce the housewife's labour across the twentieth century, because standards rose with capacity: cleanliness, diet and childcare were redefined upward as fast as the machines lightened them, and the work expanded to fill its new definition. That finding is a problem for any simple story in which capital rationalises reproduction as it rationalises production, and it is equally a problem for any theory that treats the household as a stagnant residue. What it describes is a sphere with its own dynamics, responsive to the market but not governed by the market's logic, which is precisely the sphere whose existence the second component alleges the founding texts declined to theorise. The traffic across the boundary, and the sphere's stubborn internal law, are the phenomena the repair was built to explain; their visibility in the record is the measure of what the bracket concealed.

A second look at the machinery chapter sharpens the point, because Marx there comes closer than anywhere else to theorising the household economy, and the distance he keeps is instructive. His argument runs that machinery, by dispensing with muscular strength, makes the labour of women and children employable on a new scale; that capital therefore draws the whole family into the mill; and that the value of the man's labour power is consequently spread across the family's members, since what formerly had to reproduce one worker and his dependents must now be earned by four pairs of hands, with the result that each sells cheaper. Embedded in that argument is a genuine theory of the family wage, and defenders are right to insist that it shows Marx thinking about the household as an economic unit whose composition capital actively reshapes. What the critics add is the observation that the reshaping is analysed entirely from the side of capital's requirements. The family in the passage is a supply mechanism: it furnishes labour power in adjustable quantities and qualities, and its internal arrangements interest the analysis only insofar as they affect the price of what it furnishes. Who within the family eats less so that the mill hand eats, whose unpaid hours lengthen as the paid hours of the others multiply, how the family's own hierarchy bends under the new distribution of wages: these are questions the passage generates and does not ask. The pattern is the same one visible in the treatment of the reserve army of labour, the pool of the unemployed and partially employed whose existence disciplines the wages of the employed. Marx analysed the reserve army's composition with great empirical care, distinguishing its floating, latent and stagnant layers, and he knew that women formed a large part of its latent layer in the countryside and its stagnant layer in the sweated trades. The knowledge sits in the text as description. It never becomes a premise. A theory for which the sex composition of the reserve is a fact to be noted rather than a structure to be explained is a theory that has decided, in advance, which of its facts are theoretical and which are scenery, and the critics' whole case against the second component is that the decision, not any particular oversight, is what a century of students inherited.

### What does the value of labour power quietly assume?

It assumes that the means of subsistence purchased with the wage become a maintained worker without further labour. Cooking, cleaning, nursing and child rearing fall outside the definition, so the work that reproduces labour power is priced as a basket of goods and never counted as production.

A careful statement of the reply belongs here, because the second component is often answered by an evasion that does the theory no credit. The evasion says that every analysis brackets something, that Capital is a theory of the capitalist mode of production and not of everything, and that a book cannot be faulted for the books it is not. All of that is true and none of it answers the charge, because the charge is not that Marx failed to write about everything. The charge is that the particular thing bracketed is internal to the theory's central category. The value of labour power is not a peripheral concept borrowed for convenience. It is the hinge on which the theory of exploitation turns: the difference between the value labour power has and the value it creates is surplus value, the secret of profit. A theory of exploitation whose hinge concept presupposes a vast zone of unanalysed labour has a gap at its centre, not at its margin. The critics, Lise Vogel most rigorously, pressed exactly this point: the reproduction of labour power is not an external condition of the capital relation but a necessary moment of it, and a theory that treats it as external will misdescribe both. The mature statement of that argument, Vogel's book of 1983 on Marxism and the oppression of women, concluded that the framework contained the resources to correct the bracket, in the analysis of necessary labour and of the reproduction schemas, but that the correction had to be made deliberately because the tradition had not made it. That conclusion, from one of the charge's most rigorous proponents, already foreshadows the split verdict this component receives: the bracket is real, the consequences were real, and the incapacity is not proven, because the correction came from within the categories' own logic.

It is worth pausing over the famous concession Marx himself makes in passing, since defenders and critics both cite it and read it differently. The value of labour power, he writes, differs from other commodities in containing a historical and moral element: what counts as necessary subsistence expands and contracts with civilisation, custom and the outcome of past class struggles. Defenders read this as evidence that the category is open, historically sensitive, capable of registering the social organisation of reproduction when the analysis requires it. Critics read it as a label pasted over the gap: the element is named, never analysed, and functions precisely to let the argument proceed without examining the household. Both readings capture something. The phrase shows that Marx knew the determination of subsistence was social and contested, not physiological. It also shows that he had a device for acknowledging a problem in a subordinate clause and declining to pursue it. The honest assessment is that the historical and moral element is a door the text marks and does not open. Later Marxist feminists opened it. The charge against the corpus is that for a century, the movement treated the marked door as a wall, and taught students accordingly.

The deepest version of the second component connects the bracket to the theory's picture of the worker himself. The bearer of labour power in Capital is an individual who owns his capacity to work, brings it to market, sells it for a definite period, and consumes his income in order to return. He is legally free, formally equal to the buyer, and unencumbered. This figure is an abstraction, and Marx is explicit that abstractions are his method. But abstractions select. The actual workers of the nineteenth century, like the actual workers of every century, arrived at the market encumbered: attached to households, raising children, caring for the sick and old, embedded in relations of dependence that shaped what they could sell and on what terms. The unencumbered individual of the theory resembles, as critics have not failed to notice, the situation of a man whose domestic needs are met by someone else. The resemblance is not an accident of drafting. It reflects the historical experience the abstraction generalised, in which the male industrial worker was the paradigm case and the reproduction that sustained him was performed offstage by a wife. A theory can begin from a paradigm case; all theories do. The question is whether the paradigm silently becomes the universal, so that cases departing from it register as complications rather than as data. The critics' claim is that this is what happened: the male wage worker became the revolutionary subject in the theory's imagination, and struggles that did not fit his profile, struggles over childcare, housework, sexuality, reproduction itself, were classed as secondary by a logic inherited from the abstraction rather than argued on their merits. That claim bridges the textual components and the political ones, and it is to the political record that the article now turns.

## The domestic labour debate: the repair that mapped the gap

Because the second component turns on what happened when the tradition tried to repair the bracket, the attempt deserves a fuller hearing than a summary allows. Its full history belongs to the series article on the [domestic labour debate](/2016/10/01/domestic-labour-debate/); what matters here is its value as evidence, the way a stress test is evidence about the structure it loads. The debaters of the nineteen seventies were not hostile auditors. They were Marxist feminists who believed the categories could be made to work, and they set out to prove it by applying the theory of value to the household with full technical rigour. If the bracket had been a trivial oversight, a gap at the edge of the system, their project should have succeeded in a paper or two. Instead it consumed a decade, split into schools, and ended in a settlement nobody had predicted at the start. That trajectory is the datum.

The opening move was Benston's: the material of the argument matters more than its technical dress. Women in the home, she argued, produce goods and services that are consumed directly, outside the market, and this work is production in the plain sense, the production of use values on which all other production depends, performed by a group defined by sex and assigned to them as a sex. Wally Seccombe then attempted the rigorous version: housework, he proposed, creates value, because it produces and reproduces the commodity labour power, and the housewife's labour stands in a determinate relation to the wage, which purchases the means of her work as it purchases the means of the worker's subsistence. The counterarguments refined themselves quickly. If the housewife produces labour power, is her labour productive in the technical sense, exchanged against capital? If her product never reaches a market, in what sense is its value socially validated, and by what mechanism is her labour time rendered socially necessary rather than merely long? Jean Gardiner and others pressed the dependence of the household on the wage from the other direction: the level of housework is set by the wage's purchasing power and by the commodities industry supplies, so the household is subordinate to production in ways the value categories describe only by analogy. Mariarosa Dalla Costa and the wages for housework current cut through the technicalities with a political translation: whatever the categories decided, the work was work, it was necessary to capital, and naming a wage for it would expose the whole arrangement. Each position taught the others something, and the debate's collective finding, visible only from its end, was that the household could be described in Marxist language at length without the description ever quite becoming a derivation. The categories illuminated the household by metaphor and analogy while declining to govern it.

### What did the domestic labour debate settle, and what did it leave open?

It settled that housework is real, necessary labour on which the wage system silently rests, and it mapped exactly where the inherited categories strain when applied to the home. It left open the theoretical form of the repair, which a later generation supplied through social reproduction theory.

Two judgements on the debate must be held together, since the brief of any honest treatment requires both. The first is that the mockery the debate attracted, the eye-rolling at scholastic refinements about whether a cooked meal embodies value, was partly deserved: the discussion did at times mistake classification for explanation, and a movement with limited energies spent some of them deciding labels for phenomena whose politics were already clear. The second is that the mockery misses what the debate achieved. By failing informatively, it located the bracket with precision. Anyone who has followed the argument knows, at its end, exactly which questions the founding texts do not answer: how labour power is produced, by whom, under what compulsion, and with what division between the sexes. A map of a gap is not a bridge over it, but no bridge is built without one, and the theory that eventually crossed, social reproduction theory, began from the debate's map. The episode also carries a lesson about how traditions repair themselves. The repair did not come from the tradition's guardians, who mostly regarded the debate as a distraction. It came from the dissidents the guardians marginalised, working in journals the movement did not control. That pattern, in which the charge's targets and the charge's answers are produced by the same people for the same reason, is the deepest continuity in this whole subject, and it should temper any verdict that treats the tradition as a single defendant with a single will.

## The third component: parties and the politics of deferral

The third component moves from texts to organisations, and the evidentiary ground firms up. Whatever allowances are made for abstraction and emphasis in judging a book, a party's behaviour toward half its potential membership is a matter of resolutions, campaigns, internal discipline and documented choice. The charge here is that Marxist parties, across countries and across the whole period of their mass existence, systematically subordinated women's demands to objectives they ranked higher, and justified the subordination with a theory: since women's oppression derived from class society, it would be abolished by the abolition of class society, and raising it separately in the meantime divided the movement and delayed the event that would solve it. This doctrine had a name in the movement's internal shorthand, the idea that the woman question would be resolved after the revolution, and it functioned for decades as a reason to postpone equal pay campaigns, suffrage agitation, childcare provision, and the promotion of women within the parties themselves.

The record is genuinely mixed, and an honest article has to hold both halves of it in view, because the socialist movement also did more for women's organisation, earlier, than any rival movement of the nineteenth century. The German Social Democratic Party was the largest socialist party in the world before the First World War, and it built the largest organised movement of working women in existence at the time. Clara Zetkin edited its women's paper, Die Gleichheit, for a quarter of a century, and used it to argue, against sceptics inside her own party, that working women had to be organised as workers and addressed as women, on questions from maternity protection to the vote. At the Stuttgart congress of the Second International in 1907, Zetkin carried a resolution committing socialist parties to campaign for universal women's suffrage, against delegations that preferred to settle for manhood suffrage first. At Copenhagen in 1910 she proposed the international women's day that the movement then celebrated annually. Bebel's book, already discussed, went through edition after edition and was read in workers' households across Europe. No liberal party of the period had anything comparable: the socialist movement created the institutional forms, the press, the conferences and the doctrine through which working-class women entered politics as a constituency. That achievement is part of the record and any prosecution that omits it is rigged.

The other half of the record is what the charge rests on, and it is equally documented. The suffrage case is the clearest. Zetkin's Stuttgart resolution committed the International in principle, but national parties applied it selectively. The Austrian Social Democrats, facing a franchise struggle in the years before 1907, subordinated women's suffrage to the campaign for universal male suffrage and treated the women's demand as an obstacle to the achievable; the women of the party protested and were overruled, and Austrian women waited until the collapse of the empire for the vote their party had promised to fight for. The pattern repeated, with variations, wherever a party had to choose between a biddable reform for men and an unbiddable one for women. Trade unions allied to the parties campaigned for a family wage, a wage sufficient for a male worker to support a dependent wife and children, a demand whose logic entrenched the sexual division of labour even as it raised working-class living standards, and unions in printing, engineering and other skilled trades excluded women from apprenticeships or organised to keep them out of trades entirely, sometimes with the support of socialist rhetoric about protecting the family. Within the parties, women members were concentrated in auxiliary roles, their conferences treated as recruiting grounds rather than policy bodies, and their representation in leaderships remained token across the entire period. When women objected, the doctrine of deferral supplied the answer: these were secondary contradictions, the main enemy was capital, and particular grievances would be settled in the general settlement.

The doctrine of deferral deserves examination as a piece of reasoning, because its surface plausibility is what made it durable. It rests on the theoretical position inherited from Engels: women's subordination is a consequence of private property. If that premise is granted, the inference seems to follow: abolish the property relation and the subordination loses its base. But the inference contains a gap that the critics learned to locate precisely. Even if the premise is true, it does not follow that the abolition of private property automatically transforms the relations inside households, any more than it automatically transforms any other inherited practice. A transition has to be made, by somebody, against resistance, using institutions that have to be built. The parties that preached deferral were not planning that transition; they were declining to think about it, and the doctrine functioned as the decline's justification. When revolutions actually came, the new states discovered the gap in practice, as the next section shows: legal transformation could be decreed quickly, while the sexual division of labour, untouched by decree, reproduced itself inside the new order. The critics' deepest point about deferral is therefore not that parties were hypocritical, though some were. It is that the theory told them the problem would solve itself, and a problem that will solve itself is a problem nobody prepares to solve. The deferral was the theory's abstraction, applied as a timetable.

One more element completes the party component: the fate of the movement's own feminists. The socialist women's movement produced organisers of the first rank, and its history is punctuated by their collisions with the organisations they served. Zetkin herself spent much of her career fighting on two fronts, against bourgeois feminists who rejected socialism and against socialist men who rejected feminism, and her insistence that the two rejections were symmetrical was the founding insight of the whole tradition. Alexandra Kollontai, whose work in Russia is examined in the next section, was repeatedly marginalised by her own party, posted abroad partly to remove her from domestic politics, and watched the institution she had helped build be dissolved by the leadership she had served. The pattern is not incidental. A movement that treats women's demands as deferrable will treat the women who press them as impatient, and will promote, instead, the women who do not press them. Over decades that selection pressure shapes the movement's memory: the deferrable women are commemorated, the pressing women are footnotes. Marxist feminists of the nineteen seventies, recovering Zetkin and Kollontai from near-oblivion, were performing an act of archival criticism as much as of theory. They were demonstrating that the tradition had contained its own critics all along, and had buried them.

The pattern did not end with the classical period, and its later forms are instructive because the pressures that excused the early parties had changed. In the decades after the Second World War, the communist and socialist parties of Western Europe commanded mass memberships, municipal governments and, in some countries, a plausible road to national office. Their women's organisations were large, their formal commitment to equality was emphatic, and their internal life reproduced the old distribution of labour with remarkable fidelity: women ran the social infrastructure of the party, the festivals, the charities, the local welfare networks, while the strategy committees and the parliamentary front benches remained male preserves. When the women's liberation movement erupted outside the parties at the end of the nineteen sixties, its earliest recruits in several countries were women radicalised inside those organisations, and their testimony has a common refrain: they had been told, in organisations formally committed to emancipation, that contraception was a private matter, that equal pay would price women out of jobs, that childcare was a demand for after the victory, and that feminism itself was a bourgeois import dividing the class. The refrain matters because it is so consistent across countries whose parties differed in much else. Consistency of that kind points to structure rather than to personalities, and the structure is the one the charge identifies: an organisation that has classified a constituency's demands as derivative will experience those demands as friction whenever they are pressed, and will generate, generation after generation, plausible reasons why now is not the time. The reasons were often sincerely believed. Sincerity is not a defence available to an institution across a century.

The trade union dimension deserves its own paragraph, because it shows the subordination operating through material interest and not only through doctrine. The family wage was a bargain: unions sought a wage that would support a household on one income, employers in some trades accepted the principle because it stabilised a male workforce, and the state in several countries built its benefit systems around the resulting household form. Women workers paid for the bargain twice, in lower wages justified by their supposed secondary status and in exclusion from the trades where the family wage could actually be earned. Socialist unions were not the worst offenders, and some organised women with energy, but the movement's theory gave the bargain a progressive cover it did not deserve: if the family was a pre-capitalist survival destined to dissolve, then defending the male breadwinner's wage could be represented as defending the working-class family against capital, rather than as defending one worker's advantage over another's. The critics of the nineteen seventies, several of whom had been union organisers, named this precisely. A theory that cannot distinguish between the interests of the class and the interests of its male members will reliably sacrifice the second group's claims to the first group's comfort while describing the sacrifice as unity. Hartmann's patriarchy, whatever its theoretical fate, began as a name for that distinction.

One episode from the classical period belongs in the reply's column at full weight, because it shows the socialist women's movement acting with an independence and courage the parent organisations conspicuously failed to match. When the Second International collapsed into national war camps in August 1914, its parties voted war credits, its leaders entered war governments, and its international machinery fell silent. The first international socialist body to meet again, in defiance of the war and of its own parties' leaderships, was a conference of socialist women at Bern in March 1915, convened through the network of the international women's movement that Zetkin had built, with its journal as its organising spine. The women at Bern, delegates from both belligerent and neutral countries, resolved against the war and for the resumption of international solidarity, at personal risk and in several cases against the instructions of their parties. The episode does not acquit the parties of the deferral charge; the women met because the movement's centre had failed, and they met as the movement's designated auxiliaries. But it complicates any account in which the tradition's women figure only as victims of its neglect. The infrastructure the movement had built for its secondary constituency turned out to be the one part of the structure that survived the collapse intact, precisely because its builders had organised it with a seriousness the centre had never required of them. Historians of the International have increasingly treated Bern as the seed of the wartime revival of internationalism that led, by way of Zimmerwald later that year, to the reconstitution of a revolutionary left. The seed was planted by the women's department. A movement's margins sometimes preserve what its centre abandons, and the grading of the party component should carry that sentence alongside the convictions.

Between the classical parties and the postwar ones stands the Communist International, whose record compresses the party component into two decades. The Comintern of the early nineteen twenties took women's organisation seriously as a matter of central policy: its third congress, in 1921, adopted theses on methods of work among women, drafted with the participation of Zetkin and Kollontai, which instructed communist parties to build special commissions for work among women, to address women's specific grievances as communist business rather than as an auxiliary distraction, and to draw women into the parties' full political life. An international women's secretariat coordinated the work, and national sections founded women's journals and campaigns on maternity, prices and peace. The theses are a remarkable document for the prosecution and the defence to share: they state, as official doctrine, almost everything the later charge would demand, including the insistence that the parties' neglect of women was a failure to be corrected rather than a stage to be outgrown. Then the institution followed the trajectory the charge predicts. As the Comintern centralised and its priorities narrowed to the defence of a single state and the discipline of its sections, the women's apparatus withered: the secretariat was downgraded and absorbed, the journals thinned, and the special work among women contracted toward the celebration of anniversaries. No congress voted to neglect women. The neglect arrived as a sequence of reorganisations, each defensible in the language of efficiency, whose sum was the disappearance of the structure that had existed to notice women at all. Institutional historians of several persuasions have told this story, and it generalises beyond the Comintern: the machinery for pressing a classified-as-secondary demand survives only while somebody senior defends its budget, and the classification ensures that, in a crisis, nobody does.

A Western exhibit, from the largest communist party of postwar Europe, shows the deferral doctrine operating without any of the excuses of backwardness or encirclement. The French Communist Party emerged from the Resistance as the country's largest party, with a mass women's organisation, the Union of French Women, and a doctrine of emancipation as emphatic as any in the movement. Yet across the nineteen fifties and into the nineteen sixties, the party opposed the liberalisation of contraception and aligned itself, in effect, with the French state's pronatalist family policy, treating birth control propaganda as a bourgeois diversion from the real struggle and motherhood as the working woman's patriotic fulfilment. The position was defended at the party's summit, in terms that subordinated women's control of their own fertility to the movement's demographic and electoral calculations, and it held until the pressure of the women's movement, and of the party's own women, forced a reversal toward the end of the nineteen sixties. The episode matters because it is the deferral doctrine with the theory stripped down to its function: a specific, urgent, bodily demand of women, on which the party's women were organised and vocal, postponed for reasons of general strategy and defended by the claim that the general emancipation would settle the particular one in due course. No civil war explains it, no Stalin ordered it, and no hostile historian invented it: the party's own archives and its own later self-criticism record it. When the charge says systematic, exhibits of this kind, recurring across unconnected parties and decades, are what carry the word.

### Why did parties postpone women's demands so consistently?

Because the inherited theory made postponement look rational. If women's subordination followed from private property, then the revolution that ended private property would end subordination automatically, and separate campaigns for women's claims only divided the movement before that event. The premise licensed the delay.

Assessed as a whole, the party component lands heavily, though not without remainder. The remainder is real: no other nineteenth-century movement organised working women on this scale, the doctrine of women's emancipation was official rather than heretical, and the parties' failures coexisted with genuine pioneering. But the charge does not allege that Marxist parties were worse than their rivals. It alleges that they subordinated women's demands systematically, with a theory that told them subordination was temporary and harmless. That allegation is sustained by their own records. It is also, importantly, an allegation about organisations operating under electoral pressure, wartime pressure and the pressure of illegality, organisations whose behaviour reflects their circumstances as well as their ideas. The separation this article insists on cuts both ways: the parties' record cannot be read straight off the theory, because the parties also betrayed the theory on other questions, from internationalism in 1914 to democracy thereafter. Readers should hold that thought for the verdict, where the party component is graded against the movement, decisively, while the theory's share in the parties' behaviour is graded as an enabling condition rather than a sufficient cause.

## The fourth component: the early transformation in the socialist states

If the party component tests what Marxist organisations promised, the state component tests what they delivered when postponement was no longer available as an excuse. The evidence here divides into three periods, and the charge and the reply each own one of them. The first period, the years immediately after the Russian Revolution, belongs to the reply: it shows a government ruling in Marxism's name attempting the most rapid legal transformation of women's position attempted anywhere up to that time. The second period, the nineteen thirties and after, belongs to the charge: it shows much of that transformation reversed, its institutions dissolved, and its memory suppressed. The third element, running through both periods and long after, belongs to the charge as well, but in a subtler way: it is the persistence of the double burden and the near-total masculinity of political leadership, which no decree addressed and no plan targeted. Giving these elements equal weight is not an editorial compromise. It is what the record contains, and the ledger below grades the component on the whole of it.

Begin with the transformation, because its scale is still startling. Within months of taking power in 1917, the Bolshevik government issued decrees establishing civil marriage and divorce at the request of either partner, ending the legal authority of the church over family life. The family code of 1918 went further than any legal system in the world at that date: it abolished the legal category of illegitimacy, established the formal equality of spouses, made divorce obtainable without proof of fault, and treated marriage as a civil contract between equals. In 1920 the Soviet state became the first in the world to legalise abortion on request, on the stated ground that criminalisation drove the practice underground and killed the women subjected to it. Political rights followed the same logic: the constitution of 1918 guaranteed equal rights to women, in a period when British women had only a restricted franchise and American women were still campaigning for the vote. These were not gestures. They were the application, in law, of the doctrine the movement had taught for decades, and they were carried through in the middle of civil war and economic collapse, when the new state's administrative capacity was at its weakest. The will to legislate equality was evidently stronger than the chaos that might have excused delay.

Law requires machinery, and the machinery had a name: the Zhenotdel, the women's department of the party's central committee, founded in 1919 and led first by Inessa Armand and then by Alexandra Kollontai. The Zhenotdel's task was to carry the new legal order into the lives of women who had never heard of it: peasant women in villages where custom gave husbands authority backed by violence, workers' wives in factory districts, and, hardest of all, the women of Central Asia, where seclusion and the veil governed public life and where the department's campaign of unveiling, launched in 1927, brought its activists into direct and sometimes lethal confrontation with local custom. The department organised literacy classes, legal advice, maternity provision, communal kitchens and laundries intended to socialise domestic labour, and conferences of women workers and peasants at which grievances could be voiced to the state. Kollontai, its most famous leader, brought to the work a theoretical position more radical than her party's: she argued in her writings that the family itself was an economic unit that would wither as socialised services replaced it, that sexual relations had to be freed from economic dependence, and that the liberation of women was not a by-product of revolution but one of its measures. Her position, and her insistence on pressing it, made her inconvenient. From 1922 she was posted abroad in diplomatic roles, a promotion that functioned as exile, and the department she had shaped continued without her. The episode compresses the whole state component into one biography: a revolution that built an institution for women's emancipation, staffed by a woman of genius, and then removed the woman and, in time, the institution, while keeping the rhetoric.

Why did the transformation stop where it stopped? The legal revolution addressed women's status as citizens and spouses. It did not, beyond the Zhenotdel's communal experiments, address the organisation of domestic labour, and it did not touch the composition of power. The reasons were partly circumstantial: a ruined economy could not socialise housework at scale, and the communal kitchens of the civil war years were emergency measures that contracted when the emergency did. But the reasons were also doctrinal, in the precise sense established by the earlier components. The theory said that entry into public industry was the path to emancipation, and the state pursued entry into industry with enormous energy: female literacy, technical education and industrial employment all expanded massively across the Soviet period, transforming the material lives of women in ways that no honest account can minimise. What the theory did not say, because it had never analysed it, was that drawing women into waged work while leaving the household untouched would not liberate them but load them twice. The state built the first half of the programme with a clear conscience. The second half had no programme, because the theory had bracketed the question the programme would have answered. The result is the defining artefact of the state component: the most ambitious attempt in history to emanciplate women by decree, which transformed their legal and economic position while leaving the structure of their daily labour substantially intact, because the blueprint it worked from had a blank page where the household should have been.

### What did the Zhenotdel actually do?

The party's women's department carried the new legal order to women the state could not otherwise reach. It ran literacy and legal campaigns, maternity provision and communal services meant to socialise housework, and conferences where women workers and peasants addressed officials, until its dissolution.

The transformation's limits should not be allowed to shrink it retrospectively. For women in the Soviet Union of the nineteen twenties, the changes were experienced as a rupture with a past of legal incapacity, church marriage, and the husband's authority sanctioned by law and custom. Divorce on demand, maternity protection, abortion in hospitals rather than in back rooms, literacy for grandmothers who had never held a pen: these were concrete emancipations, felt in daily life, and the women who organised to deliver them were not dupes of a cynical regime but builders of something they believed in and had reason to. Accounts of the period written by its participants convey an energy that later decades of the same state did not reproduce. Nor was the Soviet case unique in its ambition. When the Chinese Communists took power in 1949, the first major law of the new state, promulgated in 1950, was a marriage law abolishing arranged marriage, concubinage and the purchase of brides, and establishing divorce and equal rights within marriage, a sequence that placed women's legal emancipation at the head of the revolutionary programme. Cuba's family code of 1975 went further than any Soviet measure toward the household itself, declaring in law that spouses shared responsibility for domestic work and child care equally. These are the exhibits for the reply, and they are substantial. A tradition indifferent to women does not legislate like this. The charge, to land, must therefore be aimed precisely: not at indifference, but at incompleteness and reversal, and it is to the reversal that the record now turns.

The Chinese case adds a variation that the Soviet template does not predict, and a complete record should register it. The marriage law of 1950 was enforced through mass campaigns in which women's associations carried the new rights into villages where arranged marriage and the authority of mothers-in-law governed women's lives, and the early years of the People's Republic saw real ruptures in the old family order, achieved at real cost to the women who enforced them against local resistance. The slogan of the period, that women hold up half the sky, announced an official commitment to women's participation in production, and participation followed on a vast scale. Yet the same pattern of limits asserted itself through different mechanisms. The women's federation, the mass organisation charged with women's interests, was repeatedly instructed that its work was subordinate to the general line of the party, and in periods of retrenchment it was women who were urged to return home to release jobs for men, an argument that surfaced openly whenever employment tightened. The household division of labour was never made a campaign target of the first rank, and the model worker celebrated in propaganda was typically a woman who carried both shifts without complaint, which is the double burden translated into an ideal. The Chinese record therefore confirms the state component from an independent direction: a revolutionary state with a different culture, a different development level and a different party history reproduced the same structure of achievement and limit, legislating equality in status while leaving the organisation of reproduction substantially untouched, because the doctrine it shared with Moscow had no chapter on that organisation. Shared doctrine is the variable that explains the shared outcome, and that is why this component, though graded against the movement, reflects back on the theory in a way the verdict will have to register.

## The durable residue: maternity provision and the welfare precedent

Grading the state component fairly requires isolating the element of the early settlement that the reversal left standing, because its survival rearranges the moral arithmetic in a way neither side's summary usually admits. The decrees of the revolutionary winter included a system of maternity protection that was, at its introduction, without parallel: insurance-funded leave before and after childbirth, protection against dismissal for pregnancy, nursing breaks counted as working time, and a network of maternity clinics and infant consultations intended to make childbearing survivable for women whose mothers had faced it as a private gamble. Much in the revolutionary programme decayed or was dismantled, as the record above shows. The maternity provisions bent under the nineteen thirties' turn, were reframed as rewards for service to the state's demography rather than as rights of working women, and operated amid shortages that mocked their paper generosity. Yet their skeleton endured across the whole Soviet period and across the states that copied the model, and it endured for a reason the charge itself helps explain: provision for mothers at work fitted the doctrine's central commitment, the drawing of women into social production, and therefore enjoyed the protection of the theory's core in a way that autonomous women's organisation, which fitted nothing in the doctrine, never did. What the framework valued, it kept; what it merely tolerated, it discarded when keeping became expensive. The distinction between the two fates is the cleanest natural experiment the record offers on how a theory's classifications govern an institution's survival chances, and it cuts for the prosecution and the defence simultaneously. For the defence, it shows the early state's solicitude for mothers was structural, not rhetorical, and that millions of women experienced the system first through a clinic, a leave entitlement and a protected job rather than through a slogan. For the prosecution, it shows the solicitude's precise boundary: women were protected as workers and as mothers, the two identities the doctrine recognised, while their standing as an organised political constituency with claims of its own was extinguished in the same decades. A state can be generous to a category and deaf to a constituency, and the Soviet record on women is unintelligible until those two postures are held apart. The women who used the clinics were real beneficiaries. The women who might have used a Zhenotdel to complain about the clinics' queues had nowhere to go. Both sentences are the record.

## Everyday life: the Bolsheviks debate the private sphere

Before the reversal hardened, the early Soviet years contained a genuine public argument about the transformation of daily life, and its existence matters to the grading because it shows the questions this article has pursued being posed, in Russian, by revolutionaries, within five years of the revolution. The Russian word byt, everyday existence in its material and habitual detail, became a political category in the nineteen twenties: how people ate, housed themselves, married, drank, swore and raised children was debated in the press as a proper object of revolution, on the explicit ground that a new society could not be built by people whose daily lives reproduced the old one. Trotsky's essays of 1923, collected as Problems of Everyday Life, argued the case at the movement's highest level: he treated manners, the family, the position of women and the struggle against drunkenness and boorishness as fronts of the revolution itself, insisting that the liberation of women required the material reorganisation of the household, communal dining, laundries and childcare, and not merely the proclamation of rights. Architects built communal housing intended to socialise domestic functions in brick. Jurists and activists argued over whether the family would wither away or be reformed. The debate was real, sophisticated and unresolved, and it engaged the precise territory, the organisation of reproduction, that the second component of the charge finds bracketed in the founding theory.

Set against the debate, however, stand the period's controlling voices counseling restraint, and the most documented of them is Lenin's. In a conversation of 1920 recorded by Zetkin, Lenin criticised the preoccupation with sexual questions among young communists in the West as an unhealthy distraction, dismissed the glass of water theory with contempt, and redirected attention to the discipline of the general struggle. Zetkin preserved the conversation because she took its author seriously; historians read it as the leadership's centre of gravity speaking. The theoretical radicalism of the everyday life debate thus coexisted, from the start, with a hierarchy of seriousness in which the general struggle outranked the private sphere whenever the two competed, and the private sphere competed whenever resources ran short, which was always. When the turn came in the nineteen thirties, the debate was not refuted but administratively ended: the journals closed, the communal experiments were defunded or repurposed, and byt was reclassified from a front of the revolution to a private matter beneath politics. The sequence refines the state component's attribution problem one last time. The reversal cannot be told as a simple story of a theory that never saw the household, because for a decade the theory's heirs argued about the household in print and built for it in brick. It is better told as a story about priority under scarcity: the household was seen, debated, and ranked, and when ranking became survival, the sphere whose claims the theory had always treated as derivative was the sphere sacrificed, with the doctrine of its derivativeness available to certify that nothing essential had been lost. A bracket, once drawn, does its deepest work not by hiding its object but by discounting it, so that when the accounts are settled, the bracketed item is the one that can be cut without the cut registering as a loss. That is the mechanism the whole state record exhibits, and it is the mechanism the feminist critics, two generations later, learned to name.

## The reversal, the double burden and the empty chairs

The reversal began with an announcement that the work was finished. In 1930 the Zhenotdel was dissolved, on the ground that the woman question had been solved by the construction of socialism and that a separate women's organisation was therefore unnecessary. The logic is worth dwelling on, because it is the doctrine of deferral returning in a new costume. Where the old parties had said the question would be solved after the revolution, the new state said it had been solved by the revolution, and in both cases the conclusion was the same: no autonomous organisation, no separate campaigning, no institutional memory charged with noticing what remained undone. The dissolution also abolished the one body whose function was to report upward on women's actual condition. What followed in the nineteen thirties was consequently not noticed by any institution with standing to protest it. In 1936 abortion was recriminalised, permitted only on narrow medical grounds, as part of a pro-natalist turn that glorified motherhood, decorated mothers of large families, made divorce progressively more expensive and more difficult, and reasserted the family as a pillar of social order. The legal architecture of 1918 was dismantled piece by piece across the following decade, culminating in the family legislation of 1944, which restricted divorce to a court procedure, withdrew recognition from unregistered marriages, and reintroduced distinctions the revolution had abolished. The direction of travel is unambiguous: a state that had begun by treating the family as a relic to be dissolved ended by treating it as an instrument to be strengthened, and women's emancipation, redefined as the combination of industrial work and motherhood, was subordinated to the state's needs for labour and for soldiers.

Attribution matters here, and it is the strongest card the reply holds on this component. The reversal was not the unfolding of a logic present from 1917. It belonged to a specific political turn, the consolidation of Stalin's regime, which reversed or deformed a great many of the revolution's other commitments at the same time: internationalism, workers' democracy, cultural experimentation, the nationalities settlement. On that reading, the reversal indicts Stalinism, and the early transformation, which Stalinism dismantled, stands as evidence of what the tradition's doctrine produced before the deformation. The critics' answer is equally specific and should be given its force. A doctrine that contains no defence against its own reversal is implicated in the reversal. The theory had taught that women's emancipation followed from the transformation of property relations; when the property relations had been transformed and emancipation was declared complete, there was no theoretical ground left on which to object that it was not. The bracketing of reproduction, the assumption that the household would follow the factory, left the tradition without a standard by which the double burden counted as a failure. Stalinism supplied the will to reverse. The theory, the critics argue, supplied the absence of resistance. Both halves of that account can be true, and the ledger grades this component on both.

The double burden is the element that survived every regime change, because it was never a policy and so could never be repealed. Its shape is simple to state. Across the socialist states, women entered waged employment at rates that matched or exceeded the capitalist world, driven by ideology, by labour shortage and by the insufficiency of a single wage. Their employment was real employment: skilled, protected by maternity provision, and accompanied by extensive if uneven childcare. At the same time, the domestic division of labour changed slowly or not at all. Cooking, cleaning, laundering, queuing for goods in chronically short supply, and the care of children and the elderly remained women's work, performed after the shift, in housing that was crowded and services that were scarce. Time-budget research, a field in which Soviet and East European sociologists were pioneers, documented the arithmetic decade after decade: men's leisure exceeded women's by a wide and stubborn margin, and the total working day of an employed woman, paid and unpaid together, was the longest in the society. The Cuban family code's injunction that husbands share the housework is the exception that illuminates the rule: a state had to legislate, in 1975, the sharing that emancipation was supposed to have produced, and studies of its operation found the injunction honoured unevenly at best. The double burden is the state component's most damaging exhibit precisely because it cannot be blamed on reversal. It persisted through the transformation, through the reversal, and through the long stability that followed, in every country that ruled in Marxism's name, and it maps exactly onto the blank page in the blueprint: the labour the theory had not analysed was the labour the states did not reorganise.

Leadership completes the component. A movement or a state may be judged, among other ways, by whose faces appear where decisions are made, and on that measure the record is stark. In the Soviet Union, across seven decades, exactly one woman, Yekaterina Furtseva, sat in the party's highest body, and she did so briefly, in the late nineteen fifties, as an exception remarked on at the time. The pattern elsewhere matched it: women's federations and parliamentary seats existed in numbers that looked impressive in yearbooks, while the committees that decided economic plans, appointments and wars remained male in composition to a degree that no one in those systems defended as principled. The critics connect the empty chairs to the doctrine with a straight line. If women's emancipation is a consequence that follows automatically from the general transformation, then the presence of women in power is a decoration rather than a mechanism, and a movement will not fight for a decoration. Defenders reply that the same empty chairs characterised every state of the period, capitalist and socialist alike, which is true and irrelevant in equal measure: the charge is not that Marxist states were uniquely bad, but that a tradition which theorised emancipation delivered so little of it at the summit. Both the achievement and the reversal are needed for a fair grade, and both are now on the record. The state component, as the ledger records, lands: decisively as to the reversal's fact and the double burden's persistence, with the attribution shared between a political turn that willed the reversal and a theory that had disarmed its own adherents against it.

The story has a coda that neither side of the traditional argument finds comfortable, and completeness requires it. When the socialist states of Eastern Europe collapsed at the end of the nineteen eighties, the arrangements that had structured women's lives there collapsed with them, and the consequences ran in opposite directions at once. The childcare systems, maternity provisions and employment guarantees that the old order had supplied were dismantled or allowed to decay in the transition, and women bore a heavy share of the transition's costs in lost services and lost security. At the same time, the parliamentary representation women had held under the old systems, substantial on paper because it was allocated, vanished almost entirely once elections became competitive, revealing that the numbers had never been attached to power. The coda matters for the grading of this component because it separates two things the socialist record had fused. The services had been real, and their loss was a real loss, which the reply may enter. The representation had been decorative, and its evaporation proved it, which the charge may enter. A fair ledger does not choose between these entries. It also notes the variation within the old record itself: the German Democratic Republic built childcare provision on a scale the Soviet Union never matched and liberalised its abortion law in 1972, evidence that the states ruling in Marxism's name were not a single case but a family of cases, more and less attentive to the household question within the limits of the shared doctrine. Variation of that kind is itself informative. Where a state chose to spend on the infrastructure of reproduction, women's double burden measurably lightened, which suggests the burden was never a fate imposed by the theory but a cost imposed by priorities, and priorities are exactly what the charge says were set wrongly.

The long stability that followed the reversal added a paradox that the critics of the nineteen seventies documented and that any full record must include. Under the mature Soviet system, women entered the professions in proportions the West did not match: medicine and teaching became predominantly female occupations, engineering and technical work drew women in large numbers, and the figure of the woman professional was an ordinary feature of social life rather than an exception requiring comment. Yet the feminisation of a profession within that system carried a penalty familiar from other societies: as an occupation's female share rose, its relative pay and prestige fell, so that the woman doctor of the late Soviet period occupied a respected but modestly rewarded place in a hierarchy whose summit, in medicine as elsewhere, remained disproportionately male. The paradox is theoretically eloquent. A system that had abolished the private ownership of the means of production, and that directed labour by plan rather than by market, reproduced the association between women's work and lower valuation through administrative decisions about wage scales and status, which is to say through choices its theory gave it no reason to examine. Nothing in the plan's categories flagged the devaluation of feminised work as a problem, because the categories registered the work's quantity and its sector, not the sex of its performers or the history of its pricing. Here the state component and the fifth component meet on late evidence: sex-blind categories, installed as the operating system of a planned economy, generated sexed outcomes without anyone deciding that they should, which is the strongest form of Hartmann's point and was assembled, characteristically, by the system's own sociologists before it was assembled by its critics abroad.

An independent control case strengthens the finding, because it rules out the explanation that everything turned on one country's leadership. Yugoslavia broke with Stalin in 1948 and built its own road, with workers' self-management as its doctrine and a partisan war, in which women had fought in large numbers, as its founding legend. Its women's organisation, the Antifascist Front of Women, had mobilised women through the war and into the reconstruction, running literacy campaigns, health work and political education on a mass scale, and it embodied exactly the kind of autonomous women's structure the charge finds missing elsewhere. In 1953 the Front was dissolved and replaced by a looser alliance of women's societies, on the stated ground that a separate mass organisation of women was no longer necessary, its tasks having passed into the general institutions of the society. The Yugoslav case is valuable to the argument because the variables differ: a different party, a different doctrine, a leadership in open conflict with Moscow, a women's organisation with genuine wartime roots. The outcome is the same shape as the Soviet one a generation earlier: an autonomous women's structure wound up by decision from above, justified by the completeness of the emancipation it existed to pursue, in a state whose commanding heights remained male. When the same institutional gesture recurs across rival variants of the system, the explanation that fits is systemic rather than personal, and it points back to the doctrine the variants shared: emancipation as a consequence to be declared, not a process requiring its own organised guardians. The declaration, in each case, was the dissolution.

### What ended the early Soviet experiment in women's emancipation?

Its own government did, by declaration and then by law. The women's department was dissolved in 1930 with the question pronounced solved, abortion was recriminalised in 1936, and divorce was progressively restricted, while motherhood was glorified as a service to the state.

## The fifth component: sex-blind categories and the unhappy marriage

The first four components, however they are graded, concern what Marxists wrote, deferred, built and reversed. The fifth concerns what Marxist categories can think, and it is the component that cannot be answered by an archive. It was given its canonical form by Heidi Hartmann, in the article whose full title is "The Unhappy Marriage of Marxism and Feminism: Towards a More Progressive Union", published in the journal Capital & Class in 1979. Because the brief of this article requires the sharpest form of the charge to be attributed and quoted rather than reconstructed, Hartmann's argument is set out here in her own terms and at its full strength, before any answer is attempted.

Hartmann's claim begins with an observation about the construction of the categories. The central concepts of Marxist analysis, she argues, are sex-blind: the categories of class, of the reserve army of labour, of labour power itself, make no reference to sex, and were built to analyse a system, capital, whose laws of motion operate without regard to the sex of the people they move. There is nothing accidental in this. A theory of capital must abstract from the personal characteristics of workers, since capital itself is indifferent to them. But human societies are not indifferent. The division of labour in every known society assigns the work of bearing, rearing and maintaining people predominantly to women, and assigns it not randomly but through durable systems of male advantage that long predate capitalism. When sex-blind categories are applied to that sexed division, one of two things must happen. Either the division is treated as a natural fact falling outside the analysis, or it is explained by whatever the categories already contain, which is to say by class. In both cases the specific question goes unanswered: not whether someone must perform the reproductive labour, since plainly someone must, but why it is women who perform it, everywhere, under successive modes of production, in societies whose class structures differ radically. Marxism, Hartmann argues, has no resources for answering that question, because answering it requires theorising patriarchy: a system of relations among men, with its own material base in men's control of women's labour power, which interlocks with capitalism but is not reducible to it. Her remedy followed from her diagnosis. Marxism and feminism had to be treated as two analyses of two systems, in a partnership of equals, a dual systems theory. The alternative, she wrote in the essay's most quoted sentence, was the union as it stood, in which, adapting the doctrine of English common law marriage, "Marxism and feminism are one, and that one is Marxism": the wife's identity absorbed into the husband's, her property his, her legal existence extinguished. The sentence is the charge in miniature. The marriage had produced a household in which one partner's questions were the household's questions and the other partner's questions were private troubles.

The strength of the argument should be felt before it is answered. Notice first that it does not depend on the historical record at all. Grant everything the reply claims about Engels's intentions, about Zetkin's campaigns, about the decrees of 1918: the fifth component stands untouched, because it is a claim about the logical furniture of the theory. Notice second that it explains the other components rather than merely adding to them. If the categories cannot register sex, then the corpus will treat women as an afterthought, the parties will experience women's demands as external interruptions, and the states will not perceive the double burden as a theoretical problem. The fifth component offers itself as the root of which the others are symptoms, and any reply that answers the symptoms while leaving the root unexamined has not answered Hartmann. Notice third that the argument has a predictive edge. It predicts that attempts to repair Marxism by applying its categories more thoroughly to the household will generate the contortions the domestic labour debate in fact generated, because the contortions are what happens when sex-blind instruments are forced onto sexed material. A charge that retrodicts the tradition's embarrassments and locates their common source deserves the name of the strongest objection, and this article treats it as such.

The objection also has a subtler layer that its popular summaries usually miss. Hartmann is not claiming that Marxists personally overlooked women, nor that the tradition lacked a literature about them. She is claiming something about what an explanation is. To explain a phenomenon, a theory must contain terms in which the phenomenon's specific features can be derived, not merely redescribed. Marxism can redescribe women's oppression in its own vocabulary with great fluency: housework becomes unproductive labour, the wife becomes a reproducer of labour power, the family becomes an ideological form. Hartmann's point is that redescription is not derivation. Nothing in the theory of value, the theory of accumulation, or the theory of class entails that the reproducer of labour power is female. A Martian equipped with Capital could predict factories, crises and class struggle. Nothing in the text would tell the Martian which sex would be found in the kitchens of the proletariat, and the Martian might reasonably expect the kitchens to be staffed by lot, by rota, or by the class that lost the last war. That the kitchens are staffed by women, in Manchester and in Magnitogorsk alike, is a fact the theory registers and cannot generate. It must import the explanation from outside, from a theory of patriarchy, or leave the fact as a brute contingency at the heart of its account of reproduction. That is the charge at full strength. The answers to it are given in the next section, after one more witness has been heard: the tradition's own verdict on the debate Hartmann started.

### What is Hartmann's marriage analogy claiming, exactly?

It claims the union of Marxism and feminism had subordinated the second partner entirely. Like a wife under the old common law, feminism lost its separate existence inside the partnership, its questions redefined as the husband's questions, which is why the essay demands a renegotiated union of two distinct analyses.

The debate that followed Hartmann's essay is itself evidence, and it cuts in both directions, which is why this article reports it before answering. The dual systems proposal attracted immediate support from feminists who recognised their experience in it: the insistence that male dominance had its own history and its own beneficiaries, irreducible to the needs of capital, named something that class analysis had kept sliding off. It also attracted a formidable internal critique, stated most sharply by Iris Young in an essay of 1981 that took Hartmann's title as its target and argued for moving beyond the unhappy marriage altogether. Young's objection was that dual systems theory bought its clarity at the price of the original problem: by assigning gender to a separate system, it left the Marxist theory of production exactly as sex-blind as before, a theory of capitalism in general whose categories still could not register that the division of labour within production itself is gendered from the outset. Two systems running in parallel, Young argued, is not an explanation of their entanglement; it is the entanglement redescribed as a diagram. The remedy proposed by Young, and developed by Vogel along a different route, was a unitary theory: one framework, rebuilt so that the production and reproduction of life appear as a single differentiated process, in which the sexed division of labour is internal to the analysis rather than annexed to it. The significance of this exchange for the fifth component is double-edged. Hartmann's critics from within Marxist feminism did not deny her diagnosis. Young and Vogel both accepted that the inherited categories, as they stood, could not explain the assignment of reproductive work to women. They disputed the cure. A diagnosis accepted by the physicians who reject the prescription is a diagnosis in good health, and the fifth component enters the ledger with that standing: its statement of the defect is conceded on nearly all sides, and the contest is over whether the defect required a second system or a rebuilt first one.

## The replies, component by component

Replies deserve the same discipline as charges. A reply that answers a strong component with evidence belonging to a weak one is a dodge, and the Marxist literature on this subject contains enough dodges to have damaged its own credibility. The replies below are therefore matched to their components, and each is allowed its genuine force and no more.

To the corpus component, the reply exhibits the texts. The corpus is not empty: the measure of emancipation in the early manuscripts, the family as the first relation and first division of labour in The German Ideology, the polemic against the bourgeois family in the Manifesto, the machinery chapter's account of women's and children's labour and its effect on the family wage, Engels's book as a sustained treatment of the family's history. That is more than the nineteenth century's rival traditions typically offered, and the reply is right to exhibit it. Its limit is the one the charge identifies: the exhibits are fragments surrounding a silence, and a century of orthodoxy canonised the least reliable of them, Engels's anthropology, as the complete answer. The corpus reply reduces the first component from absence to unsystematic presence. It does not overturn it.

To the bracket component, the reply pleads method. Capital analyses the capitalist mode of production at a level of abstraction at which labour power appears as a commodity whose reproduction is presupposed; every science brackets the conditions it does not theorise, and economics is not refuted by the existence of chemistry. The plea is sound as far as it goes, and it goes less far than its users suppose, for the reason given in the second component: reproduction is not an external condition of the capital relation but a moment internal to its central category, the value of labour power. A bracket at the periphery of a theory is method. A bracket at its hinge is a research programme postponed. The honest form of this reply therefore concedes the postponement and contends over its significance: the bracket was a stated choice, the concepts needed to lift it were present in the founder's own toolkit, above all in the analysis of necessary labour and in the reproduction schemes of the second volume, and the lifting, when it came, was accomplished by Marxists using Marxist tools. A bracket that the framework's own resources can lift is a defect of the tradition's agenda, not of the framework's logic. That distinction is the hinge of the whole verdict, and readers should mark it: on the corpus and bracket components, the replies convert incapacity into omission, and omission, unlike incapacity, is compatible with everything the critics themselves went on to build.

To the party and state components, the replies are historical, and they have been woven into the evidence above rather than segregated here, because on this ground the article's obligation was to present the record whole. Their sum is that the movement organised women earlier and more seriously than any contemporary rival, that the early Soviet legislation was the most advanced of its era, that the reversal was a Stalinist turn rather than a founding intention, and that the failures of Marxist states were shared with, and often milder than, the failures of states that made no emancipatory promise at all. Each clause is true. Their combined limit is also clear: the charge never alleged that Marxism was worse than its rivals, or that its intentions were base. It alleged systematic subordination, justified by doctrine, and on that allegation the replies mitigate the motive without dissolving the fact. A party may subordinate women's demands for intelligible reasons, under pressures that explain the choice, and the subordination remains subordination. The verdict on the movement components is therefore lands, with the mitigation entered in the record rather than allowed to change the grade.

The reply to the fifth component is the substantive one, and it comes in two forms that should not be confused. The first form is dual systems itself, which accepts Hartmann's diagnosis and builds the second system she demanded: patriarchy theorised alongside capitalism, with its own material base and its own history. This is a reply to the charge only in a limited sense, since it concedes the charge's central claim, that Marxism alone cannot explain the sexed division of labour, and repairs the situation by supplementing Marxism rather than by vindicating it. The second form is the unitary reply, and it is the one that contests the charge. Its developed version is social reproduction theory, whose full exposition belongs to its own article in this series and is linked rather than repeated here: [social reproduction theory explained](/2016/09/15/social-reproduction-theory-explained/). Its bearing on Hartmann's argument can be stated briefly. Where Hartmann holds that sex-blind categories cannot explain why women perform the reproductive labour, the unitary reply begins by refusing the premise that the assignment is external to the process being analysed. Capital requires labour power, and labour power is produced outside the wage relation, in households, by work that is gendered through and through. The question is then historical and material, not logical: through what arrangements is that production organised, who is compelled to perform it, by what mixture of economic necessity, law, custom and coercion, and how do those arrangements change as capital's requirements change? The unitary theorist argues that these questions are askable, and answerable, within an expanded Marxist frame, because the frame's central insight, that social forms are historical products rather than natural givens, applies to the assignment of reproductive work exactly as it applies to the wage form. Vogel's version grounded the possibility of women's oppression in the specific place of labour power's reproduction within the reproduction of the whole society, and in the contradiction between capital's need for that reproduction and its refusal to organise or pay for it. Later writers in the same line have traced how the boundary between production and reproduction is itself drawn and redrawn by states and employers, so that what counts as women's natural duty in one period becomes a market service or a public provision in another. On this account the sexed division of labour is neither a natural fact nor the product of a separate eternal system, but a historically maintained arrangement that a sufficiently developed Marxism can analyse.

The unitary repair has its own internal critics, and an article committed to grading fairly should record that the debate continues rather than closing it. The sharpest internal objection holds that social reproduction theory, in explaining the household by capital's need for labour power, risks a functionalism of its own: the arrangement is explained by the need it serves, and the specific subordination of women once again threatens to become a by-product, this time of capital's requirements rather than of class in general. On that objection, the theory explains why reproduction is organised cheaply and coercively, but the sex of the reproducer still enters as a historical inheritance the theory receives rather than a result it derives, which is Hartmann's Martian returning by the back door. Defenders of the repair answer that the demand for derivation is itself the mistake: no social theory derives its historically specific arrangements from first principles, and a framework that shows how the assignment is maintained, contested and transformed has explained what can be explained, the rest being history in the honourable sense. The exchange matters for the verdict because it fixes the precise size of the concession recorded above. The fifth component survives as a claim about derivation, and derivation may be an unreasonable demand to make of any theory of society. It does not survive as a claim of permanent incapacity, because the analysis the charge declared impossible now exists and is used, by Marxists, in Marxist terms, to study exactly the phenomena whose absence from the tradition the charge deplored. A framework that can be repaired from its own resources, by its own adherents, under the pressure of its own critics, is not well described as constitutionally blind. It is better described as a framework that was blind in one eye for a century because nobody with the power to commission the repair believed the eye mattered, and that description assigns the blindness jointly, to the theory that made the omission easy and to the institutions that found the omission convenient.

The repair's key term has a lineage inside the founding texts that is worth a paragraph, because it locates the extension precisely: the new theory is built from the old one's most neglected materials. Marx's second volume is devoted to the reproduction of capital, the process by which the whole system renews itself period by period, and its schemes of reproduction, tracking how departments producing means of production and means of consumption must exchange in the right proportions for the system to continue, are among the most admired constructions in the corpus. Reproduction, in that usage, names the renewal of capital and its material elements. The feminist extension takes the same word and widens its object: the system must also renew its labour power, daily and generationally, and that renewal is a production process with its own sites, its own labour and its own costs, occurring mostly outside the wage relation. Social reproduction theory is thus not a foreign graft but a completion of the founder's own project by its own standard, since a theory of how capitalism reproduces itself that omits the reproduction of its indispensable human input is incomplete on its face, once the omission is pointed out. The pointing out took a century, which is the charge. The completion took the founder's own least-read volume as its foundation, which is the reply. Both facts belong in the grade, and their conjunction explains why the unitary theorists describe their work as a return to Marx against the Marxist tradition, a description the tradition's historians have learned to recognise as the signature of its genuine renewals.

### How does the unitary reply differ from dual systems theory?

Dual systems keeps two theories, one for capitalism and one for patriarchy, and studies their interaction. The unitary reply rebuilds a single framework in which the reproduction of labour power is internal to the analysis of capital, so the gendered division of labour is explained within one account rather than annexed from another.

An earlier internal repair deserves mention here, because it shows the tradition generating the materials of its own correction well before Hartmann, and because its fate illustrates why the correction took so long to consolidate. Mitchell's essay of 1966, written when its author was in her twenties and published in the New Left's own journal, proposed that women's condition be analysed through four distinct structures: production, reproduction, sexuality, and the socialisation of children. The proposal's importance is architectural. Instead of asking whether the existing theory had a chapter on women, it asked which structures a theory of women's condition would have to contain, and it found the inherited framework rich in the first structure and thin in the other three. Here, in embryo, is the whole later debate: the four structures are the spaces that dual systems theory and social reproduction theory would later organise in rival ways. The essay was famous, much reprinted, and programmatically inert for a decade: the movement admired it and changed nothing, because a programme requires institutions to execute it and the institutions had other priorities. Mitchell herself drew the moral in later work, moving toward the conclusion that the woman question inside Marxism had been posed correctly more than once and shelved correctly never. For the replies, the episode is double-edged in a useful way. It acquits the framework of sterility, since the framework's adherents could generate the correction from inside, repeatedly, across a century. It convicts the tradition of a specific institutional vice, which is that generating corrections and enacting them were assigned to different people, and the enactors were never the ones who had generated them.

Now the concession, which the brief of this article requires to be made without softening, because a reply that wins by evasion loses the reader the charge most deserves. The unitary answer does not show that Marxism, from its own categories alone, can derive the assignment of reproductive labour to women. It shows that Marxist categories, extended by a theory of social reproduction that the founding texts did not contain, can analyse how that assignment is produced, enforced and transformed. The extension is real work, done by feminists, a century after the texts. Hartmann claimed that the categories as inherited could not explain why women do the reproductive work. After the unitary reply has said everything it can say, that claim stands in the form she made it: no deduction from the value theory yields the sex of the cook. What the reply changes is the inference Hartmann drew from the claim. She inferred a permanent incapacity requiring a permanent second system. The unitary tradition answers that the incapacity belonged to the categories in their inherited state, that states of categories change under criticism, and that a framework is not refuted by the list of questions its first formulation could not answer, any more than physics was refuted by the questions Newton could not answer. Whether that answer satisfies depends on a judgement about what a theory is: a fixed set of propositions, or a research tradition that grows by absorbing its critics. The ledger below grades the fifth component as partly lands, recording both the claim that survives and the inference that does not.

One defensive reflex deserves isolation because it recurs wherever the charge is raised in general company, and because its weakness is diagnostic of the whole problem. Confronted with the allegation of neglect, the tradition's instinctive answer for a century was to produce Engels's book: here is the treatment, here is the theory of the family's origin, the matter was handled. The reflex is understandable and fatal. It confuses the existence of a treatment with the adequacy of one, in a field where the treatment's empirical basis had collapsed and its central silence, the organisation of domestic labour, was the very point at issue. Worse, it reveals the classification at work in real time: a question that can be answered by producing a single volume from 1884 is a question the answerer has never had to think about since, and the production of the volume is offered precisely as a substitute for thinking. Marxist feminists learned to treat the reflex as the charge's confirmation in miniature. A tradition that had a living theory of the family would have answered with the theory's development, its controversies, its second and third generations. A tradition that answers with a relic has told you how the subject was filed. The book itself deserves better than its defenders' use of it, since it was written as an intervention, argued from the best anthropology available to its author and meant to be superseded by better; its canonisation as a terminal answer betrayed its author's method in the act of celebrating his conclusions. When the critics finally forced the supersession, they were, on this point at least, more faithful to Engels than the orthodoxy that brandished him.

The comparison with liberalism, which the cross-component reply below develops, has a textual form that deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the comparison most damaging to a lazy acquittal. Liberalism produced its systematic treatise on women's subjection early: The Subjection of Women, published under John Stuart Mill's name in 1869 and shaped throughout by Harriet Taylor Mill's arguments, analysed women's legal and social subordination as a systematic wrong and demanded its abolition on the tradition's own principle of individual liberty. The treatise exists, it is rigorous, and it predates Engels's book by fifteen years. A Marxist defence that rests on the century's general backwardness must explain why the backwardness permitted a liberal to write that book in 1869 and permitted the Marxist tradition to wait until 1884 for a slighter one, and then to freeze it. The honest explanation returns to the components: liberalism's central category, the rights-bearing individual, could be extended to women by a simple act of consistency, and Mill performed it; Marxism's central categories, built for the analysis of production, required the extension to be constructed, and construction is slower than consistency. But the sequel reverses the embarrassment. Liberalism, having produced the treatise, organised almost nothing: the suffrage was won by movements of women themselves, against the resistance of liberal governments, and the treatise's tradition built no women's departments, ran no literacy campaigns among peasant women, and legislated no family codes. The tradition with the better book did less; the tradition with the worse book did more, and understood less of what it was doing. That distribution, of insight without organisation on one side and organisation without insight on the other, is the comparative landscape in which the charge should be graded, and it is more interesting than any verdict of simple guilt or innocence that the comparison is usually summoned to support.

One more reply runs across all five components and deserves separate statement, because it concerns the comparison the charge invites and usually escapes. The charge is often heard, in general discussion, as a claim that Marxism failed women in some way that distinguishes it from the alternatives on offer in its centuries. Measured comparatively, that version of the charge collapses. Liberalism, the tradition of formal rights, maintained the legal non-existence of married women for most of the nineteenth century and conceded suffrage under pressure from movements it did not generate. Conservatism sacralised the domestic order Marxism at least historicised. The churches blessed it. Against that field, a tradition that declared the family a historical institution, organised the first mass movement of working women, legislated the world's most advanced family code within a year of taking power in one country, and produced, from within itself, the most searching critique of its own failures, is not the villain of the comparison. But the comparative reply, though true, is also a trap, and the article declines to rest on it. Marxism's claim was never to be somewhat better than liberalism. Its claim was to explain the whole of social life and to emancipate the whole of humanity, and it is against its own claim that the charge is brought and must be judged. A defendant who pleads that the neighbours were worse has conceded the indictment's facts. The verdict that follows is therefore rendered against the tradition's own standard, which is the only standard its critics, who were also its adherents, ever asked for.

## Beyond the fragments: the charge generalised to the left's own forms

One late text of the Marxist feminist literature deserves individual attention because it widened the charge's jurisdiction in a way that still shapes how the subject is taught. Beyond the Fragments, published in 1979, the year of Hartmann's essay, by Sheila Rowbotham, Lynne Segal and Hilary Wainwright, argued from the British experience that the problem was not only what the socialist tradition thought but how it organised. The forms of the left, its meetings timed against the rhythms of unpaid care, its premium on platform speaking and factional endurance, its division between those who theorised and those who serviced the organisation, selected for participants without caring responsibilities and filtered out those with them, whatever the programme said. The argument's force is that it explains a residue the doctrinal account leaves behind: organisations that changed their programmes, adopted equality policies and elected women to visible posts could still reproduce the old exclusions through their daily practice, because the practice was never audited against the lives of the people it excluded. The book's title names its remedy, the joining of fragments: the women's movement, the labour movement and the other movements of the oppressed were to learn from one another's forms rather than queue for admission to a centre that would grade their claims. For this article's grading, Beyond the Fragments adds a count to the party component that the congress resolutions alone do not capture. The subordination of women's demands was not only a series of decisions, each defensible in its moment. It was sedimented in procedure, in the unexamined normal of how a meeting runs and who can attend it, and sediment of that kind explains the charge's most puzzling feature, its recurrence in organisations founded by people who had read the charge and agreed with it. Agreement, the book teaches, is the cheapest form of absorption: a tradition can absorb its critics' conclusions while leaving untouched the practices that made the conclusions necessary, and the absorption then functions as proof that nothing further is required. Readers grading the movement component should weigh that mechanism heavily, because it predicts the future better than any doctrine does, and because it applies, as its authors intended, to movements far beyond the one examined here.

## Why the charge arrived a century after the texts

A natural question hangs over the whole subject: if the defects were present from the start, why did the charge take a hundred years to formulate? The delay is not an excuse for the defects, since a defect does not expire, but explaining it sharpens the verdict, because it shows which parts of the neglect were visible all along and which required new conditions to become visible at all.

Part of the answer is institutional. For the first century of its existence, the woman question had an authorised answer inside Marxism, and authorised answers suppress the formation of unauthorised ones. Engels's book stood in the canon where a research programme should have been, and generations of party education taught it as a conclusion. Criticism of a conclusion is disloyalty; criticism of a research programme is participation. The canonisation of the Origin of the Family therefore did double damage: its anthropology froze, and the question it addressed froze with it. A second part of the answer concerns who was positioned to write theory. The Marxist tradition's theoretical production was concentrated in parties and academies whose senior ranks were male to a degree that matched the states examined above, and questions become theoretical when the people who live them gain access to the means of theorising. Women's mass entry into higher education and into the professions is a phenomenon of the middle twentieth century even in the most advanced countries. The charge arrived when a generation of women arrived who had both the grievance and the doctorate, and who had passed through the socialist movement on their way. A third part concerns the wider movement of the nineteen sixties, which loosened every orthodoxy at once. The same years that produced challenges to the tradition's economics and its philosophy produced the challenge to its silences about gender, and the challengers borrowed courage from one another. A fourth part is archival and has been noted already: the founder's own late notebooks on kinship, and the recovery of forgotten socialist women by historians working in the new feminist scholarship, meant that the critics of the nineteen seventies commanded evidence about the tradition that the tradition's own schools had never assembled. The charge arrived late partly because its archive arrived late.

There is a fifth part, and it is the most uncomfortable for the defence, because it suggests the delay was not accidental but functional. A theory that has classified a question as secondary has no internal alarm that sounds when the question is neglected; neglect of the secondary is what classification means. The woman question could be deferred for a century precisely because each generation's deferral was ratified by the doctrine the previous generation had settled. Breaking the cycle required an external shock, the arrival of an autonomous women's movement that did not accept the classification, and the shock came from outside the parties even when the critics themselves came from inside them. That is why the charge, when it arrived, had the tone of an indictment rather than a seminar paper. Its authors were not reporting a discovery. They were reporting that a discovery had been available for decades, in the movement's own experience, and that the movement's theory had given it permission not to make it.

The recovery of the tradition's own buried history formed part of the same convergence, and it changed the charge's character from complaint to documentation. Rowbotham's Hidden from History, published in 1973, reconstructed three centuries of women's resistance and of the left's entanglement with it, working from pamphlets, trial records and movement papers that the official histories had not cited. Its effect on the debate was methodological: after it, claims about what the movement had done for women, or failed to do, could be checked against a recovered archive rather than traded as impressions. A generation of feminist historians followed her into the archives of parties, unions and women's departments, in Britain, Germany, Russia and beyond, and their collective work is the reason this article can grade the party and state components with confidence: the resolutions, the dissolution orders, the time budgets and the memoirs exist in print because scholars went and found them. There is an irony in the sequence that the critics themselves savoured. The tradition that prided itself on historical materialism, on the insistence that ideas be tested against the material record, had to wait for historians formed by an external movement to assemble the material record of its own conduct toward half of humanity. The archive, once assembled, was unforgiving in both directions, acquitting the movement of indifference and convicting it of deferral, which is roughly the distribution of grades the ledger below arrives at by a different route.

The immediate prehistory of the charge, inside the New Left of the nineteen sixties, shows the grievance forming in organisations that considered themselves the tradition's renewal rather than its continuation, which is why the standard defence, that the critics mistook Stalinist practice for Marxist theory, fails to reach it. In the American civil rights movement and its student offshoots, women who had been formed politically by the struggle against racial hierarchy began documenting a hierarchy closer to home: in 1965 two veterans of that movement, Casey Hayden and Mary King, circulated a memorandum comparing the position of women in the movement to a caste, and drawing the parallel with the movement's own analysis of race in terms the movement could not easily disown. The document is a founding text of the second wave precisely because it was an internal audit, addressed to comrades, in the movement's own language of liberation. The pattern repeated across the New Left's organisations in several countries: women typed the leaflets that announced the emancipation of mankind, cooked for the meetings that theorised it, and were heard, when they objected, as a distraction from it. Robin Morgan's furious farewell of 1970 to the male left, published in the underground press, announced that a section of the women's movement no longer regarded the audit as internal at all. Hartmann's essay, nine years later, is best read as the audit's return to the tradition in scholarly form, an attempt to bring the grievance back inside the framework before the farewell became final. Read that way, the essay's marriage metaphor is not rhetoric but reportage: it describes a partnership its author had watched, from inside, and its famous sentence is a wife's account of a household, offered to a court of the husband's peers in the hope that the settlement could still be renegotiated.

### Why did the criticism surface in the nineteen seventies and not before?

Several conditions converged. A canonical answer had frozen the question for decades, the women positioned to theorise had been excluded from the institutions where theory was made, and an autonomous women's movement finally supplied the audience, the publishers and the pressure that an internal reckoning required.

## The Kollontai case: the critic the movement produced and shelved

Every tradition is best judged, perhaps, by what it does with its own best critics, and on the woman question the Marxist tradition produced a critic of genius early, used her, and then shelved her so thoroughly that her recovery became one of the feminist seventies' archival triumphs. Alexandra Kollontai's career concentrates the whole argument of this article into a single life, and it is worth following in some detail for that reason.

Kollontai came to the woman question through organising, not through study. Working among women textile workers in the years before the Russian Revolution, she concluded that the party's indifference to women workers was not an oversight but a structural habit, and she fought the party's own instincts to build a bureau for work among women, against colleagues who regarded separate organisation as a diversion. Her argument with them prefigures every later round of the charge: the party held that women would be reached by general agitation, she held that workers divided by a specific oppression are not reached by agitation that ignores it, and the party conceded her bureau the way institutions concede such things, late and grudgingly. After the revolution she became, as people's commissar for social welfare, the only woman in the first Soviet government, and from that position she built the practical programme whose legislative fruits were described above: maternity provision, the protection of mothers and infants, the first steps toward communal childcare. The Zhenotdel's founding owed as much to her persistence as to any decision of the leadership. She was, in short, the movement's own answer to the charge, produced from within its ranks, armed with its theory, and more radical than its leadership on exactly the questions this article has been examining.

What happened to her is the other half of the exhibit. Kollontai's theoretical writings pressed beyond the party's settled doctrine in two directions. She argued that the family was not merely a legal form to be reformed but an economic institution that would dissolve as its functions were socialised, and she treated sexual relations as a sphere with its own history and its own claim to freedom, refusing the position that morality in private life was a distraction from politics. Both arguments made her enemies. The party's moral conservatives, who were numerous and increasingly powerful, regarded her views on sexuality as a scandal; its centralisers regarded her independent base among women workers as a factional threat; and her participation in an internal opposition current gave the leadership its administrative pretext. From 1922 she served abroad, as a diplomat of distinction, in posts that kept her far from Soviet domestic politics for the remaining thirty years of her life. Her writings dropped out of circulation at home. The Zhenotdel, her institutional legacy, was dissolved eight years after her departure. When Marxist feminists of a later generation went looking for predecessors, they found Kollontai in foreign editions and in the memories of the movement's margins, and the story of her shelving became, for them, a parable of the whole subject: the tradition had contained the correction, and had filed it under biography.

Her theoretical position is often flattened in the retelling, and one famous misattribution deserves correction because it shows how a tradition remembers the women it shelves: by caricature. The so-called glass of water theory, the doctrine that sexual relations under communism would become as casual as drinking a glass of water, was attributed to Kollontai by her critics and has followed her name ever since. Her actual writings on sexuality, including the letter on winged and wingless love addressed to working youth in 1923, argue nearly the opposite of the caricature: she distinguished possessive, purely physical relations, which she regarded as a bourgeois inheritance, from a richer erotic comradeship that would unite affection, equality and sexual life, and she warned explicitly against reducing liberation to licence. The caricature served a function. A woman theorist of sexuality is easier to file as a scandal than as an argument, and the filing spared her opponents the labour of answering her. Marxist feminists who recovered her work in the nineteen seventies made the correction part of their larger case: the tradition had not merely neglected the questions Kollontai raised, it had actively misremembered the raising of them, converting a theorist into a cautionary tale. Misremembering of that kind is not an accident of gossip. It is what institutions do to internal critics whose criticism touches the institution's self-image, and it is the biographical form of the same classification the charge identifies everywhere else: the woman's contribution registered as temperament, the man's as theory.

The parable should not be overdrawn, and two qualifications keep it honest. The first is that Kollontai was not a feminist in the later sense and would have disputed parts of the charge herself: she believed the woman question would be resolved within the general emancipation, held the party's class framework without Hartmann's doubts, and spent her polemical energy as much against bourgeois feminism as against her own comrades. The second is that her marginalisation had causes beyond gender politics, in the factional struggles of a party moving toward dictatorship, struggles that consumed male critics in far grimmer ways. Neither qualification dissolves the exhibit. A tradition need not be uniquely cruel to its women critics for the pattern to be diagnostic. The pattern is that the woman who pressed the neglected question furthest was the one the institution found least usable, and that her pressing is precisely what the later charge cites as proof the neglect was known, and knowable, from inside. The shelving of Kollontai is the party component written as biography, and it prepares the ground for the article's remaining task: to weigh the whole record, which the ledger now does.

## The woman question as a question: how a framing did its work

Buried inside the history is a small linguistic exhibit that repays attention: the tradition's own name for its subject. For a century, Marxists did not speak of a theory of gender, or of women's emancipation as a field of analysis. They spoke of the woman question, and the phrase deserves to be treated as evidence, because a question is a thing that has an answer, and the woman question was understood to have been answered. It was answered by Engels in 1884, and the answer was reprinted in party pamphlets, summarised in study circles, and cited to close discussions. Compare the tradition's other great questions. The national question, the agrarian question, the question of the state: each generated literatures, congress debates, revisions, heresies, and recognised classics in successive generations. Each was understood to be reopened by history. The woman question alone was treated as a solved problem whose solution could be carried in a pamphlet, and the consequences of that treatment ramify through every component of the charge. A solved question attracts no researchers. It generates no chairs, no journals, no second generation of specialists correcting the first. It leaves the field to organisers, whose practical knowledge, accumulated in women's departments and union women's committees, never ascended into the theory because the theory had declared itself complete on the subject.

The framing also shaped who was imagined to be asking. A question, in the movement's grammar, was posed to the movement by history and answered by the movement's theorists, and the woman question fitted the grammar with a peculiar neatness: men theorised it, in texts addressed to other men, about a constituency construed as an object of policy. Zetkin and Kollontai broke the grammar from inside, which is part of why both were so uncomfortable to their organisations: each insisted on addressing women as the subjects of the question rather than its topic, and each built institutions in which women formulated demands instead of receiving programmes. The feminist critics of the nineteen seventies broke it completely, by the simple device of writing theory themselves, in the first person plural of the constituency concerned. Hartmann's essay is unthinkable in the older genre. It does not ask what Marxism says about women. It asks what Marxism's way of speaking has done to the women who speak it, and it files for divorce in a court the older genre did not recognise. The distance between the woman question and the unhappy marriage is the distance the tradition travelled in a century, and the travel was accomplished almost entirely by women travelling against the institutional current. That observation will matter when the verdict assigns responsibility, because responsibility for neglect is not distributed evenly across a tradition: it concentrates where the power to set agendas was held, and the agenda-setting record is the one part of this history that nobody disputes.

## The misreadings the charge attracts, corrected

Because the charge is famous and usually encountered second-hand, it arrives wrapped in misreadings, and an article that has taken the trouble to state it exactly should spend a section unwrapping them. Five recur constantly, and each distorts the verdict in a predictable direction.

The first misreading is that the charge is an attack on Marxism by its enemies, a product of anti-communist polemic or of liberal feminism's rivalry with the socialist tradition. The record contradicts it at every point. The charge's canonical statements were written by women who identified as Marxists or socialist feminists, published in Marxist journals, and framed as demands for the tradition's completion. Its fiercest early formulations came from activists who had given their youth to socialist organisations and who experienced the neglect as betrayal rather than as confirmation. Treating the charge as external is not merely a factual error. It is the error that licenses dismissal, since external attacks can be answered with solidarity, while internal audits can only be answered with evidence. Organisations that misclassified the charge in this way thereby chose the one response guaranteed to confirm it.

The second misreading is that Marx said nothing about women at all, so the whole corpus stands condemned by silence. This is the mirror image of the defence that exhibits a paragraph and claims acquittal. The corpus contains real material, some of it brilliant, and the charge as its serious proponents state it is about the absence of system, not the absence of sentences. Collapsing the distinction serves polemicists on both sides: the hostile summariser gets a cruder target, and the defender gets an easier acquittal, since refuting total silence requires only a quotation. The actual question, whether scattered insight amounts to a theory, is harder for both, which is why the misreading persists.

The third misreading reverses the second: since socialist states passed advanced laws and employed women in vast numbers, the charge of neglect is said to be refuted by the record. The record, given whole, does not support that use of it. The advanced laws were followed by a documented reversal, the employment was accompanied by a documented double burden, and the leaderships remained male to a degree the tradition itself never defended in principle. Exhibiting the first period of the record and omitting the second and third is the defensive version of the bundle fallacy the charge's proponents are accused of. The ledger below exists largely to prevent this manoeuvre, in whichever direction it is attempted.

The fourth misreading concerns the bracketing of reproduction. In the defensive literature the bracket is sometimes redescribed as an oversight, a topic Marx happened not to reach, like the unwritten volumes on the state or on foreign trade. The texts contradict the redescription. The set-aside is announced and principled: the reproduction of labour power is treated as a presupposition of the capital relation, internal to its effects and external to its analysis, and the second volume returns to the boundary and marks it again. An announced boundary is a theoretical act, and the charge against it is correspondingly theoretical: not that Marx forgot the household, but that he assigned it a place outside the theory's core and that the assignment governed a century of inquiry. Defenders who plead oversight concede more than the texts require, and surrender the only ground, the ground of method, on which the bracket can be honestly debated.

The fifth misreading is the most consequential for the verdict, because it concerns the repair. It holds that social reproduction theory, having extended the framework to cover reproduction, thereby settles the charge as a whole. The repair's scope is narrower and should be stated exactly. Social reproduction theory addresses the theoretical components: the bracket, and the sex-blindness objection in the form discussed above. It does not touch the historical components at all. No development in theory can alter what the Austrian party did about suffrage, or restore the Zhenotdel, or shorten the working day of the Soviet engineer who queued and cooked after her shift. Those facts stand where they stood, and a reader who lets an elegant theoretical repair bleach a documented historical record has misunderstood both. Conversely, the historical record cannot be used to veto the theoretical repair: the fact that the movement failed women does not show that the framework, as extended, fails to explain the failure. Keeping the two apart is the article's whole method, and the misreadings all consist, in the end, of letting them blur.

### What separates a criticism of Marxism from an attack on it?

Its location and its aim. The neglect charge was filed from inside the tradition, by adherents who wanted the framework completed, and its canonical texts argue with Marxist categories in Marxist terms. An attack seeks the framework's abandonment; the charge seeks its repair, and grades it accordingly.

## What attention would have looked like

Verdicts need a standard, and the standard applied so far has been implicit: the charge lands where the theory or the movement failed to do something it should have done. It is worth making the something explicit, if only to show that the standard is not anachronistic, not a demand that nineteenth-century writers anticipate twenty-first-century concerns. Everything on the following list was articulated, in print, by somebody inside or beside the tradition at the time it was needed, which is what makes the failure to absorb it a fair object of criticism.

Attention would have meant, first, an analysis of domestic labour as labour: its hours, its products, its place in the reproduction of the workforce, undertaken with the same seriousness the tradition devoted to the labour process in the factory. Benston supplied the outline in 1969, but the materials existed far earlier in the reports of factory inspectors, in the women's movement's own surveys, and in the communal-housekeeping experiments the early Soviet state actually ran. Second, it would have meant treating reproduction as a sphere of politics in its own right: contraception, abortion, childcare and maternity as questions on which movements take positions and states spend money, rather than as private arrangements trailing behind the real economy. The Bolsheviks of 1920 understood this, which is why their legislation ran ahead of their theory. Third, it would have meant a theory of the sexual division of labour as a division, asking why tasks are assigned by sex and how the assignment is enforced, instead of receiving the assignment as a natural premise. Kollontai asked versions of this question; the tradition filed her under biography. Fourth, it would have meant institutional self-scrutiny: a standing practice of asking who speaks, who decides and who serves the tea, inside organisations preaching emancipation. Zetkin's women's conferences were built partly for this purpose, and their marginalisation is measurable. Fifth, and most abstractly, it would have meant what Mitchell attempted in 1966: an architecture in which reproduction, sexuality and socialisation stood as named structures beside production, so that their absence from an analysis would register as a gap rather than as the world simply being that way.

Measured against that list, the pattern of the grades becomes intelligible. The tradition scored, at its best moments, on the second item, episodically and under revolutionary conditions. It scored partially on the fourth, in the heroic period of the women's departments. On the first, third and fifth, the items that constitute theory in the strict sense, it produced sketches, promises and one canonical book whose authority excused it from producing more. The list also clarifies what the repair had to be. Social reproduction theory is, in effect, the first and fifth items executed a century late: reproduction named as a structure, domestic labour analysed as labour within a unified account of how labour power comes to exist. That the repair matches the standard so exactly is the strongest evidence that the standard is the right one, since the repairers derived it from the tradition's own failures rather than importing it from a rival creed.

## The charge inside the citadel: feminism under actually existing socialism

One further body of evidence belongs in the record before the ledger, because it answers a question the defence sometimes raises: whether the charge is a Western preoccupation, a product of affluent universities judging poorer revolutions by seminar standards. The most damaging single exhibit against that suggestion is the appearance, inside the Soviet Union itself, of an independent feminist critique written by women who had grown up under the system the charge examines. In 1979, the year of Hartmann's essay, an unofficial almanac titled Woman and Russia began circulating in Leningrad, produced by a circle of women writers around Tatyana Mamonova. Its essays described, from inside, the realities the official account declared solved: the double shift as the ordinary texture of a woman's day, the coarseness of maternity care, the abortion system as an assembly line of humiliation, the alcoholism of men as a household catastrophe borne by wives, and the absence of any public language in which these things could be discussed as political facts. The state's response was the response the charge predicts. The almanac was suppressed, its contributors were harassed by the security organs, and several were forced into exile the following year. A system that had declared the woman question solved treated the women who reopened it as enemies of the state.

The episode's significance for the grading is threefold. First, it confirms the state component from testimony rather than from statistics: the double burden and the silencing of complaint were not inferences drawn by hostile outsiders but the reported experience of the system's own daughters, writing at personal risk. Second, it confirms the mechanism the party component identified, the classification of women's grievances as divisive, operating at the level of a state that no longer faced any external enemy capable of justifying it: by 1979 the Soviet Union was a superpower at peace, and the suppression of a literary almanac cannot be laid to civil war, encirclement or economic emergency. What explains it is doctrine in its sedimented form, the settled conviction that the question was closed and that reopening it was therefore sabotage. Third, the episode disciplines the attribution debate that runs through this article. Whatever share of the earlier reversal belongs to Stalin's person or to the emergencies of the nineteen thirties, the treatment of the Leningrad circle belongs to the system's ordinary functioning in its maturity, and it shows the neglect reproduced by institutions long after the emergencies ended. The women of the almanac were not theorists of sex-blind categories. Most had probably never read Hartmann. Their convergence with her, from the opposite side of the world and from inside the citadel of the alternative system, is the kind of independent corroboration that turns a charge into a finding.

## The dimension the categories reached last: sexuality and the body

Running beneath the five components is a sixth theme that the charge's canonical statements treat as part of the whole rather than as a separate count, and completeness requires naming it: the tradition's difficulty was never only with women's labour. It extended to sexuality, to the body, and to the organisation of desire, where its record is one of theoretical silence compounded by institutional prudishness. The founding texts bequeathed two opposed inheritances on this ground. Engels's book treated monogamy as a property institution and looked forward to a future in which marriage, freed of economic compulsion, would rest on inclination alone, a prospectus more liberal than most of what the nineteenth century offered. Kollontai, developing the radical edge of that inheritance, argued that sexual relations carried their own history and their own claim to transformation, and that a revolution which left intimate life untouched had changed less than it boasted. Against these stood the movement's practice, which was, in most of its organisations most of the time, respectability itself: parties recruiting in working-class districts policed the morals of their members, treated contraception as an embarrassment, and regarded open discussion of sexuality as a bourgeois decadence unfit for serious politics. The Soviet state of the nineteen thirties completed the trajectory by recriminalising male homosexuality and rebuilding the fortress family, but the prudishness long predated Stalin and survived him, in parties that considered themselves his enemies as much as in parties that mourned him.

The tradition's one systematic theorist of this dimension was expelled from both of the houses he tried to join them in, and the double expulsion is the neatest emblem the subject affords. Wilhelm Reich, an Austrian psychoanalyst and communist, argued in the early nineteen thirties that sexual repression was a material mechanism of authoritarian politics, that the authoritarian family manufactured the character structure that submitted to authority, and that a Marxism without a theory of sexuality could not explain why the oppressed so often clung to their oppression. Whatever the merits of his synthesis, and they were fiercely disputed then and since, the institutional response is the datum: the German Communist Party expelled him in 1933, and the International Psychoanalytic Association expelled him in 1934, each finding the attempt to unite their doctrines intolerable. Marxist feminists who later reconstructed this history drew the obvious moral. A tradition that had bracketed reproduction in its economics had also bracketed desire in its psychology, and the two brackets were one gesture: the private sphere, in both its labour and its love, was assigned to nature, to be endured or enjoyed but not theorised. The feminist slogan that the personal is political, coined outside the Marxist organisations at the end of the nineteen sixties, is best understood as a refusal of exactly that assignment, and its rapid absorption into Marxist feminist work shows the tradition once again repairing itself with tools forged by the people it had kept waiting outside the door of the theory.

## One issue as a tracer: abortion across the whole record

A single issue, followed through the entire history, can test the grading of the state component the way a tracer tests a current, and abortion serves because every regime in this story had to decide it and because the decisions cut across the simple narratives of both prosecution and defence. The sequence in the Soviet case has been given: decriminalised in 1920 as a public health measure, in a country where the practice was widespread and lethal in its clandestine form; recriminalised in 1936 as part of the pro-natalist restoration; legalised again in 1955, after Stalin's death, on the same public health reasoning as 1920. Around that spine, the wider record arranges itself in a pattern that should discomfort every simple verdict. The states of Eastern Europe, for most of their existence, maintained abortion laws more liberal than those of the capitalist West, where the practice remained criminal or heavily restricted in most countries until the reforms of the late nineteen sixties and nineteen seventies; East Germany's law of 1972, permitting abortion on request in the first trimester, was among the most liberal in the world at its date. Women in those states had, on this single axis, a legal freedom their Western contemporaries were still campaigning for, and the campaigns in the West were led in part by the same feminist movement that authored the neglect charge against the East's ruling doctrine. Then the tracer reverses. After 1989, in Poland, one of the first legislative fruits of the democratic transition was a severe restriction of the liberal abortion law inherited from the socialist period, carried through a freely elected parliament under the influence of the Catholic Church, against the opposition of Polish feminists who found themselves defending a provision of the old order against the new. Nothing in that sequence vindicates the old order, whose liberal law coexisted with the assembly-line conditions the Leningrad almanac described and with a politics that treated the issue as an instrument of demographic management rather than of women's autonomy. But the sequence does refute the lazy version of the charge, in which neglect is a uniform fog covering everything the tradition touched. On this axis the tradition's states were, for decades, ahead of the democracies that judged them, and the freedom was withdrawn not by commissars but by deputies. The ledger's treatment of the state component, lands with shared attribution, is built to hold facts of exactly this inconvenient shape: a record in which emancipation and subordination are not periods but simultaneous properties, distributed unevenly across the domains of a woman's life.

## The philosophical root: how the concept of labour was formed against the household

The deepest stratum of the charge, reached only by its most philosophical proponents, holds that the bracketing of reproduction was not a decision Marx took but an inheritance he received, built into the concept of labour as European thought transmitted it. The inheritance is traceable. In the classical world the household, the oikos, was the sphere of necessity, of the labour that sustains life, and it was rigorously separated from the public sphere where free men acted and spoke; the labour of the household was performed by women and slaves precisely so that it would not count as part of the life that mattered. Political economy, when it arose, took the productive sphere as its object and left the oikos where philosophy had put it. The classical economists analysed the labour that produces commodities and treated the labour that produces labourers as part of nature's background, and Marx, who radicalised the labour theory of value into a theory of exploitation, inherited the boundary along with the theory. Hannah Arendt, whose reading of Marx in The Human Condition of 1958 remains the most formidable philosophical treatment the tradition has received from outside, pressed a version of this point from her own direction: she argued that Marx had exalted labour, the activity bound to the life process, above all other human activities, while never examining the life process's own organisation, and that the glorification and the blindness were the same gesture seen from two sides. Arendt is not a Marxist feminist and her conclusions are not theirs; her categories have been contested by Marxists and feminists alike. But her diagnosis converges with theirs at the root, and the convergence of hostile, internal and philosophical critics on the same structural point is the kind of triangulation that strengthens a finding. The boundary between the labour that counts and the labour that sustains was drawn before Marx, was drawn again by Marx at the hinge of his system, and was policed by his tradition for a century. The charge, at this depth, is not that a man failed to see a fact. It is that a civilisation's division between the visible economy and the invisible household was reproduced inside the theory that set out to make the economy visible whole, and that the reproduction went unnoticed because it wore the respectable dress of a definition.

This depth also changes what a repair has to be, and helps explain why the repair took the form it did. If the omission were a gap in coverage, a chapter on the household appended to Capital would have closed it, and the domestic labour debate's contortions record the failure of exactly that strategy. If the omission is a boundary internal to the concept of labour, the repair must redraw the boundary: reproduction must be brought inside the definition of the economy's working, not annexed to it. That redrawing is social reproduction theory's actual achievement, beneath its particular claims: it redefines the object from the production of commodities to the production of life under capitalism, of which commodities are one moment and labour power's renewal another. Critics of the repair who find it too large a change to count as Marxist, and defenders who find it the tradition's own logic unfolded, are arguing about the identity of frameworks across revision, an argument as old as science. For this article's grading the point is narrower. A defect that requires redrawing the central concept's boundary is more serious than a gap in coverage, and less fatal than a contradiction: it is the kind of defect a living framework survives by growing, at the price of admitting that the growth was necessary. The second part of the verdict, partly lands, is calibrated to exactly that size.

## The wages for housework campaign: the charge translated into a demand

Between the theoretical charge and its academic settlement stands a campaign that translated the argument into a demand, and the translation deserves a section because it tested, in public, what the charge meant when it left the page. The wages for housework campaign, launched in the early nineteen seventies from networks in Italy, Britain and North America, proposed that the state pay a wage for domestic labour. Its theorists, Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James prominent among them, were explicit that the wage was not the point: the demand was a strategy for making visible, in the one language the society reliably understands, the work whose invisibility the second component of the charge describes. To demand a wage for housework was to announce that housework is work, that capital depends on it, and that its performance without pay is not a natural arrangement but a relation of power that could be refused. The campaign's literature developed the charge's sharpest practical corollary: that the wage relation itself, by paying one member of the household and not the other, organises the dependence of the unpaid on the paid, and so manufactures inside the working class a hierarchy the class analysis could not see because it stopped at the front door. The fullest later statement of this current, associated with Silvia Federici, has its own treatment in this series and is linked rather than summarised here: the [federici wages for housework](/2016/10/15/federici-wages-for-housework/) article.

The campaign also attracted the charge's most instructive internal criticisms, and recording them keeps the article's method intact, since a critique article that protects its own side's icons has abandoned its brief. Many feminists outside the campaign, and many Marxist feminists within the wider movement, objected that demanding wages for housework would entrench the sexual division of labour it protested, paying women to remain in the kitchen and converting a claim to liberation into a claim to a salary for subordination. Others objected that the demand's economics were gestural, a provocation mistaken for a programme. The campaign's defenders answered that the entrenchment objection mistook a lever for a destination: the wage was demanded as the means of refusing the work's naturalisation, and a woman with an income of her own negotiates the division of housework from a different position than a woman without one. The argument was never settled, and the campaign faded as an organisation while its central contention, that unpaid domestic labour is labour and that its invisibility is political, passed into the common sense of the subsequent feminism and into the national accounting debates about valuing unpaid work that continue in statistical offices. For the grading of this article's components, the campaign functions as the second component's public experiment. The proposition that the household's labour is real, necessary and concealed by the wage form was put to audiences far beyond the seminar, in leaflets and rent strikes and welfare organising, and it survived the putting. Whatever the technical fate of the value-theoretic claims the debaters attached to it, the proposition at its core is no longer seriously deniable, and a theory from whose pages that proposition had to be excavated by a campaign has the excavation entered in its record.

## The charge in the seminar room: how the debate reorganised a field

The charge's consequences were not confined to polemic, and one measure of its seriousness is the amount of ordinary scholarship it reorganised. Before the nineteen seventies, a student could complete an education in Marxist thought at most universities without encountering the woman question as a problem, since the canon presented it as solved and the syllabuses followed the canon. After the debate, the neglect itself became a standard object of study: courses on Marxist theory acquired a unit on the feminist critique as a matter of course, editions of Engels's book began to appear with introductions that flagged its anthropology as superseded and its silences as data, and the recovery literature, Rowbotham's generation and its successors, repopulated the tradition's history with the women the official versions had footnoted. Michèle Barrett's book of 1980, whose argument examined how ideology and the family wage together reproduce women's subordination within capitalism, became for a generation of students the standard sophisticated statement of the Marxist feminist position, and its presence on reading lists in sociology departments that taught no other Marxism is itself a datum about where the tradition's centre of gravity had moved. The debate also generated a recognised specialism with its own journals, conferences and internal controversies, of which the argument between dual systems and unitary theory, and the later argument over social reproduction theory's functionalism, are the organising spines. None of this is consistent with the picture of a charge that bounced off a monolith. The tradition's academic wing absorbed the charge, institutionalised it, and set examinations on it, a sequence this article's exam-facing pages below take for granted. What the academic wing could not do was retroactively supply the movement's missing century, and the coexistence of a sophisticated seminar literature with an unreconstructed popular orthodoxy, in which Engels's book still circulates as the whole answer, is one of the odder features of the tradition's divided life: the correction is complete in the journals and optional in the movement, and readers meet one or the other depending on which door they enter by.

## A second-generation charge: difference feminism and the recursion of neglect

The pattern this article has traced, in which a tradition's adherents discover a dimension their framework classified as secondary, did not stop with Hartmann, and its recurrence one level down is worth recording because it disciplines any triumphalism about the repair. Within Italian feminism of the nineteen seventies and eighties, a current associated with the women's bookstore collective in Milan developed a critique aimed not at orthodox Marxism but at Marxist and socialist feminism itself. Its charge, in outline, was that the feminism of equality, including its Marxist form, had accepted the terms of the world it opposed: it measured women's condition against men's, demanded entry into institutions men had built, and theorised women as a disadvantaged class of workers, while neglecting the symbolic and relational order in which female identity is formed and in which, these critics argued, the deeper unfreedom lay. The parallel with Hartmann is structural and exact. A constituency inside a movement reports that the movement's categories cannot see the dimension of its own oppression that matters most; the movement's first response is to classify the new dimension as secondary, private or divisive; the critics reply that the classification is the oppression's mechanism. Whatever the merits of difference feminism's positive doctrine, and they remain contested within feminism itself, its historical function in this article's story is to show that neglect is not a Marxist peculiarity but a standing temptation of every emancipatory framework, which always discovers, later than it should, that its universal subject had a particular biography. Marxism's distinction is not that it succumbed. It is that it succumbed on the largest stage, with the fullest documentation, and then produced, from the ranks of the neglected, the most systematic account of the succumbing. The charge against Marxism is therefore also, read generously, the best case study available of a general law of movements, and students of any tradition can be set to find its analogues in their own.

## What the women's movement inside socialism left behind

The reply's final exhibit is institutional and still visible, and it belongs in the record before judgement because the verdict's first part, left unqualified, would imply a movement that built nothing durable for women, which is false. The socialist women's movement invented or normalised a set of forms that outlived the movement itself. The annual women's day proposed at Copenhagen became a fixture of the international calendar, marked by states and movements that have forgotten or never known its origin in a socialist women's conference. The women's press as a genre, a paper addressed to working women as political readers, began with the movement's journals and set the template for a century of successors. The demand for maternity provision as a public entitlement, argued in the movement's programmes when it was still treated elsewhere as a private misfortune, passed into the welfare settlements of states that defined themselves against socialism, carried there partly by the competitive pressure of the socialist example and partly by socialist parties in government. Even the form of the internal audit this article has examined, the constituency's organised self-criticism addressed to its own movement in the movement's own language, was pioneered by the socialist women's conferences before it was perfected by their feminist grandchildren. None of this softens the grades on deferral and dissolution, and it is not entered to soften them. It is entered because the verdict's architecture requires the whole record in view at the moment of judgement, and because the legacy illuminates the tragedy the charge describes with unusual clarity. The movement built the instruments of women's political existence and then declined, repeatedly, to let those instruments set its agenda. The instruments survived the declining. A daughter movement inherited them, sharpened them on the parent movement's record, and returned them as an indictment, which is why this article's subject exists at all: the charge was written with tools the charged tradition had forged. Judgement that forgets that lineage will mistake a family quarrel for a foreign war, and will misread both the bitterness and the loyalty that run through every page of the evidence.

A note on proportion before the grades are entered. The evidence assembled above is weighted toward failure, because the article's commission is a charge and charges are proved by failures. Readers should resist converting that weighting into an estimate of the tradition's total balance sheet, which would have to include the literacy of grandmothers, the clinics, the women engineers and judges and pilots for whom the socialist states were, whatever else they were, the first societies in their families' histories to treat a daughter's education as a public investment. Those lives are not exhibits in this trial and their weight is not denied by their absence from it. The ledger grades a charge, not a civilisation, and a charge can be sustained, as this one largely is, against a record that also contains genuine emancipation imperfectly distributed and cynically withdrawn. Keeping that proportion in mind is the reader's part of the discipline the article has demanded from both sides throughout.

## The charge and verdict ledger

The evidence is now complete enough to be tabulated, and tabulation is a discipline as well as a convenience: it prevents a strong component from smuggling a weak one past the reader, and it forces the article's verdicts into the open where they can be disputed line by line. The charge and verdict ledger grades each of the five components separately, on the evidence presented above, using three grades. A component lands where the evidence sustains the charge substantially as stated. It partly lands where the charge identifies a real defect but overstates its scope or its implications. It does not land where the evidence contradicts the charge or where the charge's own logic fails. Readers who want the historical record in its full narrative form should consult the series treatment of the [soviet marxism complete guide](/2019/03/01/soviet-marxism-complete-guide/), and those who want the founding text's own exposition should go to the [engels origin of the family guide](/2016/11/01/engels-origin-of-the-family-guide/); the wider landscape of criticism, of which this charge forms one province, is surveyed in the [critiques of marxism complete guide](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/).

Reading the ledger vertically is as instructive as reading it across. The two components that land outright are the historical ones, where archives decide. The three that partly land are the theoretical ones, where the defect is real but the inference to incapacity fails. That distribution is not a compromise manufactured for symmetry. It follows from the kind of evidence each component rests on: behaviour can be documented to a verdict, while the capacity of a framework can only be tested by attempts to use it, and the attempts, once made, succeeded often enough to break the stronger claim. The ledger also exposes the two errors that the whole article has been organised against. The first error reads the table horizontally as a single score, five components summed into a conviction or an acquittal of Marxism as such; the table's point is that no such sum exists, because the components have different defendants. The second error reads the grades as permanent. A ledger is a snapshot of an argument at a date, and the theoretical grades in particular are hostage to the framework's continued development: they record that the repair existed and worked at the time of grading, which is a fact, and not that it will satisfy every future critic, which is a hope.

### How does the ledger keep the verdict honest?

By refusing aggregation. Each component is graded on its own evidence against its own defendant, the corpus against the texts, the parties against their records, the categories against the attempts to use them, so no strong row can carry a weak one and no verdict about the whole is smuggled out of a part.

Two of the ledger's grades will predictably be disputed, and an article that has insisted on argument owes its readers the strongest form of each dispute before the verdict section begins. The corpus grade, partly lands, will be attacked from the defence side as ungenerous: a founder who made the emancipation of women a measure of human progress in his first manuscripts, who anatomised the bourgeois family as a property form, and whose notes prepared the movement's standard treatise, might be thought to deserve acquittal on intention even if the system went unwritten. The grade survives the attack because intention is not the row's subject. The row grades coverage and consequence, and a corpus can be honourably intentioned and consequentially thin at once; the manuscripts' fine sentence about emancipation was unpublished for a century, and unpublished insight organises nothing. The same grade will be attacked from the prosecution side as indulgent, on the ground that a silence at the centre, maintained across forty years of writing on everything else, deserves the full grade of lands. That attack undervalues what distinguishes an absence from an incapacity, and it would make the corpus row carry the weight of the fifth row, where the logic of the categories is separately judged. Partly lands is the grade that keeps the rows distinct, which is the ledger's reason for existing.

The states grade, lands, will be disputed on the attribution the article itself entered: if the reversal was a Stalinist turn, deforming commitments across the board, why should the doctrine answer for it in this row rather than the regime alone? The dispute deserves a precise answer, since it is the strongest available objection to the article's distribution of blame. The regime answers for the reversal's acts: the dissolution order, the recriminalisation, the restored patriarchal family code were decisions, taken by identifiable men, against the grain of the revolution's first settlement. The doctrine answers for a different thing, the absence of antibodies. In every domain where the founding theory had built a positive account and a constituency to defend it, the turn met friction: economists argued, national movements resisted, even the party's own factions fought over the heritage. On the woman question the turn met a declared solution and a dissolved constituency, and it proceeded with an ease that contemporaries remarked on in other fields and that no one was institutionally placed to remark on in this one. A theory may fairly be charged with the defences it failed to build into its adherents' common sense, and the charge here is exactly that: the doctrine had taught its holders that emancipation was a consequence, and consequences do not organise their own defence. Lands, with the attribution shared in those proportions, is the grade that fits the mechanism, and the mechanism, not the body count of grievances, is what the charge at its best was always about. Readers unpersuaded should test the alternative: a grade of partly lands for the states row would require evidence that the subordination was episodic or contested from within at the moments it mattered, and the article has searched the record for that evidence, in Bern, in the Zhenotdel's last years, in the Leningrad circle, and found it heroic, marginal and crushed. Heroism at the margins is the prosecution's exhibit, not the defence's.

## The three-part verdict

Everything above this section is evidence, and the article has been careful to keep it so: charges stated, records exhibited, replies given their force, concessions entered where they belong. What follows is judgement, and it is separated from the evidence deliberately, because a verdict that cannot be distinguished from the testimony it weighs cannot be argued with, and this verdict is offered for argument. It has three parts, corresponding to the three defendants the charge actually names once it is stated exactly: the movement of parties and states, the theory as its founders left it, and the framework as a continuing enterprise. The parts have different outcomes, and the article's central claim is that any honest assessment must hold all three at once.

Against the record of parties and states, the charge lands, and lands decisively. This is the part of the verdict that no reasonable reading of the evidence can soften beyond the mitigations already entered. The movement that theorised women's emancipation as no rival did also postponed women's demands as a matter of routine, traded their suffrage where it was inconvenient, dissolved their organisations when they became superfluous to purposes set elsewhere, and governed states in which a woman carried the longest working day in the society while the rooms of decision stayed closed to her sex across three generations and several continents. The mitigations are genuine and they are entered in the ledger: the early legislation was real, the organising was pioneering, the reversal had an author and a date, and the democracies that judge the record kept their own women legally subordinate for much of the same period. None of it changes the grade, because the charge was never that the movement was the worst of its time. The charge was that subordination was systematic, doctrinally licensed, and uncorrected from within over a century, and on the evidence of congresses, codes and dissolutions, it was. Readers who take one sentence from this article should take the distinction this grade rests on: the movement is convicted not on the standard of a later age but on its own standard, the standard of Zetkin's resolutions and the Zhenotdel's founding purposes, which its practice betrayed in ways its own members documented at the time. A tradition is entitled to be judged by its enemies' standards only when its own are lower. Marxism's were higher, and the height is the measure of the fall.

Against the theory as the founders left it, the charge lands in part, and the part should be specified exactly, because this is where public discussion most often loses the thread. The theory is convicted of an omission with a structure. It is not that Marx and Engels failed to mention women: the corpus component records the mentions and grades them as fragments around a silence. The omission is that the theory's central machinery was built so that the production of labour power itself, the labour on which every other labour depends, fell outside the analysis by design, and that the design was then inherited as a boundary of the thinkable for a hundred years. The bracketing was a legitimate act of abstraction in a book about capital. Its hardening into a doctrine, the treatment of Engels's derivative volume as the closed answer to a question the founders had barely opened, was the tradition's act, and the theory cannot be acquitted of it entirely, because the theory supplied the hardening's rationale: if reproduction is presupposition rather than process, then a movement formed by the theory will reliably attend to it late, lightly, or never. At the same time, the stronger theoretical charge, that the omission was dictated by the categories' logic and could not have been otherwise, is not sustained, and Hartmann's own critics from within Marxist feminism are the witnesses. The materials of the repair were present in the founder's toolkit, in the analysis of necessary labour, in the reproduction schemes, above all in the founding insight that social arrangements are historical and therefore analysable. The verdict on the theory is therefore conviction on a specified count: an omission, consequential and self-concealing, repairable from within and in the event repaired, but not repaired by the theory's guardians and not repaired for a century, during which the omission did its work in the world. An omission of that duration is not a footnote to a theory's history. It is a chapter of it, and the chapter is this article's subject.

Against the framework as a continuing enterprise, the charge of incapacity does not land, and this is the part of the verdict that the charge's popular form most needs to hear. The claim under examination is that Marxism, by the structure of its categories, cannot address gender: that its sex-blindness is constitutional, so that feminist work within it is condemned to absorption, the wife's fate in Hartmann's sentence. The evidence against that claim is the work itself. Social reproduction theory exists; it analyses the production and maintenance of labour power as a process internal to capitalism's reproduction; it explains how the boundary between paid production and unpaid reproduction is drawn, contested and redrawn; it does so with Marxist tools, by Marxist scholars, in a literature continuous with the tradition it corrects. One may dispute its details, and its internal critics do, sharply. What cannot be maintained, in the presence of the literature, is that the framework forbade it. The comparative exhibit makes the same point from the other side: the dual systems alternative that Hartmann proposed as the necessary remedy is assessed, alongside its rivals, in the series comparison of the feminisms at [marxist radical liberal feminism](/2016/11/15/marxist-radical-liberal-feminism/), and the assessment the field has converged on is that the remedy's two-systems architecture created as many problems as it solved, while the unitary repair Hartmann argued against became the tradition's working settlement. The wife, to borrow her sentence once more, turned out to have a legal existence after all, and used it to rewrite the marriage contract rather than to leave the house. A framework whose adherents can do that is not acquitted of the first two parts of this verdict, and this article does not acquit it. It is acquitted of the third charge, incapacity, on the plain ground that the capacity was demonstrated.

A verdict that cannot say what would change it is a prejudice with footnotes, so the conditions of revision should be entered before the open questions. The first grade, against the movement, is a judgement on a closed record and could be revised only by archival discoveries of a kind that no longer occur for this period: the congresses have been minuted, the codes published, the departments dissolved in documents that survive. It stands. The second grade, against the founders' theory, would soften if scholarship established that the bracketing of reproduction was less complete in the texts than the critics read it, for example by a reading of the reproduction schemes that found the household's labour systematically present in the founder's own analysis rather than presupposed by it; such readings are attempted from time to time, and the article's grade records that none has yet persuaded the field, including most of the theory's defenders, who generally prefer to concede the bracket and contest its significance. The third grade, the acquittal on incapacity, is the most revisable in principle and the least likely to be revised in fact: it would fall if the social reproduction literature collapsed under its internal criticisms, leaving the assignment of reproductive labour unexplained by any Marxist account, and it strengthens with every successful application of that literature to new material. Stating the conditions matters because it converts the verdict from a temperament into a claim. Readers who disagree now know what to bring: not a warmer or colder feeling about the tradition, but a reading, an archive, or a refutation, addressed to the specific grade they dispute.

The falsifiability exercise has a further use, since it exposes an asymmetry in how the two sides of this debate usually argue. Defenders of the tradition, pressed on the movement's record, tend to retreat to the theory's potential, while prosecutors, pressed on the theory's repair, tend to advance to the movement's record, and the argument circles because each side answers the grade it can win. A reader equipped with the revision conditions can hold both sides to their own rows: the defender who cites social reproduction theory has answered the third grade and owes the first; the prosecutor who cites the dissolved departments has established the first grade and owes the third. Much of the published quarrel dissolves once the owing is made explicit, and what remains is the genuine residue: a disagreement about how much a framework should answer for the institutions built in its name, which is a real philosophical question and deserves better than the proxy war fought over it here. The article's position on that residue is implicit in its grades and may as well be explicit: a framework answers for its characteristic silences, because silences are instructions to the future about what may be safely ignored, and movements are built by people who follow instructions. It does not answer for every crime committed under its flag, since flags are stolen as easily as they are inherited. Between those two propositions lies the whole difficulty of judging traditions, and this article has tried to stand exactly on the line between them.

What the verdict deliberately leaves open should be stated with the same clarity, since a judgement that hides its undecided questions invites the reader to over-read it. Three questions remain genuinely open, and the article takes no position that the evidence cannot carry. The first is whether social reproduction theory, in its developed forms, fully answers the derivation problem Hartmann posed, or whether the sex of the reproducer remains, inside the repair, a historical premise rather than a derived result; the internal debate on functionalism, recorded above, is alive, and its outcome will decide whether the fifth component's grade should be revised from partly lands to lands, or the other way. The second concerns the neighbouring critique this article has not examined: the argument, developed by Black feminists and formalised in the concept examined in the series article on [intersectionality and class](/2017/01/01/intersectionality-and-class/), that the neglect charged here against gender generalises to race, and that the two charges together require a different architecture again. That argument has its own evidence and its own ledger, and nothing in this verdict prejudges it. The third is practical rather than theoretical: whether a tradition whose academic wing has absorbed the correction can transmit it to its remaining political organisations, or whether the divided life noted above, complete in the journals and optional in the movement, is the stable outcome. History will grade that component. The article's business was with the record, and the record, stated whole, supports exactly the three grades given: the movement convicted, the theory convicted of a repairable omission it left unrepaired for a century, and the framework acquitted of the incapacity its sharpest internal critic alleged. That is the three-part verdict, and it is available, as a single judgement, nowhere else in the literature this article has surveyed, because the literature's polemical economy rewards the summed verdict, guilty or innocent, and punishes the separated one. The separation is the contribution.

## What accepting the verdict changes

A separated verdict is not merely a scholarly refinement; it changes what a reader does next, and the article closes its argument by making the practical consequences explicit. A reader who accepts the summed verdict of the polemics, guilty or innocent, is licensed to stop thinking: the guilty tradition is shelved, the innocent one is repeated. A reader who accepts the three-part verdict inherits three different tasks. From the first part, a rule for judging organisations: measure any movement that promises general emancipation by the standing of its specific constituencies inside it, by whether their demands have machinery, budgets and vetoes or only anniversaries, since that is the test the Marxist parties failed while passing every test of doctrine. From the second part, a rule for reading theories: when a framework designates a sphere as presupposition, ask whose labour the designation hides and what the framework's adherents will therefore reliably neglect, since the designation, not any individual's prejudice, is the mechanism by which neglect reproduces itself innocently. From the third part, a rule for conducting criticism: file charges from inside, with the evidence separated by defendant and the strongest form of the opposing case stated fairly, because that is the method that produced every genuine correction in this history, from Zetkin's resolutions to Hartmann's essay to the theory that answered her. The charge examined in this article was, at bottom, a demand that a tradition which taught the world to look for the labour hidden inside every social form should look for the labour hidden inside its own. Accepting the verdict means accepting the demand as a standing instruction, addressed to every framework that arrives claiming completeness, including whichever framework the reader brought to the article.

There is a fourth inheritance, for the historians, and it concerns the writing of movement history itself. The record assembled in this article was recoverable only because scholars treated the movement's margins as archives rather than as colour: the women's papers, the departmental reports, the time budgets, the memoirs of the shelved. Official histories of the tradition, written from its summits, recorded the woman question as a paragraph of good intentions, and the paragraph stood unchallenged for decades because the sources that contradict it sat in the files of institutions the historians did not visit. Accepting the verdict therefore carries a methodological sentence for anyone who writes about emancipatory movements, including the ones still forming. The test of a movement's universalism is not in its programmes, which are written by its most universal members, nor in its founding moments, which are carried by its most generous ones. The test is in its filing system: which constituencies have departments, which departments have budgets, whose reports reach the centre unedited, and whose experience is minuted as data rather than applauded as testimony. A historian who reads a movement through those files will find its real constitution, the one its members lived under, and will find, as this article found, that the distance between the written constitution and the real one is the precise space in which neglect does its quiet work. The Marxist tradition, which taught historiography to distrust the formal and excavate the material, is entitled to be read by its own method, and the reading, conducted fairly, is what the three grades record: a movement convicted by its files, a theory convicted by its silences, and a framework acquitted, in the end, by the quality of the enemies it made inside its own house, whose indictment is also its best evidence of life.

### Why must the verdict be delivered in three parts rather than one?

Because the charge names three different defendants with three different records. The movement's behaviour, the founders' theory and the framework's capacity are separate questions with separate evidence, and a single verdict would convict the innocent party, acquit the guilty one, or both at once.

## For students and exam candidates

This question is a standard evaluation exercise, and it rewards exactly the discipline this article practises: separate the defendants before you grade them. Open by stating the charge in its components, attribute its sharpest form to Hartmann and the 1979 essay, and announce the three-part verdict as your structure. In the body, spend your evidence asymmetrically: the parties and states convict on documents, so cite the dissolutions, the dates and the double burden; the theory convicts only of a repairable omission, so concede the bracket and deploy the repair; the framework is acquitted of incapacity, so close with social reproduction theory as the demonstrated capacity. The scripts that fail this question sum the evidence into one verdict or defend the tradition by exhibiting Engels. Worked model answers and the marking logic for the whole cluster are set out in the [marxist feminism exam answers](/2017/01/15/marxist-feminism-exam-answers/), and the charge and verdict ledger transfers neatly into the structured notes at [VaultBook study notes for this series](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), where the five components make a revision card of unusual efficiency.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The ledger is built to be lifted into the classroom as a structured assessment exercise. Give students the five components with the evidence columns blank and a class period to fill them from the set texts: the Manifesto's family passages and the machinery chapter for the corpus rows, the Hartmann extract for the categories row, and a documentary packet, a suffrage resolution, a dissolution order, a time-budget summary, for the movement rows. The pedagogical value lies in the grading discussion that follows, since groups reliably diverge on the theoretical rows and converge on the historical ones, and the divergence teaches the article's central distinction better than a lecture can: behaviour admits verdicts, capacities admit only tests. A productive seminar question is whether the students' own institutions would survive the audit they have just performed on the parties, judged by machinery rather than by mission statements. Set the Kollontai recovery as the written assignment: students who must explain how a tradition shelves its own best critic have understood the mechanism the whole topic turns on.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Two cautions govern further work on this subject. The first is archival: the grades on the movement components rest on documents assembled largely by feminist historians working against official silences, and the archive remains uneven, rich for Germany and Russia, thinner for the parties of Asia and Latin America, where the same questions await their Rowbotham. The second is analytical: resist the summed verdict in your own copy. A report that a tradition neglected gender is the beginning of a story, not its conclusion, and the publishable distinctions are the ones this article separates: what the texts omit, what the organisations did, what the categories can bear. For the tradition's own self-understanding, start from the [marxist feminism complete guide](/2016/09/01/marxist-feminism-complete-guide/), and for the repair that reorganised the field's research agenda, the account of [social reproduction theory explained](/2016/09/15/social-reproduction-theory-explained/) sets out the programme and its evidence. Journalists covering any movement's internal reckoning with its own constituencies will find the deferral mechanism documented here, the classification of a demand as derivative followed by its indefinite postponement, a reliable template for recognising the pattern in fresher material.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Did Marx write about women's oppression?

He touched it repeatedly without ever making it a subject in its own right. The youthful manuscripts use women's position as an index of a society's humanity. The Manifesto attacks the property basis of bourgeois marriage. Capital examines how machinery pulls women and children into mills and recomposes the family wage. A youthful fragment, a polemic and a mechanism do not add up to an analysis, and the tradition filled the gap with Engels's volume of 1884, whose account of the family's origins stood as doctrine long after its anthropology had dated. The fair summary is presence without system: enough writing to refute a claim of total silence, far too little to refute the charge of unsystematic treatment.

### Q: Is the worker in Capital implicitly male?

In construction, substantially yes. The seller of labour power enters the analysis as an unencumbered individual who converts a wage into his own maintenance and returns to market. Behind that figure stands an unexamined household in which somebody else turns purchased provisions into meals, rest and raised children, and in the society Marx observed that somebody was a wife. He knew the worker had dependants, since the wage is made to cover a family's subsistence and the worker's replacements, and the machinery chapter shows whole families drawn into employment. But the abstractions proceed as if reproduction organised itself. Critics therefore describe the model worker as male by default: not by declaration, but by the shape of a life the theory assumes and never inspects.

### Q: Did Marxist parties subordinate women's demands?

As a pattern across countries and decades, yes. The standard justification was derivative status: since female subordination grew from private property, it would perish alongside it, and separate agitation risked splitting the movement beforehand. In practice that reasoning postponed suffrage campaigns when male enfranchisement seemed winnable first, blessed the family wage bargain that priced women's labour as secondary, confined women members to auxiliary labour inside the parties, and allowed women's commissions and journals to wither whenever central priorities tightened. The record needs its counterweight stated too: no rival movement organised working women on the socialists' scale or preached emancipation as doctrine. Subordination inside a pioneering movement is still subordination, and the parties' own resolutions supply the standard they failed.

### Q: What was the record of socialist states on women?

Genuinely divided, and dishonest in any single-word summary. The revolutionary governments opened with the most advanced family legislation of their age: civil marriage, divorce on request, the end of illegitimacy as a legal stain, abortion decriminalised in Soviet Russia before anywhere else, and a women's department charged with carrying the new rights into villages and factories. Within two decades much of that settlement was reversed: the department dissolved with emancipation declared complete, abortion recriminalised, divorce burdened, motherhood conscripted into demographic policy. Through both phases women worked a double shift of employment and unpaid domestic labour, and the highest committees stayed almost entirely male. Achievements, reversal and burden have to be reported together or the record is propaganda for one side.

### Q: What is the unhappy marriage argument?

It is Heidi Hartmann's formulation, from a 1979 essay in Capital & Class whose title joins the two movements as spouses. Hartmann argued that the partnership had worked like a marriage under the old common law she invoked, in which the wife's legal personhood vanished into the husband's: feminism's questions were repeatedly redefined as Marxism's questions, its distinctive subject matter dissolved, its priorities set elsewhere. Her prescription was a renegotiated union of two distinct analyses, capitalism and patriarchy, each with its own material base, studied in their interaction. Later Marxist feminists accepted her diagnosis of absorption while disputing the two-systems remedy, preferring a single rebuilt framework. The metaphor endures because it names an experience of subordination inside an alliance that formal accounts of doctrine miss.

### Q: Are Marxist categories sex-blind?

In their inherited form, that is the charge, and it is largely conceded. Class, labour power, the reserve army: none of the core concepts refers to sex, since they were built to track a system that exploits without asking who the worker is. Applied to a world where reproductive labour is assigned by sex, such concepts can register that women perform the work while offering no route to explaining why women, rather than some other group, perform it everywhere and across successive social orders. Hartmann concluded that a separate theory of patriarchy was indispensable. Unitary theorists counter that the blind spot belongs to the concepts as inherited, not to the framework as such, and that an account centred on how labour power is produced can internalise the question. The blindness of the inherited toolkit is common ground; its permanence is the dispute.

### Q: How do Marxist feminists reply to the charge?

They divide over remedy while sharing much of the diagnosis. Dual systems theorists accept that the inherited categories cannot explain women's subordination and pair Marxism with an autonomous theory of patriarchy. Unitary theorists answer that a single framework, widened to place the making and remaking of labour power at its centre, can explain the assignment of reproductive work as a historical arrangement rather than a natural fact. Both camps cite the same exhibits against complacency: the bracketed household in Capital, the deferred demands, the dissolved women's departments. Both also insist, against outside prosecutors, that the critics who built the charge were the tradition's own adherents repairing their inheritance. Where they unite completely is in rejecting the defensive reply that Engels settled the matter in 1884.

### Q: Does social reproduction theory answer the criticism?

It answers the theoretical counts directly and the historical counts not at all, and keeping that boundary clear is essential. Against the claim that the framework cannot think reproduction, the theory demonstrates a working analysis: labour power is produced and restored outside the wage relation, chiefly by unpaid work assigned to women, and the boundary between paid production and unpaid renewal is drawn by employers, states and custom in ways the theory can trace. Against Hartmann, it shows the assignment of that work can be analysed as history rather than assumed as nature, though whether it derives the sex of the worker or inherits it remains contested. What it cannot do is revise the past. No conceptual advance restores a dissolved department or shortens a double shift worked in 1960.

### Q: What is the double burden?

It names the combined load carried by women in the socialist states, and by extension wherever employment and domestic assignment coexist: a full shift of waged work, performed on formally equal terms, followed by a second shift of cooking, cleaning, laundering, queuing and caring that fell to women by custom rather than by law. The burden was measured, not merely alleged; time-budget researchers in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe documented for decade after decade that employed women's total working hours exceeded men's and their leisure fell correspondingly short. Its persistence is the state record's most telling feature, because it survived every change of policy and leadership. Legal equality and mass employment were delivered. The reorganisation of domestic life that emancipation required was never seriously attempted, and the double burden is the shape of that omission in daily hours.

### Q: Does the criticism land on the theory or on the movement?

On both, in different weights, which is why single-verdict summaries mislead. Against the movement of parties and states it lands with full force: the deferrals, dissolutions, reversals and empty leadership chairs are documented behaviour, mitigated by pioneering organisation and early legislation but not erased by them. Against the theory it lands as an omission rather than a refutation: reproduction was bracketed by design in the founding texts, the bracket governed a century of inquiry, and the concepts as inherited could not say why women perform the labour that renews the workforce. Against the framework's capacity it fails, since Marxist scholars themselves built the analysis whose absence the charge deplored. Theory or movement is therefore a false choice; the accurate answer assigns each defendant its own grade.

### Q: How does the three-part verdict grade the neglect charge?

It returns three grades for three defendants instead of one verdict for an abstraction. The record of parties and states is convicted: subordination of women's demands was systematic, doctrinally licensed and sustained across regimes, and it is judged against the movement's own proclaimed standard. The founders' theory is convicted of a lesser and specified offence, an omission at its centre, consequential because it disarmed its inheritors, yet repairable from resources the theory itself contained. The framework as a living enterprise is acquitted of the incapacity charge, because the repair the critics demanded was in fact produced inside it. Grading separately prevents the commonest errors in this debate: using the movement's guilt to condemn a method, or the method's resilience to launder the movement's record.

### Q: What should a student separate between Marx's own writings and the tradition's record?

Three separations earn marks. First, separate the corpus from the canon: what Marx and Engels wrote is a handful of penetrating fragments and one derivative treatise, while what the tradition taught was that treatise frozen into a closed answer, and the freezing was an institutional act, not a textual one. Second, separate doctrine from behaviour: parties invoked the theory to license deferrals the theory's own resolutions sometimes contradicted, so a party's practice is evidence about the party before it is evidence about the books. Third, separate the founders' omissions from the framework's limits: an unanswered question in 1883 is a biographical and historical fact, while whether the categories can be extended to answer it is a testable claim, tested since by social reproduction theory. Answers that keep those layers distinct can evaluate; answers that merge them can only denounce or absolve.

### Q: Why did the charge arrive a century after the texts?

Because several locks had to open together. A canonical answer had closed the question institutionally: while Engels's book counted as the settled doctrine, formulating a criticism meant contradicting the catechism, work for which parties offered no career. The people positioned to write theory were filtered by the same exclusions the charge describes, and women's access to universities, journals and publishing expanded only in the middle twentieth century. An autonomous women's movement then supplied audience, presses and protection, while feminist historians recovered the buried archive, from the socialist women's press to the founder's own late notebooks on kinship, which reached print in the same decade as the charge itself. Finally, a generation of activists had accumulated direct experience of deferral inside the New Left. When the locks opened, the indictment arrived fully documented rather than tentative.

### Q: How does the charge and verdict ledger order the evidence?

It arrays the indictment as five components in rows, each with its charge, its supporting evidence, the strongest reply and a grade of lands, partly lands or does not land. The rows follow the article's analytical order: the corpus, the construction of Capital, the parties, the states and the categories. Columns enforce the discipline the prose argues for, since a reply is entered against the specific charge it answers and cannot borrow strength from a neighbouring row, and a grade must be justified by the evidence in its own row rather than by the table's general atmosphere. Read across, the ledger shows why bundled verdicts fail: the historical rows convict outright, the theoretical rows convict in part, and no arithmetic over the rows yields a single honest sentence about Marxism as such. The three-part verdict is the summary the table licenses.

### Q: What separates an absence in the corpus from an absence in the movement?

The kind of evidence and the kind of remedy. An absence in the corpus is a fact about texts: pages where an analysis should stand and does not, demonstrable by reading, and remediable by writing, as later Marxist feminists wrote the missing analyses of reproduction and the household. An absence in the movement is a fact about conduct: demands unmet, organisations dissolved, women missing from the rooms of decision, demonstrable only from archives, and not remediable by any subsequent writing, since the women who queued and cooked and waited cannot be compensated by a better theory published after their deaths. Confusing the two produces the debate's characteristic bad arguments, in which a newly written chapter is offered as an answer to a dissolved department, or a dissolved department is cited to prove that no chapter could ever be written.
