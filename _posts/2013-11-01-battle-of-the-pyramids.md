On the afternoon of 21 July 1798, on flat cultivated ground beside the Nile some distance north of Giza, several thousand of the finest heavy cavalry in the world charged five formations of French infantry and were destroyed in under two hours. The French lost somewhere in the region of thirty dead. The victors named the engagement after monuments that were visible on the southern horizon but played no part in it, and the name stuck, which is why the Battle of the Pyramids is remembered as a clash beneath the tombs of the pharaohs rather than as what it was: a fight for a fortified village called Embabeh, decided by drill, discipline, and canister shot.

![The Battle of the Pyramids in 1798 and how French squares broke the Mamluk cavalry - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The engagement deserves close attention for two reasons that pull in opposite directions. It is the most famous battle fought on Egyptian soil in the modern era, and it is militarily one of the least surprising. Nothing about the outcome would have puzzled a European staff officer of the 1790s, because the tactical question it posed, whether cavalry without artillery support can break steady infantry in square, had been answered in the negative for at least a century of European warfare. What made it extraordinary was that one side did not know the answer, and the price of not knowing was the end of a political order that had governed Egypt since 1517.

The framework this article advances can be called the square-beats-the-charge thesis. The battle was not decided by numbers, by terrain, by the pyramids, by genius, or by anything Egyptian about the setting. It was decided by a formation and by the guns placed at its corners, and by the fact that the Mamluk military system had no answer to either because it had never had to develop one. The elite whose cavalry rode onto that field had spent two and a half centuries beating every opponent it faced inside Egypt, and every one of those opponents had fought the same way it did.

That is the deeper point, and it is the one the picturesque version of the battle conceals. Fifteen hundred horsemen of extraordinary individual skill were beaten by soldiers who were, man for man, less impressive riders, less impressive swordsmen, and considerably less well equipped. What the French had was a system. What the Mamluks had was a collection of superb individuals. The battle is the clearest demonstration in Egyptian history of the difference between the two.

## Where the armies were on 21 July

Understanding the battle requires knowing how both forces arrived on that stretch of riverbank, because the position each was in determined what it could do.

The French had landed at Alexandria on 1 July and marched inland, crossing the desert to strike the Nile and then moving south along the river toward Cairo. The full account of that campaign and its strategic setting is in the article on [Napoleon's invasion of Egypt](/2013/10/01/napoleon-invasion-of-egypt/). By 21 July the army had been ashore three weeks, had fought one serious engagement at Shubra Khit on 13 July, had marched a considerable distance in July heat in wool uniforms, and was tired, thirsty, and irritable. It had almost no cavalry, having lost most of its horses at sea and on the march, and its artillery was light field guns rather than anything heavy.

Its strength on the field is usually given as somewhere between twenty and twenty five thousand men, organized in five divisions. Desaix and Reynier held the right, closest to the desert edge; Dugua held the center near the river; Bon and Vial held the left toward the Nile and the village. Two of those divisions were commanded by substitutes because Kleber had been wounded at Alexandria and Menou at Rosetta, which is a detail worth registering because it shows how little the French command arrangements mattered to the outcome.

The Egyptian side was divided by the river, and that division was the single largest error of the day. Murad Bey, the senior military figure among the Mamluk households and the man who had commanded at Shubra Khit, was on the west bank with the cavalry and an entrenched camp at Embabeh. Ibrahim Bey, his partner in the duumvirate that had governed Egypt for two decades, was on the east bank with a second force, the Ottoman governor, and much of the Cairo establishment. The Nile between them was in flood. Neither could reinforce the other once the fighting began.

Murad's force is generally estimated at around six thousand Mamluk cavalry, with the usual caution that these figures come from participant accounts rather than muster rolls and that European observers tended to round upward. Behind the cavalry, in the entrenchments at Embabeh, stood a much larger body of infantry, perhaps fifteen to twenty thousand, consisting of janissaries from the Cairo regiments and levied peasants, armed variably and trained hardly at all. Bedouin horsemen hovered on the flanks, as they had throughout the French advance, watching to see which way the day would go before committing to anything.

## The forty five guns that could not move

The most consequential fact about Murad's dispositions is one that rarely appears in popular accounts: he had artillery and it was useless to him.

The Embabeh entrenchment mounted something in the region of forty guns, the number varies in the sources, and they were emplaced in fixed positions facing the expected direction of the French approach. They were not field pieces on mobile carriages served by trained crews who could limber up, displace, and reposition. They were static, poorly served, and sited on the assumption that the French would obligingly march straight into them.

This mattered enormously because artillery is the one thing that breaks an infantry square. A square is a dense mass of men standing shoulder to shoulder in the open. It is close to invulnerable to cavalry and it is the ideal target for round shot, which cuts lanes through packed ranks, and for canister at close range. Any European army of the period that wanted to break a square used guns to blow gaps in it and then sent cavalry into the gaps. The combination is the standard answer, and it was well known.

Murad could not perform that sequence, because his guns could not move to where the squares were and his cavalry could not wait while guns that could not move did their work. His artillery was therefore a fortification asset, capable only of defending the entrenchment it sat in, while the decisive action happened out on the plain beyond its effective reach.

That single incapacity, the absence of mobile field artillery, is the whole battle in one sentence. It was not a failure of courage or of command on the day. It was the consequence of a political system, described in the article on [the Mamluk beys of Ottoman Egypt](/2013/09/01/mamluk-beys-of-ottoman-egypt/), in which each household funded its own cavalry from its own tax farms and no central treasury existed that could pay for guns, gun crews, drivers, horses, and the training establishment that makes artillery work.

## Shubra Khit: the rehearsal nobody learned from

Eight days before the main battle, the same two commanders had fought a smaller action that settled every tactical question in advance, and the fact that nothing changed between the two engagements is the most revealing thing about the Mamluk command.

At Shubra Khit on 13 July, Murad attacked the French column as it moved south along the Nile, supported by a river flotilla of armed gunboats that engaged the French river craft. Napoleon formed his divisions into large hollow squares, put his guns at the corners, placed the baggage, the wounded, and the scientific commission in the middle, and waited. The Mamluk cavalry charged, could not enter the formations, was shot at close range by musketry and canister, and withdrew. On the river the action was sharper and at one point went badly for the French before a lucky shot blew up the Mamluk flagship, at which the flotilla broke off.

French casualties were negligible. Mamluk losses were real but not crippling, perhaps a few hundred. In purely material terms Shubra Khit decided nothing.

In informational terms it decided everything, and only one side read the information. The French learned that the formation worked against these opponents and that they could march in confidence. The Mamluk commanders learned, apparently, that the charge had not been pressed home hard enough.

That reading was not stupid by the standards of their own experience. Within the Egyptian military world, a cavalry charge that failed usually failed because it was delivered without sufficient weight or resolve, and the remedy was more horsemen and greater commitment. Two and a half centuries of fighting other cavalry, bedouin confederations, Ottoman expeditionary forces with poor infantry, and rival households had validated that lesson repeatedly. Nothing in that experience contained a case of a charge failing because the target was structurally immune to it.

So Murad went into 21 July intending to do the same thing on a larger scale with more men and more determination, which is precisely the response that guaranteed the scale of the disaster.

## The approach march

The French moved south from Wardan on the night of 20 July and came up toward Embabeh through the morning of the twenty first, marching along the west bank through a landscape of irrigated fields, canals, and villages.

The conditions were bad. The men had been up since the small hours, the heat was severe, water discipline had been poor throughout the campaign, and the army had been living on melons, unripe grain, and whatever could be requisitioned from villages that had frequently been emptied before it arrived. Several accounts describe soldiers falling out of the ranks, and the mood of the army by the time it deployed was closer to grim endurance than to anticipation.

Around two in the afternoon the leading divisions came in sight of the Mamluk position. Ahead and to the left, on the riverbank, lay Embabeh with its entrenchments and guns. Out on the plain in front of it stood the cavalry, several thousand horsemen in rich equipment, visible across open ground. Beyond them, far to the south across the flood plain, the pyramids of Giza stood on the desert edge.

Napoleon formed the army into five divisional squares. These were not small battalion squares of the kind used later against cavalry in Europe but large formations, each division forming an oblong of several ranks with grenadier companies and guns at the angles and everything vulnerable placed inside. They advanced slowly, maintaining intervals, with the left-hand divisions angling toward the village and the right-hand divisions positioned to meet the cavalry on the open flank.

The formation the French adopted is worth pausing on, because it explains the outcome better than anything either commander did afterward.

### Why could cavalry not break an infantry square?

Because horses will not charge into a solid hedge of levelled bayonets, and a square presents that hedge in every direction. Riders who pull up short at the face of the formation are stationary targets for volleys at thirty yards, and canister from guns at the corners tears through a mounted mass that cannot disperse.

## The scholars inside the formation

One detail of the square's composition deserves notice because it captures what made this expedition unlike any other campaign of the period.

The French squares were hollow, and what went into the hollow was everything that could not fight: the baggage train, the draft animals, the wounded, and the civilian members of the scientific commission. Roughly a hundred and sixty scholars, engineers, surveyors, naturalists, draftsmen, and printers had sailed with the army, and on 21 July they stood in the middle of infantry formations while cavalry charged the outside of them.

The army's opinion of these passengers is preserved in a standing jest of the campaign, the order to place the donkeys and the savants in the center, which the soldiers repeated with the affection reserved for people who are useless and harmless. The scholars, for their part, recorded the experience of being protected objects in a battle with the mixture of terror and curiosity their diaries display throughout the expedition.

The arrangement is a neat physical illustration of what the whole venture was. This was not a raid. An army does not carry surveyors and a printing press across a sea unless it intends to stay, study, catalogue, and govern, and the men in the middle of those squares were the instrument of that intention. Their work, described in the article on [how Napoleon's scholars revealed Egypt](/2013/12/01/napoleon-scholars-rediscovered-egypt/), turned out to be the only thing the expedition produced that outlasted it.

There is a further irony in the geography. The scholars standing inside the squares that afternoon could see, on the southern horizon, the monuments they had come to measure, and which several of them would be surveying within weeks. The battle that put the pyramids into its own name was also the battle that opened them to the people who would first record them systematically.

## What Napoleon was trying to do

The French plan on 21 July was simple and it is worth stating, because the simplicity is itself evidence about how the commander read his opponent.

He wanted a decisive battle immediately, and he wanted Cairo. The reasoning was arithmetic rather than inspiration. His army was deteriorating: three weeks ashore, marching in July heat, short of water discipline, losing men to heat, dysentery, and eye disease, with no cavalry and no secure supply. Every additional week in the field cost him strength he could not replace. An enemy whose whole military identity rested on the cavalry charge was an enemy who would give him a battle, and a battle was the fastest possible route to the capital, the granaries, and a base.

So he did not maneuver. He marched straight down the west bank toward the fortified position, formed his divisions where the ground allowed, and let Murad come to him. The dispositions reflect the plan exactly: the divisions nearest the desert flank positioned to receive cavalry, the divisions nearest the river positioned to take the village. Receive the charge on the right, take the camp on the left, occupy the crossing, walk into Cairo.

There is no evidence of anything more elaborate and no reason there should be. Napoleon had seen at Shubra Khit precisely what his formations did to this opponent, and a commander who knows the enemy's only move has no need for subtlety. The frequently repeated claim that the battle showcased his genius does not survive contact with what he actually did, which was standard divisional organization, standard squares, standard artillery placement, and a straightforward assault on an entrenchment with an exposed flank.

What the plan did require was accepting risk. Marching a tired army into contact with a numerically comparable enemy, in a country he did not know, with no cavalry to screen him and no reserve behind him, was not cautious. It was correct, because time was the enemy that would actually kill his army and Murad was the one he could beat in an afternoon.

## The charge

Murad attacked in the middle of the afternoon, and the sources agree on the character of what followed even where they differ on the timing and the numbers.

The Mamluk cavalry came forward at speed against the French right, where Desaix's and Reynier's divisions stood closest to the desert edge and furthest from support. The horsemen were magnificently mounted and magnificently equipped, carrying sabres, lances, javelins, pistols, and carbines, many of them wearing mail and helmets of a pattern that had changed little in centuries. They came on in a loose mass rather than in ordered ranks, because Mamluk tactics were built around the individual's skill and impetus rather than around a formation that held together.

The squares let them come. French infantry of this period were trained to hold fire until the target was close enough that a volley could not miss, and the discipline required to stand with levelled muskets while several thousand horsemen bear down on you is precisely the thing that separates a trained army from an armed crowd. The front ranks knelt with bayonets fixed and angled upward, the rear ranks fired over them, and the guns at the angles waited until the mass was inside canister range.

The result was immediate and total. The charge broke against the front face of the squares, riders wheeled along the sides looking for an opening and found none, and the formations fired into them at ranges where a musket ball will not miss a horse. Canister at that distance does not so much kill individuals as dismantle a formation. Horses went down and the men behind piled into them. Some riders got in among the ranks at the corners, where the accounts describe hand-to-hand fighting, and were killed. Others, unable to break in, rode around the squares and back out again, an act of pointless courage that the French remarked on with genuine respect.

Murad reformed and charged again, and the second attempt produced the same outcome. Some accounts describe a third effort. What none of them describes is any attempt to do anything other than charge: no attempt to bring the cavalry off and let artillery work, which was impossible anyway, no attempt to envelop and cut the French from water, no attempt to break off and shadow the army and fight another day in better circumstances.

## The storming of Embabeh

While the cavalry was destroying itself on the right, the French left was doing the work that actually decided the position.

The divisions of Bon and Vial moved against the entrenched camp at Embabeh, using the cover of the ground and the irrigation embankments to get close. The entrenchment's guns fired, and being fixed in their emplacements they fired along the axes they had been sited for and could not be redirected usefully as the French came in obliquely.

The infantry inside the works consisted of Cairo janissaries, whose corps had long since become urban stipend-drawing corporations rather than fighting formations, and levied peasants with no training at all. They had been placed in a fortified position on the assumption that the cavalry would win the battle out on the plain and that their job was to hold ground and look numerous.

When the French assaulted, the position collapsed quickly. The defenders were pushed back through the village toward the river, and the river was the problem. Behind Embabeh the Nile was in flood, wide and fast. Men who fled had nowhere to flee to. Large numbers went into the water, and large numbers drowned. The sources on this are consistent, and it is here rather than in the cavalry fight that most of the day's deaths occurred.

### What decided the fight for the Embabeh entrenchments?

The assault columns of Bon and Vial, arriving obliquely where the fixed guns could not bear, against defenders who had no training and no line of retreat except a flooded river. The position fell in a short, one-sided action, and the rout into the Nile produced most of the day's casualties.

## The end of the battle

By early evening it was over. Murad, seeing the camp lost and his cavalry shattered, broke off with what he could gather, set fire to the boats and the flotilla to prevent their capture, and rode south. Perhaps three thousand horsemen went with him, a figure that appears in several accounts and should be treated as approximate.

On the east bank, Ibrahim Bey had watched the destruction of the army he could not reach. He withdrew east with his own forces, taking the Ottoman governor with him, heading for Sinai and eventually Syria. The Cairo establishment was left to negotiate with the victors.

Cairo itself, across the river, had watched the battle from rooftops. The chronicler al-Jabarti, who was in the city, records the arrival of the news and the panic that followed, the flight of those who could leave, and the looting of abandoned Mamluk properties by the city's own population before the French ever entered. The morning after the battle the Cairo populace sacked the houses of the beys who had failed to protect them, which is a detail that says a good deal about how those beys were regarded.

## The casualty figures and what to do with them

The numbers usually quoted for this battle are among the most lopsided in modern military history, and they need handling with care rather than either credulity or dismissal.

French losses are given in the French official accounts as roughly twenty nine killed and something over two hundred wounded, with the total commonly rounded to under three hundred casualties of all kinds. These figures come from the victor's own returns and were published for political effect, so a degree of understatement is plausible. Even doubling or tripling them, however, leaves the French loss trivial in relation to the result, and no source on any side suggests the French suffered seriously.

Egyptian losses are far harder to establish and are usually given in ranges between about three thousand and six thousand, sometimes higher. The uncertainty is structural rather than careless. Nobody counted. A large proportion of the dead drowned in the Nile and were never recovered or tallied. The infantry in the entrenchment were levies with no muster rolls. And the figures that circulate derive from French estimates made by men with an interest in the number being large.

What can be said with confidence is the shape rather than the arithmetic: the Mamluk cavalry lost a substantial fraction of its trained strength in an afternoon, the Embabeh infantry was scattered and suffered heavily in the rout, and the French lost almost nothing. That shape is what matters, because it is what made the political consequences so complete.

### Why were French losses so low?

Because the formation was never broken. Almost all casualties in a cavalry action occur after a square gives way and horsemen get in among men who have lost their order. The French squares held throughout, so the cavalry never got its chance, and losses stayed at the level of men hit by fire.

## The gold in the river

One of the campaign's most frequently repeated details is grounded in something real, and it illuminates the nature of the elite that had just been destroyed.

Mamluk commanders carried their wealth on their persons. A senior horseman might be wearing several hundred gold coins sewn into his clothing, along with jeweled weapons, gold-mounted harness, and fine cloth. This was not vanity alone; in a political system where a defeated household's property was seized by the victor, portable wealth was rational.

After the battle French soldiers stripped the dead, and for days afterward men fished bodies out of the Nile for the gold they carried. Officers' memoirs record this without embarrassment, and the sums recovered were substantial enough to change the economic condition of individual soldiers in an army that had not been paid reliably. It became one of the campaign's standing jokes among the troops.

The detail matters beyond its color. An elite that keeps its capital on its body rather than in an institution is an elite with no state behind it. There was no treasury to fall back on, no bank, no system of public credit, no way to reconstitute a defeated force from central resources. When the men who carried the wealth went into the river, the wealth went with them.

## Cairo

The French entered Cairo on 24 July 1798, three days after the battle, effectively unopposed.

What they took was a great city, one of the largest in the world, with a dense commercial quarter, a major religious university, an extensive urban economy, and no government. The beys had fled, the Ottoman governor had gone with Ibrahim, the janissary officers had scattered, and the men left holding authority were the shaykhs of al-Azhar and the merchant notables, who had spent the previous two days trying to keep order in a city that had already begun looting itself.

A delegation met the French and arranged terms. Napoleon installed himself, set up a council of notables, and began the administration whose three-year record belongs to the article on the invasion as a whole. The speed of the transition is the point here: from the destruction of the field army to the occupation of the capital took seventy two hours, and no further fighting was required to accomplish it.

That is what a decisive battle means. Not that it was hard fought, and not that it was close, but that it settled the question of who held the country and made further resistance in the Delta pointless.

## Where the battle was actually fought

The single most persistent error about this engagement concerns its location, and correcting it is one of the two jobs the brief for this article exists to do.

The fighting took place at and around Embabeh, known also as Imbaba, a village on the west bank of the Nile directly across the river from the northern part of Cairo. The Giza pyramids stand on the desert edge roughly nine miles, some fifteen kilometers, to the south southwest. The squares were formed on cultivated flood-plain ground beside the river, among canals and embankments, in a landscape of fields and villages that looks nothing like the desert setting of the paintings.

The pyramids were visible. On a clear July afternoon, from open ground with no intervening high land, monuments of that size are visible from a great distance and would have stood on the horizon throughout the action. Visible is the operative word. No fighting occurred at them, near them, or within several hours' march of them. No French soldier that day laid a hand on a pyramid, and the divisions that were closest to the desert edge were still many miles north of the Giza plateau.

The name was a choice. Napoleon had a consistently sharp instinct for how an event would read in Paris, and an engagement named after a village nobody in France had heard of is a dispatch nobody reads, while an engagement named after the pyramids is a legend. Egyptian sources, including al-Jabarti, refer to the affair at Imbaba, which is the accurate name and the one that never travelled.

### What could the soldiers actually see from the field?

Cultivated fields, canals, the village and its earthworks on the riverbank, the Nile in flood behind it, the minarets of Cairo across the water, and the pyramids as distant shapes on the southern horizon. The monuments framed the scene and stood far outside it, which is exactly how the paintings invert the geography.

## The forty centuries

The second famous element of the battle is a sentence, and its status is genuinely uncertain in a way that most retellings flatten.

The line usually given is that Napoleon told his soldiers that from the summit of these pyramids forty centuries looked down upon them. It is the most quotable thing he ever said in Egypt and it appears in innumerable accounts, paintings, and school textbooks.

The evidence for it is thinner than its fame suggests. The version that circulates derives principally from the memoirs of Bourrienne, Napoleon's secretary, written long afterward by a man whose reliability is contested on multiple points and who had strong incentives in the reconstruction of his own importance. Other participant accounts do not record it. Napoleon's own contemporary dispatches and proclamations from Egypt are considerably more prosaic in tone, and his documented address to the troops before the battle was practical soldier's talk about what they were facing and what they would gain.

There is also a question of when such a thing could have been said. The pyramids were a distant feature on the horizon, the army was deploying under the eye of enemy cavalry, and an address of that kind delivered to twenty thousand men strung across a wide front is a physical impossibility. Whatever was said was said to whoever was within earshot of the commander.

The reasonable position is that the sentiment fits Napoleon's known rhetorical style entirely, that he may well have said something of the sort to officers around him, that the polished form in which it survives is a later literary product, and that it should be quoted with an attribution rather than as established fact. That is a duller conclusion than either believing it or debunking it, and it is what the evidence supports.

## The paintings and the invention of the battle

The visual tradition did more than the written one to fix the false picture, and it repays a moment's attention because it shows how a battle becomes a myth.

The best known images date from a decade or more after the event and were produced under an imperial regime with a direct interest in the legend. They show desert rather than irrigated field, place the pyramids close enough to loom over the fighting, render the Mamluks as exotic and picturesque, and centre the composition on the figure of the commander. They are magnificent paintings and they are documents about the Empire's self-presentation rather than records of 21 July 1798.

The more reliable visual evidence comes from the expedition's own draftsmen, who drew what they saw for the scientific commission rather than for a salon, and whose work belonged to the scientific commission rather than to any salon. Their renderings of the terrain around Cairo show the flood plain and the relationship between the city, the river, and the distant monuments accurately, because accuracy was their assignment.

The lesson generalizes beyond this battle. Where a famous engagement has a famous image attached, the image was usually made later, by someone who was not there, for a purpose that was not documentary.

## The anatomy of the square

The formation that won the battle deserves a proper explanation, because most accounts describe it as though it were a clever idea Napoleon had rather than standard doctrine executed well.

A square, which in practice was usually an oblong, is a closed formation in which a body of infantry faces outward on all four sides with no flanks and no rear. It is formed from a column or a line by wheeling the component units, and it is slow, cumbersome, and almost incapable of offensive movement. Its single virtue is that it cannot be taken from behind.

Against cavalry this is decisive, and the reason is physiological before it is tactical. A horse will not run into a solid obstacle it can see. Trained cavalry mounts can be pushed to remarkable things, but riding at speed into a continuous hedge of bayonets held by men who do not flinch is not among them. Horses shy, swerve, or pull up at the face of the formation, and at that moment the rider is stationary at thirty yards in front of several hundred loaded muskets.

The guns are what turn a defensive success into a slaughter. Field pieces sited at the angles of the formation cover the faces with enfilading fire, and loaded with canister, essentially a tin of musket balls that turns a cannon into a giant shotgun, they sweep the ground in front at ranges up to a few hundred yards. A mass of cavalry approaching in the open has no way to spread out enough to reduce the effect and no way to close fast enough to escape it.

The requirements are severe and they are all requirements of training rather than of equipment. The infantry must form square quickly, which takes drill. It must hold fire under the psychological pressure of an oncoming charge, which takes discipline and confidence in the officers. It must not fire high, which is the universal fault of frightened infantry. It must close gaps as men fall. And the gun crews must serve their pieces while cavalry rides at them, which requires the knowledge that the infantry beside them will not run.

The French had every one of these, because the Revolutionary armies had spent six years fighting European professionals and had learned or died. The veterans of the Army of Italy who made up the bulk of the Egyptian expedition were as good as infantry got in the 1790s.

The square's weaknesses were equally well known and none of them was available to Murad. It is a superb target for artillery. It is vulnerable to skirmishers who can shoot into a packed mass from beyond charge range without being charged themselves. And it cannot pursue, which means a mobile enemy who declines to attack it can simply refuse battle indefinitely. Every one of those counters requires either guns or infantry with firearms and the discipline to use them at range, and the Mamluk system produced neither.

## Why the Mamluk system had no answer

The defeat was the product of an institutional arrangement rather than of any decision taken on 21 July, and the case for that is set out fully in the article on [the Mamluk war machine](/2013/02/01/mamluk-war-machine-explained/). The essentials as they bore on this battle are worth restating.

Mamluk military power rested on heavy cavalry produced by purchasing boys, usually from the Caucasus, training them for years in horsemanship and arms, and manumitting them into the household of the commander who had bought them. The training was long, expensive, and excellent by its own standards. Observers on the French side, who had every reason to disparage a beaten enemy, wrote about the horsemanship and swordsmanship with something close to awe. Individually these were among the finest cavalrymen in the world.

They had no infantry worth the name. The Cairo garrison regiments, the ocaks, had been founded as an Ottoman infantry establishment and had degenerated over two centuries into hereditary urban corporations whose members drew stipends, ran protection rackets, and practiced trades. They did not drill. When infantry was needed in bulk it was levied from the villages, armed with whatever came to hand, and put in a trench.

They had no field artillery. Guns require a central treasury, an arsenal, foundries, trained crews, drivers, teams, and a doctrine for moving them with an army, and every one of those is a state function. Egypt under the beys had no central treasury in any meaningful sense, because revenue was collected by tax farmers who were themselves the military commanders.

They had no unified command. The force on the field was an assembly of households, each fighting under its own bey, each free in practice to leave, plus tribal allies with their own calculations.

And they had no doctrine of combined arms, because they had never faced an enemy that required one. Every opponent inside Egypt for two and a half centuries had been another cavalry force fighting the same way. The system was not backward relative to its own competitive environment. It was perfectly adapted to an environment that a French expeditionary force had just removed.

## Could the battle have gone differently?

Counterfactuals are worth running when they isolate a variable, and here there are two worth examining and one that is usually raised and is worthless.

The worthless one is the idea that a harder, better-coordinated charge could have broken the squares. It could not. The formation's immunity to unsupported cavalry is structural, and more horsemen arriving at the same wall of bayonets produces more casualties rather than a breakthrough. This is the counterfactual Murad himself believed in after Shubra Khit, and it is why he lost.

The first serious one concerns the river. Had Murad and Ibrahim combined on the west bank rather than splitting across a flooded Nile, the Egyptian force would have been substantially larger and the French would have faced a longer fight. It would not have changed the result, because the additional strength would have been more of what did not work, but it would have raised the cost and preserved the option of a fighting withdrawal with the elite intact.

The second serious one concerns refusing battle altogether. This is the only course that had a real chance, and it was available. The French had almost no cavalry, were operating far from their base, were physically deteriorating in the heat, could not be reinforced after the fleet's destruction eleven days later, and depended on the Egyptian countryside for food. A force of six thousand horsemen that declined to fight, harassed the columns, poisoned wells, drove off livestock, cut stragglers off, and let the summer do the work would have been an extremely difficult problem. Desaix's year-long pursuit of Murad through Upper Egypt afterward demonstrates exactly how difficult, because that is precisely the campaign Murad eventually fought, and the French could never bring him to a decision.

Why did Murad not do that on 21 July? Because the political logic of his position forbade it. A bey's standing rested on armed force and on the perception that he could protect his clients, his tax farms, and the capital. Abandoning Cairo without a battle would have destroyed his authority among the other households as surely as losing one. He fought because a man who rules by cavalry cannot decline the cavalry's test, which is the same structural trap that prevented the beys from ever reforming their military system in the first place.

### Could the Mamluks have fought the battle differently?

Their only real option was to refuse the engagement and wage a campaign of harassment against an army with no cavalry, no local supply, and no reinforcement. Murad later fought exactly that way in Upper Egypt with considerable success. On 21 July the political cost of abandoning Cairo made it unavailable.

The table below sets the two sides against each other in the categories that actually decided the day, which is the quickest way to see that the outcome was settled before either force deployed.

| Element | French | Egyptian force under Murad Bey | What it meant on the day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength engaged | Roughly 20,000 to 25,000 in five divisions | Around 6,000 Mamluk cavalry plus 15,000 to 20,000 untrained infantry in the Embabeh works | Numbers close to even and irrelevant to the outcome |
| Main arm | Veteran drilled infantry of the Army of Italy | Heavy cavalry of exceptional individual quality | One arm could hold a formation, the other could only charge |
| Formation | Five large divisional squares, guns at the angles, baggage inside | Loose cavalry mass, infantry static in earthworks | The square denied the charge any flank or rear to exploit |
| Artillery | Light field pieces, mobile, trained crews, firing canister | Roughly 40 guns fixed in the entrenchment, poorly served | French guns went where the battle was; Egyptian guns could not |
| Infantry | The decisive arm | Cairo janissaries and village levies with no drill | The entrenchment fell to assault in a short one-sided action |
| Command | Single commander, five divisions acting to one plan | Split across a flooded river between Murad and Ibrahim Bey | Half the Egyptian force watched from the far bank |
| Losses | About 29 killed, some 300 casualties in all by French returns | Estimated 3,000 to 6,000, most drowned in the rout, no reliable count | A trained elite lost a large share of its strength in an afternoon |

## The ground and the season

The terrain of 21 July gets almost no attention and it shaped the battle more than the pyramids did.

The engagement was fought in late July, which in Egypt is the beginning of the flood. The Nile was rising toward its annual peak, which meant the river behind Embabeh was wide, fast, and impassable without boats, and it meant the surrounding flood plain was a network of irrigation canals, raised embankments, and fields in varying states of saturation.

For the French this was mixed. The embankments gave cover to the divisions moving against the village on the left, which helped the assault considerably. The canals broke up the ground and made the maintenance of large square formations harder, since a square that loses its dressing crossing a ditch is a square with a gap in it, and the French managed this well enough that the cavalry never found an opening.

For the Egyptians the flood was a catastrophe waiting to happen. It divided the force, because Murad on the west bank and Ibrahim on the east could not combine or reinforce across a river at that stage of its rise. And it turned the Embabeh position into a trap, because a fortified camp with a swollen river directly behind it has no line of retreat, which is the single worst property a defensive position can have.

The heat matters too. The action was fought in the afternoon of a July day in the Nile valley, on men who in the French case were in wool and in the Mamluk case were frequently in mail. Combat in those conditions is short by necessity, and the compressed timescale of the battle, a couple of hours from the first charge to the collapse of the camp, is partly a function of what human beings and horses can do in that temperature.

Choosing to stand at Embabeh with the flood behind him was Murad's worst decision of the day, worse than charging the squares, because charging was at least consistent with his system while accepting a position with no retreat was simply bad ground-reading.

## Arms and equipment

The material difference between the two sides is usually described as though the French were modern and the Mamluks medieval, and the reality is more specific and more interesting than that.

A Mamluk horseman was superbly equipped by the standards of cavalry. He typically carried a curved sabre of very high quality steel, frequently a lance or javelins, a pair of pistols, and often a carbine or short musket, and he might wear a mail shirt and a helmet of a pattern that had changed little in three centuries. His horse was excellent and his saddlery was fine. The equipment was expensive, personal, and frequently decorated with gold and jewels, since it doubled as portable wealth.

What is striking is that he was not short of firearms. Mamluk cavalry carried and used pistols and carbines, and the idea that they faced gunpowder weapons with swords alone is simply wrong. What they lacked was the disciplined massed use of firearms, which is a matter of drill and formation rather than of technology. A hundred cavalrymen each firing a pistol as they ride past is not a volley, and its effect on a formed square is negligible.

The French infantryman carried a standard flintlock musket with a socket bayonet, equipment that was individually inferior in workmanship to what he faced. Its advantage lay entirely in how it was used: fired in volleys on command, at a range where accuracy did not matter, by men standing in a formation that let the ranks reload in safety.

The artillery comparison is the one that is genuinely categorical. The French brought light field pieces on mobile carriages with trained crews, horses, and ammunition including canister. The Egyptian guns sat in earthworks and could not move. That is not a difference in technology, since a cannon is a cannon, but a difference in whether a state exists to organize one.

## After the fighting stopped

The hours and days following the battle are worth a paragraph because they show how such a victory was consolidated in practice.

The French bivouacked on the field and in the captured camp, which yielded substantial supplies, the entrenchment's guns, and a considerable quantity of material abandoned by a force that had fled toward a river. Foraging parties went out into villages that had, for the first time in the campaign, no armed protector anywhere nearby.

Prisoners were relatively few, which follows from the shape of the action: cavalry that breaks off rides away, and infantry with a river behind it either gets across or does not. Some Mamluks were taken and a number subsequently entered French service, and a mamluk squadron later served in Napoleon's armies in Europe, which is one of the campaign's odder afterlives.

The wounded were handled by a medical service that was, by contemporary standards, unusually well organized, including the surgeon Larrey whose work on field evacuation came substantially out of this campaign. French wounded numbered in the low hundreds and the facilities coped. Egyptian wounded, in the absence of any comparable service on their side and with their army dispersed, were largely left to the villages.

And the dead in the river kept surfacing for days, which is how the story of soldiers fishing for gold became one of the best-attested details of the whole engagement.

## What happened to the beaten commanders

The two men who lost the battle had very different afterlives, and both are instructive about what the defeat did and did not destroy.

Murad Bey rode south with perhaps three thousand horsemen and became, for the next two years, the most difficult military problem the French faced inside Egypt. Napoleon sent Desaix after him with a division in late August 1798, and the pursuit ran the length of Upper Egypt to the First Cataract and beyond. Murad was beaten in the field whenever he accepted an engagement, at Sediman in October 1798 and repeatedly afterward, and each time he withdrew, gathered fresh men from tribal allies and surviving households, and reappeared somewhere else.

This was the campaign he should have fought from the beginning, and he fought it well. The French could win every battle and could not catch him, because infantry does not catch cavalry and the French had barely any horses. Desaix adapted by mounting troops, using the river flotilla for mobility, and garrisoning posts, which is recognizably counterinsurgency, and it took the better part of a year to make Upper Egypt secure. Murad eventually came to terms, governing part of the south on French sufferance, and died of plague in 1801.

Ibrahim Bey withdrew east across Sinai toward Syria, taking the Ottoman governor with him, and spent the occupation outside Egypt. He returned after the French left and lived on into the confused decade that followed, another claimant among several in a country that no longer had room for the old arrangement. He was among the households that Muhammad Ali dismantled.

Neither man ever recovered the position he had held on the morning of 21 July, and neither household regained the revenue, the trained cavalry, or the political monopoly it had lost. The duumvirate that had run Egypt for two decades ended on the riverbank at Embabeh, even though both of its members outlived the battle by years.

## What the battle destroyed

The political consequence of the engagement was out of all proportion to its military difficulty, and setting out exactly what was broken shows why.

It destroyed the bey households' military capital. Trained mamluk cavalry represented a decade of investment per man: purchase price, transport, conversion, years of instruction, horses, arms, and equipment. It could only be replaced by buying more boys from the Caucasus and waiting ten years, and replacement required revenue that the French were about to take over.

It destroyed the revenue base almost immediately. The French seized the tax farms of the absent beys, confiscated their properties, and reorganized collection. A household without its iltizam contracts cannot pay retainers, cannot buy recruits, and dissolves within a generation.

It destroyed the presumption of invincibility, which is harder to measure and mattered as much as either. For two and a half centuries the beys had been the arbiters of Egyptian politics because everyone in the country, including the Ottoman governor, the bedouin confederations, the merchant community, and the Cairo populace, calculated on the assumption that nobody could beat them. That assumption died in an afternoon in front of an audience on the Cairo rooftops. The sacking of bey properties by the city's own population the following morning is the first visible consequence.

And it destroyed the political arrangement described in the article on [Ottoman Egypt](/2013/05/01/ottoman-egypt-complete-guide/), in which a Mamluk elite governed under a nominal imperial framework. When the French left in 1801, the households came back as one faction among several in a country containing an Ottoman expeditionary army, Albanian irregulars, and an urban population that had learned to act politically. They were never again the strongest force in Egypt, and thirteen years after the battle Muhammad Ali finished what the French had begun, a sequence traced in the article on [Muhammad Ali and the birth of modern Egypt](/2014/03/01/muhammad-ali-and-modern-egypt/).

## Battle or massacre?

The question is asked often enough to deserve a direct answer, and the answer is that it was both, in sequence, and that distinguishing the two phases matters.

The cavalry fight was a battle. Both sides were armed, both intended to fight, one side attacked and the other received the attack, and the losing side lost because its tactics could not work against the winning side's formation. That men died in very unequal numbers does not make an engagement a massacre; it makes it a decisive one. The Mamluk horsemen were volunteers in the fullest sense, they attacked repeatedly, and several of them died inside the French formations having got that far.

The collapse at Embabeh was closer to a slaughter, and honest accounts should say so. Untrained levies in an entrenchment with a flooded river at their backs, assaulted by veteran infantry, had no realistic option except surrender, and the circumstances of a village assault in the confusion of an afternoon did not offer many opportunities to take it. Most of the day's dead were these men, and most of them drowned. Nobody was pursuing a policy of extermination, and nobody was making much effort to stop what was happening either.

There is a third category that gets less attention. In the days after the battle, French detachments operating in the surrounding countryside dealt harshly with villages suspected of killing stragglers, and the bedouin who had shadowed the army and picked off isolated men were treated without quarter when caught. This was normal practice in the warfare of the period and it was ugly.

The useful distinction for a reader is between the mechanism and the morality. Mechanically, the disparity in casualties is explained entirely by the fact that the French formation never broke and the Egyptian position had no retreat. Morally, the assault phase is where the questions sit, and they sit there for most storming actions of that century.

## The bedouin who watched

A significant armed force was present on 21 July and did almost no fighting, and its behavior is a good index of how the day was read at the time.

Bedouin confederations had shadowed the French from the moment they left Alexandria, killing stragglers, cutting off foragers, and taking what they could from both sides. They were a genuine military factor in Egypt, and the beys' system depended on them: a household that secured a powerful tribal alliance acquired thousands of horsemen at short notice, and that relationship was maintained by subsidy, marriage, and periodic punitive expedition.

At Embabeh they hovered on the flanks and stayed out of it. Their calculation was straightforward and entirely rational: commit to the winning side, or to neither. When the outcome became clear they turned to the profitable work of the aftermath, stripping the dead and the fugitives from both armies with complete impartiality.

The significance is that the beys' most important auxiliary force declined to fight for them at the decisive moment. That is not treachery in a system where tribal alliance was always conditional. It is evidence that the people best placed to judge the odds had judged them, and it is another measure of how completely the elite's standing collapsed that afternoon.

## What ordinary Egyptians saw

The battle was watched from Cairo, which is unusual for a decisive engagement and which means the Egyptian record of it is unusually immediate.

The city stands on the east bank and Embabeh lies opposite the northern quarters, so the smoke, the noise, and the movement were visible from rooftops and minarets across the river. Al-Jabarti describes the population going up to watch, the sound of the guns, and the moment when it became apparent that the Mamluk force was breaking. Crowds saw men drowning in the river in front of them.

What followed that night and the next morning is the detail that tells you most about Egyptian attitudes to the ruling elite. Rather than mourning, the Cairo populace looted the houses of the beys. The properties of men who had taxed the city hard, quarrelled in its streets, and then failed to protect it were stripped by its inhabitants before any French soldier crossed the river.

This should not be read as enthusiasm for the invaders, and al-Jabarti's chronicle makes the distinction very clearly. The city had no affection for the beys and no illusions about the French. It was a population that had watched its government be destroyed by foreigners, understood that a worse arrangement was probably coming, and took what it could in the interval. The experience of being spectators to a battle that decided their country's fate without involving them is, in its way, the most accurate summary of what Egyptians got out of the whole Napoleonic episode.

## How we know what happened

The evidence for a single afternoon in 1798 is abundant and lopsided, and knowing which way it leans is necessary for reading any account of the battle including this one.

The bulk of the narrative material is French and comes in three forms. There are Napoleon's own dispatches, written immediately and composed for political effect in Paris, which are valuable for the outline and unreliable for proportion. There are the official reports of divisional commanders, closer to the fighting and less shaped. And there are participant memoirs, written years or decades afterward, some by men with reputations to build and one, Bourrienne's, whose reliability is contested in detail. The memoirs supply nearly all the vivid material, including the famous sentence, and they are the weakest category of evidence in the file.

The Egyptian record rests principally on al-Jabarti, the Cairo scholar whose chronicle covers the occupation year by year. He was in the city, he watched from the east bank, and he recorded the reaction of the population, the flight of the elite, and the looting that followed. He was not on the battlefield and does not offer a tactical account, which is the correct limitation for a witness across a river. What he gives, and nothing else gives, is the view from the side that lost.

Ottoman and British material is thinner on the battle itself and useful for the setting. British naval correspondence concerns the hunt for the French fleet in the same weeks. Ottoman records concern the news reaching Istanbul and the decision to declare war in September.

The scholars of the expedition contribute indirectly but significantly. Their survey work established the terrain, the distances, the relationship of Embabeh to Cairo and to the Giza plateau, and the character of the flood plain, which is the material that allows the location myth to be corrected with confidence rather than asserted.

What no source provides is an Egyptian tactical account or a reliable casualty count for the losing side. Both gaps are permanent, and any writer who gives a precise figure for Mamluk dead is reporting a French estimate as though it were a count.

## The battles this one gets confused with

The campaign produced several engagements in similar places with similar names, and untangling them prevents a common set of errors.

The Battle of the Pyramids is the land action of 21 July 1798 at Embabeh, described here.

The Battle of the Nile is the naval action of 1 and 2 August 1798 in Aboukir Bay, in which Nelson destroyed the French fleet. It is frequently confused with this battle because both happened within a fortnight and both involve the word that names the river, and the confusion matters because the two engagements point in opposite directions. One was a total French victory that decided who held Egypt. The other was a total French defeat that decided whether holding Egypt was worth anything.

The Battle of Aboukir is a land action of 25 July 1799 in which Napoleon destroyed an Ottoman force that had landed in the same bay where the fleet had been destroyed a year earlier. Same place, different year, different type of battle.

The Battle of Heliopolis, on 20 March 1800, was fought by Kleber against a far larger Ottoman army outside Cairo and was arguably a more impressive feat of arms than the Battle of the Pyramids, since the French were outnumbered several times over and won decisively. Almost nobody outside specialist literature has heard of it, which is a useful illustration of how fame attaches to battles.

The Battle of Alexandria, on 21 March 1801, is the one that ended the occupation, fought between Menou and a British expeditionary force under Abercromby. It is the only engagement of the campaign the French lost on land, and it is the one that actually mattered to the outcome of the war.

Arranged that way, the sequence makes a point worth holding onto. The most famous battle of the campaign was the easiest, and the battles that determined what happened to Egypt were fought by other people in other places.

## Eleven days later

The battle's most important property is one that could not be seen on the day, and it is that the victory expired almost immediately.

On the night of 1 August 1798, eleven days after the squares broke the cavalry at Embabeh, Nelson found the French fleet at anchor in Aboukir Bay and destroyed it. Eleven of thirteen ships of the line were taken or burned. From that moment the army that had just conquered Egypt could not be reinforced, could not be supplied from France, could not be paid, and could not leave.

Everything the Battle of the Pyramids had won was therefore held by a force that had become a hostage. The country was in French hands and French hands could not use it. The strategic purposes the campaign had been sold on, a colony, a base on the route toward India, a threat that forced Britain to divert resources, all required communication with France, and communication with France ended in Aboukir Bay.

This is why the battle occupies such a strange position in military history. It was tactically decisive, politically transformative for Egypt, and strategically worthless for France inside a fortnight. A commander can win the decisive battle of a campaign and lose the campaign in the same month, and this is the textbook case.

### What did the victory actually give Napoleon?

Cairo, the Delta, the destruction of the bey field army, and three years of occupation. It gave France nothing lasting, because the fleet's destruction eleven days later removed every use the conquest could have been put to. The battle decided who held Egypt and not whether holding it was worth anything.

## What armies took from it

The engagement entered professional military education quickly, and what was drawn from it is a reasonable measure of what it actually demonstrated.

The first lesson was about the square and it was a confirmation rather than a discovery. European doctrine already held that steady infantry with artillery support could not be broken by cavalry alone, and Embabeh was an unusually clean experiment because the variables were so cleanly separated: superb cavalry on one side, no artillery support, no infantry support, no skirmishers, against trained infantry with guns. The result was the doctrine's prediction, precisely.

The second lesson was about combined arms and it was the more important one. What beat the Mamluks was not any single element but the integration of infantry, artillery, and command into a system where each covered the others' weaknesses. Armies that had been drifting toward that integration through the eighteenth century saw it demonstrated against an opponent who had none of it.

The third lesson was strategic and it was learned by everyone except the French. A force that depends on a sea lane it does not control is not an expedition, it is a wager. The British in particular built the following century of imperial military planning around securing the sea route first.

The fourth was about asymmetry, and it took much longer to absorb. The engagement is one of the earliest modern cases of a European army meeting a non-European force whose military system was highly developed along a different axis, and the ease of the victory encouraged a set of conclusions about European superiority that were partly true, considerably overstated, and enormously consequential for the nineteenth century. The accurate conclusion was narrow: a state that can fund artillery, drill, and a general staff will beat one that cannot, whoever they are. The conclusion frequently drawn instead was about civilizations, and Desaix's inability to catch Murad for a year afterward should have complicated it.

## The men in the squares

The formation that held is usually described as though it were a machine, and it is worth putting the people back into it, because the battle turned on what those particular soldiers were willing to do.

They were mostly veterans of the Army of Italy, men who had campaigned through 1796 and 1797 against Austrian regulars and had won. Many were conscripts of the Revolutionary levies who had been soldiering for four or five years by 1798, which in that era made them experienced professionals whatever their origin. Their officers had been promoted rapidly on merit through a decade in which the old aristocratic officer corps had been swept away, and the divisional commanders on this field were men in their late twenties and thirties.

Their condition on 21 July was poor. They had marched across desert in July, they had been on short and unfamiliar rations, they were suffering from dysentery and eye inflammation, they had not been paid, and they had spent three weeks in a country that had comprehensively failed to match the expectations they had sailed with. Officer diaries from the march describe an army in a black mood, grumbling openly about the whole enterprise.

What that army did anyway was stand in ranks in the open while several thousand of the best heavy cavalry in the world rode at it, hold its fire until the range was point blank, and not break. That is the whole battle. The formation is a piece of geometry; whether it holds is a question about men.

It is worth naming the counterpart too. The Mamluk horsemen who charged squares that had already shown at Shubra Khit that they could not be broken, and who charged again after the first attempt failed, were not fools acting on ignorance by the second charge. They were men whose entire social identity rested on the charge, riding at something that had already beaten them. French accounts written by people with every reason to sneer are consistently respectful about it.

## Why the numbers vary so much

Every account of this battle gives different figures, and understanding why prevents the common mistake of treating any of them as precise.

French strength is the firmest of the numbers and still ranges between about twenty and twenty five thousand, because sources differ on whether to count the troops present on the field, the divisions detached, the sick, and the sailors and non-combatants with the column. The army that landed was larger than the army that fought, and attrition over three weeks was significant.

Mamluk cavalry strength rests almost entirely on French estimates made by men looking across open ground at a mass of horsemen, which is a notoriously unreliable way to count. Six thousand is the conventional figure and figures from three thousand to ten thousand appear.

Egyptian infantry strength in the entrenchment is the vaguest of all, because these were levies raised days earlier with no record of who was called up. Fifteen to twenty thousand is the usual range, and it may be considerably too high.

Casualty figures are the worst case. The French returns for their own losses are specific, self-reported, and published for effect. Egyptian losses were never counted by anyone, a large share of the dead were in the river, and every number in circulation traces back to a French guess.

The correct handling for a student or a writer is to give ranges, attribute them, and avoid building an argument on any single figure. The argument this article makes does not depend on the numbers, which is a useful property, because the outcome would have been the same at twice the Egyptian strength.

## Why this battle became famous

Several engagements of the period were harder fought and more consequential, so the fame requires explaining, and the explanation is mostly about naming and imagery.

The name did most of the work. An engagement at Embabeh is a footnote; an engagement at the Pyramids is a story that writes itself, connecting a modern general to four thousand years of monuments. Napoleon chose the name and he chose it knowing exactly what it would do.

The imagery did the rest. The battle offered an unusually pictorial contrast, disciplined European ranks against charging horsemen in silks and mail, and the painters of the imperial period made the most of it, moving the pyramids close enough to dominate the canvas.

The politics sustained it. The Egyptian campaign was a failure that could not be advertised as one, so the elements that could be celebrated were celebrated hard, and this battle was the best available material. When Napoleon became emperor, the version of his Egyptian record that circulated was the version he wanted, and the Battle of the Pyramids sat at the center of it.

And the sentiment kept it alive. The forty centuries line, whatever its actual provenance, gave the event a phrase, and events with phrases outlive events without them. Heliopolis, which was a genuinely remarkable victory against much worse odds, had no phrase and no painting, and it is forgotten.

## How Egypt remembered it

Egyptian memory of 21 July 1798 is not a memory of a national defeat, and understanding why requires setting aside modern assumptions about who was fighting for whom.

The army that lost was not an Egyptian army in any sense a nineteenth or twentieth century nationalist would recognize. Its commanders were Georgian and Circassian men bought as boys, who spoke Turkish among themselves, who had never intermarried with the population at the top of their households, and who taxed the country hard. Its infantry were Cairo stipend-holders and conscripted villagers put in a trench. Egyptians watching from the rooftops were watching one set of foreigners destroy another, and they looted the loser's houses the next morning.

Al-Jabarti's record carries no grief for the beys and no welcome for the French. What it carries is a scholar's dismay at disorder, at the collapse of a known arrangement, and at the arrival of people whose assumptions about the world were alien to everything his society took for granted.

When Egyptian national memory formed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the events it fixed on from this period were the Cairo risings of 1798 and 1800, where Egyptians acted, and the figure of Suleiman al-Halabi, who killed Kleber. The battle in which a foreign military caste was destroyed by a foreign army did not become a national wound, because there was no national party to the fight.

## The reaction in Istanbul

The battle reached Istanbul as news of a provincial catastrophe, and the response reveals what the empire actually thought it had lost.

The formal position had been that Egypt was an Ottoman province governed by an imperial pasha, with the beys as an irritating local military establishment. The practical position, set out in the article on [how Egypt was governed by the Ottomans](/2013/07/01/egypt-under-ottoman-rule/), was that the beys ran the country and remitted a fixed annual sum. The destruction of the beys therefore cost the empire the men who collected and forwarded its Egyptian revenue and secured the pilgrimage caravan, without giving it any capacity of its own to replace them.

Selim III declared war in September 1798, allied with Britain and, remarkably, with Russia, and began raising armies. What the empire could not do was take Egypt back by itself, as the defeats at Aboukir in 1799 and Heliopolis in 1800 demonstrated. The province was recovered in 1801 by British sea power and a British expeditionary force with Ottoman participation, which is a fact that shaped Ottoman policy for the following century.

## Turning point or symptom?

The framework this article belongs to is the turning point, and it is worth testing whether the label fits, because the honest answer is more interesting than a straight yes.

The case for turning point is strong on consequences. A political order that had governed Egypt since 1517 was destroyed in an afternoon, the country passed under European occupation, and the sequence that produced Muhammad Ali's state began that week. If a turning point is an event after which the plausible futures are different, this qualifies comprehensively.

The case for symptom is strong on causes. Everything that decided the battle had been true for a century before it was fought. The beys had no infantry, no artillery, no central treasury, and no unified command in 1698 as much as in 1798, and the reason was structural and known. Hasan Pasha's expedition of 1786 had already shown that an outside force with a proper army could take Egypt whenever it chose to pay for the attempt. What the French supplied was not a new weakness but an opponent willing to exploit an old one.

The resolution is that both are correct at different levels, and the useful formulation is this: the battle revealed rather than caused, and the revelation was itself causal. The beys' military obsolescence was decades old and invisible because untested. Testing it in public, in front of Cairo, converted a latent condition into a political fact that everybody then acted on, including the Cairo crowd that night, the bedouin who declined to fight, the Ottoman council that declared war, and the Albanian officer who arrived four years later. A symptom that everyone can see becomes a cause.

That formulation also explains the timing of what followed. The beys had been militarily hollow for generations without consequence, because the hollowness was never demonstrated and nobody acted on a suspicion. Once it was demonstrated in a single afternoon, in front of the capital, every actor in Egypt revised their calculation within days: the city looted the beys' houses, the tribal confederations declined to commit, the religious establishment opened negotiations with the occupier, and Istanbul concluded that the province would have to be recovered by an outside army rather than through its existing arrangements. None of those decisions required new information about Mamluk weakness. They required the old information to become undeniable, and that is what a battle does that a century of structural analysis cannot.

## How historians have treated it

Scholarly assessment has moved a good deal, and the direction of movement is instructive.

Nineteenth-century military writing, working largely from French memoirs and the Napoleonic legend, treated the battle as a demonstration of genius and of the superiority of modern European arms, with the Mamluks cast as magnificent anachronisms. The framing was flattering to the victor and incurious about the loser.

Twentieth-century military history reduced the role of genius considerably. The square was standard doctrine, the divisional organization was standard, the artillery handling was competent rather than inspired, and Napoleon's dispositions on 21 July were what any capable commander of the period would have made. The battle is now generally rated as a well-executed application of known technique against an opponent who could not answer it, which is not a criticism but is a substantial deflation.

Work on the Ottoman and Egyptian side, drawing on al-Jabarti, the Ottoman archives, and the court records, changed the picture more fundamentally by taking the losing side seriously as a political system rather than as a colorful obstacle. Scholarship on the household politics of Ottoman Egypt, including the research that established the eighteenth-century elite's Ottoman regimental origins, makes the military outcome legible as a consequence of the way the province was governed rather than as a cultural fact about the Middle East.

Historians of the wider campaign, including Henry Laurens in the most detailed French treatment and Juan Cole in the most direct corrective to the heroic version, place the battle where it belongs, as the easy opening of a venture that failed within a fortnight and collapsed within three years.

The interpretive question still genuinely open is how much weight to give the engagement in the long story of Egyptian change. Treating it as the moment Egypt entered the modern world is a piece of European self-flattery that ignores everything already in motion in the province. Treating it as militarily trivial ignores that it removed the governing class. The defensible position sits between them.

## What the battle did not change

A clear account of consequences requires saying what stayed the same, and a surprising amount did.

It did not change who worked the land or how. The fellahin cultivated the same basins under the same flood regime, and their tax burden went up rather than changing in kind, because the French needed more revenue than the beys had taken.

It did not change the legal or religious structure of Egyptian society. The courts continued, the ulama remained the effective leadership of urban society and in fact gained standing, and the communal arrangements of the country survived the occupation largely intact.

It did not immediately end Mamluk power, which is the point most often overstated. Murad held Upper Egypt in some form for two more years, the households returned after 1801, and the final destruction came in 1811 at the hands of Muhammad Ali. What the battle did was break their monopoly, not their existence.

It did not bring modernity, technology, or institutions that lasted. The French administrative innovations, the surveys, the quarantine measures, the street lighting, the institute, went with the army in 1801. What survived was knowledge on both sides: Europeans knew what Egypt contained, and Egyptians had watched a European state operate.

And it did not, despite the phrase, involve the pyramids in any way whatever.

## The verdict

The square-beats-the-charge thesis holds, and stating it precisely is the point of the article.

The Battle of the Pyramids was decided by a formation and by the guns at its corners. Not by the location, which was nine miles from the monuments the battle is named after. Not by numbers, which were roughly comparable and would not have changed the result at any ratio. Not by terrain, which was flat cultivated ground favoring neither side. Not by generalship, since Napoleon did exactly what any competent commander of his army would have done and Murad did exactly what his own system's logic required. And not by courage, which the losing side displayed in quantities the winners recorded with respect.

It was decided by the fact that one army was a system and the other was a collection. The French could combine infantry, artillery, and command into a whole in which each element covered another's weakness. The Mamluk force could deliver a cavalry charge of exceptional quality and could do nothing else, because the political arrangement that produced it, competing self-funding households, structurally could not produce anything else. Artillery, drilled infantry, and a staff are collective goods that require a central treasury, and the beys had spent two and a half centuries ensuring no central treasury existed, because a treasury strong enough to fund an army is a treasury strong enough to tax them.

The myth correction is the article's second job and it is straightforward. The fighting was at Embabeh on the west bank opposite Cairo. The pyramids were distant shapes on the southern horizon. The name was a propaganda choice, the paintings are imperial-era inventions that moved the monuments for composition, and the famous sentence about forty centuries rests on a contested memoir rather than on contemporary record. None of that diminishes the engagement. It relocates it from legend back into history, which is where it is more interesting.

The consequence and the futility have to be held together, and this is the hardest part to teach. Within seventy two hours the battle had delivered Cairo and destroyed the governing class of Egypt. Within eleven days it had been rendered strategically worthless by a naval action twenty miles up the coast. Within three years the victors were sailing home in the ships of the enemy that had beaten them at sea. And within thirteen years the political vacuum the battle had opened had been filled by a man who built precisely the fiscal-military state the beys could never build, and who used it to destroy what remained of them.

So the fairest summary is that the most famous battle in modern Egyptian history was militarily one-sided, strategically pointless for the side that won it, and politically transformative for the country it was fought in. It settled who governed Egypt and it settled nothing about the war. That combination is rare enough to be worth remembering, and it is a far better lesson than forty centuries looking down on anyone.

## Using this for study and revision

The engagement is easy to memorize and easy to get wrong, so the useful approach is to fix four things and reason from them rather than to learn a narrative.

Fix the date and place: 21 July 1798, at Embabeh on the west bank of the Nile opposite Cairo, roughly nine miles north of the Giza pyramids. Fix the mechanism: five French divisional squares with artillery at the angles, cavalry charges that could not break them, and an assault on the entrenched camp that ended in a rout into the flooded river. Fix the asymmetry: no mobile field artillery, no trained infantry, and no unified command on the Egyptian side, and the structural reason for each. Fix the sequence that follows: Cairo on 24 July, the destruction of the French fleet on 1 August, and the occupation running to 1801.

From those four, every examination question about this battle can be answered, including the ones that ask you to assess significance, because significance here is a matter of separating the tactical result from the strategic one and of connecting the political vacuum to what came after.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What happened at the Battle of the Pyramids?

On the afternoon of 21 July 1798, a French army of roughly twenty to twenty five thousand men formed five large divisional squares with artillery at the angles and met an attack by several thousand Mamluk heavy cavalry under Murad Bey on the west bank of the Nile at Embabeh, opposite Cairo. The charges could not break the formations and were destroyed by massed musketry and canister at close range. While that was happening, the French divisions on the left assaulted the entrenched camp at Embabeh, held by Cairo janissaries and village levies, and broke it. The defenders were driven back against a Nile in flood and large numbers drowned. Murad fled south with what cavalry he could gather, Ibrahim Bey withdrew east from the far bank, and the French entered Cairo three days later.

### Q: What year was the Battle of the Pyramids?

It was fought on 21 July 1798, three weeks after the French landing near Alexandria on 1 July. The chronology around it is worth fixing because the events came in quick succession. A smaller preliminary action had been fought at Shubra Khit on 13 July, where the same tactics produced the same result on a smaller scale. Cairo was occupied on 24 July, three days after the battle. Eleven days after that, on 1 and 2 August 1798, Nelson destroyed the French fleet in Aboukir Bay, which stranded the army that had just conquered the country. The French occupation that this battle opened ran until the capitulation of Alexandria on 2 September 1801.

### Q: Who fought at the Battle of the Pyramids?

On one side, the French Army of the Orient under Napoleon Bonaparte, roughly twenty to twenty five thousand men in five divisions, most of them veterans of the Italian campaigns, commanded by Desaix, Reynier, Dugua, Bon, and Vial, two of them standing in for Kleber and Menou who had been wounded earlier in the campaign. On the other, the forces of Murad Bey, one of the two Mamluk grandees who had governed Egypt for two decades: around six thousand Mamluk heavy cavalry, a much larger body of untrained infantry in the Embabeh earthworks drawn from the Cairo garrison regiments and levied villagers, and bedouin horsemen who watched from the flanks and did not commit. Ibrahim Bey, Murad's partner, was on the east bank with a second force and never crossed.

### Q: How did the French win the Battle of the Pyramids?

By forming squares and holding them. Each division formed a large hollow oblong with infantry facing outward on every side, grenadiers and field guns at the angles, and the baggage, wounded, and scientific commission inside. Cavalry cannot break that formation, because horses will not run into levelled bayonets and riders who pull up in front of it are stationary targets for volleys at thirty yards and canister from the corner guns. The charges came in, failed, reformed, and failed again. Meanwhile the divisions on the French left assaulted the fortified camp at Embabeh from an angle its fixed guns could not cover, broke defenders who had no training and no retreat except a flooded river, and finished the day.

### Q: Was the battle actually near the pyramids?

No. The fighting took place at Embabeh, also written Imbaba, a village on the west bank of the Nile directly across the river from the northern quarters of Cairo. The Giza pyramids stand on the desert edge roughly nine miles, about fifteen kilometers, to the south southwest. The monuments were visible on the horizon from the battlefield, as structures that size are visible from a great distance across flat flood plain, but no fighting occurred at them or anywhere near them. The ground was irrigated farmland cut by canals and embankments, not desert. Napoleon chose the name because an engagement named after the pyramids reads far better in a Paris dispatch than an engagement named after a village. Egyptian sources, including al-Jabarti, call it the affair at Imbaba.

### Q: How did the French cannons beat the Mamluk cavalry?

Through canister at close range, fired from guns positioned at the angles of the squares. Canister is essentially a tin canister packed with musket balls that bursts on leaving the muzzle, turning a field piece into an enormous shotgun effective out to a few hundred yards. Against a mass of horsemen approaching across open ground it is devastating, because cavalry cannot disperse enough to reduce the effect and cannot close fast enough to escape it. The guns also enfiladed the faces of the squares, sweeping along the front where riders bunched up after failing to break in. What made this possible was mobility: French guns were on carriages with teams and trained crews and went wherever the infantry went, while the roughly forty Egyptian guns sat fixed in the Embabeh earthworks and could not be brought to the fight.

### Q: What did Napoleon say at the Battle of the Pyramids?

He is traditionally reported to have told his soldiers that from the summit of these pyramids forty centuries looked down upon them. The line should be quoted with an attribution rather than as established fact. It comes principally from the memoirs of Bourrienne, his secretary, written long afterward by a man whose reliability is contested on several points, and other participant accounts do not record it. There is also a practical difficulty: the pyramids were distant shapes on the horizon, the army was deploying across a wide front under the eye of enemy cavalry, and no address could have reached more than the officers near him. The sentiment fits his rhetorical style entirely, so he may well have said something of the sort, but the polished sentence is very likely a later literary product.

### Q: How did the Battle of the Pyramids change Egypt?

It destroyed the political order that had governed the country since the Ottoman conquest of 1517. The Mamluk bey households lost a large share of their trained cavalry in a single afternoon, and trained cavalry took a decade and a fortune to replace. They lost their tax farms, which the French seized, and with them the revenue that funded new recruits. Most importantly they lost the presumption of invincibility, in public, in front of a Cairo that promptly looted their houses. They returned after 1801 as one faction among several rather than as the strongest force in the country, and Muhammad Ali destroyed what remained of them in 1811. The battle also opened three years of European occupation whose consequences reached far beyond the military outcome.

### Q: What was Embabeh and why did it matter?

Embabeh was a village on the west bank of the Nile opposite the northern part of Cairo, and Murad Bey had fortified it as the anchor of his position. The earthworks mounted somewhere around forty guns and held a large body of infantry drawn from the Cairo garrison regiments and levied villagers. It mattered for two reasons. First, its guns were fixed in emplacements facing an expected line of approach and could not be moved to support the cavalry fighting out on the plain, which made most of Murad's artillery irrelevant to the decisive action. Second, it had the Nile in flood directly behind it, so when the French divisions of Bon and Vial assaulted, the defenders had nowhere to retreat and most of the day's deaths occurred in the water.

### Q: How many horsemen did the Mamluks bring?

The conventional figure is around six thousand Mamluk cavalry, and it should be treated as an estimate rather than a count. Figures from three thousand to ten thousand appear in different accounts. The problem is that every number in circulation derives from French observers looking across open ground at a mass of horsemen, which is a notoriously unreliable way to count, and Mamluk forces kept no muster rolls that survive. Behind the cavalry stood a much larger body of infantry in the Embabeh works, usually estimated at fifteen to twenty thousand and quite possibly fewer. Ibrahim Bey held a further force on the east bank that never engaged. The honest approach is to give ranges and note that the outcome would have been identical at twice the strength.

### Q: Who commanded the Mamluk forces that day?

Murad Bey commanded on the west bank where the fighting happened. He was one of the two grandees of the duumvirate that had effectively governed Egypt since the later 1770s, a Caucasian mamluk of Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab, and he held the military side of the partnership: the cavalry, the campaigns in Upper Egypt, and relations with the bedouin confederations. His partner Ibrahim Bey, who held the civil and fiscal side and the informal leadership of the country, was on the east bank with a second force, the Ottoman governor, and much of the Cairo establishment, and could not cross a Nile in flood to intervene. Splitting the two forces across a rising river was the day's largest command error, and it meant half the Egyptian strength watched the destruction of the other half from the opposite shore.

### Q: What was the divisional square formation?

A closed formation in which an entire division, several thousand men, formed a large hollow oblong facing outward on all four sides, with grenadier companies and field guns at the angles and the baggage, draft animals, wounded, and civilian scientific commission placed inside. It has no flank and no rear, which is what makes it immune to cavalry, and it is slow and almost incapable of offensive movement, which is its price. Forming one quickly from column takes serious drill, holding it under a charge takes discipline and trust in the officers, and closing the gaps as men fall takes veteran steadiness. The French had all three because the Revolutionary armies had spent six years learning against European professionals. This was standard doctrine of the period rather than an invention for the occasion.

### Q: What happened to Murad Bey afterwards?

He rode south with perhaps three thousand horsemen and became the hardest military problem the French faced inside Egypt. Napoleon sent Desaix after him with a division in late August 1798, and the pursuit ran the length of Upper Egypt to the First Cataract and beyond over the better part of a year. Murad lost every engagement he accepted, beginning at Sediman in October 1798 where the square again broke his charge, and each time he withdrew, gathered fresh men from tribal allies and surviving households, and reappeared elsewhere. The French could not catch him because they had almost no cavalry. This harassment campaign was the war he should have fought from the start. He eventually came to terms, governed part of Upper Egypt on French sufferance, and died of plague in 1801.

### Q: Did the Mamluks have artillery of their own?

Yes, and it was almost useless to them. The Embabeh entrenchment mounted somewhere in the region of forty guns, though the figure varies across the sources. They were static pieces in fixed emplacements, poorly served, sited to cover an expected line of approach, and incapable of being limbered up, moved, and repositioned during the action. Artillery is the one thing that breaks an infantry square, because round shot cuts lanes through packed ranks and canister sweeps the face, and the standard European answer to a square was to blow gaps with guns and then send cavalry into the gaps. Murad could not perform that sequence because his guns could not reach the squares out on the plain. The absence was structural: mobile field artillery requires a central treasury, foundries, crews, and drivers, none of which the household system produced.

### Q: Why did the Mamluks charge instead of waiting?

Because the political logic of a bey's position forbade anything else. Murad's standing among the other households rested on armed force and on being seen to protect Cairo, his clients, and his tax farms, and abandoning the capital without a battle would have destroyed his authority as surely as losing one. There was also a tactical misreading. At Shubra Khit eight days earlier the same squares had broken the same cavalry, and the conclusion drawn was that the charge had not been pressed home with enough weight, which was a reasonable inference from two and a half centuries of fighting opponents who fought the same way. Refusing battle and waging a harassment campaign was the only course with a real chance, and it was the one he adopted afterward in Upper Egypt with considerable success.

### Q: What happened at Shubra Khit before it?

On 13 July 1798 Murad attacked the French column as it moved south along the Nile, supported by a flotilla of armed river craft. Napoleon formed his divisions into large squares with guns at the corners and everything vulnerable in the middle, the cavalry charged, failed to break in, was shot at close range, and withdrew. The river fight was sharper and went badly for the French at one point until a chance shot blew up the Mamluk flagship. French losses were negligible and Mamluk losses were modest. Materially it settled nothing. Informationally it settled everything, and only one side read the information correctly: the French learned the formation worked, while the Mamluk command concluded the charge needed more weight and repeated it at greater scale eight days later.

### Q: When did the French enter Cairo?

On 24 July 1798, three days after the battle, effectively unopposed. The city they took was one of the largest in the world and had no government. The beys had fled in both directions, the Ottoman governor had gone east with Ibrahim Bey, the janissary officers had scattered, and authority had fallen to the shaykhs of al-Azhar and the merchant notables, who had spent two days trying to keep order in a city that had already begun looting the abandoned properties of its own ruling class. A delegation met the French and arranged terms. Napoleon installed himself, created a council of notables with consultative powers only, and began the administration that ran until 1801. The speed is the point: from the destruction of the field army to the occupation of the capital took seventy two hours.

### Q: Is the famous painting of the battle accurate?

No, and the best known images are the main reason the false picture persists. They date from a decade or more after the event, were produced under an imperial regime with a direct interest in the legend, and were painted by artists who had not been there. They show desert instead of irrigated flood plain cut by canals, place the pyramids close enough to loom over the fighting when they were about nine miles away on the horizon, render the Mamluks as exotic and picturesque, and centre the composition on the commander. They are fine paintings and they are documents about imperial self-presentation rather than records of the day. The more reliable visual evidence comes from the expedition's own draftsmen, whose surveys of the terrain around Cairo were made for accuracy rather than for a salon.

### Q: How many French soldiers were killed?

The French returns give roughly twenty nine killed and something over two hundred wounded, with the total usually rounded to under three hundred casualties of every kind. These are self-reported figures published for political effect, so some understatement is plausible, but even doubling or tripling them leaves the loss trivial relative to the result and no source on any side suggests the French suffered seriously. The reason is that the formation never broke. Almost all casualties in a cavalry-versus-infantry action occur after a square gives way and horsemen get in among men who have lost their order, and that moment never came. Egyptian losses are far harder to establish, are usually given between three thousand and six thousand, and rest entirely on French estimates, since most of the dead drowned and nobody counted.

### Q: What happened to Ibrahim Bey on the far bank?

He watched. Ibrahim Bey held the east bank with a second force, the Ottoman governor, and much of the Cairo establishment, and the Nile at the height of its annual rise made crossing impossible once the action began. He could see the destruction of the army he could not reach. When it was over he withdrew eastward across Sinai toward Syria, taking the governor with him, and spent the occupation outside Egypt. He returned after the French left in 1801 into a country that no longer had room for the old arrangement, and he was among the household leaders whom Muhammad Ali eventually dismantled. The decision to divide the two commands across a flooded river cost the Egyptian side perhaps half its potential strength before a shot was fired.
