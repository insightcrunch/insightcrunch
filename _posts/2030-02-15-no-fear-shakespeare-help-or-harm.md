No Fear Shakespeare, the SparkNotes series that prints Romeo and Juliet with a complete modern English paraphrase on the page facing the original, is neither the rescue its publisher promises nor the cheat its detractors describe. It is a contract. Used under one set of terms, as a scaffold erected against a hard text and then deliberately taken down, the aid helps: readers who would otherwise abandon the play in its first scene instead finish it, follow its argument, and arrive at the original lines with the plot already in hand. Used under the opposite terms, as a permanent substitute that a student reads while the verse sits unread across the gutter, the aid harms: the student completes the assignment, passes the quiz, and never once performs the act the play exists to demand, which is the reading of Shakespeare's words. The verdict of this article is therefore a grading, not a slogan. The No Fear volume is acquitted of the charge of cheating, because the book never hides what it is and never removes the original. It is convicted of a lesser and more precise offence: its design makes the harmful use easier than the helpful one, and any teacher, parent, or solitary reader who hands it over without terms has accepted the publisher's marketing in place of a pedagogy. Help or harm is decided at the moment of use, and the evidence assembled here, from reading research, from a classic of literary criticism, from national test data, and from the paraphrase's own pages, shows exactly which uses fall on which side of the line.

![A student comparing the original text and the modern paraphrase in a No Fear Shakespeare volume of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The debate matters because this play is where the question bites hardest. Romeo and Juliet is the tragedy most students in the English-speaking school systems meet first, set for early secondary classes whose readers are the least equipped for early modern syntax and the most likely to be handed a rescue device. A study aid attached to a later, harder play would reach older readers with more defences. Attached to this one, the volume lands on desks at the precise point where a lifelong relation to Shakespeare is either founded or foreclosed. If the first encounter teaches a student that the verse is an obstacle to be routed around, the routing habit is what gets learned. If the first encounter teaches that the verse yields to effort, with the paraphrase standing by as a checked and temporary support, the effort habit is what gets learned. Everything in the argument that follows is a working out of that single fork.

## What is No Fear Shakespeare? The book on the desk

The product record comes first, because much of the public argument about this aid proceeds in ignorance of what the object actually is. No Fear Shakespeare is a series of editions published by SparkNotes, the study-guide company founded in 1999 by four Harvard students, Sam Yagan, Max Krohn, Chris Coyne, and Eli Bolotin, and acquired by the bookseller Barnes & Noble in 2001. The Romeo and Juliet volume carries a 2003 copyright in the name of Spark Publishing, identified on its copyright page as a division of Barnes & Noble, with a New York address on Fifth Avenue. The Library of Congress catalogued the volume in 2003 under the control number 2003004307, with the paperback ISBN 1-5866-3845-9 and a hardcover issue at 1-4114-0044-3. The edition is credited to an editor, John Crowther, who prepared several volumes in the series. These are the unglamorous facts of a catalogue record, and they already correct two common errors. The volume is not an anonymous internet printout; it is a conventionally published, conventionally edited book. And it is not a summary with a famous name attached; the Library of Congress summary describes it as presenting the original text of the play side by side with a modern version, with marginal notes and explanations and descriptions of each character.

The scale of the enterprise was visible from the start. The front matter of the Romeo and Juliet volume lists eighteen companion volumes available in the series, covering eighteen plays from As You Like It to Twelfth Night, together with the Sonnets. The parent company's print programme was equally deliberate. Library Journal reported in April 2002 that SparkNotes would put some 150 of its study guides into bookshops that July, priced at 4.95 dollars to sit directly beside the established competition, with distribution handled by Sterling Publishing, the house Barnes & Noble itself acquired in 2003. Publishers Weekly reported in February 2004 that the chain had gone further, clearing rival study guides from its own stores so that SparkNotes stood as the house brand, and the same report described No Fear Shakespeare as a line of page-by-page translations into modern English. The aid, in other words, was not a cottage product that drifted into classrooms. It was a vertically integrated one: written by a publisher the retailer owned, printed under the retailer's imprint, and shelved in the retailer's shops with the competitors removed. The business design behind that arrangement belongs to the history of the study-guide trade; what matters for the verdict here is the classroom effect, and the effect begins with the physical page.

### Is the No Fear Romeo and Juliet the whole play, or an abridgement?

It is the whole play. The volume prints the complete text of Romeo and Juliet, from the Chorus's fourteen-line Prologue through the Prince's closing couplet, and sets a modern English rendering against every passage of it. Nothing is cut to make the paraphrase fit, and nothing in the original is modernised, silently corrected, or replaced.

Abridgement is a different and older trade, the trade of the shortened school text, and this book does not practise it. A reader who works through the left-hand pages alone has read the entire tragedy, in a text that follows the standard modern editions in substance. The paraphrase on the facing pages is additional matter, not substituted matter, and that distinction between addition and substitution carries the whole moral weight of the case examined here. A book that adds a crutch beside a difficult text invites one judgement; a book that quietly swaps the difficult text for an easy one invites another. No Fear Shakespeare is the first kind of book, whatever its critics sometimes say, and its failures, where they occur, are failures of use built on top of an honest design.

The volume's honesty about itself deserves a paragraph, because it bears directly on the cheating charge. The book announces its method on its cover, in its title, and in an introduction that states the transaction in plain terms. The introduction opens beneath an epigraph taken from Hamlet, "There's matter in these sighs, these profound heaves. You must translate: 'tis fit we understand them" (Hamlet, 4.1.1-2), a line the editors borrow to license the whole enterprise from within the canon itself. The prose that follows speaks directly to the stranded reader: "Have you ever found yourself looking at a Shakespeare play, then down at the footnotes, then back at the play, and still not understanding?" The promise is then made without hedging. "Soon you'll be reading Shakespeare's own words fearlessly, and actually enjoying it," the introduction pledges, and it describes the mechanism exactly: the series "puts Shakespeare's language side-by-side with a facing-page translation into modern English, the kind of English people actually speak today," so that "when Shakespeare's words make your head spin, our translation will help you sort out what's happening, who's saying what, and why." Whatever else is said about this book, its intentions are not concealed. It does not pretend to be a scholarly edition. It does not smuggle the paraphrase in as the text. It stands in the open and makes its offer, and the offer is the thing to be judged.

## Two pages, one contract: how the No Fear format is meant to be read

Open the volume anywhere and the architecture is identical. The original text occupies the left-hand page, set as verse and prose in the traditional manner, with its act and scene headings, its speech prefixes, and its stage directions. The modern rendering occupies the right-hand page, set as continuous prose under the same speech headings, passage by passage, so that an eye travelling across the gutter moves from a block of Shakespeare to the same block restated. The original is never out of sight. That single design decision separates this aid from every summary, crib, and plot digest in the study-guide trade, and it is the decision on which the defence of the book ultimately rests. A summary takes the reader away from the text and keeps the reader away; the facing page keeps the text in the same field of vision as its explanation, one saccade away. The design assumes a reader who shuttles. It cannot compel the shuttle, and therein lies the whole difficulty, but the design at least holds the door open in both directions.

### Which page carries the original, and which carries the paraphrase?

The original stands on the left-hand page and the paraphrase on the right-hand page, throughout the volume, in print and in the free online edition that SparkNotes maintains on its site. The order is not a triviality. In a left-to-right reading culture, the first page encountered is the position of authority, and the volume gives that position to Shakespeare.

The paraphrase is by design the second text, the one consulted after. Publishers of bilingual editions have made the same allocation for centuries, placing the original in the place of honour and the translation in the place of service, and the No Fear volume inherits the convention even if its marketing inverts the emphasis by selling the service rather than the honour. The online edition reproduces the arrangement as paired columns under the headings Original Text and Modern Text, with the original again first. A critic who describes the book as replacing Shakespeare with a paraphrase is describing a book that does not exist; the physical object keeps the replacement permanently adjacent to, and visibly secondary to, the thing replaced.

The scholarly world's own standard classroom edition supplies the comparison that disciplines this whole debate, because the Folger Shakespeare Library editions, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine and published from 1992 onward, also build their books around a facing page. In a Folger volume the play stands on the right-hand page and the facing left-hand page carries full explanatory notes, scene summaries, and illustrations of objects and words drawn from the Folger's collections. The Folger's own description of the series states that each volume contains full explanatory notes on the page facing the text they explain, and the library presents the editions as designed to make the plays available to all readers. Facing-page help, in other words, is not a study-guide vulgarity. It is the format the most respected teaching edition in the Anglophone world chose for itself, adopted by scholars, and placed in more school classrooms than any rival. The difference between the two books is therefore not the architecture but the cargo. The Folger's facing page glosses: it explains a word, unpacks an allusion, identifies a custom, and then stops, leaving the sentence itself in Shakespeare's keeping. The No Fear facing page translates: it restates the whole sentence in modern prose and leaves nothing in the sentence for the reader to do. A gloss is a ladder rung; a paraphrase is an escalator. Both devices move a reader upward, and the educational question, which the research sections below take up, is what each device does to the muscles of the person being moved.

That distinction, gloss against paraphrase, is the hinge of the verdict, and it should be stated with precision before the evidence is weighed. A gloss intervenes at the point of blockage and nowhere else. It assumes the reader can construe early modern syntax once individual words are unlocked, and for a great many lines of this play that assumption holds: "wherefore" is a single word, and unlocking it unlocks Juliet's whole speech. A paraphrase intervenes everywhere at once. It assumes the reader cannot be trusted with any part of the sentence, and it therefore performs the entire act of reading on the reader's behalf: the vocabulary, the syntax, the word order, the idiom, the tone, and the decisions about what a line means where a line can mean two things. The gloss leaves the reader in possession of a text partly understood and partly still to conquer. The paraphrase leaves the reader in possession of a story fully understood and a text not yet begun. Whether that trade is help or harm depends on what happens next, on whether the story so gained becomes a platform for returning to the text or a reason never to return. The aid itself is neutral between those futures. The contract under which it is used is not.

## Inside the modern page: what the paraphrase does to five passages

General claims about paraphrase, whether generous or hostile, dissolve on contact with the actual pages. The No Fear rendering of Romeo and Juliet is a specific piece of work, careful in some places, blunt in others, occasionally resourceful, occasionally tone-deaf, and it deserves to be inspected rather than characterised. Five passages, spread across the play's registers, show its habits more honestly than any testimonial.

The Prologue shows the first habit: the resolution of ambiguity in the direction of plot. Shakespeare's Chorus compresses the entire tragedy into a sonnet of loaded phrases, among them the statement that "a pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, line 6). The phrase is double-jointed. On its surface it says that the lovers are born, that they take their life, meaning their existence, from the loins of their enemies; underneath, it carries the sense every audience soon learns, that they take their own lives. The sonnet holds both meanings in suspension and lets the second dawn as the play proceeds. The modern page of the No Fear volume cannot hold a suspension, because its job is to state. It renders the passage as a narrative digest in which "two unlucky children of these enemy families become lovers and commit suicide," and the doom is announced as fact in the second sentence. "Star-cross'd," a term of astrological machinery that locates the lovers' ruin in the configuration of the heavens, comes out as "unlucky," a term of weather. The rendering is not wrong about the plot; the lovers do commit suicide, and they are unlucky. It is wrong about the experience, because it converts a phrase designed to be understood fully only in retrospect into information delivered in advance. A student who reads the modern Prologue first has been handed the answer to a question the sonnet was written to make the audience carry. Nothing in that exchange is an error, and everything in it is a loss.

The opening scene shows a second habit: the paraphrase translates words faithfully and wordplay not at all, because wordplay cannot survive the crossing. Sampson and Gregory enter trading a chain of puns on coals, colliers, choler, and collar (1.1.1-8), a verbal fencing match in which each term slides into the next and the servants' bravado is established as verbal before it becomes physical. The chain depends on the sounds of early modern English, and no modern prose can rebuild it, since the modern words for coal-carriers and anger and neckbands no longer rhyme with each other. The No Fear rendering does the only thing available: it gives the sense and drops the music, producing "Gregory, I swear, we can't let them humiliate us. We won't take their garbage," and Gregory's reply, "No, because then we'd be garbagemen." The exchange is intelligible, mildly funny, and entirely different in kind from its original, which is a display of wit between two men whose trade is words before it is swords. The volume knows what has happened and confesses it in the margin of the modern page, where an explanatory aside informs the reader that Gregory continually implies that Sampson is not as tough as he is acting. The confession is honest and the failure is structural. Where Shakespeare characterises through a game the audience watches, the paraphrase reports the result of the game in a footnote's voice. The student reading only the right-hand page meets two servants who boast; the student reading the left-hand page meets two servants whose boasting is itself a performance of intelligence. The play's first lesson, that in Verona language is the arena in which all the other fighting is rehearsed, is available on one page and absent on the other.

The same scene contains the evidence against a lazy assumption about this book, the assumption that a school-marketed aid must sanitise. Shakespeare's servants descend within fifty lines into sexual menace, Sampson vowing to cut off the maids' heads and clarifying, under Gregory's prompting, that he means their maidenheads (1.1). School editions of earlier generations handled such passages by silence or by asterisks. The No Fear modern page handles the passage by translation into unmistakable terms, glossing "maidenhead" in the margin as virginity and rendering Sampson's closing boast with the word "erection" where Shakespeare wrote "stand." The paraphrase, that is, refuses to protect its reader from the play. It makes the bawdy harder to miss than the original does, since a modern student can skate over "maidenheads" without comprehension but cannot skate over the modern page's explicitness. Critics who file this series under bowdlerisation, as the teacher Jeannette DeCelles-Zwerneman does in the advice examined below, have the product wrong on a point of fact. The volume's fault is not prudery. Its fault lies in the opposite direction: it trusts no implication to survive, and so it states outright things Shakespeare left for the audience to catch, converting the pleasure of catching them into the passivity of being told.

### Does the paraphrase ever explain a joke instead of translating it?

Repeatedly; the habit is most visible in the volume's margins, where the modern page breaks off from restating dialogue to annotate it. In the first scene, as the colliers pun collapses past rescue, the modern text pauses to note that Gregory is implying Sampson is less brave than his talk, information the original delivers through the exchange itself.

Elsewhere in the scene the margin supplies a blunt gloss on the servants' sexual joking, explaining that the two men are joking about Sampson's body. These asides are the volume's most revealing feature, because they show the editors discovering in practice the limit of their own method. A line-by-line restatement can carry plot, statement, and insult across the centuries, but it cannot carry the dramatic fact that a line is a feint, a trap, or a performance for an onstage listener, and at those points the book stops translating and starts lecturing. The lectures are accurate. They are also an admission that the play's meaning lives partly in things no paraphrase can hold, a point the volume's defenders rarely quote and its critics rarely credit it with conceding.

The balcony scene shows the paraphrase at its best, and the best is genuinely useful. Juliet's "O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33) is the most persistently misread line in the play, taken by generations of first-time readers as a call across a garden to a man whose location she is asking after. The misconception is so durable that it has become a set piece of the play's classroom afterlife, the first entry in every catalogue of student errors, and the correction of it is a small rite of passage in the teaching of the play. The No Fear rendering dissolves the error in a single stroke: "Oh, Romeo, Romeo, why do you have to be Romeo? Forget about your father and change your name. Or else, if you won't change your name, just swear you love me and I'll stop being a Capulet." A student who glances right at this point cannot retain the wrong reading, because the modern page has already construed the sentence correctly and completely, motive included. The same service is performed a few lines earlier on Romeo's aside, "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" (2.2.1), which the modern page gives as "It's easy for someone to joke about scars if they've never been cut," a rendering that preserves the proverb's shape while removing the archaic verb that blocks it. These are the paraphrase's clean victories, and they should be conceded without grudging. For the reader whose alternative is not a glossed edition but abandonment, the right-hand page at 2.2 is the difference between meeting Juliet's argument and missing it entirely.

Yet the same passage shows the tariff. Juliet's speech is an argument conducted in the form of an enchantment, and its power in performance depends on the audience hearing a mind think in balanced, incantatory periods: the triple repetition of the name, the conditional structure that offers Romeo two doors and finds both locked, the slide into the rose meditation in which a metaphysical claim about names and essences is made by a girl on a balcony at night. The modern page preserves the argument and cancels the enchantment. "Why do you have to be Romeo?" is a complaint, reasonable and slightly peevish, where "wherefore art thou Romeo" is a spell spoken to the dark by someone who does not know she is overheard, and the difference is not decorative. Romeo is hidden below, listening; the audience is listening; the verse is the sound of a consciousness that has not yet adjusted itself for a listener, and the prose, by making everything plain, makes it social. A student can pass any comprehension test set on the modern page's version of this scene. What the student cannot get from that page is why the scene is famous, why actors and audiences have treasured it for four centuries, why it is the scene the culture chose as the emblem of young love. Fame of that order is not produced by arguments, however elegant. It is produced by language doing things prose cannot do, and a reader who has only ever received the argument has been given the scene's skeleton with the spell removed, and has no way of knowing anything is missing.

The death of Mercutio shows the tariff collected on the play's most famous pun. Dying, Mercutio keeps his register to the end: "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1). The line is a hinge with two leaves. Grave means solemn, and grave means burial place, and Mercutio means both at once, announcing his death in the form of a joke about his temperament, so that the laughter of the moment curdles in the hearer's ear as the sentence completes itself. It is the character in a single line: wit operating at full stretch on the edge of extinction. The modern page must choose, and it chooses the plot: "Ask for me tomorrow, and you'll find me in a grave." The sentence is clear, correct about the outcome, and emptied of the event, because the event was the doubleness. The same choice recurs a breath earlier, where the curse "A plague o' both your houses" becomes "May a plague curse both your families," houses flattened into families and the malediction's archaic violence softened into a wish. And it recurs in miniature when Mercutio's "Go, villain, fetch a surgeon" emerges as "Go, boy. Get me a doctor," intelligible, serviceable, and stripped of the contempt in "villain" and the period texture of "surgeon." No single one of these renderings is a scandal. Their accumulation is the point. Passage by passage, the modern page keeps faith with everything in the play that can be stated and parts company with everything that can only be performed, until a reader of the right-hand pages alone has absorbed a tragedy whose facts are all present and whose greatness is all absent, and who may reasonably conclude that the reputation of the work is a mystery or a fraud.

The register problem completes the inspection, and the trade press documented its purest instance outside this play. Publishers Weekly, describing the series in February 2004, offered as a specimen of the method the Tempest rendering in which "What a blow was there given" becomes "Ouch, what a comeback." The example was quoted by the magazine without hostility, as an illustration of the line's approach, and it deserves to be weighed as the method's reductio. A paraphrase committed to the publisher's formula, the kind of English people actually speak, will repeatedly trade the original's elevation for demotic equivalents, because elevation is precisely the quality modern conversational prose does not carry. Sometimes the trade is harmless, as when servants and clowns speak, since their originals are demotic already. Sometimes it reverses the meaning of a moment, because in this play register is information: the audience measures the distance between the servants' prose bawdry, Mercutio's fireworks, the lovers' shared sonnet at their first meeting, and the Prince's legal verse, and the measurements are the sociology of Verona rendered audible. Flatten the registers into one conversational level and the sociology goes silent. The modern page of the Romeo and Juliet volume is more careful than the Tempest specimen suggests; its lovers are allowed a measure of formality and its clowns a measure of slang. But the direction of travel is set by the method, and the method's destination is a play in which everyone sounds like the same helpful narrator. Against that destination the verdict must be entered in advance: a rendering that equalises all voices has not translated the play, however accurate its sentences, because the play is, in large part, the differences among its voices.

## The lineage of the rescue: what earlier aids did differently

The facing-page paraphrase feels like an internet-era invention, and the feeling is wrong. The devices for rescuing readers from this playwright form a lineage two centuries long, and placing the No Fear volume in it clarifies exactly what is and is not new in its design. The lineage's founding document is Charles and Mary Lamb's Tales from Shakespeare, published in 1807, which retold twenty plays as prose stories for young readers. Mary Lamb's preface states the contract with unusual honesty: the Tales are offered as an introduction to the study of Shakespeare, with his words used wherever they could be brought in, and the expectation is declared in advance that the child who begins with the stories will graduate to the plays themselves. The Lambs' book is a substitute by design, a replacement text for readers judged too young for the original, and it says so. Its defence is its candour about the sequence: story first, play after, the tale as a porch built onto a house the reader is expected eventually to enter. The same era produced the lineage's cautionary branch, the expurgated Family Shakespeare associated with Thomas Bowdler, which kept the plays' words but removed whatever the editors judged unfit for family reading, and gave English the verb to bowdlerise. Between them the two books define the twin temptations of every later aid: replacement, which changes the object, and expurgation, which changes the evidence. The No Fear volume commits neither offence in form. It replaces nothing, since the complete original stands on its left-hand pages, and it expurgates nothing, since its modern page renders the servants' bawdy more explicitly than the Lambs or the Bowdlers would have printed at all.

The twentieth century commercialised the porch. CliffsNotes, the American study-guide line founded by Clifton Hillegass in a Lincoln, Nebraska basement in 1958, began its life with sixteen Shakespeare titles adapted from a Canadian series, and built an empire on the plot summary and the critical essay in pamphlet form. The guides carried their own stated contract on the inside cover: "A thorough appreciation of literature allows no short cuts." The sentence is the whole history of the trade in one line of type, a publisher simultaneously selling the shortcut and disclaiming it, insisting that the guide promotes reading of the original while its critics insisted the guide existed to bypass that reading. Hillegass's successors eventually produced a side-by-side format of their own, the CliffsComplete line, which set the complete text of a play beside a running glossary, so that by the time SparkNotes launched No Fear Shakespeare the facing-page principle was contested commercial ground rather than a novelty. What No Fear added to the ground was the grain of the help. The CliffsComplete page glosses words and phrases and leaves the sentence standing; the Folger page annotates and leaves the sentence standing; the SparkNotes page replaces the sentence with a modern twin and leaves only the comparison standing. The innovation is real and it is precisely the innovation Brooks's argument targets, because grain is destiny in these matters. Help delivered word by word trains the reader in the words. Help delivered sentence by sentence trains the reader in the plot. A lineage that begins with the Lambs' honestly labelled doorway thus ends, two centuries on, with a doorway so well built that generations of readers have moved into it and lived there, mistaking the porch for the house because the porch, alone among porches, contains a complete retelling of everything inside.

That last fact deserves emphasis, because it distinguishes the No Fear problem from every earlier version of the aid problem and explains why the old arguments fit it badly. The Lambs' reader who never graduated still possessed a story, and knew it was a story, because the Tales announce themselves as tales. The CliffsNotes reader who never graduated possessed a summary, and knew it, because the pamphlet's compression is visible on its face. The No Fear reader who never graduates possesses something more dangerous: a complete, line-matched, cover-bearing edition of the play, indistinguishable in the hand and on the shelf from the real thing, whose unread half is the play itself. The substitution leaves no visible residue. A bookshelf, a book bag, a completed reading log all testify to an encounter that half happened. Earlier aids failed honestly, in the open, as abridgements and digests always had. This aid can fail invisibly, inside the covers of the complete works, and the invisibility is why the debate about it generates more heat than the debate about any summary: the evidence of harm dissolves into the evidence of study, and only the student's actual competence, probed at the level of the passage, can tell the two apart.

## The access case: what reading research actually shows

The case for the aid, made at its strongest, is an empirical case, and it deserves to be stated in the form its evidence actually takes rather than in the form of publisher's rhetoric. The rhetoric is familiar: the introduction promises that the stranded reader will soon be reading Shakespeare's own words fearlessly, and the brief for this very article describes the series as hugely popular. The popularity claim is worth grading before the learning claim, because the two are often run together. No audited sales figure for the No Fear series has been published by its owner, and none is cited here; what the record supports is distribution, not numbers. The record shows a series launched in 2003 with eighteen volumes in print at first issue, placed by its owner-retailer as an exclusive house brand with rival guides removed from the shelves, distributed to bookshops nationally at a student's price, extended by a companion volume in 2007, and given away in full on the publisher's website, where the entire paraphrase of this play stands open to any reader with a browser. A product does not need invented sales figures to be called widespread; two decades of continuous availability through the largest bookstore chain in its home market, plus free universal access online, establish the reach. What reach establishes is exposure, not benefit. The benefit must be found in the research literature, and the literature, handled carefully, supports a narrower and more interesting claim than either camp usually makes.

The strongest relevant evidence comes from the study of glossed reading in second-language research, where the facing-page question has been tested under controlled conditions for decades. In a study published in The Modern Language Journal in 1996, Jan H. Hulstijn, Margriet Hollander, and Tine Greidanus assigned seventy-eight advanced Dutch university students of French to read a French short story under three conditions: with marginal glosses giving the meanings of difficult words, with dictionary access, or with no support. The students were then tested, without warning, on the story's vocabulary. Incidental learning of new words was measurably better in the glossed and dictionary conditions than in the unsupported one, and the frequency with which a word recurred in the story strengthened the effect where support existed. Hulstijn's related work from 1992 added the finding that matters most for the classroom debate: readers left to infer unknown words from context alone did not merely learn less; they guessed wrong more often, producing confident mistranslations that a gloss would have prevented. I. S. P. Nation, synthesising the field in Learning Vocabulary in Another Language in 2001, drew the practical conclusion that glosses are an efficient support for reading, cheap to provide and reliably better than unaided guessing, while insisting that the gains they produce are modest and need consolidation. The preference studies add the learner's side of the same transaction: readers consistently prefer glossed texts, in the research by Jacobs, Dufon, and Fong among others, as Nation's synthesis records, and preference is not a trivial datum in a school subject whose first casualty is the will to continue.

### What did the gloss experiments measure, and what did they leave unmeasured?

They measured vocabulary: whether readers retained the meanings of specific unfamiliar words after a single supported reading. The measurement was incidental, taken by surprise test after the reading ended. That is a real and relevant measurement, and its findings transfer to the No Fear page in one direction only.

A student blocked by "wherefore," "collier," or "maidenhead" is in the same position as Hulstijn's Dutch readers blocked by an unknown French word, and the evidence says the blocked student who is handed the meaning will retain more and misconstrue less than the blocked student left to guess. What the experiments did not measure is everything the Shakespeare debate is actually about. They did not test comprehension of an argument sustained over three thousand lines. They did not test whether supported readers later approached unsupported texts with more or less confidence or skill. They did not test anything resembling the experience of verse, because their subjects read prose stories in a language they were learning as adults. And they tested glosses, single words and short phrases in a margin, not continuous paraphrase, which is a different instrument: the gloss answers the question the reader has just formed, while the paraphrase answers every question in advance, including the ones the reader has not yet learned to ask. The honest summary of the access evidence is therefore a bounded one. Support at the point of difficulty demonstrably beats abandonment and beats guessing. The literature contains no experiment that crowns a full facing-page paraphrase as the best form of support, and none that follows paraphrase-fed readers to see whether the scaffold ever comes away. Anyone who claims the research proves the aid works, in the strong sense, is claiming more than the research contains; anyone who claims the research shows support is pointless is ignoring the one solid finding in the field, which is that unsupported struggling readers guess wrong and stop.

The practising teacher's version of the access case adds a population the experiments miss: the student for whom English itself is the second language. Genevieve White, writing for the British Council's teacher audience against the motion that Shakespeare should not be taught to learners of English, recommends the No Fear site by name as a classroom resource, proposing mix-and-match exercises in which a line of the original is paired with its modern rendering and students must join them. The recommendation is modest, practical, and aimed at the readers with the strongest claim on the aid: those carrying a double load, an unfamiliar language inside an archaic one. For such readers the paraphrase does not replace an attainable experience; it substitutes for an unattainable one, and the calculus of help and harm changes accordingly. The same logic extends, with decreasing force, to readers with dyslexia and other print difficulties, and to the simply underprepared: wherever the realistic alternative to the modern page is not the original unaided but the original abandoned, the aid's benefit is not in dispute. The dispute begins only where the alternative is genuine reading with lighter support, a glossed scholarly edition, a performed scene, a teacher's voice in the room. In that territory the access case must compete, and the competition is the subject of the next two sections.

## The poetry case: Brooks and the heresy of paraphrase

The case against the aid, made at its strongest, is not an empirical case at all, and its defenders lose ground whenever they pretend otherwise. It is a claim about what a poem is. Its classic statement predates the No Fear series by more than half a century and has nothing to do with study guides: the closing chapter of Cleanth Brooks's The Well Wrought Urn, published in 1947, titled "The Heresy of Paraphrase." Brooks, the most influential American critic of his generation and a founder of the New Criticism, argued that a poem's meaning is not a statement the poem contains, detachable from its wording like a nut from a shell. A true poem, in his formulation, is "an experience rather than any mere statement about experience or any mere abstraction from experience," and its structure is a structure of meanings, evaluations, and interpretations held in tension, not a logical outline waiting to be extracted. To paraphrase such a work is therefore not to simplify it but to commit a category error, a heresy: the restatement necessarily selects one strand of the tension, resolves the ironies and paradoxes into a single assertion, and discards the rest, while wearing the air of having delivered the whole. Brooks was writing about lyric poems and about the criticism of them, not about schoolchildren and cribs. His argument nonetheless lands on the facing page of the No Fear volume with undiminished force, because the volume's method is paraphrase industrialised: every line of a five-act verse drama passed through exactly the operation Brooks described, its tensions resolved one by one into single assertions, its doublings flattened into choices, its experience converted into statement at industrial scale.

The Brooks argument should be tested rather than merely brandished, and its own discipline has tested it. The philosopher Ernie Lepore, examining the heresy thesis, concluded that the strong form of it fails: a paraphrase can succeed in carrying a poem's semantic content, since a new expression can in principle mean what the old one meant. But Lepore's concession has a boundary that matters here, because he allowed that a poem's form and sound are not paraphrasable, and no one seriously maintains that its aesthetic effect survives the crossing. Alex Neill, writing in The Oxford Handbook of Aesthetics, pressed from the other side, arguing that paraphrase is a legitimate and often necessary instrument for talking about poems at all, and that nobody expects a paraphrase to contain a poem's exact meaning. Between them, the two philosophers mark the ground on which the dispute can be honestly settled, and the settlement is this. Brooks is wrong if his heresy is read as a ban on restatement: restatement is how readers check understanding, how teachers open doors, how the Prologue's plot reaches a beginner, and a pedagogy that forbade it would forbid teaching. Brooks is right in the claim his title actually makes, which is about substitution: the moment a paraphrase is treated as the poem, as the thing itself rather than as a report about the thing, the poem has been lost and a bulletin about it enthroned in its place. The heresy is not the paraphrase. The heresy is the equivalence. A student who reads the modern page to discover what the balcony scene says and then reads the balcony scene has committed no heresy. A student who reads the modern page and believes the balcony scene has been read has committed it in full, with the aid as accomplice. Brooks, who never saw a No Fear volume, thus supplies the exact legal theory the verdict requires: the offence is located not in the instrument but in the claim made for its product.

### Did Cleanth Brooks write about study guides?

No. Brooks wrote about poems, in 1947, decades before SparkNotes existed, and his chapter addresses critics who reduced poems to paraphrasable messages, not students reaching for help. His appearance in this debate is an application, not a quotation from the debate itself, and the distinction matters for honesty's sake.

What Brooks contributes is a definition of the loss, stated with a precision the education literature lacks: he explains why a reader who has absorbed a perfect paraphrase of Juliet's balcony speech may still have encountered nothing of what makes the speech an object of study. He does not and cannot contribute evidence about whether the aid, used as a scaffold, raises or lowers eventual attainment, because that is an empirical question his method does not touch. The debate over this aid goes wrong in both directions when its partisans forget the limits of their authorities: the research literature measures small gains in word learning and is silent about poetry, while Brooks anatomises poetry and is silent about word learning. Each authority governs its own province. The verdict at the end of this article is built by keeping the provinces separate and letting each decide the questions it is competent to decide.

Apply Brooks to the worked examples and the losses recorded there stop looking like accidents of one editor's taste. The Prologue's "take their life" had to become "commit suicide," because a paraphrase is a machine for resolving suspension and the line's whole art is suspension. Mercutio's "grave man" had to become "in a grave," because a paraphrase must pick one leaf of the hinge. Sampson's pun chain had to be reported rather than rendered, because the chain exists only in sounds the target language no longer makes. None of these outcomes reflects incompetence; the No Fear volume is, within its method, a competent piece of work. They reflect the method itself, which guarantees in advance that everything in the play that is double, suspended, sounded, or performed will be the price of everything that is single, settled, stated, and read. Brooks gives the criticism its proper name and its proper scope: the price is real, it is charged on every page, and it is worth paying only when what is bought with it is worth more, namely a reader who then crosses the gutter and pays the original the attention the original can repay. A price paid for nothing, by a reader who never crosses, is the harm case in its purest form.

## The complexity warning: what the test data can and cannot prove

One body of evidence speaks to the harm side with numbers, and it too must be kept within its competence. In 2006 the testing organisation ACT published Reading Between the Lines, an analysis of the performance of more than half a million American students on its college-readiness reading test. The researchers set out to discover what separated students who met the readiness benchmark from students who fell below it, expecting the difference to lie in the kinds of questions answered: inference against recall, main idea against detail. The data refused the expectation. Question type did not differentiate the groups; students above and below the benchmark answered literal and inferential questions at similar rates. What differentiated them, in the report's own summary, was the texts: "performance on complex texts is the clearest differentiator in reading between students who are likely to be ready for college and those who are not." On passages rated complex, students below the benchmark performed at the level of chance. The finding held across sex, income, and racial groups, and it reshaped American reading standards in the decade that followed, because it implied that the route to readiness runs through sustained contact with difficult text and cannot be shortcut by teaching question-answering technique in the abstract.

The bearing on the No Fear debate is indirect but real, and both camps mishandle it. The aid's defenders sometimes answer the complexity finding by noting that their book does not lower the text, since the original stands intact on the left-hand page. That answer is correct about the object and evasive about the practice: ACT's variable was never the difficulty of the book on the desk but the difficulty of the text the student actually processed, and a student who processes only the modern page has, for the purposes of the finding, been given a simple text wearing a complex text's cover. The aid's attackers mishandle the finding in the opposite direction, brandishing it as proof that supported reading damages attainment. It proves nothing of the kind. The ACT analysis is correlational and population-wide; it identifies the capacity to handle complex text as the marker of readiness, and it is silent about the best way to build that capacity in a reader who does not yet have it. A scaffold that ends in independent reading of the original is fully compatible with the finding, because the finding concerns the destination, not the road. What the data forbid is the destination's quiet replacement: a course of study in which the complex text is perpetually present and perpetually unread produces graduates whose transcript certifies an encounter their reading never had. The complexity warning, precisely stated, is not an argument against the aid. It is an argument against a syllabus that lets the aid's page do the work the syllabus exists to demand, and it converts the verdict's central condition from a piety into a measurable requirement: whatever else a unit on this play achieves, it must terminate in the student's unaided engagement with passages of the original, because that engagement is the only outcome the evidence connects to readiness.

## In the classroom: prohibition, permission, and the withdrawal that rarely happens

Between the research laboratory and the philosophy seminar stands the classroom, where the aid is actually governed, and the documented practice there is a spectrum with failure modes at both ends. At one end stands the prohibitionist teacher, represented in print by Jeannette DeCelles-Zwerneman, a master teacher and director of instruction at Cana Academy, whose published advice to teachers of the plays begins with the instruction not to use No Fear Shakespeare or other editions she groups with it, on the ground that such books seek to make the verse more relatable and the language more common, and that the plays are not the sum of their plotlines. Her deeper counsel is sound and even stirring: read the play aloud, keep the reading lively, treat the students' single encounter with the text as precious. Her labelling, however, fails the product record, since she files the series among bowdlerised editions, and the No Fear volume, as the inspection of its first scene showed, expurgates nothing and renders the bawdy more explicit than the school editions of her own tradition ever dared. The error is instructive. It shows how the debate's vocabulary lags its object: the prohibitionist reaches for the nearest historical insult, the expurgated text, when the accurate charge is different and more modern. The No Fear volume does not protect students from the play. It relieves them of the play, which is another matter and requires another argument, the argument from substitution that Brooks supplies and the prohibitionist's own practice implicitly makes.

At the other end of the spectrum stands permission without terms, the classroom in which the aid is assigned as the reading itself, set for homework, tested by quiz, and never withdrawn. No teacher advertises this practice, but its existence is the worst-kept secret of the play's school life, and the aid's design abets it: the online paraphrase is free, searchable, and divisible into scenes, so a student can discharge a night's reading in minutes without opening a book. The result is the substitution equilibrium, in which every institutional signal is satisfied and no reading of the play occurs: the teacher has covered the text, the student has completed the work, the assessment has been passed on the strength of plot knowledge, and the verse has functioned as a ceremonial object, present at its own replacement. Defenders of the aid reply that misuse is not an argument against a tool, and the reply is correct as far as it goes; hammers are not indicted by thumbs. But a tool designed for schools must be judged partly by the use its design makes easiest, and the No Fear design makes substitution easier than scaffolding in every particular: the paraphrase is complete rather than partial, continuous rather than intermittent, free rather than issued, and silent about its own withdrawal. Nothing in the volume instructs the reader in how to stop needing it. The Folger edition, by contrast, is a withdrawal machine: its glosses answer the question asked and withhold everything else, so that dependence on them diminishes as the reader's stock of words and constructions grows. The contrast is not between good and bad books. It is between a support engineered to be outgrown and a support engineered to be used, and the engineering decides what usually happens in rooms where nobody has issued terms.

The middle of the spectrum, where the aid earns its keep, is documented in the same record. Genevieve White's British Council recommendation, pairing lines with renderings in matching exercises, treats the paraphrase as raw material for active work on the original rather than as a substitute for it: the student must locate the correspondence, and locating it requires reading both columns with attention. The same structure appears wherever the aid is used well. A class reads a scene in the original with the modern page open, and the teacher's questions are set on the left-hand text, so the paraphrase functions as the gloss the research literature vindicates, an answer available at the moment of blockage. A teacher preparing a unit assembles the paired passages into a classroom presentation, projecting the two columns for joint inspection, the kind of paired display that a presentation viewer such as [ReportMedic's PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) exists to open and share, and the lesson consists of watching the paraphrase succeed and fail line by line. A student revises for an examination by testing recall of a scene against the original and dropping to the modern page only where recall collapses. These uses share a grammar: the original is the object of study, the paraphrase is an instrument trained on it, and the instrument is set down when the object can be handled alone. They also share a dependence on the wider pedagogy of the play: the strategies by which a teacher makes the tragedy land with teenagers in the first place, treated in the companion study of [teaching Romeo and Juliet to teenagers](/2030/01/25/teaching-romeo-and-juliet-to-teens/), determine whether any aid is needed and where it can help, and the durable student errors the aid so efficiently corrects, the misread "wherefore" chief among them, are catalogued in the survey of [common student misconceptions about the play](/2030/02/01/common-student-misconceptions/). The aid does not float free of that pedagogy. It is a component in it, powerful in proportion to the intelligence of the whole.

### Can a school ban a book that is given away free online?

Only in the ceremonial sense. A prohibition can remove the printed volume from a classroom shelf and can set rules for examinations, and such rules have real force inside assessed work, where the original can be made the sole admissible text.

Outside the examination room the ban meets the distribution facts: the entire paraphrase stands free on the publisher's website, on the same class of device the school itself issues to its students, so the prohibition governs the book as furniture rather than the text as information. This is why the prohibitionist position, for all its moral clarity, keeps losing the practical war and retreating into exhortation. The realistic alternatives have never been ban or surrender. They are the terms of use: which column is read first, which column is assessed, at what point in the unit the modern page is withdrawn, and what a student must be able to do with the left-hand page before the unit is allowed to close. A department that writes those terms has regulated the aid. A department that merely denounces it has regulated nothing but its own conscience, while the aid circulates beneath the denunciation, unexamined and unwithdrawn, doing its easiest work in the dark.

## Each side at its strongest, and where each strong case breaks

Debates decay when each camp answers the other's weakest formulation, so the two positions deserve one hearing apiece at full strength before the verdict. The access case at full strength runs as follows. A school subject that most students meet once, briefly, and under compulsion does not have the luxury of purity, because the alternative to supported reading for the bottom half of a class is not heroic unaided reading; it is the performance of reading, eyes moving over uncomprehended verse while the mind waits for the lesson to end, followed by a lifetime's settled conviction that Shakespeare is a fraud practised on the young. Against that outcome, the paraphrase offers a transaction with real goods on its side of the counter: a completed play, a followed argument, a set of characters whose fates a student can discuss, quote at second hand, recognise in films and references for the rest of a life. Cultural literacy of that order is not nothing. It is the form in which most adults possess most classics, and the graduate who knows the tragedy through its modern twin can still be moved by a stage production, still follow an allusion, still choose, at thirty, to attempt the original with an adult's patience. The aid, on this account, is not the enemy of the play's survival but one of its life-support systems, and the purist who would withdraw it is prescribing an ideal reader's medicine to actual classrooms and killing the patient to save the doctrine.

The abolitionist case at full strength answers at the level of habit rather than outcome. Reading, on this account, is not the extraction of content but a trained behaviour, and the first Shakespeare a student meets sets the template for every later one: what counts as having read, how much difficulty is normal, where help is sought and in what grain. A first play consumed through a continuous paraphrase teaches a definition of the activity in which difficulty is an error state, the text is the obstacle rather than the object, and help means a replacement sentence rather than an unlocked word. Habits of that kind do not stay in their first classroom. They transfer, silently, to the second play and the third, to other authors and other centuries, until a reader has been formed whose tolerance for any prose older or denser than the conversational is close to zero, and whose certificates testify otherwise. The abolitionist concedes the completion figures and asks what was completed. A student can be carried across a river in a boat and truthfully report the crossing; the report does not make a swimmer, and a curriculum that boats every class across every river will graduate passengers and call them swimmers, which is worse than graduating fewer swimmers honestly, because the passengers believe the certificate and stop practising.

Both cases break at identifiable points, and the breaks are as instructive as the strengths. The access case breaks on its silence about mechanism: it can show that support prevents wrong guesses and abandonment in the short term, and it cannot show that the specific support of continuous paraphrase leaves any residue of skill, because the residue studies have not been done at the grain of whole plays over whole school careers. Its favourite evidence, the gloss experiments, tested an instrument ten times lighter than the one being sold. The abolitionist case breaks on its silence about the counterfactual: it describes, vividly, the reader the aid prevents from forming, and it cannot show that the readers actually present in classrooms would have formed that reader without the aid, because the pre-aid baseline was not a nation of close readers but a nation of skimmers, cribs, and classroom performances of attention. Each side's strong case is a true description of one real population: the access case describes the bottom of the class distribution, for whom the aid is the difference between the play and nothing; the abolitionist case describes the middle, for whom the aid is the difference between the play and an easier simulacrum of it. Policy that ignores the first population is cruelty dressed as standards. Policy that ignores the second is surrender dressed as compassion. The graded verdict is what remains when both descriptions are honoured, and it explains why the grades in the table below are assigned by use and reader rather than by the book's existence: the same object is life-support at one desk and a simulacrum at the next, and a ruling that cannot tell the desks apart deserves to fail in both rooms.

## The free text: what the website did to the terms of use

Every argument so far has treated the aid as a book, and the book is only half the phenomenon. SparkNotes gives the entire No Fear text away on its website, where the play is served scene by scene in paired columns headed Original Text and Modern Text, the original again placed first. Free distribution changes the debate's physics in three ways, each unfavourable to casual governance. The first is price: a school can decline to buy the volume, a department can vote against adopting it, and the text remains one search away from every student in the building, at no cost and with no gatekeeper. The second is granularity: the printed book at least forces a physical encounter with the whole object, its weight and pagination testifying that a play is a large thing, while the website delivers the paraphrase in scene-sized portions that match the units of homework evasion exactly. A student directed to read one scene can retrieve that scene's modern text in seconds, read it in minutes, and close the tab with the assignment's letter satisfied. The third is invisibility, compounding the invisibility already noted in the print object: screen reading leaves no annotated margins, no dog-ears, no evidence for a teacher circulating the room to read. The governance problem of the print era, which was the problem of a visible book used in an unobserved way, becomes in the screen era the problem of an invisible text used in an unobservable way, and policies written for the book address a world that no longer contains the transaction.

The honest conclusion from the distribution facts is that the locus of control has moved, permanently, from supply to assessment. A profession that cannot restrict the aid's availability can still restrict its usefulness as a substitute, by deciding what counts as evidence of reading. Plot-level instruments, the factual quiz and the chapter-check question, are indifferent between the columns and therefore certify whichever column the student happened to read; they are, in the strictest sense, paraphrase-compatible assessments, and a department that relies on them has voted for substitution whether it knows it or not. Passage-level instruments break the compatibility. A question that sets an unannounced passage from the original and asks what its language is doing cannot be answered from the modern page, because the modern page's whole service was to make that language unnecessary; a quotation requirement in essays, enforced by checking quotations against the edition, performs the same office for written work. Such instruments are not new technology or extra labour of any magnitude. They are the ordinary tools of literary study, and the aid's existence simply raises the cost of neglecting them. Departments that complain the paraphrase has made their students' reading undetectable are usually describing assessments that never detected reading in the first place, in an age when the undetectability was merely harder to achieve. The website did not create the substitution problem. It industrialised it, and in doing so it exposed which parts of the assessment regime were measuring the play and which were measuring the plot.

There is a final consequence of free distribution that cuts in the aid's favour and should be recorded in fairness. The printed volume, for all its virtues, was an object a student had to obtain, and the students least likely to obtain it were the ones the access case most concerns: the under-resourced, the unparented in literary matters, the reader whose home holds no books. The website abolished that barrier. The same page that serves the evader serves the stranded, and it serves the stranded first in many households, since the evader usually has alternatives. Any verdict that treats the free text as a pure governance disaster has failed to count its beneficiaries, and any policy that could magically withdraw it would withdraw it from the readers with the weakest claim to be refused help. The terms-of-use approach escapes the dilemma because it regulates conduct rather than access: the text may stand open to all, while credit is given only for the work the text was meant to support. That separation, access free and credit earned, is the only settlement compatible with both the research on support and the data on complexity, and it is available to any school willing to write two sentences of rubric.

## Beside the text or instead of it: the border with replacement

One distinction protects this verdict from being smuggled into a neighbouring quarrel. The No Fear volume keeps the original on the page and adds a servant text beside it. A different and more radical proposal removes the original from the stage altogether and performs a modern rendering in its place, asking the audience to accept the new words as the play. That proposal, and the storm it raised, belong to the debate over full modern translation examined in [the 'Play On!' modern translations debate](/2030/02/22/play-on-modern-translations-debate/), and the border between the two questions should be kept bright. Everything said in defence of the aid here depends on the survival of the original in the same opening, within the same glance, under the same cover. The moment the servant text is detached and issued alone, as a reading text in its own right, the defence collapses and the Brooks charge applies without remainder. Publishers have tested that border with paraphrase-only spin-offs and graphic adaptations, and teachers meet it whenever a student prints the modern column and discards the rest. The verdict's line is therefore architectural as much as pedagogical: beside, permissible under terms; instead, not a study aid at all but a replacement work, to be judged as literature and found, on the evidence of the worked examples, thin. Keeping the two debates separate is not pedantry. The side-by-side book is accused daily of the replacement book's offences, and the replacement book shelters behind the side-by-side book's innocence, and both confusions serve only the marketers of each.

## Setting the terms: the contract that makes the aid honest

A verdict that grades by use owes its readers the use, specified closely enough to be enforced by an ordinary teacher in an ordinary week. The contract has four clauses, each aimed at a failure documented in the sections above. The first clause governs order: the original is read first at every sitting, and the modern page is consulted only at a genuine stop, the point where rereading the passage has failed to unlock it. Order is the clause that converts the paraphrase from a text into a tool, because a reader who always begins on the left-hand page is practising construal with a safety net, and a reader who begins on the right-hand page is practising nothing. The second clause governs grain: where a lighter instrument exists for the difficulty at hand, the lighter instrument is used first, a gloss for a word, a note for an allusion, the paraphrase only for the sentence or speech that remains dark after the smaller helps have been tried. Grain is the clause that keeps the reader's labour at the highest level the reader can sustain, which is where learning lives in every other skilled activity. The third clause governs withdrawal: the aid's licence expires by stages across a unit, generous in the first act, restricted in the middle acts to a dictionary and the margin, and absent for the final act and for all assessed work, so that the student's last encounters with the play are unaccompanied and the certificate at the end attests to an unaided capacity. Withdrawal is the clause the publisher will never print and the teacher must therefore supply, since a scaffold with no removal date is a building material, and a class that finishes the play as dependent as it began has been housed, not taught. The fourth clause governs evidence: nothing from the modern page may be quoted, cited, or paraphrased into assessed work as if it were the play, and questions are set on passages, not on plot, so that the only route to credit runs through the left-hand page. Evidence is the clause that aligns the incentives, converting the other three from exhortations into the path of least resistance.

Two features of the contract deserve notice. It costs nothing. Every clause is a rule about conduct with a book and a website that already exist in the students' hands, and the school that cannot afford glossed editions for a whole cohort can enforce the same terms around the free text, which is one more reason the settlement is framed around use rather than purchase. And it is honest with students about its reasons, which matters more than adults usually allow. A class told that the paraphrase is banned because it is cheating learns to conceal its use; a class told that the paraphrase is licensed for two acts and withdrawn for three, because the certificate must mean an unaided reader and because the verse is where the play actually is, has been given the argument itself as part of the curriculum. The debate this article has weighed is not a distraction from the study of Romeo and Juliet. Conducted at that level of candour, it is a piece of the study, the piece in which students learn what kind of object a poem is by watching what happens when its words are exchanged for others.

## The InsightCrunch No Fear verdict

A debate conducted in generalities ends in a shrug, so the verdict is set out here as a graded table, use by use, each grade tied to the evidence that supports it. The market that manufactured and distributed this aid, and the deformations that market visits on the play's school life, are traced in [the study-guide industry and its distortions](/2030/04/19/study-guide-industry-distortions/); the grades below concern the single volume and its uses, which is the ground this article owns.

| Use of the aid | Grade | Ground of the grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First contact for a reader who would otherwise abandon the play | Help | Supported reading beats abandonment and beats confident wrong guessing, the twin findings of the gloss research |
| Companion reading for students working in a second language | Help | The double language load makes the realistic alternative non-reading; endorsed in teacher practice for language learners |
| Consultation at a moment of blockage while reading the original | Help | The gloss function; the same service the Folger facing page performs at smaller grain |
| Teacher-led comparison of the two columns, line by line | Help | The paraphrase becomes an object of study; errors like the misread "wherefore" are corrected in the open |
| Reading a scene in paraphrase before attempting the original | Qualified help | Plot security aids construal, on condition the original follows and is assessed |
| Revision after the original has been studied in class | Qualified help | Consolidation of a reading already made; worthless as a substitute for making it |
| Homework discharged from the modern page alone | Harm | Substitution; the assignment's signals are satisfied and no reading of the play occurs |
| Essay evidence quoted from the modern page | Harm | The equivalence Brooks named: a report about the poem presented as the poem |
| A unit assessed without any unaided passage of the original | Harm | The complexity warning: readiness attaches to the text actually processed, not the book issued |

The pattern across the rows is the article's whole argument in miniature. Every grade of help attaches to a use in which the paraphrase is subordinated to an encounter with the original that it prepares, supports, or consolidates. Every grade of harm attaches to a use in which the paraphrase discharges the encounter, standing in for it in the student's labour and the institution's records. The qualified rows are the hinge cases, and their conditions are the same condition stated twice: the original must follow, and it must be the thing assessed. A teacher can enforce both conditions with ordinary instruments, a reading order and an examination rubric. A student studying alone can enforce both with a private rule, paraphrase second and summary last, original first wherever the will allows. The aid's publisher enforces neither, because its commercial interest is served by the rows graded harm as readily as by the rows graded help, and a company that sells the same book to the scaffolder and the substituter will never print the terms on the cover. The terms must therefore be supplied by the profession and the reader, which is what verdicts are for.

## Help or harm: the adjudication

The three authorities the brief required can be given their sentences. The education research, represented by Hulstijn and his colleagues and by Nation's synthesis, wins its province outright: at the point of blockage, support demonstrably protects a reader from error and abandonment, and no honest verdict can forbid the protection. The critic on the poetry, Cleanth Brooks, wins his province outright in the other direction: a paraphrase received as the work is the work lost, because the work is an experience structured in language and not a statement that survives extraction. The teacher's perspective, represented by the prohibitionist advice of Cana Academy's Jeannette DeCelles-Zwerneman, is vindicated in its aim and defeated in its instrument: she is right that the encounter with the verse is the irreplaceable good and wrong that a ban protects it, wrong also about the book she bans, which expurgates nothing, and her own positive method, voices raised on the original text, is exactly the destination the scaffold exists to serve. Between the research and the critic there is no contradiction to resolve, only a sequence to enforce: the scaffold belongs at the beginning of the encounter and must be gone by its end, and the distance between those two moments is the unit of teaching. The ACT data supply the enforcement's reason. A system that certifies readiness by the complexity of texts handled cannot honour a certificate earned on simplified ones, and a student processed through two years of the modern page has been handled by no complex text at all, whatever the timetable claims.

The final verdict is therefore neither of the brief's two banners. No Fear Shakespeare helps when it is used as the Folger's notes are used, consulted in the service of a text that remains the object; it harms when it is used as its own marketing invites, as the text itself with the original retained as a certificate of authenticity on the facing page. The book is a good servant and a bad master, and since books do not choose their masters, the responsibility lands where it has always landed: on the teacher who sets the terms, the examiner who decides what counts as reading, and the reader who decides which column to live in. Judged as an object, the volume is honest, complete, and often clumsy; judged as a practice, it is exactly as good as the contract under which it is opened. Set the contract, and the aid is a help of real if modest power. Leave the contract unset, and the harm case makes itself, one quietly unread left-hand page at a time.

The verdict is falsifiable, and its terms should be stated so that better evidence can overturn it. A longitudinal study that followed paraphrase-fed classes into their next plays and measured unaided performance there would settle the transfer question on which the abolitionist case rests: if scaffolded readers emerged reading a second play as well as gloss-taught readers, the grades for pre-reading and first contact would rise, and if they emerged measurably worse, the scaffold's licence would deserve shortening in every row of the table. A controlled comparison of the three facing-page instruments, gloss, note, and paraphrase, set on the same scenes with the same students, would likewise discipline the access case's habit of borrowing evidence gathered for lighter tools. Until such studies exist, the grades stand on the best evidence available: experiments that vindicate support, data that certify the destination, and a theory that names the loss. Verdicts in education are usually slogans in search of a constituency. This one is offered as a contract in search of a test.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is No Fear Shakespeare?

No Fear Shakespeare is a series of editions published by SparkNotes that prints a complete play by Shakespeare on the left-hand pages of a book and a complete modern English paraphrase of the play on the right-hand pages, passage by passage. The Romeo and Juliet volume appeared in 2003 from Spark Publishing, a division of Barnes & Noble, in an edition prepared by John Crowther. The original text is neither cut nor modernised, and the paraphrase covers every speech, including the Prologue and the closing scene. The same paired text is given away on the SparkNotes website, in scene-by-scene columns headed Original Text and Modern Text. The series was built for readers who stall on early modern English: the publisher's introduction promises a facing-page translation into the kind of English people actually speak, offered as a way to sort out what is happening, who is speaking, and why. The format is the fact from which the whole debate grows, because the aid never removes the original and never pretends to be anything other than a servant text standing beside it.

### Q: Who publishes No Fear Shakespeare, and when did the Romeo and Juliet volume appear?

The series is published by SparkNotes, the study-guide company founded in 1999 by four Harvard students, Sam Yagan, Max Krohn, Chris Coyne, and Eli Bolotin, and acquired by Barnes & Noble in 2001. The Romeo and Juliet volume carries a 2003 copyright in the name of Spark Publishing, identified on its copyright page as a division of Barnes & Noble, and the Library of Congress catalogued it in 2003 under control number 2003004307, with the paperback ISBN 1-5866-3845-9 and a hardcover issue at 1-4114-0044-3. The edition is credited to John Crowther, who prepared several volumes in the series. The front matter of the first issue lists eighteen companion volumes, covering eighteen plays and the Sonnets, so this play's volume was part of a full shelf from the start rather than an experiment. The parent company's wider print programme had begun a year earlier, when Library Journal reported some 150 SparkNotes study guides entering bookshops in July 2002 at 4.95 dollars, distributed by Sterling Publishing. The No Fear line was thus a conventionally published, conventionally edited product of a vertically integrated education business.

### Q: Does the No Fear volume change or shorten Shakespeare's text?

No. The volume prints the complete text of Romeo and Juliet on its left-hand pages, from the Chorus's Prologue to the Prince's closing speech, following the substance of the standard modern editions, and nothing is abridged, modernised, silently corrected, or replaced. The modern rendering is additional matter set on the facing pages, not substituted matter woven into the play. That fact separates the book from two older kinds of aid with which it is often confused. It is not a retelling like the Lambs' Tales from Shakespeare of 1807, which replaced the plays with prose stories for children, and it is not an expurgation like the Bowdler family's Family Shakespeare, which kept the words but removed whatever the editors judged unfit. A reader who works through the left-hand pages alone has read the entire tragedy exactly as an ordinary edition presents it. The volume's disputed element is entirely on the right-hand side: a complete paraphrase whose presence invites a use the original's presence cannot prevent. Judgements of the book that begin from cuts or censorship begin from a false description of the object.

### Q: What does Juliet's "wherefore" line become on the No Fear modern page?

Juliet's "O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33) becomes "Oh, Romeo, Romeo, why do you have to be Romeo?" and the speech continues in the same plain register: "Forget about your father and change your name. Or else, if you won't change your name, just swear you love me and I'll stop being a Capulet." The rendering performs, in one glance, the single most famous correction in the play's classroom life, since generations of first readers have taken "wherefore" to mean "where" and pictured Juliet searching the garden for a man standing beneath her window. The modern page makes that misreading impossible to keep, and it supplies the motive the misreading hides: the obstacle is the name and the feud it carries, not the geography of the orchard. Teachers of the play treat the correction of this line as a rite of passage, and the aid administers the correction silently and completely. What the rendering cannot supply is the speech's music, the triple naming and the balanced conditions that make the passage an enchantment as well as an argument. The fact arrives intact; the spell does not survive the crossing.

### Q: What happens to Mercutio's "grave man" pun in the No Fear paraphrase?

It is resolved, and the resolution is the cleanest demonstration in the volume of what a paraphrase must do to wordplay. Mercutio, dying in 3.1, jokes on the edge of the grave: "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man." The sentence turns on the two senses of "grave," solemn and buried, and Mercutio means both at once, announcing his death in the grammar of a jest about his temperament. A paraphrase cannot hold two senses in one word, because its task is to state one meaning plainly, and the No Fear page accordingly chooses the plot sense: "Ask for me tomorrow, and you'll find me in a grave." The outcome is preserved and the event is lost, since the event was the doubleness, the laugh that curdles as the sentence completes itself. The same pressure flattens the surrounding lines, where the curse "A plague o' both your houses" becomes "May a plague curse both your families," and "Go, villain, fetch a surgeon" becomes "Go, boy. Get me a doctor." Each rendering is accurate about what happens. None carries what the line does while it happens.

### Q: Do experiments show that glosses beside a difficult text help readers learn its words?

Yes, within limits that the debate habitually ignores. In the best-known experiment, published in The Modern Language Journal in 1996, Jan H. Hulstijn, Margriet Hollander, and Tine Greidanus gave seventy-eight advanced Dutch university students of French a French short story in three conditions: with marginal glosses, with dictionary access, or unsupported. On a surprise vocabulary test afterward, the supported groups had incidentally learned measurably more of the story's difficult words than the unsupported group, and words that recurred in the story were learned better still where support existed. Hulstijn's earlier work added the finding that unsupported readers who guessed at unknown words from context produced many more wrong guesses, confident errors a gloss would have prevented. I. S. P. Nation's synthesis of the field concluded that glosses are an efficient, inexpensive support whose gains are real and modest. The limits are equally clear. The experiments measured single words after single readings of prose, in adults learning a foreign language. They tested glosses, not continuous paraphrase, and they measured nothing about interpretation, verse, or later unaided performance.

### Q: What did the ACT reading study of 2006 find about complex texts?

The ACT organisation's 2006 report Reading Between the Lines analysed the test performance of more than half a million American students against its college-readiness benchmark in reading, and its central finding concerned the texts rather than the questions. The researchers expected readiness to separate students by question type, with those above the benchmark answering inferential questions better than literal ones. The data refused the expectation: question type did not differentiate the groups, while the complexity of the passages did. In the report's own summary, performance on complex texts was the clearest differentiator between students likely to be ready for college and students who were not, and on complex passages the below-benchmark group performed at the level of chance. The finding held across sex, family income, and racial groups, and it shaped the reading standards adopted across the United States in the following years. For the debate over study aids, the report's bearing is precise and limited. It certifies the destination, unaided command of complex text, and says nothing about the best road toward it, which is why it condemns substitution while leaving scaffolding untouched.

### Q: Which part of a line can a paraphrase carry across, and which part can it never carry?

A paraphrase can carry a line's statement: its plot content, its argument, its information about who wants what and what happens next. The No Fear page delivers that cargo reliably, which is why supported readers follow the play and unsupported guessers lose it. What a paraphrase cannot carry is the line's form and sound, the metre that times its delivery, the wordplay that doubles its meaning, the register that places its speaker in Verona's social order, and the ambiguity that lets one phrase ripen across five acts. The philosopher Ernie Lepore, testing Cleanth Brooks's claim that paraphrase is a heresy, conceded exactly this boundary: a restatement may match a poem's semantic content in principle, while its form and sound remain unparaphrasable and its aesthetic effect unreplicated. Alex Neill's defence of paraphrase accepts the same boundary from the other side, treating restatement as a legitimate instrument that nobody expects to contain a poem's exact meaning. The two halves of the answer define the aid's competence. Everything on the statement side of the line is delivered. Everything on the form side is the price, and the price is charged on every page.

### Q: Is reading the modern page instead of the original cheating?

Not in itself, and the distinction turns on what the reader's work was supposed to be. Cheating is the misrepresentation of one performance as another: submitting, as evidence of having read the play, a familiarity gained entirely from its paraphrase. A student who reads the modern page to follow the story and then studies the original has committed no offence against anything except a house rule, because the aid is a published instrument whose nature is declared on its cover. A student who reads only the modern page and allows a teacher, an examination, or a reading log to record the play as read has misrepresented the performance, and the aid's completeness makes the misrepresentation easy: the book in the bag looks like the book that was set. The moral weight therefore falls on the specification of the task. Where a course defines its object as the play's language, substitution defeats the course and is fairly called dishonest. Where a course has silently defined its object as plot knowledge, through factual quizzes and recall questions, the student who uses the paraphrase has merely obeyed the course's real instructions, and the dishonesty is the institution's.

### Q: Why do some teachers ban the aid while others build lessons on it?

The two camps are answering different questions. The prohibitionist teacher is protecting the encounter with the verse, and the position survives in print in the advice of Jeannette DeCelles-Zwerneman, a master teacher at Cana Academy, whose first rule for teaching the plays is to refuse No Fear Shakespeare and kindred editions, on the ground that the plays are not the sum of their plotlines and that a student's single encounter with the text should be spent on the text. Her positive method, the play read aloud and kept lively, is the destination the scaffold exists to serve, although her filing of the series among bowdlerised editions misdescribes a book that expurgates nothing. The permissive teacher is protecting access, and the position survives in Genevieve White's recommendation, for the British Council's teacher audience, of the No Fear text as material for exercises that pair original lines with their renderings and set students to matching them. Between the camps stand the majority of departments, which neither ban nor assign the book and leave its use unregulated, the one arrangement under which substitution flourishes unobserved.

### Q: How does the No Fear page differ from the Folger edition's facing page?

Both books are built around a facing page, and the difference is the cargo the facing page carries. The Folger Shakespeare Library editions, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine and published from 1992 onward, set the play on the right-hand page and fill the facing page with explanatory notes, scene summaries, and illustrations of the objects and customs the text mentions. The Folger's own description states that each volume contains full explanatory notes on the page facing the text they explain. Those notes gloss: they unlock a word, identify an allusion, explain a custom, and stop, leaving the sentence itself in the reader's hands. The No Fear page translates: it restates every sentence in modern prose, performing the vocabulary, syntax, word order, and interpretation in advance. The practical difference is dependence. A reader of the Folger volume needs its notes less with every act, as the stock of unlocked words grows, so the apparatus is engineered to be outgrown. A reader of the No Fear volume can finish the play exactly as dependent as at the start, because the apparatus has done the recurring work rather than teaching it.

### Q: How is a side-by-side paraphrase different from a modern version performed in place of the play?

The difference is the survival of the original inside the same act of attention. The No Fear volume keeps Shakespeare's text on the left-hand page, in the position of honour, and sets the modern rendering beside it as a servant text, consulted at the reader's discretion and never allowed to stand alone between the same covers. A full modern translation prepared for performance removes the original from the occasion altogether: the audience hears only the new words, offered as the play itself, and no glance sideways can recover what was exchanged. The two proposals therefore attract different verdicts. The side-by-side book can be regulated by terms of use, because its offence, where it offends, is a use; the replacement text must be judged as a work, on whether its sentences are worthy of the stage they occupy. Confusing the two debates serves both lobbies badly. The aid is routinely accused of the replacement's presumption, which it does not commit, and the replacement shelters behind the aid's innocence, which it does not share. Each question deserves its own hearing, and the replacement question belongs to the controversy over full modern translation.

### Q: Does the No Fear paraphrase clean up the play's bawdy jokes?

No, and the first scene proves the opposite. Shakespeare's servants Sampson and Gregory descend within fifty lines into sexual menace, Sampson vowing to cut off the maids' heads and clarifying, under Gregory's questioning, that he means their maidenheads (1.1). Editions of earlier generations met such passages with silence or asterisks. The No Fear modern page meets the passage with translation into unmistakable terms, glossing "maidenhead" in the margin as virginity and rendering Sampson's closing boast about his ability to "stand" with the word "erection." The paraphrase therefore makes the play's bawdy harder to miss than the original does, since a modern reader can pass over the early modern term without comprehension but cannot pass over the modern page's explicitness. The description of the series as bowdlerised, heard in the prohibitionist literature, fails on this point of fact. The volume's editorial policy is not protection but explicitness: it trusts no implication to survive unaided, and so it states outright what the original leaves to be caught. What the policy sacrifices is the catching, the small act of complicity by which an audience joins the joke. The information is delivered entire, and the delivery is the loss.

### Q: Can the aid help a student who is learning English as a second language?

Yes, and for that reader the aid's case is at its strongest, because the load is doubled: an unfamiliar modern language carrying an archaic form of itself. Genevieve White, writing against the motion that Shakespeare should not be taught to learners of English in the British Council's magazine for teachers, recommends the No Fear text by name as a classroom resource, proposing exercises in which a line of the original is paired with its rendering and learners must join the two. The research on glossed reading points the same way, since its subjects were precisely readers working in an additional language, and its findings, that support prevents confident wrong guessing and measurably improves word learning, were established on that population. For such a reader the realistic alternative to the modern page is rarely the original read unaided; it is the original abandoned, or the play encountered only through a teacher's summary. The verdict's grading reflects the difference in alternatives. Help is measured against what would otherwise happen, and where what would otherwise happen is nothing, the paraphrase's delivery of the whole play, its plot, its people, and its argument is a substantial good.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch No Fear verdict, and how are its grades assigned?

The InsightCrunch No Fear verdict is the graded table in this article that assigns a judgement to each distinct use of the aid, rather than to the book as a whole. Nine uses are graded. First contact for a reader who would otherwise abandon the play, companion reading for students working in a second language, consultation at a moment of blockage, and teacher-led comparison of the two columns are graded as help. Pre-reading a scene in paraphrase and revision after study are graded as qualified help, each carrying the condition that the original follows and is the text assessed. Homework discharged from the modern page alone, essay evidence quoted from the modern page, and any unit assessed without an unaided passage of the original are graded as harm. The grades are assigned by a single test applied to each use: whether the paraphrase is subordinated to an encounter with the original that it prepares, supports, or consolidates, or whether it discharges that encounter and stands in its place in the student's labour and the institution's records. The table's consistency is the article's argument, that help and harm are properties of uses and readers, not of bindings.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch No Fear verdict to argue that a study aid should be judged by its terms of use?

An essay should begin from the verdict's method rather than from its conclusions, since the method is the transferable part. The first step is to refuse the question in its banner form, help or harm, and to specify the uses of the aid separately, because the evidence in this debate attaches to uses: the gloss research vindicates supported reading at the point of blockage, the ACT data condemn outcomes in which complex text was never processed, and Brooks's heresy of paraphrase condemns the claim of equivalence between a poem and its restatement. The second step is to assign each use its grade by the subordination test, asking whether the paraphrase serves an encounter with the original or replaces it, and to exhibit the grading in cases such as the resolved pun and the corrected "wherefore." The third step is to draw the institutional conclusion: since benefits and harms are produced by terms of use, the responsible agents are the teacher who sets the terms, the examiner who defines what counts as reading, and the reader who chooses a column, and an essay that ends by blaming or blessing the book has mistaken the level at which the outcome is decided.

### Q: What is the strongest argument that the aid helps?

The strongest argument is that support at the point of difficulty demonstrably changes what struggling readers do next: they continue, and they understand more accurately than guesswork allows. The gloss experiments give the claim its evidence. Readers given the meanings of hard words learned more of them and misconstrued less than readers left to infer, and unsupported guessers produced confident errors that support would have prevented. Applied to this play, the argument runs through the reader the curriculum actually contains: a fourteen-year-old meeting inverted syntax, dead vocabulary, and a feudal honour code in the same fifty lines, for whom the choice is seldom between the paraphrase and a scholarly edition. The choice is between the paraphrase and a performance of reading that ends in a settled conviction of incompetence, carried into every later encounter with older literature. A completed play, followed with understanding, is a platform: it supports discussion, performance, recognition, and the possibility of a later return to the original with adult patience. The argument does not show the aid to be the best support available. It shows the alternative most often on offer, abandonment disguised as rigour, to be worse.

### Q: What is the strongest argument that the aid harms?

The strongest argument is that the aid trains a definition of reading in which the text is an obstacle and help means its replacement, and that the training occurs at the reader's most impressionable point, the first encounter with the author the culture most values. The mechanism is habit. A student whose first play is consumed through a continuous paraphrase learns that difficulty is an error state, that understanding a line means receiving its modern twin, and that the verse on the left-hand page is a ceremonial survival rather than the object of study. Brooks supplies the loss's anatomy: everything in the play that is double, sounded, or performed is surrendered in the exchange, so the student is certified as having read a work whose greatness was never present in the version consumed. The ACT data supply the consequence: readiness attaches to the capacity to process complex text, and a student processed through the modern page has been processed by no complex text at all. The argument's force is conditional, and the condition is its honesty: it condemns the aid as a substitute, in the use where substitution happens, and it does not touch the scaffold.

### Q: How can a teacher withdraw the No Fear scaffold once a class can read unaided?

Withdrawal works when it is scheduled rather than announced, and the schedule follows the play's own structure. In the opening act the modern page stands open without restriction while the class learns the play's world and the teacher establishes that questions are set on the original. Through the middle acts the licence narrows: the paraphrase is permitted at a genuine stop, after rereading, while single words and allusions are handled by gloss and note, so the grain of help rises as the class's stock of the play's language grows. For the final act the modern page is closed in class and for homework alike, and all assessed work, from passage questions to essay quotation, is drawn from the original alone. Two instruments make the schedule real rather than rhetorical. The first is assessment design, since questions set on unannounced passages cannot be answered from a paraphrase and quietly enforce the destination. The second is candour: a class told why the scaffold is being removed, that the certificate must attest an unaided reader and that the verse is where the play lives, has been given the debate itself as instruction, and compliance becomes a shared project rather than a policing problem.

### Q: Does the verdict change for a reader studying alone, with no teacher to set terms?

The verdict's logic survives, and its enforcement changes hands. The solitary reader has no examiner and no rubric to align incentives, so the contract's clauses must be self-imposed: the original attempted first at every sitting, the modern page consulted at genuine stops, the grain of help kept as fine as the difficulty allows, and the aid's licence expired by re-reading favourite scenes without it. The dangers are the same as the classroom's, in private form. The substitution equilibrium is easier to reach alone, because no institution even pretends to detect it, and the reader who finishes the volume by its right-hand pages holds a sincere and worthless certificate. The protections are also the same. A reader who treats the paraphrase as a commentary, consulted after the passage and against it, gains the documented benefits of support: the plot held, the errors prevented, the play completed. The solitary case even adds one grade of help the classroom lacks, since the adult returning to the play after years away can use the modern page as a refresher without any pretence that school is in session. The book remains a servant. Alone, the reader must be both parties to the contract.
