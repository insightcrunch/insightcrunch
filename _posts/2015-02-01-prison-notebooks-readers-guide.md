The Prison Notebooks are quoted on every continent and read whole by very few of the people who quote them. They are cited in arguments about politics, education, culture, language, and the state, usually from a handful of famous passages, and those passages almost always reach the citer through several layers of other people's decisions. A political party's editors first rearranged the material by subject. A pair of English translators then selected from that rearrangement. A critical edition later restored the order of composition and marked which passages were drafts. Each layer changed what a reader could see, and therefore what a reader could claim. This guide exists so that the next quotation you meet, including your own, can be checked against the edition it came from.

![The Prison Notebooks, a reader's guide, editions, fragments and the prison censorship - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Three facts govern everything that follows, and a reader who holds them can navigate the rest without assistance. The notebooks were written in a deliberate code, because a prison censor read the pages and the vocabulary had to pass inspection while still carrying a forbidden theory. The edition through which most English readers meet the text is a selection taken from a thematically rearranged edition prepared under the direction of a political party, so the order in which an English reader encounters the arguments is an editor's order, not the author's. The complete critical edition in English, the one that prints every note in sequence with its draft status marked, covers only the first eight of the thirty-three notebooks. Order, emphasis, and completeness are therefore editorial facts before they are interpretive ones. The claim has a name in this guide, the edition dependency, and the sections ahead state it, test it against particular cases, and answer the strongest objection to it.

The guide stays inside its own territory. Gramsci's central concept receives its full treatment in the companion study of [gramsci hegemony complete guide](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/), and nothing here attempts a summary of it, because a summary would have to quote the passages whose provenance is the subject, and would smuggle in the very carelessness the guide exists to correct. The life belongs to the [antonio gramsci intellectual biography](/2015/01/01/antonio-gramsci-intellectual-biography/), which carries the arrest, the trial, the prisons, and the decline of his health at the length they require. What this article does is narrower and, for anyone who cites the notebooks, more urgent. It teaches the reader to ask of every fragment three questions. Which edition printed it. Which notebook contains it, and at what draft stage. And what the words on the page would have meant to the censor who was their first reader.

## The Prison Notebooks are not a book

Begin with the misreading that does the most damage, because it is the most natural. The notebooks are shelved, catalogued, and assigned as though they were a book, a single sustained work with a beginning, a development, and a design. They are not. They are thirty-three school exercise books filled in a prison cell between 1929 and 1935 with working notes of radically unequal finish: reading notes on other men's books, translations made as exercises, sketches of arguments, memoranda to himself, second and third attempts at the same problem, and passages of polished analysis that stand complete. Gramsci never prepared the collection for publication, never gave it a title, never ordered its parts into a whole, and never saw a line of it in print. The object the world calls the Prison Notebooks is a corpus, assembled after his death by other hands, and every printed version of it is an editorial construction built on that corpus.

The point is not pedantry about genres. A book licenses a kind of reading that a corpus forbids. From a book, a reader may legitimately take a sentence on page four hundred as the author's settled view and set it against a sentence on page forty, expecting the two to have been reconciled by the mind that placed them in one volume. From a corpus of working notes, that expectation collapses. A note written in 1930 and a note written in 1934 may contradict each other because the author changed his mind, tested a position and discarded it, or addressed different opponents in different polemical seasons. The first note may be a draft that its own author later rewrote in stricter terms, in which case quoting the draft against the revision attributes to him a view he had already corrected. None of this is visible unless the edition tells you when a note was written and whether it was superseded. Editions differ precisely in how much of that information they carry, which is why this guide treats the choice among them as an interpretive act and not a shopping decision.

The corpus is also unfinished in a way that matters more than its fragmentary surface. Gramsci's health broke down in stages across the prison years: the writing slows, the hand deteriorates, whole projects announced in the early notebooks are taken up late, transformed, or left as headings with a few lines beneath them. By 1935 the writing had effectively stopped, two years before his death in a Roman clinic in 1937. A reader who treats the notebooks as a completed system will therefore mistake the accidents of a failing body for the architecture of a doctrine. Passages are missing not because the argument excludes them but because the prisoner could no longer sit upright to write them. The critical editions register the slowdown in the dating of the notebooks. The thematic arrangement concealed it entirely, distributing strong and weak passages side by side under subject headings as though all had been written from the same chair in the same season.

There is a further, quieter distortion that the book illusion produces. Because the corpus circulates under one title, its parts acquire an artificial equality. A two-line reminder to check a reference sits in the printed volume with the same typographic dignity as a ten-page analysis of the Risorgimento, and quotation databases harvest both without distinction. Gramsci's own practice was the opposite of equal. He returned to the notes that mattered to him, copied them into special notebooks, expanded some, compressed others, and left the rest in the miscellanies where they began. The pattern of return is the nearest thing the corpus has to an author's own table of contents, and it is legible only in an edition that preserves the sequence of the notebooks and identifies which texts are reworkings. Where an edition dissolves that pattern, the reader loses the author's own weighting of his material and substitutes the editor's.

None of this makes the notebooks unusable. It makes them usable in a specific way, the way historians use an archive rather than the way students use a textbook. Archives reward provenance and punish decontextualised extraction, and the skills are learnable: date the note, place the notebook, identify the draft stage, decode the vocabulary, and only then interpret. The remainder of this guide builds those skills in order, starting where the text itself starts, with the locked room in which it was written and the official who read over the writer's shoulder.

## A cell, a censor, and a ration of paper

Gramsci was arrested in November 1926, in the wave of repression that followed the fascist regime's consolidation, and after an interval in confinement and transit prisons he was sentenced by the regime's Special Tribunal to a term of more than twenty years. The sentence's arithmetic matters less here than its destination. From the summer of 1928 he was held in the prison at Turi, in Puglia, where the great majority of the notebooks were written, and his circumstances there set the physical terms of the entire corpus. The biography in this series reconstructs those years fully; what a reader of the text needs is the narrower set of facts that shaped the writing itself, because the writing bears their marks on every page.

He wrote in his cell, at set hours, in ordinary exercise books of the kind sold for schoolchildren, and the exercise books were prison property in everything but his filling of them. Writing materials came by permission and in limited quantity. Every completed notebook remained within the institution's reach, subject to inspection, and the regime's censorship of prisoners' correspondence extended in practice to anything a prisoner produced on paper. Letters home were read, copied in some cases, delayed, and sometimes suppressed, and Gramsci knew the letters' reader intimately enough to write for him. The notebooks occupied a greyer zone, tolerated as study yet never private, and the tolerance itself was a fact he could not test without risking the whole enterprise. A prisoner who loses writing privileges loses everything, and the notebooks' survival depended on never giving the administration a pretext.

Permission to write at all was not immediate. Gramsci petitioned and waited, and the first notebook opens on 8 February 1929, more than two years after his arrest. The date is worth holding precisely because the corpus's chronology is one of its interpretive instruments: everything in the thirty-three notebooks belongs to the six years that follow, years in which his teeth, his digestion, his sleep, and eventually his arteries failed in sequence. The handwriting tells the same story to anyone who consults the manuscripts or their facsimiles, expansive and confident in the first notebooks, cramped and laboured in the last, and the critical edition's dating of each notebook lets a reader correlate the state of an argument with the state of the man. No thematic arrangement can show that correlation, which is one reason the rearrangement, however useful as an anthology, is treacherous as a portrait of a mind.

His study plan survives, and it is the corpus's only authorial prospectus. In a letter of March 1927 to his sister-in-law Tatiana Schucht, written from Milan before the transfer to Turi, he set out the subjects he meant to work on for the rest of his confinement: the formation of Italian intellectuals, comparative linguistics, the theatre of Pirandello, and the serial novel and popular taste in literature. The letter is famous for its declaration that he wanted to do something, in his German phrase, für ewig, for good, and the four topics map recognisably onto the notebooks' great clusters. But the plan also warns the reader. The notebooks were a programme of study executed under sentence, not a treatise composed for a publisher, and their author revised the programme as he went, adding the long engagement with Machiavelli, with Croce, with the history of the subaltern classes, as the reading fed the questions. An edition that respects the sequence lets you watch the programme change. An edition arranged by subject freezes the programme into a false simultaneity, in which the prisoner of 1929 and the prisoner of 1934 appear to hold every position at once.

The censor stood inside this arrangement as a permanent second reader, and his presence explains the corpus's most famous stylistic feature. Political writing of the kind Gramsci had practised as a journalist and party leader was impossible on inspected paper. Names of living politicians, references to his own party and its strategy, the vocabulary of the movement he had led: any of these, read plainly, could end the writing experiment and perhaps worsen his classification as a prisoner. The solution he adopted was systematic rather than occasional, a substitution vocabulary maintained with a translator's consistency across six years and thousands of pages, and it is the subject of the next two sections. First, the guide's snippet question deserves its direct answer, because everything in those sections expands it.

### Why did Gramsci write in code?

Because a prison censor read everything he wrote, and plain political language would have ended the writing and endangered the writer. Marxism appears as the philosophy of praxis, Marx as that philosophy's founder, and living contemporaries under oblique designations. Read literally rather than as substitution, the vocabulary misleads systematically.

The code was not a cipher, and the distinction matters for how a reader should handle it. A cipher conceals meaning from everyone without the key. Gramsci's substitutions concealed nothing from an informed reader, then or since: anyone acquainted with the movement's literature could decode philosophy of praxis on sight, and the censor, if he understood what he was reading, was evidently willing to tolerate abstraction where he would not have tolerated agitation. What the code bought was deniability and register. It moved the writing from the genre of the political pamphlet to the genre of scholarly inquiry, from names and parties to concepts and periods, and in that register the notebooks could pursue arguments of real subversive reach while presenting, page by page, the aspect of a historian's commonplace book. The code is therefore best understood as a change of costume that became a change of method. Writing about his own tradition as though it were a philosophy among philosophies forced a generality on the arguments that journalism would never have required, and readers who decode the substitutions recover not only the forbidden words but the discipline the costume imposed.

That last point guards against an opposite error. Because the code was a costume, some readers treat the coded terms as though they were merely the forbidden words in disguise, interchangeable in every context, and miss the places where the substitution does conceptual work of its own. Philosophy of praxis, used across hundreds of notes, accumulates emphases that the word Marxism, in the polemical usage of the time, did not carry: an insistence on philosophy as activity, on theory as a practical force, on the unity of knowing and doing. The guide's position is the brief's position, that the term is a substitution and not a separate doctrine, and the misreading that elevates it into a distinct system, Gramsci's supposed alternative to the theory he led a party in the name of, is corrected wherever it appears below. But a substitution repeated for six years becomes an instrument with its own grain, and the reader is told, here and in the decoding section, to hold both truths: decode first, then watch what the decoded term does in the sentence.

## The vocabulary of substitution, decoded

The substitution system has a core, a middle ring, and an outer haze, and a reader should learn to recognise all three. At the core stand the replacements for the theory itself and its founders, maintained without exception across the corpus. Around them stand designations for the movement's organisations, factions, and events, where the substitution is often a periphrasis rather than a term of art. At the edges, ordinary scholarly words do double duty, and only context tells the reader whether history, philosophy, or politics is being discussed in its plain sense or as a screen for a more specific referent. The critical edition's notes and indexes exist largely to stabilise these identifications, and one practical difference between editions is how much decoding help each supplies. The English selection decodes silently, often simply printing the referent, which spares the beginner confusion at the price of hiding the fact that a substitution occurred.

### What does philosophy of praxis stand for?

It stands for Marxism, named so that the word could pass a censor who searched pages for the movement's vocabulary. The substitution is systematic across the notebooks, covering the theory, its history, and its adherents' activity, and it should be decoded on every occurrence rather than treated as the title of a separate doctrine.

Hold the decoding steady and a hundred passages change their temperature. A note that appears to discuss, in the abstract, the relation between a philosophy and the masses is discussing the relation between Marxism and the working class, with strategic intent. A note on the philosophy of praxis becoming a cultural battle is a note on the movement's struggle for intellectual leadership, not a musing on metaphysics. The substitution also explains a feature of the corpus that puzzles literal readers: its apparent reticence about its own tradition's name at exactly the moments of deepest engagement with that tradition. The reticence is the censor's fingerprint. Where the argument comes closest to the forbidden centre, the costume grows thickest, and the reader who has learned to feel that thickening has acquired a rough instrument for locating the notes Gramsci most cared about, an instrument no index supplies.

The term's prehistory deserves one paragraph, because it protects the decoding from a sophisticated objection. Philosophy of praxis was not invented in the cell. Antonio Labriola, the Italian Marxist philosopher of the generation before Gramsci, had used the phrase for the theory, and it circulated in Italian Marxist writing as an honourable synonym with a philosophical accent. Gramsci's adoption of it was therefore doubly convenient: it was camouflage woven from genuine cloth, a term with a pedigree that a censor could read as academic tradition and an initiate could read as allegiance. The scholarly debate over whether Gramsci meant more by it than camouflage is real, and this guide does not pretend otherwise. It insists only on the order of operations. The substitution must be recognised first, as the condition of the text's existence; arguments about what else the term accomplishes are legitimate second-order questions, and they are unanswerable by any reader who has not performed the decoding they presuppose.

The founders receive the same treatment as the theory, one level down. Marx appears in the notebooks overwhelmingly under a title rather than a name, and the title's stiffness is itself a signal, marking the passages where the argument turns on the tradition's authority. The next snippet-sized question answers itself almost too neatly, which is why it is worth stating plainly: readers who search the notebooks for the name Marx, in an index or a digital text, will find strikingly few hits and may conclude that the notebooks are strangely post-Marxist in orientation. The conclusion is an artefact of the code. Search for the title instead and the founder is everywhere, presiding over the corpus like a patron whose portrait has been replaced by his office.

### Who is the founder of the philosophy of praxis?

The founder is Marx, designated by office rather than by name so that the name would not appear on inspected paper. Engels appears in a supporting designation, as the founder's collaborator and systematiser, and the pair under their titles carry the whole weight of the tradition's founding authority throughout the notebooks.

Around the core, the middle ring handles the movement's more recent history, and here the substitutions grow more allusive. Lenin appears as Ilyich, the patronymic by which the Russian movement knew him, a designation affectionate and unmistakable at once. Trotsky appears under his family name, Bronstein, or by initials and descriptions that shift with the polemical weather. Bukharin, whose manual of historical materialism the notebooks subject to an extended critique in the eleventh notebook, is engaged through his book, designated as a popular treatise or essay on the theory of historical materialism, so that the critique reads as a review of a textbook rather than an attack on a leading figure of the international movement. Stalin is handled most obliquely of all, approached through policies, texts, and the pronouncements of others, a circumspection that the chronology explains: notes written while the succession struggle hardened into terror had reasons beyond the prison's walls for their care.

The outer haze is where inexperienced readers are lost, and where editions earn their keep. Words like intellectuals, common sense, folklore, religion, and language all carry technical charges in the notebooks that their ordinary usage does not announce, and part of that charge comes from the code's pressure: forbidden distinctions migrate into permitted vocabulary and overload it. The point for this guide is not to gloss each term, since glossing the concepts belongs to their own articles, but to mark the mechanism. Censorship did not merely rename a list of forbidden words. It deformed the whole lexicon, concentrating meaning in the words that remained available, so that the notebooks' key terms are denser than the same words in the author's journalism, and any reading that ignores the pressure will systematically under-read them. The thematic edition, prepared by men who had known the author and shared his world, decoded by instinct and rarely signalled the operation. Readers two generations and one language away have no instinct to rely on, and for them the critical apparatus is not scaffolding but eyesight.

A practical corollary follows for quotation. When you quote the notebooks in an argument, quote the words as printed, and if the passage turns on a substitution, say so in your own sentence rather than silently replacing the term with its referent. Silent replacement falsifies the evidence twice over: it attributes to the prisoner a boldness the censor forbade, and it hides from your reader the fact that the passage's abstraction was compulsory, which is often the key to its tone. The protocol section later in this guide returns to quotation discipline at length. For now the rule stands as the decoding section's whole yield: the words on the page are a costume, the costume is evidence, and evidence is not improved by being dressed in modern clothes.

## Names under erasure: contemporaries in the notebooks

A census of the notebooks' dramatis personae would look, to a literal reader, eccentric. The Italian scene of the 1920s and 1930s, the regime, its Duce, its philosophers of state, appear largely in historical costume or by function. Croce, the great exception, is named constantly and at length, because Croce could be named: a senator, a liberal philosopher, the regime's licensed internal critic, and therefore a permissible object of sustained attack. The long engagement with Croce that fills the tenth notebook and threads through the corpus is genuine intellectual combat, and it is also combat conducted on the one field the censor reliably allowed, the field of Italian cultural history. Readers who wonder why a imprisoned communist leader devoted so much of his rationed paper to a liberal philosopher of the previous generation find part of their answer in the prison's geography of the sayable. Croce was the permitted giant, and through Croce, Gramsci could fight the larger war about the role of intellectuals in a nation's life with the censor as an unwilling spectator.

The same geography explains the corpus's strange temporal tilt. Ancient and early modern figures, Machiavelli above all, carry weights that a free writer would have distributed among contemporaries. Machiavelli could be discussed at any length, in any depth, with any degree of strategic specificity, because a Florentine secretary dead for four centuries threatens no prison administration. The notes on Machiavelli in the thirteenth notebook therefore do double service, as Renaissance scholarship and as the nearest the corpus comes to a treatise on the modern political party and its founder figure, a transposition every informed reader performs and no censor could forbid. When this guide reaches the cases where editions change readings, the Machiavelli notes will serve as the cleanest example, since the thematic edition gathered them under a title that announces the transposition, while the critical edition restores them to a notebook whose sequence shows the argument being built note by note.

Giovanni Gentile, the regime's philosopher, appears under the shelter of his school, actual idealism, and is handled as a tendency rather than a man. The Catholic Church and its orders appear constantly, as they had to in any serious analysis of Italian society, and the treatment is historical and sociological, a register the censorship tolerated because it resembled scholarship. The leaders of Gramsci's own movement, by contrast, shrink to initials, functions, and silences, and the silences are data. A reader mapping the notebooks' world from the names alone would conclude that Italian communism had no personnel, which is the precise inverse of the truth: the personnel were unsayable, and the unsayability is the fact to be read. Editions differ here as elsewhere. The thematic editors, publishing in the years when many of the persons concerned were alive and leading the party that published the edition, had their own reasons for discretion, and their selection among the notes was shaped by pieties as well as by censorship's long shadow. The critical edition prints what the manuscripts contain and lets the apparatus identify whom it can.

One more class of names requires the reader's care: the dead of the movement's first generation, Marx and Engels under their titles, and behind them the figures of the Risorgimento, of the French Revolution, of the Reformation, who crowd the notebooks as the permitted population of a political imagination in enforced historical dress. The density of historical example in the corpus is not antiquarianism. It is the shape political thought takes when the present can only be approached in costume, and it gave the notebooks their distinctive method, in which a note on the Jacobins or on Piedmontese moderates is simultaneously an analysis of a past conjuncture and a probe of a present one. Readers decode substitutions at the level of the word, and they must learn to decode them at the level of the example as well. An edition that scatters the historical notes among thematic chapters breaks the reader's ability to watch a single historical obsession recur and deepen across the years of writing, which is among the strongest reasons this guide gives for reading, at least sometimes, in the notebooks' own order.

## Two strata in one corpus: miscellanies and special notebooks

From the writing desk's point of view, the thirty-three notebooks fall into families, and the families are the reader's first map. There are translation notebooks, in which the prisoner exercised his languages on other men's texts, rendering German and Russian prose into Italian as a discipline of the mind and, not incidentally, as a way of possessing books the prison library could not supply. There are the miscellaneous notebooks, the great majority of the early and middle sequence, in which notes on a dozen subjects share a page-run in the order his reading supplied them: a remark on a newspaper article, a page on a sixteenth-century historian, a memorandum on linguistics, a sketch on the party, all neighbours because a week in a cell made them neighbours. And there are the special notebooks, begun from 1932, in which Gramsci took up the miscellanies and rewrote their scattered notes under single themes, gathering, expanding, and sometimes correcting what he had written before. The distinction between the strata is the single most consequential structural fact in the corpus, because it creates the corpus's second-order problem: for many of its most important arguments, the text exists twice.

### What are the special notebooks?

They are the thematic notebooks begun in the final writing years, into which earlier scattered notes were copied, reworked, and expanded under single subjects: philosophy, Machiavelli and politics, the intellectuals, Americanism, the subaltern groups. A passage in a special notebook is the author's own second treatment and normally supersedes its miscellaneous draft.

The special notebooks are Gramsci's own first edition of himself, and the reader should register both halves of that description. They are his: no editor chose their themes, and their existence proves that the thematic organisation later imposed on the whole corpus had a warrant in the author's own practice. He was moving, in the last writing years, from accumulation toward composition, from the commonplace book toward the treatise, and the special notebooks are the treatise in embryo. But they are also an edition: a selection and revision performed on earlier material, which left other material behind, compressed some arguments and abandoned others mid-path. The scholarly question the critical edition forces, of why a given note was or was not carried into a special notebook, has no mechanical answer. Health, access to the earlier notebooks, and the changing pressure of his questions all played their parts, and the absence of a theme from the special notebooks is not evidence that he had discarded it.

The census of the special notebooks reads like a syllabus of the corpus's afterlife. The tenth notebook carries the confrontation with Croce's philosophy. The eleventh assembles the introduction to the study of philosophy, including the sustained critique of the popular manual of historical materialism that passes, in the code, as a review of a textbook. The twelfth gathers the notes on intellectuals and education. The thirteenth, on Machiavelli and politics, contains the notes on the modern Prince that became the corpus's most famous political pages. The twenty-second assembles Americanism and Fordism, the analysis of the new industrial civilisation across the Atlantic. The twenty-fifth collects the notes on the history of subaltern groups, with their famous methodological cautions. A reader who knows only these notebooks knows the corpus as its author was learning to present it. A reader who knows only the miscellanies knows the workshop without the showroom. The editions, as the next sections show, distribute these strata very differently, and a reader's Gramsci tilts accordingly.

The miscellanies reward a different attention. Because their order is the order of his reading and thinking, they show the questions arriving: a review in a journal triggers a note, the note attracts a second, a historical example is tried and found wanting, a term is coined in passing and picked up three notebooks later with new weight. Concepts that the thematic edition presents as doctrines appear in the miscellanies as experiments, and some experiments fail in full view. There is a note-taking honesty in the early notebooks that the special notebooks, by their compositional nature, partly conceal. Neither stratum is the true Gramsci to the other's falsehood. They are draft and revision in the most literal sense, and the corpus's scholarly editions are valued exactly in proportion as they let the reader move between the strata with the crossings marked. That marking system, the draft apparatus, is important enough to deserve its own section, and it follows after one more structural observation.

The two strata also differ in their censorship weather. The miscellanies were written closest to the censor's eye in time, each page filled under the standing possibility of inspection, while the special notebooks reworked material that had already survived in the cell, in a hand by then practised in the costume. It would be too much to claim that the revisions are bolder because safer; illness pressed harder in the special-notebook years than caution ever had. But the reader comparing a draft with its revision should know that two different constraints, the censor over the first and the failing body over the second, separate the versions, and that the revision's greater polish was bought with scarcer strength. When the two disagree, the disagreement is a small biography in itself.

## First drafts, single drafts, and revised texts

The critical edition classifies every text in the corpus by its draft status, and the classification, once learned, changes how any passage may be used. The scheme has three categories, conventionally labelled with the letters A, B, and C. A texts are first drafts: notes written in a miscellaneous notebook and later taken up, reworked, and superseded by a revised version elsewhere. B texts are single drafts: notes written once and never reworked, which stand in the only form their author gave them. C texts are the revised versions themselves, the reworkings in the special notebooks, which represent the author's final handling of their material. The letters are the critical editor's, not the author's, but the realities they name are the author's own acts of writing and rewriting, recovered from the manuscripts by editorial scholarship.

### What do the draft-stage letters signify?

They record what the author did with a note after writing it. A marks a first draft later reworked and superseded. B marks a note written once and never revisited. C marks the revised text in a special notebook, the author's own second version, which carries his final formulation of the argument.

The practical force of the scheme lands on quotation. An A text quoted as its author's position may attribute to him a formulation he himself replaced, sometimes softening it, sometimes sharpening it, sometimes reversing its emphasis. The thematic edition, which drew freely on both strata without marking the crossings, made this error unavoidable for its readers, and a number of celebrated formulations in the secondary literature are A texts whose C versions say something measurably different. The critical edition prints the A texts in a reduced typeface within the notebook sequence, cross-referred to their C counterparts, so that the reader watches the supersession happen. The Buttigieg English edition follows the same practice and explains it in its apparatus, one of the strongest reasons for preferring it wherever it reaches. The English selection, by contrast, chose whichever version suited its arrangement and gave the reader no signal that a choice had been made.

B texts carry the opposite warning. A single draft was never tested by its author's second thoughts, and the corpus's B stratum includes some of its most quoted provocations, notes whose aphoristic force owes something to their never having been revised into caution. The reader is not forbidden to quote them. The reader is obliged to know what is being quoted: a jotting at full stretch, not a considered position, and to let that knowledge temper the weight the quotation is asked to bear in an argument. Scholars quarrel, properly, about how much tempering particular B texts require. What is not quarrelsome is the preliminary duty, which editions either enable or obstruct. An edition without the apparatus makes the duty unperformable, and arguments built on such editions inherit the defect silently.

There is a third use of the scheme, less defensive and more exciting. Where an A text and its C version both survive, the pair constitutes the closest thing the corpus offers to a recording of its author thinking. The differences are rarely cosmetic. Sentences are re-aimed at new opponents, examples are swapped, a concession is inserted or withdrawn, a term is replaced by a more exact successor. Studying the pairs teaches the direction of the author's intellectual travel in his last years more reliably than any summary, and seminar leaders in particular should build exercises on them, since a student who has watched one argument migrate from draft to revision will never again quote a fragment without asking after its stage. The persona section for teachers returns to this exercise. Here it is enough to fix the scheme in the reader's equipment: A superseded, B single, C revised, and no quotation fully described until its letter is known.

## Survival: a sister-in-law, a Cambridge economist, and an archive in Moscow

The notebooks' existence as a readable corpus was never guaranteed, and their survival story explains several features of their later editorial life. Gramsci wrote without any assurance that the exercise books would leave the prison, let alone reach print. As his health collapsed he was moved, first to a clinic at Formia in 1933 and then to Rome, and the notebooks travelled in the care of Tatiana Schucht, his wife's sister, who had settled near him, visited him, managed his correspondence and his books, and understood, as perhaps no one else did, what the filled exercise books contained. On his death in April 1937 she took possession of the manuscripts and became their first guardian, numbering and listing them in an inventory whose care scholars still rely on, before the question of their permanent keeping had to be answered in a Europe moving toward war.

### Who preserved the notebooks, and how?

Tatiana Schucht preserved them, taking charge of the manuscripts at the author's death, inventorying them, and arranging their transfer out of fascist Italy. With help from the economist Piero Sraffa, the notebooks were sent to Moscow, where an institute archive held them through the war years.

Sraffa's part deserves its sentence of emphasis, because it links the corpus's survival to the same Cambridge world that shaped so much twentieth-century economics. Piero Sraffa, the Italian economist at Cambridge, an old acquaintance from the Turin years, had supported Gramsci materially through the prison period, supplying books and an account at a London bookseller that let the prisoner order beyond the prison library's poverty. When the manuscripts needed a custodian beyond fascist reach, the channel ran through him to Moscow, where the notebooks were deposited in the archive of the Marx-Engels-Lenin Institute. There they survived the war. Microfilm and diplomatic negotiation did their slower work in the years after, and the originals returned to Italy in the postwar settlement, into the custody of the party Gramsci had led and of the institute that would bear his name. The chain of custody, cell to sister-in-law to Cambridge economist to Moscow archive to Rome, is improbable enough to deserve the reader's pause. Every printed edition descends from that chain, and the chain's links explain the editions' characters: the Moscow sojourn placed the corpus, at the moment of its first publication, in the hands of a party with a strategy to serve.

The inventory matters in a drier way. Because Tatiana numbered the notebooks and the Moscow archive preserved the bundle intact, the critical editors of the 1970s could reconstruct the corpus's physical extent and order with confidence, and the occasional scholarly skirmish over whether notebooks are missing can be conducted against evidence rather than legend. The consensus the editions work from, thirty-three notebooks comprising roughly two thousand eight hundred manuscript pages by the count used in the critical edition's presentation, rests on that guardianship. Numbers in this field attract mythology, and this guide follows the standing protocol for contested figures: the counts are given as the approximations the critical scholarship presents them as, and the reader who needs them exactly should take them from the critical edition's own introduction, where the counting is argued rather than asserted.

One more survival deserves recording, because it conditions a whole branch of reading. The prison letters, more than four hundred of them, survived by the ordinary route of family keeping, and they were published before the notebooks, in 1947, giving the world a Gramsci of voice and circumstance before it had a Gramsci of theory. The letters section near the end of this guide takes up their use as a companion to the notebooks. Here their priority in print matters for a simpler reason. The first postwar image of the author, humane, ironic, suffering, formed from the letters, and the thematic edition of the notebooks was prepared inside that image's warmth, by editors for whom the author was a martyred leader whose thought the party was charged to transmit. Piety is not bad faith, and the neutrality this guide owes the thematic edition begins with remembering the hands it passed through and what those hands had buried.

## The thematic edition: arrangement as argument

Between 1948 and 1951 the Turin publisher Einaudi issued the notebooks in six volumes, and for a generation that edition was the notebooks for every reader in every language. Its preparation was directed politically by Palmiro Togliatti, the party's leader and Gramsci's successor, and executed editorially by Felice Platone. The volumes did not follow the notebooks. They dissolved them, distributing the notes among thematic titles that announced a system: historical materialism and the philosophy of Benedetto Croce; intellectuals and the organisation of culture; the Risorgimento; notes on Machiavelli, on politics and on the modern state; literature and national life; past and present. Within each volume, notes from different years and different strata stood in an editor's sequence, undated for the common reader, their draft status unmarked. What emerged was readable, teachable, and magnificent in its way: a shelf of treatises by an author who had written none.

### What did the thematic edition change?

It changed sequence into system. Notes written years apart, in different notebooks and at different draft stages, were gathered under subject titles and printed as continuous treatises, so readers received an architecture the author had not built and could not see draft, date, or supersession anywhere in the volumes.

Judge the edition by its circumstances before judging it by its effects, as this guide's neutrality requires. Italy in 1948 was a republic being founded in the shadow of a civil war's end, the party that published Gramsci was contesting the state's future, and the notebooks were at once a patrimony, a weapon, and a proof that Italian communism possessed a national intellectual tradition of the first rank. A critical edition in the philological sense, with apparatus and chronology, would have taken decades and reached scholars. A thematic edition reached the movement and the reading public at once, and its arrangement embodied, quite openly, the party's reading of its founder for the strategy of the hour: Gramsci the national theorist, the analyst of intellectuals and of the Risorgimento, the philosopher who had defeated Croce on Croce's ground. The selections and emphases served that portrait. They were not concealed; the volumes announced their titles on their spines. What later readers forgot was that a portrait is not a corpus, and the forgetting, more than the editing, is what the critical reaction of the 1970s had to correct.

The edition's defenders can also say, truly, that the arrangement taught the world to read Gramsci at all. The thematic volumes created the audience, the translations, the university courses, and eventually the demand for philological rigour that produced the critical edition. Without 1948 there is no 1975. This guide's claim, the edition dependency, is not an indictment of the thematic editors, whose work it treats as an honourable act of transmission under political conditions they did not choose. The claim is about the reader's duty in the present tense of reading: whatever one's gratitude to an edition, an argument that turns on the notebooks' order, emphasis, or completeness must reckon with the fact that the order on the shelf is Platone's and Togliatti's before it is Gramsci's.

Two distortions followed from the arrangement with particular consistency, and the cases section later in this guide exhibits both in detail. The first is synchronic flattening. A note of 1930 and a note of 1934, printed adjacently under one heading, read as a single position, and the author's movement between them, which is often the argument, disappears. The second is emphasis by gathering. A theme the editors prized swelled into a volume, while a theme they had less use for, the notes on language and linguistics, say, or the translation exercises and the American notes in their full oddity, contracted into excerpts or vanished from the early printings. Readers formed their sense of what the corpus centrally contains from a table of contents written in an office in Rome and Turin. Every subsequent edition has been, in part, an argument with that table of contents.

The thematic edition also fixed the corpus's textual surface for its era in ways beneath the level of arrangement. Editors regularised, chose between draft variants silently, and occasionally adjusted readings, as editors of posthumous papers then did without the modern apparatus of accountability. Nothing in this was scandalous by the standards of postwar publishing, and much of it was done with love. But it means that the thematic text, at the level of the sentence, is itself an interpretation, and the critical edition's departures from its readings, where the manuscripts support them, are corrections of the record rather than novelties. A reader citing a sentence that exists only in the thematic printing is citing an editorial event of 1949 as though it were a prison event of 1932, and the edition dependency, at that resolution, becomes a matter of single words.

## The edition map

The guide's findable artifact gathers the four editions through which readers meet the corpus into one table. It is called the edition map, and its purpose is practical before it is scholarly. Given a quotation, a course text, or a library shelf, the map tells the reader what kind of object is in hand, what it can support, and where its testimony runs out. The editions are identified by editor and publisher throughout this guide, never by their novelty, and the reader who acts on the map should verify the publication details of any edition against the publisher's own catalogue, since printings multiply and projects in progress change their shape.

Read the map's rows as four different answers to one question: what does a reader owe a text that its author never finished? The thematic edition answers with usefulness, the critical edition with fidelity, the selection with access, the Buttigieg volumes with fidelity at the price of incompleteness. The rows also mark a gradient of trust for citation. Arguments about what Gramsci thought can begin anywhere on the map. Arguments about what Gramsci wrote, in what order, with what revisions, and with what relative weight in his own economy of attention, can only be settled in the map's critical rows, and any writer who attempts them from the thematic rows is building on an arrangement while believing it is a foundation. The sections that follow take each critical and English row in turn, because those are the rows an English reader will actually hold.

## The critical edition: Gerratana restores the order

In 1975 Einaudi published the edition that reorganised Gramsci scholarship: the Quaderni del carcere edited by Valentino Gerratana, prepared under the auspices of the Gramsci Institute in Rome. Four volumes printed the entire corpus in the order of the notebooks themselves, as the editors reconstructed that order from the manuscripts, Tatiana's inventory, and the internal evidence of the writing. Each note received its place in a sequence and a number, the famous paragraph numbering by which scholars now cite the text, notebook and section, in a form any reader anywhere can verify. Draft status was marked by the A, B, C scheme described above, with first drafts set in smaller type and cross-referred to their revisions. An apparatus recorded variants, datings, identifications of persons and books, and the sources of the reading notes. What had been a set of treatises became, again, a prisoner's workshop, this time with the lights on.

### What does the critical edition restore?

It restores sequence, and with sequence, time. The notes return to the notebooks and years in which they were written, drafts stand visibly beneath their revisions, and the reader can watch arguments arrive, migrate, and harden or soften across the writing life instead of receiving them as a finished system.

The restoration changed scholarship in ways that have filtered only slowly into general reading, and the lag is itself an illustration of the edition dependency. Arguments established from the thematic arrangement did not evaporate when the critical edition appeared; they persisted in textbooks, in translation, and in the habits of teachers who had learned the corpus from the selection. The critical edition's Gramsci is more hesitant, more historical, more interested in language, philology, and the granular life of subaltern classes, and less like the systematic political theorist of the thematic volumes. The notebooks' chronology also redistributed emphasis among the famous themes. Some passages that the thematic edition had placed at the centre of volumes turned out to be early drafts their author had reworked into different shapes. Others, buried by the arrangement, emerged in the sequence as persistent obsessions. None of this reveals a suppressed Gramsci, and this guide follows its brief in refusing that melodrama. The critical edition restores an order. What the order then shows, about emphasis, development, and second thoughts, is scholarship's ordinary work, not a jailbreak.

Gerratana's editorial decisions deserve the same clear-eyed treatment the guide gives the thematic edition, since fidelity is a practice, not a spell. The reconstruction of the notebooks' order involved judgement where the manuscripts underdetermine sequence. The dating of individual notes is often approximate, argued from handwriting, ink, paper, and internal reference, and the apparatus is honest about degrees of certainty. The classification of texts into draft stages rests on identifications of reworking that are occasionally contestable at the margin. None of this diminishes the edition; it defines the terms on which it should be used, as an argued reconstruction whose reasons are printed alongside its results, open to inspection in a way the thematic edition's silent arrangements never were. The difference between the editions is finally not that one has editors and the other does not. Both have editors. The difference is that the critical edition shows its working.

For the English reader, the critical edition's chief frustration is linguistic, and its chief gift is referential. Even a reader who cannot follow the Italian can use its numbering as the corpus's coordinate system, because the Buttigieg translation keys its text to the same notebook and paragraph numbers, and scholarly citation in every language converges on those coordinates. A quotation given as the thirteenth notebook, such and such a section, can be found in Gerratana by anyone, checked against the manuscript tradition, and compared with its draft. A quotation given by a thematic volume's page number can be found only in that volume. The coordinate system is the critical edition's most democratic legacy: it made verification a public act, available to any reader with a library, rather than a privilege of those who shared the first editors' world.

## The English selection: Hoare and Nowell Smith

The Selections from the Prison Notebooks, published by Lawrence and Wishart in 1971 in the edition prepared and translated by Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell Smith, is the book through which the English-speaking world learned Gramsci, and its influence dwarfs every other row of the edition map. It is a selection, of a selection's lineage: its texts were drawn from the thematic Italian edition, whose arrangement it broadly follows, and its translators worked with the thematic volumes as their base. Within those limits the work was done superbly. The translation is strong, the editors' introduction and notes schooled two generations, and the volume's contents, the notes on history and culture, on philosophy, on the modern Prince, on state and civil society, on Americanism and Fordism, became the canonical English Gramsci, the Gramsci of the seminar room and the quotation file.

### What did the English selection leave out?

It left out the majority of the corpus: most of the miscellaneous notebooks' range, the translation exercises, the notes on language and linguistics in their full extent, the Risorgimento material in its detail, and the draft strata entirely. Its Gramsci is the thematic edition's Gramsci, further concentrated: political, philosophical, quotable.

The selection's virtues and its defects are the same property viewed from two sides, and the guide insists on holding both in view, because the selection remains, by the argument of the objection section below, the practical entry point for English readers and will go on being assigned wherever the corpus is taught. Its concentration made a difficult author teachable. Its arrangement made his arguments appear as a body of doctrine, systematically ordered by topic, with the political notes enthroned at the centre. Generations of readers absorbed, along with the arguments, a map of their relative importance that was drawn in Turin and London rather than in the cell at Turi. When the critical edition redistributed that importance, Anglophone discussion lagged by decades, partly because the selection's readers had no way to see what had moved. The words edition dependency name exactly that lag: not an error by the translators, whose work was exemplary, but a structural property of meeting a corpus through a selection of a rearrangement.

The translation itself, at the level of the sentence, is the selection's glory and requires one caution. Hoare and Nowell Smith translated from the thematic texts, so where the thematic printing had regularised or chosen between variants, the English inherits the choice invisibly. They also decoded as they translated, frequently printing the referent where the manuscript has the costume, a kindness to beginners that erases the censorship evidence discussed in this guide's early sections. And their rendering of the key terms settled into English usage with the force of standard versions, so that debates conducted in English sometimes turn on a translator's solution to an Italian ambiguity that the critical text leaves visibly open. None of this is grounds for discarding the selection. It is grounds for the rule this guide repeats until it becomes reflex: cite the selection for access, verify in the critical text for argument, and never let a claim about order, emphasis, or completeness rest on a volume that was designed, honourably and openly, for other purposes.

There is also the question of what the selection did to the corpus's temperature in English. The notebooks in sequence are strange, digressive, philological, frequently concerned with matters, a dialect dictionary, a popular novel, a point of Italian grammar, that no political anthology would keep. The selection's Gramsci is uniformly intense, a political philosopher at full stretch for nearly five hundred pages, and readers formed on it often find the actual corpus, when they reach its miscellanies, disconcertingly miscellaneous. The disappointment is diagnostic. It measures the distance between the author and his anthology, and a reader who has felt it has learned the edition dependency in the body, which is where such lessons stay learned.

## The Buttigieg edition: eight notebooks in full

The critical English edition, published by Columbia University Press in the edition prepared by Joseph A. Buttigieg, who edited and translated the text with collaborators, undertakes to give English readers what Gerratana gave Italian ones: the whole corpus, notebook by notebook, with apparatus, draft stages marked, and the coordinate system of notebook and paragraph numbers carried over intact. Three volumes appeared between 1992 and 2007, carrying the text through the first eight notebooks. There the published English critical text stops, and the remaining twenty-five notebooks, which include most of the special notebooks and the great majority of the corpus's famous material, await the project's continuation. Readers should establish the project's publication position from the publisher's catalogue rather than from any secondary source, this guide included, since a multi-decade scholarly enterprise changes its state between any two descriptions of it.

### Why does the critical English edition cover only the opening notebooks?

Because it proceeds notebook by notebook in the corpus's order, translating the complete text with its full apparatus, and that pace, sustained across three volumes over fifteen years, has so far carried it through the eighth notebook. Completeness per notebook was chosen over coverage of the whole, deliberately.

The choice embodied in those three volumes deserves a moment's respect, because it inverts the selection's philosophy exactly. The selection gave English readers the famous Gramsci, concentrated and arranged. The Buttigieg volumes give whoever will sit still for them the actual Gramsci, in sequence, including the translation exercises, the reading notes in their full strangeness, the false starts, and the slow formation of the questions the famous passages later answer. Reading the first eight notebooks in this form is the single most corrective experience available to an English reader formed on the selection, precisely because so little of the canonical material is there. What is there is the workshop: the prisoner's methods of reading, excerpting, translating, and worrying at problems, and the early appearances, in draft, of themes whose C versions sit in notebooks the English critical text has not reached. The edition dependency acquires, in these volumes, an almost experimental purity. The same reader, meeting the same author in the selection and in the critical text, meets two different writers, and the difference is attributable to nothing but editions.

The volumes' apparatus also performs, for English readers, the decoding service this guide described as eyesight. Persons are identified, including those the costume concealed; books excerpted are traced; draft relations are stated; the Italian text's difficulties are flagged where translation had to choose. A citation taken from these volumes, with notebook and paragraph numbers, is checkable by any scholar on earth against Gerratana's text, and the check will land on the same words. That interoperability, humble as it sounds, is what separates a scholarly instrument from a reading edition, and its absence from the selection is the deepest reason the selection cannot bear the weight that quotation culture loads onto it.

The incompleteness cuts the other way as well, and honesty requires the guide to state the resulting predicament plainly. For the notebooks beyond the eighth, the English reader who wants critical standards must work through the Italian critical edition, in whole or in the company of translations of particular notebooks and essays that circulate in journals and collections, of very uneven apparatus. The famous special notebooks, the tenth through the thirteenth above all, exist in English chiefly in the selection's versions, taken from the thematic text. The corpus's most argued-over pages are therefore, in English, its least critically secured. Arguments in English about the modern Prince or the philosophy of praxis notes proceed, at their foundations, on a 1971 selection of a postwar rearrangement, and every participant in those arguments who knows this fact knows also that the ground could shift under particular formulations when a complete critical English text exists. The edition dependency is not an antiquarian scruple. For English readers it is the standing condition of the field.

## The edition dependency, stated and tested

The claim can now be stated in its full form. The edition dependency holds that the Gramsci an English reader meets is determined twice over by editorial acts: first by a rearrangement of the corpus prepared under a political party's direction for that party's strategic purposes, and then by a selection from that rearrangement made for English publication. Any argument that turns on the order of the notebooks' arguments, on the relative emphasis the corpus gives its themes, or on the completeness of what is presented as Gramsci's treatment of a subject, is therefore an argument about editions before it is an argument about Gramsci, and it stands only when it names its edition and survives translation into the critical text. Arguments that do not turn on order, emphasis, or completeness are untouched by the claim, and the guide is careful to leave them untouched: the selection's rendering of a famous passage's doctrine is not impugned by anything said here.

### Why does the order of notes matter?

Because the corpus is a mind in motion, and sequence is the evidence of movement. A note's neighbours, its notebook, and its year tell the reader whether a formulation is a first try, a settled recurrence, or a position under revision, and rearrangement destroys exactly that evidence while leaving the words intact.

Test the claim against cases, since a claim about reading should be made to earn its keep on readings. Consider first the notes on Machiavelli. In the thematic edition they appeared as a treatise, under a title announcing notes on Machiavelli, on politics and on the modern state, and generations read them as the corpus's political centrepiece, a freestanding theory of the party as modern Prince. In the critical edition the same notes sit inside the thirteenth notebook among other materials, in the sequence of their writing, visibly continuous with notes on the state, on the Risorgimento, and on the history of political doctrines, and interleaved with the author's other concerns of those months. The doctrine does not change. The reading does: what looked like a self-contained system reveals itself as one strand of a historical investigation, argued through examples, and several celebrated claims about the theory's completeness quietly lose their basis. The words were never the problem. The frame was the argument.

Consider, second, the notes gathered under the philosophy of praxis. The thematic arrangement assembled them into a philosophical treatise and placed that treatise at the front of the corpus's public identity, supporting a reading of Gramsci as primarily a philosopher of a system. The critical order shows the philosophy notes arriving across the whole writing life, thickening around the engagement with Croce and the critique of the popular manual, and interleaved with philology, history, and politics at every stage. Scholars working from the sequence have accordingly redescribed the philosophy as a method being forged inside historical work, rather than a doctrine being applied to it. The dispute between those descriptions is real and continuing, and it cannot even be stated, let alone settled, from within the thematic arrangement, because the arrangement presupposes the first description. That is the edition dependency in its purest form: an editorial decision of the late 1940s silently voting in a scholarly dispute of every decade since.

Consider, third, the question of emphasis by gathering, applied to language. The notes on linguistics, grammar, and language history are extensive in the corpus, the deposit of the author's university training and of his conviction that questions of language are questions of social order in miniature. The thematic edition, whose portrait had little room for the philologist, printed a fraction of them, and Anglophone Gramsci consequently acquired a reputation innocent of one of his deepest competences, until scholars working from the critical text restored the linguistic Gramsci to view and whole subfields adjusted. A reader of only the selection still meets a Gramsci who barely thinks about language. The corpus thinks about little else, some weeks. Between those two figures stands nothing but an edition.

### How can the same passage support two readings?

When one reading takes it from its notebook, dated and placed among its neighbours and its draft, and the other takes it from a thematic chapter where an editor has seated it among passages from other years. The words agree; the evidence surrounding the words, which fixes their force, has been exchanged.

A fourth case touches completeness rather than order, and it is the one English readers feel most directly. Claims of the form Gramsci never addressed this, or Gramsci's treatment of that is cursory, circulate freely in the secondary literature, and a share of them are claims about the selection wearing the corpus's clothes. The translation notebooks, the sustained attention to popular literature and serial fiction, the notes on education in their full extent, the American material before its special-notebook concentration: all exist in the corpus at weights the selection does not transmit. Before any argument from silence is run on this author, the silence must be verified in the critical text, because the selection is silent about most things by design. The rule generalises beyond Gramsci, and the guide states it as one of its transferable yields: with posthumous corpora, absence of evidence in an anthology is evidence only of the anthology.

The dependency also runs forward, into the corpus's future editions, and the guide marks the direction without pretending to a forecast. Digital publication of the manuscripts and their transcriptions, national editions of the writings, and the continuation of the Columbia project each promise to move the evidential ground again, and arguments stated with their editions named will survive those movements, while arguments stated as though the text were a fixed star will need silent repair. The reader is not asked to wait for a final edition, since final editions of living archives are a mirage. The reader is asked to build the habit of the named edition now, on the texts that exist, which is a habit that transfers intact to whatever succeeds them.

## The pedantry objection, at full strength

The strongest objection to everything above deserves to be made by its best advocate, and it runs as follows. The notebooks are already among the most difficult texts in the tradition, written in code, in fragments, across an alien scholarly landscape. To surround them with a second discipline of notebook numbers, draft letters, edition genealogies, and citation protocols is to raise the drawbridge against the very readers, students, activists, generalists of every kind, whom the author wrote to form. The selection, whatever its lineage, has served generations perfectly well: it carried the arguments into the world's classrooms, founded research programmes, and changed how several disciplines think. A reader who has grasped hegemony from Hoare and Nowell Smith has grasped something real, and the scholar who replies that the grasp is edition-dependent has confused the conditions of perfect knowledge with the conditions of knowledge. Philology, on this view, is a guild's toll charged on a public road, and the toll deters more travellers than it protects.

### Does the apparatus deter ordinary readers?

It can, when it is worn as a badge rather than used as a tool. A reader frightened away by sigla has been failed by the scholarship's manners, not by its findings. The apparatus exists so that trust can be checked, and a text whose trust cannot be checked is the one that should deter.

The answer begins with a concession, plainly made. For a first reading, the selection remains the practical entry point in English, and this guide recommends it as such without embarrassment. Nobody should begin with Gerratana's apparatus, and nobody needs a draft letter to feel the force of the famous passages. The objection is right about beginnings and wrong about what follows, and the difference is the whole of the guide's case. The protocols bind at the point of claim, not at the point of encounter. Read where you like; the dependency wakes when you quote, teach, build an argument, or report the corpus's emphases to others, because at that point your reader is trusting your transmission, and the question of what your edition did to the text becomes a question about your work, not the prisoner's. The toll, to borrow the objection's metaphor, is charged only on freight, not on walking.

Specific cases, rather than principles, are the answer's second movement, and the cases section above has supplied them: a treatise that was a strand, a philosophy that was a method in formation, a silence that was a selection. In each case the misreading was not a beginner's stumble but the considered position of serious scholars working in good faith from the editions they had, and in each case the correction arrived from the archive, not from cleverer interpretation of the same pages. The objection asks what the apparatus is for. It is for the difference between those outcomes. A guild's toll is charged for the guild's benefit; this apparatus charges a paragraph of checking and pays out the ability to know when an editor, however honourable, has been thinking in your place. Readers who find the price high for their purposes may decline it, and read the selection with pleasure and profit. Writers may not decline it on their readers' behalf.

There is a final turn of the answer, and it concerns the author rather than the reader. Gramsci spent his rationed strength, in failing health, performing the first act of this discipline upon himself: returning to his drafts, reworking them into the special notebooks, correcting his own emphases, refusing to let the miscellaneous first word stand as the last. The draft apparatus that the objection finds so forbidding is nothing but the fossil record of the author's own refusal to treat his notes as a finished book. To honour the refusal by reading with the stages visible is not pedantry imposed on the text from outside. It is the text's own practice, extended by its editors, and the reader who learns it is apprenticed to the prisoner at his desk rather than to any guild.

## Before you cite: a reader's protocol

The protocol gathers the guide's teaching into a sequence of checks short enough to be used at a desk. Run it on any fragment before the fragment is quoted, assigned, or made to carry weight in an argument, and run it with special rigour on the famous fragments, since fame is no certificate of critical security. The checks move from the object in hand to the claim being made, and each corresponds to a failure mode documented in the sections above.

Identify the edition first, by editor and publisher, and write the identification into your notes before anything else. A quotation whose edition is unknown is a quotation whose evidence is unknown, and the two minutes spent establishing it are the cheapest scholarship in this entire field. If the edition is the English selection, mark the quotation as selection-grade: fit for conveying doctrine, unfit, unverified, for claims about order, emphasis, or completeness. If the edition is thematic in any language, the same mark applies, with the additional caution that silent editorial choices may separate its sentences from the manuscripts' at points the page does not signal. Only a critical text, Gerratana's or Buttigieg's where it reaches, supports the full range of claims.

Second, locate the fragment by notebook and paragraph number, and record the coordinates wherever the quotation is stored. The coordinates are the corpus's addressing system, recognised across languages and editions, and they convert a quotation from a souvenir into a findable object. The discipline of citation that the coordinates belong to is treated in this series' guide to [citing marx and engels](/2021/12/15/citing-marx-and-engels/), whose protocols for posthumous and edited texts apply to the notebooks with only the names changed. A quotation that cannot be given coordinates should be treated as unverified until it can, however many websites repeat it.

Third, establish the draft stage. Determine whether the fragment is an A text with a C revision standing over it, a B text written once, or itself a C text in a special notebook, and if an A and C pair exists, read both before quoting either. Where the pair disagrees, the C text is the author's later word and an honest quotation says which stage it is using. This check is performable only in the critical editions, which is one more reason arguments of any ambition migrate there. It is also the check that quotation databases and excerpt collections most reliably fail, since their harvest is indifferent to stages by design.

Fourth, decode before interpreting. Run the substitution check over the fragment's key terms: the philosophy of praxis, the founder, the titled and initialled persons, the historical examples doing contemporary work. Ask what the sentence says when the costume is named, and whether your interpretation survives the naming. Where the fragment's force depends on a term's ordinary meaning, suspect the reading, because the code's pressure deformed ordinary meanings throughout the lexicon. The critical apparatus and the selection's notes both help here, the selection silently, the apparatus explicitly, and the difference between silent and explicit help is itself worth a line in a careful writer's notes.

Fifth, check the neighbours. Read the notes immediately around the fragment in its notebook, and establish the local weather: the book being excerpted, the polemic being answered, the example being tested. Fragments in this corpus are moves in arguments whose other moves are usually on the same or facing pages in the manuscript order, and thematic editions, by extracting the move and discarding the game, license the most confident misreadings in the literature. Ten minutes with the neighbours is the difference between quoting a position and quoting a step toward one.

The protocol's working home, for readers who keep their scholarship in files, is a citation card set keyed to notebook numbers, one card per fragment, carrying the coordinates, the edition, the draft stage, the decoded terms, and the neighbour notes, so that a quotation's evidence travels with it from desk to draft. The study notes system at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is built for exactly this kind of keyed card work, and candidates preparing for examinations that set this author will find the companion discipline in the [upsc pyq explorer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html), where the questions asked across past papers show which fragments the examiners keep returning to, and therefore which cards deserve the protocol's full rigour first. Students are sent onward to the cluster's exam guide in the persona section below; the protocol is the bridge, since an answer that cites by notebook and stage reads to a marker as scholarship rather than as anthology.

## The notebooks among edited texts

Nothing about the notebooks' editorial condition is unique, and placing them among their peers in this series sharpens what is distinctive. The German Ideology survived as abandoned manuscripts whose editors had to construct a text from drafts its authors never finished; the Paris manuscripts reached print through an editorial frame that shaped their reception for decades; the Dialectics of Nature is an editor's assembly of a book its author never wrote. The parallel editorial problem across the tradition is examined in the guide to the [mega2 marx manuscripts guide](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/), where the great historical-critical edition of Marx and Engels supplies the standard against which other editorial regimes are measured. The notebooks' case is the strongest of the family for one reason: the mediation was not only editorial but political in the direct sense, performed by the author's own party, under its leader's direction, as an act of strategy. The MEGA editors answer to philology. Platone and Togliatti answered also to history as they were making it, and the edition they produced bears both loyalties in its design.

The comparison also disciplines an overreaction the guide has been careful to prevent. Editorial mediation is the normal condition of posthumous political writing, not a scandal attaching to this corpus, and the reader who finishes this article unable to trust any edition has misunderstood it as thoroughly as the reader who trusts every edition equally. The critical editions earn trust by showing their working; the thematic edition earned its generation's trust by giving it a usable Gramsci at the moment of need; the selection earns a beginner's trust by the quality of its translations and notes. Trust in this field is not a verdict but a calibration, matched to the claim being made, and the edition map exists to make the calibration quick. The scholarly world's slow migration of its citations from thematic to critical coordinates, across the decades since 1975, is what calibrated trust looks like at the scale of a literature.

## The letters as companion

The Prison Letters, published in 1947, a year before the notebooks' first volume, are the corpus's companion and in some respects its key. More than four hundred letters survive, written to Tatiana, to his wife Giulia, to his mother, to his children, and they were composed under the same censorship as the notebooks, a fact the reader must hold as firmly for the intimate texts as for the theoretical ones. The letters' famous warmth, irony, and stoicism are real, and they are also performances for several audiences at once: the family being reassured, the prisoner keeping himself intact, and the censor being given nothing to seize. Reading the letters naively, as transparent autobiography, repeats with the correspondence the error this guide has corrected for the notebooks, mistaking a censored surface for an unmediated one.

Used with that caution, the letters do three kinds of work no other source does. They document the writing's conditions from inside: requests for books and paper, reports of reading, the study plan of 1927, the running account of a body failing by instalments, so that the notebooks' chronology acquires a human timescale. They supply the author's own statements of intent at dates the notebooks can be checked against, including the ambitions and second thoughts that never became notes. And they train the reader's ear for the code's register, since the letters practise, on family matters, the same arts of indirection, understatement, and saying by not saying that the notebooks practise on theory. A reader who has learned to hear the letters will not read the notebooks' plainest sentence at face value again, which is the beginning of competence in this corpus.

The letters have their own editorial history, shorter and quieter than the notebooks' but not absent: selections, family pieties, and the slow completion of the correspondence in scholarly editions all mediate them too. The guide's protocol applies, mutatis mutandis, and the reader who cites a letter should name its edition with the same reflex as for a notebook fragment. What the letters finally offer the notebooks' reader is proportion. The corpus can make its author sound like a system with a biography attached. The letters restore the proportions of a life in which the system was written in the margins of catastrophe, in exercise books, between a toothache and a censored parcel, by a man writing to his small sons about hedgehogs and the sea. The theory is not diminished by the restoration. It is correctly weighted at last, as the work of a prisoner who knew exactly how much of it the future would be allowed to see, and who wrote, for good, anyway.

## Beginning: where a new reader starts

A first path through the corpus, for the reader this guide has been equipping, can be stated briefly because the equipment matters more than the order. Begin in the selection, honestly labelled as such, with the notes on the intellectuals and on the modern Prince, and read them for the arguments. Then go back through the same material in whatever critical form is reachable, Gerratana in a library, Buttigieg for the notebooks he covers, and watch what the sequence and the apparatus do to passages already met: the exercise, performed once, teaches the edition dependency more durably than any exposition, this one included. From there, read by notebook rather than by theme for a stretch, one miscellaneous notebook whole, to acquire the corpus's native weather of digression and return. Readers assembling a course or a reading programme will find the corpus placed among the tradition's texts in the [marxism reading list](/2021/09/15/marxism-reading-list/), and the concept that draws most readers here is treated whole in the hegemony guide already linked at this article's opening, which is the right destination once the fragments that carry it can be handled with the protocol's care.

The path ends where the guide began, with the reader alone before a quotation, in a book, an article, a feed, an examination. The quotation will be famous, or will want to be. It will arrive without coordinates, in a translation whose edition goes unnamed, possibly in words its author replaced with better ones in a notebook the quotation has never heard of. Everything in this article exists for that moment. Ask the three questions. Which edition. Which notebook, at what stage. And what the words meant to the censor who read them first. A reader who asks them has already joined the small company of those who read the Prison Notebooks rather than their reputation, and the corpus, which waited out a prison and an archive for its readers, can wait out the extra ten minutes the questions cost.

What follows this opening guidance is the guide's evidence, arranged in the order a reader needs it: the corpus's nature, its conditions of writing, its code, its strata and stages, its survival, and then the four editions in turn, with the claim of the edition dependency stated, tested against cases, and defended against the strongest objection that can be made to it. Readers in a hurry may go straight to the edition map and the citation protocol and return for the rest; the map and the protocol are designed to stand alone, and the sections between them exist to make both believable. Readers with a scholarly formation will find the walk through the sequence, the Pascal parallel, and the account of what the critical restoration changed in the discipline's questions addressed to their concerns. Nothing in the arrangement is decorative. Each part answers the objection the previous part invites, and the whole is built, as the notebooks themselves were built, by accumulation and return, so that a concept met early, the costume, the stage, the arrangement, comes back later with its weight increased. That is the nearest a guide can come to teaching by the corpus's own method, and it is deliberate.

## The sequence walked: what the notebooks contain, in their order

A reader equipped with the strata, the stages, and the map deserves one walk through the corpus in its own direction, since the sequence is this guide's hero and has so far been described more than shown. The walk is selective by necessity. It follows the notebooks' numbering as the critical edition established it, and it pauses where the sequence teaches something that no thematic table of contents can.

The first notebooks are the workshop being built. Translations occupy much of the opening labour, renderings from German and Russian that exercised the prisoner's languages and stocked his mind with other men's arguments in his own Italian. Around the translations, the miscellaneous notes begin: observations on Italian history, on journalism, on popular literature, on the southern question's long aftermath, brief and provisional, the record of a reader finding his questions again after the silence of arrest and transfer. Already the hand is doing two things at once, storing and testing, and already the notes refuse to sort themselves into the chapters later editors would build from them. A reader who starts here learns patience, which the corpus rewards at every stage.

By the third and fourth notebooks the characteristic movement is visible. Historical examples arrive in runs, the French Revolution, the Risorgimento, the history of Italian intellectuals, and against them the theoretical notes begin to thicken: the relation of philosophy to common sense, the status of the popular manual of the theory, the first sustained wrestling with the problems that the eleventh notebook would later organise. The famous observation that a crisis consists in the old dying while the new cannot be born belongs to this stratum, a single note among notes on the past and the present, and its anthology afterlife, severed from the notebook's surrounding examinations of interregnum and Caesarism, is a small parable of everything this guide has argued. In sequence it is a historian thinking. In extraction it became a slogan, available for any crisis at all, which is a fate its author, who wrote it about a specific pathology of transition, would have recognised as the common fate of phrases.

The middle notebooks deepen the two great engagements that organise the corpus's centre. Croce is met on his own ground, the history of Europe and of Italy as cultural history, in an argument that runs for years and notebooks. The Risorgimento becomes a laboratory: how a national revolution was made by moderates, what the Action Party failed to become, how intellectuals were formed and deployed in a society whose masses were spectators of their own unification. These notes, gathered by the thematic edition into a volume on the Risorgimento, sit in the sequence amid philosophy and philology, and their neighbours explain their method: the historical work is where the concepts are earned, tested against archives rather than asserted. A reader travelling in order watches the concepts of hegemony and passive revolution being smelted in this furnace, note by note, long before the political notebooks state them in concentrated form.

The special notebooks, from the tenth onward in the thematic heart of the sequence, are the author's return upon himself, and the walk has already described their census: Croce in the tenth, the study of philosophy in the eleventh, intellectuals and schooling in the twelfth, Machiavelli and politics in the thirteenth. Walking them in order adds one perception the census cannot give. The reworking is not neutral copying. The C texts breathe differently from their A drafts: examples are pruned, formulations are generalised one careful step, and the polemical temperature is lowered as the strategic temperature rises, the prisoner's late style. Where the special notebooks were never made, in the themes illness interrupted, the miscellaneous treatment stands alone, and the corpus's map of its author's priorities must be drawn with that interruption marked, or the drawing lies.

Beyond the thirteenth, the sequence continues into territories the selection taught English readers to undervalue: the long nineteenth notebook on the Risorgimento, returning to the laboratory with the political concepts now in hand; the twenty-second on Americanism and Fordism, where the notes on the new industrial civilisation, on the sexual question, on the formation of a new type of worker, compose the corpus's strangest and most prophetic pages; the twenty-fifth on subaltern groups, with its austere methodological programme for histories written from below. Then the writing thins. The last notebooks show the hand and the strength failing together, notes shortening, projects contracting to memoranda. The sequence ends not with a conclusion but with a cessation, and the critical edition's honesty about that ending, its refusal to arrange a finale the author never wrote, is the final argument for reading in order at least once. The thematic edition gave the corpus a shape like a shelf of treatises, complete and closed. The sequence gives it the shape of a life, which is the shape it actually has.

## Counting the corpus: notebooks, pages, and the mythology of numbers

The figures attached to this corpus attract a mythology of their own, and the guide's protocol for contested numbers applies to them with full force. Thirty-three notebooks is the working count of the critical tradition, and within it the composition varies by reckoning: notebooks of translations, notebooks of miscellaneous notes, notebooks of special composition, and the boundary cases argued in the apparatus. The page total, roughly two thousand eight hundred manuscript pages in the critical edition's presentation, is likewise a count with conventions inside it, depending on how partially filled pages and the translation exercises are reckoned. Scholars cite these figures from the critical edition's introduction, where the counting is performed in the open. Popular accounts cite them from each other, and in transit the approximations harden into spurious precision, acquiring extra notebooks, missing notebooks, and romantic losses that the inventory does not support.

The lost-notebook mythology deserves its sentence of correction, because it feeds a genre of speculation the evidence cannot nourish. Tatiana's inventory and the Moscow deposit give the corpus a chain of custody unusually complete for a prisoner's papers, and the critical editors worked from a bundle whose extent was documented at the author's death. Claims that further notebooks were written and destroyed, suppressed, or withheld belong to the literature of legend unless they can be brought against that documentation, and the apparatus records the argument where it has been seriously made. The honest uncertainties are drier and more interesting: the dating of particular notebooks within spans of months, the order of some sequences, the identification of some hands and sources. A literature's maturity shows in which uncertainties it finds exciting.

Counting the notes themselves, as opposed to the notebooks, produces a different and instructive instability. The corpus's paragraph numbers run into the thousands across the thirty-three notebooks, yet what counts as one note is itself an editorial perception, since the manuscripts' separations, by spacing, by numbering in the author's hand in places, by changes of ink and subject, do not always coincide with the printed sections. Gerratana's sectioning is the standard the world's citations use, and it is, like the rest of that edition, an argued reconstruction rather than a transcription of obvious joints. The practical moral returns to the protocol. Cite the coordinates as the critical edition gives them, since the coordinate system is a convention the whole field shares, and conventions shared are what make verification possible. A citation is not weakened by resting on a convention. It is weakened by resting on nothing findable at all.

## Translation, the second costume

Every argument in this guide about costume applies a second time, in English, at the level of the word. The notebooks were written in a coded Italian; they are read, in the Anglophone world, in a translated English whose choices were made by particular hands at particular dates, and those choices set the terms in which whole debates have been conducted. The selection's translators faced the coded lexicon and resolved it toward readability, often printing the referent where the manuscript wears the costume, as this guide has noted. They also faced the ordinary terrors of the text: a Sardinian intellectual's Italian, saturated with the vocabularies of philosophy, philology, military metaphor, and party slang, written at speed, in a cell, without a reference library. That the resulting English is as lucid as it is constitutes one of the great unthanked services in the tradition's transmission. It does not make the translation transparent, and the reader's protocol gains a clause from the fact: where an English argument turns on a single word, the Italian must be consulted, in the critical text, before the argument proceeds.

Certain words carry whole disputes inside them. The Italian senso comune names both the ordinary thinking of a social stratum and, in the corpus's usage, a sedimented philosophy to be criticised and renovated, while buon senso names the healthy nucleus within it that deserves development; English common sense flattens the pair toward a single commendation the author never intended, and generations of English readers have met a folksier Gramsci than the Italian wrote. Egemonia moves between leadership, direction, and hegemony in English mouths, and the choice among them is an interpretation, since the corpus itself plays the word against dominio, domination, in a contrast that single-word translation cannot hold steady. Società civile and stato carry a Hegelian and Marxian prehistory that civil society and state only partly import. None of this indicts the translators, who flagged what they could in notes and glossaries. It indicts only the reader who supposes that translation is a window rather than a second edition, with an editor's hand in every pane.

The Buttigieg volumes, translating the critical text, make different choices from the selection's at many of these points, and the differences are visible wherever the two Englishes overlap, which is itself an education: set the versions side by side on a contested term and the term's Italian range, wider than either rendering, comes into view. Scholars of the corpus in English increasingly work trilingually in effect, among the Italian, the selection's English, and the critical English, and the practice should be named as the standard it is becoming. A claim that cannot survive the crossing back into Italian is a claim about a translation, and there is no shame in such claims, provided they are labelled. The shame, in this field as in the edition question generally, attaches only to the unlabelled.

There is a deeper symmetry worth pausing on, because it closes the section's circle. Gramsci was himself a translator in the cell, working from German and Russian as an exercise of possession, and his translation notebooks show the same problems from the inside: how to carry a concept across a border without naturalising it, how to let a foreign term stay foreign where its foreignness is the point. His own practice, literal where possible, annotated where necessary, reads like a rebuke in advance to every smooth version of his own text, including the ones this guide has praised. The corpus thus contains, in its first stratum, the theory of its own transmission problem, written by the man who would become the problem's most famous victim. A reader who notices that has understood something about the notebooks that no summary transmits: they are a text that foresaw its readers' difficulties, including the difficulty of reading it.

## The philologist in the cell: the notes on language

No stratum of the corpus better demonstrates the thematic edition's power to reweight an author than the notes on language. Gramsci came to prison as a formed linguist: a scholarship boy from Sardinia who had studied at Turin under Matteo Bartoli, one of the founders of neolinguistics, and who nearly took the academic path before politics claimed him. The notebooks return to language constantly: to dialect and national language, to grammar normative and spontaneous, to Esperanto and translation theory, to the question of how a linguistic innovation spreads from a centre of prestige through a population, which he treats as a model for how any cultural innovation, a philosophy included, propagates through a society. The linguistic notes are not a hobby interleaved with the politics. They are one of the places where the politics' central mechanism, the winning of consent through prestige and education rather than through command, is worked out in its purest observable form.

The thematic arrangement, building its portrait of the national political theorist, printed a selection of these notes and scattered the rest, and the selection's English compounded the reduction. The result was an Anglophone Gramsci whose linguistics figured as biographical colour, the young man's abandoned discipline, rather than as a load-bearing wall of the mature method. The critical edition restored the stratum's extent, and scholarship followed: the linguistic Gramsci, the theorist of grammar as a world-view's sediment and of language change as hegemony's laboratory, is now a recognised centre of the field, with the late notebook devoted to the study of grammar standing as his most concentrated statement. The episode is this guide's cleanest exhibit of emphasis by gathering, because no one involved acted in bad faith and the distortion was nonetheless total: an author was misdescribed for decades, in the proportions of his own mind, by a table of contents.

The language notes also return the reader to the code with new understanding. A linguist in a cell, professionally aware that meanings are social facts policed by institutions, built his substitution system with a craftsman's knowledge of how vocabularies carry authority. The philosophy of praxis was chosen as a scholar chooses a term that will survive in the mouths of others. When the guide earlier called the code a change of costume that became a change of method, the linguistics notes are where that becoming can be watched: the prisoner thinking about how words bind and loose social groups while binding his own words for survival. Editions that marginalise the stratum do not merely omit a topic. They remove the workshop in which the corpus's most famous instrument was forged, and with it the reader's best evidence that the code was a theory in action and not a prisoner's trick.

## The manuscripts themselves: handwriting, facsimile, and the national edition

Behind every edition stands the paper, and the paper has a scholarship of its own that a serious reader should know exists. The manuscripts, held in Rome in the keeping of the foundation that bears the author's name and its institute, are exercise books of standard manufacture, their pages covered in a hand that the critical editors learned to date almost forensically: letterforms, ink, pressure, and the progressive deterioration that illness imposed, all serve as evidence for the sequence the printed edition asserts. Facsimile reproductions, long the privilege of visiting scholars, have in the era of digital libraries been progressively opened to remote readers through the foundation's reproductions, and the reader who wants to see what a page of the corpus actually looks like, the crossings, the marginalia, the cramped late hand, should pursue those reproductions through the foundation rather than trust any description, this guide's included, of their effect.

The effect, reported consistently by those who make the pilgrimage from print to paper, is a recalibration of scale. The printed critical edition's four thousand pages of text and apparatus suggest a monument. The exercise books suggest a schoolboy's satchel, which is what they were: the entire theoretical legacy of a tradition, carried out of a prison in a sister-in-law's custody, small enough to hide, light enough to smuggle. The physical modesty of the object is not a sentimental detail. It explains the corpus's compression, its mnemonic density, its habit of the loaded phrase and the example made to work three shifts, all of them adaptations to a ration of paper and strength. A reader who has seen the pages reads the aphorisms differently afterwards, as feats of packing.

Scholarly Italy has also undertaken a national edition of the author's writings as a whole, the kind of state-sponsored complete works that nations reserve for their classics, and its volumes proceed on critical principles with the letters and the pre-prison writings in their own series. Its existence matters here for one reason: it marks the corpus's final institutional transformation, from a party's patrimony to a nation's classic, with all the gains in editorial resource and all the risks of monumental freezing that such transformations bring. The reader should establish the national edition's published extent from the foundation and the publishers, not from secondary accounts, and should expect the notebooks' text within it to refine, rather than replace, the Gerratana coordinates on which the world's citations run. Canons are not made by decree, and the coordinate system's forty years of accumulated scholarship will not be lightly re-based. But the direction of travel in this field, for a century now, has been from the movement's Gramsci toward the archive's, and the national edition is that direction's largest single monument.

## Five misreadings and their editorial causes

The guide has corrected its misreadings as they arose; gathering them exposes the pattern that makes the collection worth more than its parts. Each error below is a specific, documented habit of the secondary literature and the quotation culture, and each has an editorial cause that can be named, which is what makes the errors corrigible rather than merely regrettable.

The first misreading takes the notebooks for a book, with an author's design and a reader's licence to set any page against any other. Its cause is bibliographical innocence reinforced by print: a bound volume behaves like a book whatever its contents confess. The correction is the corpus's biography: exercise books, a censor, an unfinished programme, an editor between every reader and the paper. Once the correction lands, a family of interpretive habits, quoting across decades as though across chapters, weighing a jotting against a treatise, falls with it.

The second misreading takes the thematic arrangement for the author's architecture, and writes about the corpus's system, its balance of parts, its philosophy-first design. Its cause is the 1948 edition's success: arrangements that serve readers brilliantly become invisible to them, and the treatise titles were received as the author's own chapter headings for a generation. The correction is the sequence, in which the parts arrive as life supplied them and the system dissolves into a practice. Scholars who now describe the thought's architecture take care to say which edition's architecture they mean, and the care dates, precisely, from the critical edition's appearance.

The third misreading takes the English selection for the complete work, and argues from its proportions and its silences. Its cause is the most forgivable of the five: the selection was for decades the only Gramsci most of the Anglophone world could buy, borrow, or assign, and its very excellence disguised its partiality. The correction is arithmetic plus apparatus: a fifth or so of the corpus by bulk, chosen through a rearrangement, without the draft strata. Every claim of the form the author never treated a subject must now survive a search of the critical text before it is repeated, and the literature is slowly, unevenly, acquiring that reflex.

The fourth misreading takes philosophy of praxis for a doctrine, the author's personal alternative to the theory he inherited, and builds accounts of his originality on the term. Its cause is the code misread as a coinage: a reader innocent of censorship sees a philosopher naming his system, where the informed see a prisoner renaming his allegiance. The correction is the substitution system and its Labriolan pedigree, set out in this guide's decoding sections. Originality the author has in abundance, in method, in the historical work, in the concepts forged under costume; it does not need, and is not served by, a foundation myth about a phrase.

The fifth misreading gives every passage equal authority, quoting a first draft against its own revision, a B text's aphorism as a settled doctrine, a note its author abandoned as his last word. Its cause is the flatness of print before the apparatus: on the thematic page, and still more on the quotation site, all fragments wear the same type. The correction is the draft scheme, A superseded, B single, C revised, and the discipline of asking after a fragment's stage before asking after its meaning. Of the five errors this is the one the digital quotation culture now commits at industrial scale, and the one whose correction costs least: a single question, asked of a database or a book, before the copying begins.

Read as a set, the five misreadings teach the guide's thesis better than its arguments do. Not one of them is an error of intelligence or of politics. Every one is an error of transmission, produced by good editions doing their jobs and readers mistaking the job for the text. The tradition that reads this author has never lacked cleverness. It has lacked, at times, the habit of asking what stands between it and the exercise books in Rome. The habit is the whole of the edition dependency, and it generalises to every posthumous corpus the reader will ever meet, which is why this article, in a series about a theory, has spent its length on paper, ink, and other people's tables of contents.

## One fragment, worked whole: the inventory of traces

Abstractions about protocol persuade few readers; a single fragment, carried through the protocol in public, persuades more. Take the most famous methodological passage in the corpus, the opening of the notes on the study of philosophy in the eleventh notebook, where the author writes that the starting point of critical elaboration is knowing oneself as the product of a historical process that has deposited in each person an inventory of traces, and that the first philosophical act is to compile that inventory. In the selection the passage stands as a polished manifesto, much quoted in education and cultural studies, its force apparently aphoristic and complete.

Now run the checks. The edition of the polished version is the special notebook, which is to say the passage is a C text, the author's revision, and the critical edition's cross-references lead backward to its earlier form in a miscellaneous notebook, where the same thought arrives more tentatively, embedded in a different argument about the history of philosophy, and carrying qualifications the revision pruned. The draft does not contradict the revision; it shows the revision working, tightening a psychological observation into a methodological programme, and a reader who has seen both knows the famous sentences as the end of a process rather than a tablet from the mountain. The code check matters as much as the stage check. The passage's key nouns, philosophy, history, traces, are the permitted vocabulary doing its double duty, and the sentence's apparent abstraction from politics is the costume at its most elegant: a theory of how consciousness is historically made, stated so that a censor read a meditation. Finally, the neighbours: in the eleventh notebook the passage opens a sustained engagement with the popular manual of the theory, and its programme of self-inventory is aimed, among other targets, at readers who imagine their ideas are their own because they have never audited their provenance. Quoted in the wild, the passage is used to bless exactly that imagining. The protocol does not impoverish the fragment. It returns the fragment's teeth.

The worked example also shows what the protocol is not. It is not a demand that every classroom quotation carry a paragraph of apparatus, nor a claim that the selection's readers have been reading a forgery. The passage in the selection is genuine, well translated, and recognisably the author's. What the checks add is resolution: the difference between a quotation as decoration and a quotation as evidence. Scholars, teachers, and candidates are paid, in their different currencies, for resolution. The general reader may take the passage as it comes and be honestly served. The guide's quarrel has never been with reading. It is with claiming, and the inventory passage, whose whole doctrine is that unexamined inheritances govern the unexamining, is the fitting place to end the demonstration: the reader who quotes it without auditing its provenance refutes it in the act of quoting, and the reader who audits first has already begun to obey it.

## What the editions cannot tell us

Philology's honesty includes an account of its own limits, and a guide drunk on apparatus would fail its subject. There are things about this corpus that no edition, present or projected, can recover, and the reader should know their names so as not to ask the critical text for miracles.

The dating of the notes, however refined, remains a band and not a diary. Editors argue from hand, ink, paper, and internal reference to place notebooks within spans of months, and notes within notebooks in an order the manuscripts support rather than dictate. Arguments that require a formulation to precede a particular external event by weeks, rather than by seasons, ask the evidence for more than it holds, and the literature contains celebrated instances of such over-asking. The apparatus is candid about its certainties; readers should import the candour along with the dates.

The conversations are gone. The corpus is one side of a dialogue conducted with books, with correspondents, with the author's own earlier journalism, and with a movement whose living voices reached the cell only as print and report. What Gramsci would have said to an objection he never met, in a seminar he never held, is unrecoverable, and the notebooks' afterlife as a system owes something to the vacuum: fragments unresisted by their author's replies can be arranged into doctrines he might have disowned by the second question. Equally gone are any notes he may have destroyed, withheld from the bundles, or never written because the body or the censor forbade that week's work. The archive's silence at those points is not evidence about the thought, in either direction, and the protocol's discipline about silence applies to the critical text's silences as much as to the selection's.

Last, the author's intention toward the corpus itself is a documented ambiguity that editions cannot settle. The special notebooks show a writer moving toward composition, gathering themes, revising for a reader beyond the cell. Whether that reader was a posterity he imagined, a party he hoped would be refounded, or simply himself at a later reading, the manuscripts do not say, and the letters, which say much, do not say this. Every edition is therefore also a wager about what the corpus was for, the thematic editors wagering on transmission, the critical editors on evidence. The reader who knows the wagers are wagers reads all the editions better, including the best of them, and holds the corpus itself, paper, code, drafts and all, as the only party to the argument that never placed a bet.

## The anthology shelf: the corpus in quotation culture

Between the selection and the database stands a shelf of intermediate forms that the guide should acknowledge, because most encounters with this author happen there. Later anthologies, readers edited for students, thematic selections of the cultural writings and of the pre-prison journalism, have multiplied the available Gramscis, each with its own table of contents and therefore its own emphases. The best of them are honest instruments, introduced by scholars who state their selections' principles. All of them descend from the same base texts this guide has mapped, thematic or critical, and the map's rows grade them without further argument: an anthology drawn from the selection is a selection of a selection of a rearrangement, and its testimony about the corpus is third-hand however distinguished its editor.

The quotation database is the anthology's heir and its pathology in concentrated form. It harvests the famous sentences from whichever text its compiler held, strips the coordinates as a condition of its format, and serves the fragments to writers who cite them onward in perfect good faith. The result is a stratum of floating Gramsci, sentences about hegemony, pessimism and optimism, common sense, and the interregnum, circulating with the author's name and nothing else, some of them accurate, some of them drafts he replaced, some of them translations of translations of the costume, a few of them, in the way of these ecologies, not his at all. The corpus's genuine sentences about how phrases detach from their makers and rule in their stead read, against this backdrop, less like theory than like prophecy.

The defence available to ordinary writers is cheap, and this guide ends its practical teaching by repeating it in its shortest form. A quotation with coordinates can be checked; a quotation without them is a rumour with a famous name. Search the sentence against the notebook and paragraph system before use. If it cannot be found there, it does not enter the argument, however many sites repeat it, because repetition is the one property the corpus's author taught his readers to distrust as a source of authority. The discipline is ten minutes deep. The tradition's founder-figures deserve the ten minutes, and so does every reader downstream of the quotation, who will trust it exactly as far as its teller can be trusted to have checked.

## Reading a miscellaneous notebook from the inside

The protocol tells a reader what to check; it does not teach the physical skill of reading a miscellaneous notebook, which is an acquired craft with its own pleasures. A miscellany argues the way a commonplace book argues, by accretion and return. Its unit is not the chapter but the thread, and threads are picked up and dropped across tens of pages, sometimes across notebooks, as the reading that fed them came and went. The beginner who reads a miscellany straight through, expecting cumulative argument, will pronounce it formless. The reader who learns to track three or four threads at once, noting each recurrence and its slight change of angle, discovers the corpus's real compositional unit and with it the author's pace of thought.

The critical edition's indexes are the instruments of that tracking, and learning to use them is part of the craft. Gerratana's volumes close with apparatus that lets a reader gather every note on a subject, a person, a book, across the whole corpus, and the gathering, performed by hand across the sequence rather than by a thematic chapter, is precisely the exercise the thematic edition made unnecessary and therefore unlearned. The difference between receiving a gathered theme and gathering it is the difference between the two editions pedagogies in miniature. One produces readers who know the conclusions. The other produces readers who know how conclusions in this corpus are reached, and who can therefore be trusted with themes nobody has gathered yet.

A second craft skill is the reading of excerpt and comment. Many notes begin as excerpts or close paraphrases of the book on the prisoner's desk, and turn, sometimes mid-paragraph, into the author's own voice answering the excerpt. Editions help variously here: the critical apparatus identifies sources and marks the seams it can detect, while less equipped printings let excerpt and comment flow as one voice, so that positions the author was examining are absorbed into positions he is taken to hold. The error is documented in the literature and is among the most consequential a fragmentary corpus invites, since the notebooks examine adversaries at length and with sympathy before answering them. A reader's rule follows: in this corpus, attribution inside a note is as important as attribution of the note, and a sentence's owner must be established before its doctrine is assigned.

The third skill is patience of a scholarly kind, for recurrence with variation. When a formulation returns after two hundred pages with one term changed, the change is the news. The prisoner had no search function but memory and his own indexes of intent, and his returns to a phrase are deliberate in a way that a modern writer's, assisted by files, may not be. Critics of the corpus sometimes treat its repetitions as the marks of an unedited draft state, which they are, and stop there. The reader trained by the sequence treats them as the author's own footnotes to himself, the visible record of a mind checking its instruments. Between those two treatments lies most of the distance between the anthology Gramsci and the archival one.

## Croce in the cell: the permitted giant at full length

The engagement with Benedetto Croce deserves, within a reader's guide, more than the paragraph the names section could spare it, because Croce is the corpus's second protagonist and the channel through which much of its philosophy flows. Croce was the dominant Italian intellectual of the age, a liberal philosopher and historian whose neo-idealism had formed the culture the author grew up in, whose journals had published the young Gramsci's generation, and whose authority under the regime was that of a licensed internal opposition: too eminent to silence, too institutionally embedded to ignore. For the prisoner, Croce was therefore three things at once: a genuine philosophical adversary whose historicism had to be answered if the philosophy of praxis was to stand as a philosophy; a permissible subject, safe for the censor in a way no living communist could be; and a mirror, since Croce's account of how ideas rule ages was the nearest rival to the author's own.

The tenth notebook, the special notebook devoted to philosophical problems, conducts the engagement as a siege. Its notes work through Croce's system and his histories, acknowledge his achievement in rescuing Italian culture from positivist flatness, and then press the charge that defines the corpus's Croce: that his history of liberty is a history without the masses who make and suffer it, a religion of liberty for a caste of intellectuals, and that his philosophy, for all its historicism, stops history's motor at the door of his own class. The author spoke, in the prison correspondence and in the notes' programme, of the need for an Anti-Croce, a systematic counterpart that would do for his tradition what Engels's polemic had done against Dühring, and the tenth notebook is that project's surviving torso. Its torso state matters for the reader: the Anti-Croce was never finished, and the corpus's philosophy must be reconstructed from a siege's trenches rather than read from a completed counterpart treatise, one more reason the system the thematic edition presented was an editorial achievement beyond the author's own.

For the guide's purposes, the Croce case adds a refinement to the edition map. The thematic edition placed the Croce material at the head of its first volume, historical materialism and the philosophy of Benedetto Croce, enthroning the engagement as the corpus's philosophical front door. The critical order distributes the Croce notes across the writing life, with the siege's concentration late, after the historical work had built its positions. Readers of the thematic arrangement meet a philosopher-duellist and then a political theorist, in that order. Readers of the sequence meet a historian whose philosophy precipitates out of historical work and returns to it. The difference governs syllabi to this day, and it was decided, irrevocably for two generations, by the order in which six volumes were stacked in Turin.

## How the party edition was made: hands, years, and a strategy

The thematic edition's making rewards the same concrete attention the guide has given the manuscripts, because an edition is an event with personnel and dates, not a weather system. The party that inherited the notebooks after the war's end had to decide, under its leader Togliatti, what its founder's papers were for. The decision taken was transmission with a portrait: the corpus would be published promptly, arranged so that a movement and a nation could read it, and the portrait it would carry was of the national theorist, the philosopher who had met Croce, the analyst of the Risorgimento and of intellectuals, the founder whose thought rooted Italian communism in the country's own history rather than in imported doctrine. That portrait was a strategy for a party contesting the postwar republic's direction, presenting itself as the heir of the national tradition at its most creative. Nothing about the strategy required falsifying the text, and the edition did not set out to falsify. It required selection, arrangement, and emphasis, the three levers this guide has tracked throughout, and through those levers the strategy entered the corpus's bloodstream for decades.

Felice Platone, the editor who performed the work, was a party intellectual of the author's generation, and his labour on the manuscripts was immense and, by the testimony of the critical editors who later retraced it, conscientious within its instructions. The six volumes appeared across four years, from 1948 to 1951, each a substantial book, and their reception made the corpus a European event: translations and discussions followed across the continent, many of them working from the thematic arrangement and reproducing its emphases in other languages, so that the edition's architecture propagated internationally before any alternative existed. In France, in Spain, in Latin America, the first Gramsci was the thematic Gramsci, and the scholarly generations formed on those translations carried the portrait into universities on three continents. The edition dependency, measured at world scale, is largely the radius of that propagation.

The guide's neutrality toward the makers holds, and should be stated once more with its reason attached. The alternative histories are not obviously better. A party that had locked the manuscripts away pending a perfect critical edition, in the conditions of 1947, might have lost them to politics, to war's aftershocks, or to the simple entropy of archives, and would certainly have lost the decades of readers the thematic edition created, among them the scholars who staffed the critical edition's making. Editorial history rarely offers a choice between mediation and purity. It offers choices among mediations, made under pressure, with consequences that outlive the pressures. The critical spirit this article teaches consists not in resenting the choice that was made but in remembering that a choice was made, and in keeping the memory attached to the text wherever the text is used. Gratitude and vigilance are compatible. The corpus's best readers have always practised both.

## The Pascal parallel: what another archive teaches

The notebooks' editorial condition feels singular from inside the tradition, and a comparison outside it is a useful solvent of singularity. Consider the Pensées of Pascal, the most famous fragmentary corpus in European letters. Pascal died leaving bundles of paper slips, some classified by his own hand into labelled packets, most unclassified, toward a work of apologetics he never composed. His editors across three centuries published the fragments in orders of their devising, and the orders produced different Pascals: the rationalist of one arrangement, the fideist of another, each supported by genuine fragments genuinely his. Twentieth-century scholarship transformed the field by returning to the physical evidence of the packets, establishing which fragments the author had himself grouped, and printing an edition that respected the recoverable order. The parallel with the notebooks needs no labouring: a posthumous author, a bundle of fragments, editors whose arrangements are arguments, a critical recovery of sequence, and a scholarly world that discovered its portrait had been painted by a table of contents.

The differences instruct as much as the similarities. Pascal's editors were not his party, and no strategy of state rode on his fragments' order; the distortion available to them was only the ordinary vanity of systematisers. The notebooks' first editors carried a movement's future in their hands, which raised both the stakes of arrangement and the achievement of the critical correction. And Pascal's fragments were destined for a single book whose plan partly survives, while Gramsci's prospectus, the 1927 letter's four topics, covers only the workshop's opening programme, leaving the corpus's intended shape, had health allowed, genuinely unknowable. The comparison therefore ends by returning the reader to this corpus with sharpened sight: edition dependency is a general law of fragmentary classics, and the notebooks are its most politically charged modern case, which is why they repay, as a training ground, more than their own considerable intrinsic interest.

Other mirrors glint at the edges of the comparison and can be acknowledged without being pursued. Nietzsche's Nachlass, arranged by hands with an ideology to sell, taught philosophy the same lesson in a darker key, and the critical edition of his papers redrew a discipline's map exactly as Gerratana's redrew this one's. Wittgenstein's remains, Kafka's rescued manuscripts, the Dickinson fascicles with their editorial smoothings progressively undone by facsimile: the modern reader of any of them learns the notebooks' lesson in another accent. What distinguishes the prison corpus within this company is the censor standing at the point of composition, upstream of all editors: the text was mediated before it was written down, in the very choice of its words. Pascal's slips say what their author meant, in whatever order strangers place them. Gramsci's pages cannot be read at all until a substitution is reversed. The corpus is thus the limiting case of the edited classic, mediated at birth, at survival, at publication, and at translation, and a reader who can hold all four mediations in view while still reading with pleasure and profit has acquired the complete skill this guide exists to teach.

## The corpus in other languages: the dependency repeated

English is not the only language in which the corpus arrived mediated, and a brief survey of its other crossings confirms that the pattern is structural rather than Anglophone. In most languages the first substantial Gramsci arrived through the thematic arrangement or through selections descended from it, since that was the text the postwar decades possessed. French readers received, in due course, a Gallimard edition of the prison writings prepared under the direction of Robert Paris, which followed the critical text and gave French scholarship its own apparatus-based footing; the French case shows a language crossing from the thematic regime to the critical one within a generation, with the predictable lag in which citations from the two regimes collide in the same footnotes. In Spanish and Portuguese, the corpus's immense Latin American and Iberian reception ran for decades on thematic foundations and on selections, and the region's Gramsci, formative for whole schools of political and educational thought, is correspondingly the most portrait-heavy of all, a fact its own scholars have been the first to document and correct.

German readers met the corpus late and partially, through selections whose emphases reflected their editors' debates, and the German critical enterprise on the notebooks proceeded by instalments across decades, so that German-language scholarship long cited the Italian editions directly, a discipline the Anglophone world might envy. The pattern generalises without exception: the order in which a language receives its Gramsci is the order of that language's editions, and the portrait each language carries is its first edition's portrait, slowly corrected where critical texts arrive. International conferences on this author are, at the level of the text, negotiations among editions, and the notebook and paragraph coordinate system is the negotiation's only common currency. That is the deepest practical argument for the coordinates' universal adoption, and for the protocol's insistence that they travel with every quotation across every border.

The digital era adds a final twist that the reader should register without exaggerating. Searchable texts and online excerpts have made the corpus's famous sentences available to anyone in seconds, while the apparatus that grades those sentences remains, in the main, in libraries and paywalled editions. Availability and verifiability have thus decoupled in a way the print centuries never permitted: it has never been easier to quote this author, and never harder, relative to the ease, to quote him responsibly. The guide's protocol is written for exactly that decoupling. It assumes the reader's screen is full of fragments and the nearest critical edition is a bus ride away, and it insists that the bus ride, or its digital equivalent in a licensed text, is the price of a load-bearing claim. Readers who pay it join the corpus's real tradition, which was never the tradition of those who quoted fastest. It was the tradition of a prisoner who checked his sources in a cell, with a ration of paper, and got them right.

## Searching the corpus: names that are not there

A practical section on method belongs in any honest guide to this text, because the corpus defeats the research habits that digital tools instil. The full-text search, applied to the notebooks, lies with a straight face. Search for the founder's name and the results are sparse, because the founder travels under a title. Search for the theory's name and the results approach zero, because the theory travels under a philosophy. Search for the party, the international, the leaders of the author's own movement, and the silence deepens, because the unsayable was the point of the exercise. A researcher formed by databases will conclude, from such searches, that the corpus is oddly impersonal and posthumous to its own tradition, and the conclusion will be an artefact of the code, exactly as the thematic proportions were an artefact of the arrangement. The corpus defends itself against the keyword with the same system it used against the censor, and for the same reason: the names were the danger.

The remedies are the old scholarly ones, refurbished. Search by the substitution system's terms and by the titles of office. Use the critical edition's indexes of names and subjects, compiled by editors who read the whole corpus with pencils, which remain superior instruments to any string search for everything except the verbatim phrase. Read in sequence around a hit, since the paragraph system makes neighbours findable, and let one note's references recruit the next. Where the corpus is consulted in digital form, remember that the digital text descends from one of the map's rows and inherits its grade: a searchable selection is still a selection, and its silences prove nothing. The researcher's persona section has already demanded coordinates in citation; this section adds the matching demand in discovery. Find in the critical text, or do not report the finding as the corpus's.

There is a historiographical moral in the searching problem that the guide does not want to leave implicit. Every technology of access flatters a theory of the text. The thematic edition flattered the theory that the corpus was a system; the selection flattered the theory that it was a doctrine; the database flatters the theory that it is a bag of retrievable sentences. The notebook order flatters no theory, which is why it is trusted: it presents the text as a life of reading and writing, in which systems precipitate and doctrines form and dissolve, and in which retrieval is the last step of understanding rather than the first. A reader who has searched the corpus well ends by reading it, which the prisoner, who had no search but memory and who reread his own notebooks into new ones, would have recognised as the only method he ever had.

## The Risorgimento laboratory: watching a concept precipitate

The cases section showed what rearrangement does to single arguments. The Risorgimento notes show what it does to a whole method, and they are the best place in the corpus to watch a concept being made. The prisoner returned to Italy's nineteenth-century unification for years, across many notebooks, because it was his laboratory case: a national transformation accomplished without a mass revolution, by diplomacy, by Piedmontese expansion, by moderate elites absorbing their opponents in the practice he named transformism, while the Action Party that might have mobilised the countryside failed to become a Jacobin force. Out of that laboratory came the concepts the world now quotes, passive revolution above all, the formula for change accomplished from above, in molecular doses, precisely so that the popular classes remain spectators of their own history.

In the sequence, the precipitation is visible. Early notes try the Risorgimento as narrative, testing the actors and the turning points. Middle notes begin to generalise, comparing the Italian case with France's Jacobin road and with other national paths, and the vocabulary of the laboratory starts to migrate: what was true of Cavour's moderation becomes a question about every revolution that arrives as a reform. The late special treatment, in the nineteenth notebook, states the results with the historical evidence organised beneath them. A reader travelling in order watches a term earn its generality, and learns the discipline the concept itself demands: passive revolution names a specific mechanism with specific evidence, not a synonym for any disappointing change. The inflation of the term in later quotation culture, where it decorates any outcome its user dislikes, is the precise disability that sequence-reading cures.

The thematic edition gathered the Risorgimento into a volume and titled it as a subject, and the gathering, once again, was both a gift and a levy. The gift was a readable book on the unification that Italian historiography itself had to reckon with; the volume taught historians outside the tradition to take the prisoner seriously as one of their own. The levy was the laboratory's invisibility. Seated between covers as a finished monograph, the notes' years of experiment vanished, and with them the reader's sight of the concepts in their molten state, still attached to the evidence that constrains them. Scholars citing passive revolution from the volume cite a result. Scholars citing it from the sequence cite a result and its warrant together. The difference, multiplied across the corpus's whole conceptual vocabulary, is the difference between the two Gramscis this guide has been distinguishing from its first page.

## The women who carried the text

The corpus's survival had a human infrastructure, and a reader's guide should name it accurately, because the names explain features of the evidence that purely editorial history cannot. At its centre stands Tatiana Schucht, the author's sister-in-law, who gave the prison years their practical shape. It was Tatiana who lived within reach of the prisons, who visited, who wrote the letters that carried books, news, money, and medical advocacy into the cell, who received the prisoner's own letters with their coded health reports and their requests, and who understood that the filled exercise books accumulating under prison tolerance were a legacy in physical danger. Her inventory of the notebooks at his death, her arrangement of their passage to Moscow, and her decades of guardianship over his memory and papers make her the corpus's first editor in the only sense that then mattered: she decided that it would exist.

Her position was not simple, and the evidence bears the marks of its complexity. Tatiana was the family's Italian anchor while her sister Giulia, the author's wife, remained in Moscow with their two sons, her health fragile, her own letters to the prisoner rarer and more painful than the correspondence either of them wanted. The prison letters are therefore asymmetric by circumstance: the great run of intimate, practical, intellectually candid letters flows to the sister who could answer weekly, while the wife receives letters strained through distance, illness, and the knowledge that they were read in two capitals. Readers who mine the correspondence for the author's state of mind without that map will misjudge both women and the man. The letters section of this guide has already warned against reading censored surfaces naively; the warning doubles where the surface is also a family's triage.

The Schucht family's role did not end with the transfer to Moscow. The sisters' keeping of the correspondence, the eventual return of materials, and the slow passage of the papers from family custody to institutional custody shaped the archive from which every edition descends, and the scholarly world's access to the author's remains ran for decades through doors the family and then the party's institute held. Modern critical scholarship, with the papers in a foundation's keeping and the national edition proceeding, works in a different regime of access, and the difference should be registered as part of the corpus's editorial history: what could be known about the notebooks, at any date between 1937 and the critical edition, depended on custodians whose permissions were themselves historical facts. The edition dependency has a prehistory in the archive dependency, and the archive, for this author, was kept first by a woman at a prison gate, which is as fitting a beginning as the corpus's long mediation could have.

## After the restoration: what scholars began to ask

The critical edition's deepest effect was not on answers but on questions, and a guide to reading should end its survey there. Before the restoration of sequence, the standing questions about the corpus were doctrinal: what the system taught, where its concepts ranked, which passages governed which. After 1975, a different family of questions became askable, because the evidence for them existed in print for the first time. When was a formulation written, and what had its author been reading that month. Which notes are drafts, and what do the revisions reveal about the direction of travel. How large, really, is the linguistics stratum, the education stratum, the stratum on popular literature, when the corpus is counted rather than anthologised. What did the author return to, in the special notebooks, and what did failing strength leave behind.

Whole research programmes grew from the new questions. The philological study of the corpus became a discipline with its own journal literature, its own controversies over datings and identifications, and its own cumulative findings: concepts redated, influences re-sourced, the engagement with particular books and articles pinned to particular notebooks. The linguistic Gramsci and the Gramsci of the subaltern-history programme emerged from the sequence as major figures whom the thematic portrait had kept in shadow. International scholarship reorganised itself around the new coordinates, and the arguments that followed, about the thought's unity, its development, its unfinished directions, are recognisably arguments among people reading the same object for the first time. The thematic Gramsci had been, perforce, several objects wearing one name, varying by the reader's language and anthology. The critical text made disagreement productive by making it checkable.

The guide draws one moral for its readers from this scholarly history, and it is the same moral at a larger scale. The restoration did not make the corpus easy, or settle its interpretation, or retire the selection from the classroom. It made the corpus common: one object, publicly numbered, against which any claim by any reader can be tested by any other. Communities of inquiry run on such common objects or they run on authority, and the decades in which the corpus was uncommon are the decades in which its discussion most resembled a contest of authorities, party against academy, language against language, anthology against anthology. A student meeting the notebooks now inherits the common object ready-made, and may never know what its absence cost. That inheritance is Gerratana's edition's real monument, and it is why this guide, which has distributed criticism and gratitude among all the editions, ends by assigning the critical text the deciding vote wherever claims conflict. Not because its editors were wiser than the others, though their patience was heroic, but because they built the only version of the corpus on which a disagreement can be settled without appealing to anyone's authority at all.

## Within the note: the manuscript's smaller revisions

Beneath the draft stages, which record the author's returns across notebooks and years, the manuscripts preserve a finer stratum of second thoughts that belongs in a complete account of the text's layers. Single notes carry their own internal weather: words struck through and replaced above the line, insertions in margins, passages re-inked, sentences whose endings were rewritten as the thought outgrew its first formulation. The critical apparatus records these variants where they affect sense, and the record matters beyond its scale. A corpus whose highest stratum shows a mind revising between notebooks shows the same mind revising within the sentence, and the two strata together refute, at the level of the physical object, the picture of the notebooks as tablets of doctrine delivered whole. They are writing, with writing's hesitations preserved in ink.

The fine stratum also disciplines a temptation of digital reading. A clean transcription, scrolling on a screen, presents every sentence as co-equal and final, and the reader's imagination supplies a confidence the object never had. The apparatus's variant notes are the printed edition's way of reintroducing friction, of making the reader feel the pen stop. Scholars working on contested passages consult the facsimiles for exactly this friction, and a reader who has once seen a famous sentence arrive at its wording through three visible attempts will quote it afterwards with a different and better kind of respect: not less confidence in the text, but confidence correctly located, in the final wording of a man who earned his formulations against resistance from his own first thoughts as well as from his jailers.

There the layers rest. A substitution at the level of the word, imposed by a censor. A revision at the level of the sentence, imposed by the author's standards. A reworking at the level of the notebook, imposed by his programme. An arrangement at the level of the corpus, imposed by his party. A selection at the level of the language, imposed by his translators. Five mediations, each documented, each with its own personnel and its own excuses, and the reader's protocol is nothing but the habit of keeping the five distinct while reading through them all to the desk at Turi, where a man with failing teeth and an exercise book was trying, for good, to think clearly about his world. The editions are not obstacles between the reader and that desk. Rightly used, they are the only road to it that has ever existed.

## What the prisoner read: the library behind the notes

The notebooks are a record of reading as much as of thinking, and their evidence about books deserves a section, because the conditions of that reading mark every page. A prisoner does not browse. Books reached the cell through channels: requests in censored letters, parcels from Tatiana, the London bookseller's account that Sraffa maintained, the prison library's meagre shelves, and periodicals admitted under the regime's rules for a political prisoner's reading. Each channel had delays, refusals, and accidents, and the notebooks' engagement with the literature of their moment is correspondingly eccentric: deep where a book happened to arrive, silent where the parcel failed, conducted at times from reviews, extracts, and memory. The critical apparatus, which identifies the source of note after note, doubles as the reconstruction of a library that chance and censorship assembled, and reading the apparatus with that question in mind is one of the sequence's quiet revelations.

The eccentricity explains features of the corpus that thematic reading mistakes for doctrine. Arguments are sometimes pressed against a position the author knew through an adversary's summary. Whole debates are joined late, when the book finally crossed the prison's threshold, so that a note's date records a delivery as much as a decision. The famous engagement with the popular manual of historical materialism was conducted against a text its author had known before prison and carried in memory's detail; other engagements wait visibly for their materials. None of this diminishes the thought. It dates and situates it, which is the sequence's gift, and it warns the interpreter that the corpus's map of its era's ideas is a map drawn from a cell's window: accurate in what it shows, partial in what it could see, and honest, in its apparatus, about the window's frame.

The newspapers matter as much as the books. The regime's press was the prisoner's daily evidence about the world, and the notebooks' running commentary on journalism, its manufacturing of opinion, its stock of ready phrases, was written by a man whose information diet consisted largely of the product he was analysing. The analysis gains its edge from that captivity: he read the censored papers as a theorist of the press and as its captive specimen at once, alert to what the papers' silences and emphases revealed about the order that licensed them. Readers meeting those notes in a thematic chapter on the press receive media theory. Readers meeting them in sequence receive media theory written from inside the experiment, by its subject, which is a different and stronger thing, and the difference is the sequence's to give and no one else's.

## The schoolroom notes: a stratum restored

The education notes supply a second exhibit for the restoration's effect, distinct from the linguistics case because the mechanism of distortion differed. The corpus's reflections on schooling are extensive: on the unitary school that would give every child a common formative education before specialisation, on the classical curriculum's hidden function as a sorting machine wrapped in disinterestedness, on the teacher as an intellectual formed by the same system he serves, on the active school and its romantic confusions. The twelfth notebook gathers much of this material with the notes on intellectuals, and the conjunction is the author's own: education, in his handling, is the factory in which a society's intellectual strata are made, and the school question is the intellectuals question viewed from its intake side.

The thematic edition did not suppress this stratum; it dispersed it, filing the school notes under the intellectuals' volume and the organisation of culture, where they read as corollaries of a theory of intellectuals. The sequence restores their weight and their company: in the miscellaneous notebooks the education notes keep company with the linguistics and the history, and the reader watches a single conviction, that formation is the decisive political process, being worked out simultaneously in grammar, in schooling, and in the party school of the movement's own practice. Teachers who assign the unitary school notes from the selection give their students a policy text. Teachers who can show the same notes in their notebook company give their students a method. The persona section for teachers has spoken of choosing extracts by stage and theme together; the schoolroom stratum is where that choice pays its highest dividend, since no theme in the corpus better rewards being watched in formation.

There is also a biographical poignancy here that the sequence alone discloses, and guides are allowed one paragraph of it. The school notes were written by a scholarship boy from a poor Sardinian family, whose own formation was the accident of teachers who noticed him, and they were written within sight of his two sons growing up in Moscow without him, their schooling a subject of the censored letters' most anxious passages. The theory of education in the corpus is therefore also a father's theory at a distance, and the notes' insistence that formation is slow, public, and owed to every child alike carries the weight of that distance. The thematic arrangement, filing the notes as doctrine, lets the weight evaporate. The sequence, which places the school notes among the years when the letters were asking after the boys' lessons, keeps it. Scholarship is not sentiment, but evidence is evidence, and the juxtaposition is evidence about the theory's sources that only the order can supply.

## Projects announced, projects abandoned: the corpus's gaps

A finished book has no gaps, only choices. A corpus has gaps, and they are among its most instructive features, provided the edition does not paper them over. The notebooks announce more than they complete. The Anti-Croce survives as a torso. The comparative study of Italian intellectuals, first of the 1927 programme's topics, was realised in fragments across the historical notebooks rather than as the treatise the letter projected. The history of the subaltern classes exists as a methodological programme of great austerity, in the twenty-fifth notebook, surrounded by the beginnings of its execution rather than its completion. The linguistics study that the young scholar might have written in another life haunts the grammar notes without ever standing up as a book. Each gap has the same two causes, the censor's narrowing of the present and the body's narrowing of the future, and each gap was widened or hidden by the editions in turn: the thematic arrangement, redistributing the fragments of an abandoned project among finished-looking volumes, manufactured a completeness the corpus does not possess, while the critical sequence leaves the scaffolding standing, which is the truthful and the more moving sight.

The gaps carry a rule for interpreters that complements the protocol's other rules. Where a project was abandoned, its fragments' authority is that of a direction, not a destination, and the interpreter who completes the author's trajectory for him, in the author's name, has crossed from scholarship into ventriloquism. The literature contains magnificent completions of this kind, whole doctrines extrapolated from the programme notes of interrupted projects, and the critical edition's service is to make the extrapolation visible as such: here the text stops, here the commentary begins. A reader trained on the sequence develops an instinct for the stopping places, and the instinct generalises, since every fragmentary corpus in the tradition, and most outside it, is surrounded by the same temptation to finish what history interrupted. Refusing the temptation is not sterility. It is the condition of hearing what the interrupted man actually managed to say, which was, in this cell and under these constraints, more than enough.

With the gaps acknowledged, the guide's survey of the corpus is complete: its writing, its code, its strata and stages, its survival, its four editions, its misreadings, its limits, and its unfinished edges. What remains is the use, and the persona sections that follow address the three readerships the series serves. The survey's own conclusion can be stated in the guide's shortest form. The Prison Notebooks reach every reader through mediations that can be named, dated, and graded, and the reader who names, dates, and grades them reads a harder, stranger, and better book than the one the quotation culture circulates, a book that turns out, on inspection, not to be a book at all, but a life of thinking preserved in exercise books, awaiting readers willing to meet it on its own terms.

## The prose itself: style as evidence

A reader's guide owes its readers one section on the writing as writing, because style in this corpus is not decoration but data. The prose of the notebooks is dense, periodic, and ironic, built by a man formed in three schools of Italian: the Sardinian world of his childhood, where Italian itself was a school language over a dialect ground; the university, where philology taught him that a sentence is a historical deposit; and the newspaper office, where the young editor of the Turin socialist press learned compression, polemic, and the headline's economy. The notes move among these registers without announcement: a page of archival patience can turn, in a sentence, into a feuilletonist's dart, and the dart is often the argument's point rather than its ornament. Readers in translation meet the density but lose the register shifts, one more tax the second costume levies, and the loss matters because contemporaries read his irony as precisely as his doctrine and took instructions from both.

The corpus contains its own reflection on the matter, characteristically displaced into theory. A note in the philosophy sequence observes that language is a continually evolving metaphor and that phrases fossilise into literalness, so that a tradition's slogans are the dead metaphors of its living thought. The observation, aimed at the manualised doctrine he was criticising, doubles as the notebooks' ars poetica and as a warning to their future quoters, delivered in advance: the phrases that survive him will fossilise exactly as the manual's had, unless readers restore their metaphorical first life by returning them to their contexts. The protocol of this guide is, in that light, simply the author's stylistic theory applied to his own afterlife, and the quotation culture's floating sentences are the fossil bed his note predicts.

His irony deserves its own caution. The notebooks practise a dry, structural irony that depends on the reader hearing the distance between a position's costume and its conduct, and irony is the first casualty of both censorship decoding and anthology extraction: a sentence whose target is the gap between a word and a thing will be quoted, somewhere, as its author's straightforward belief in the word. The Croce notes are mined in this way regularly, their paraphrases of the adversary's elegance absorbed into the author's own account. The remedy is the old one, attribution inside the note, practised with special vigilance wherever the prose turns graceful. In this corpus, fluency is a warning sign: the author is often most fluent when ventriloquising, and most himself in the knot where the sentence resists.

## The life as clock: dating the writing by its conditions

The biography article in this series owns the life; this guide claims one narrow use of it, as the clock by which the text is dated in the reader's imagination. The composition's rhythm follows the sentence's conditions with a fidelity no thematic edition can show. The Turi years, from 1929, give the corpus its great sustained effort: a regime of cell, exercise book, and reading, harsh but stable, under which the miscellanies accumulate and the special notebooks become possible, since reworking requires the earlier notebooks to be at hand in the same cell. The transfers of the final years, to the Formia clinic in 1933 and then toward Rome, break the rhythm: conditions eased as the body failed, and the writing thins in exact proportion as the man's strength is consumed by the effort to continue it. The last phase, from 1935, is silence with a date on it, two years of survival after the work stopped, during which the corpus existed complete in its incompleteness, in exercise books, awaiting the custody this guide has described.

Holding that clock changes how the famous concentrations are read. The special notebooks cluster in the years when stability and remaining strength overlapped, and their existence is not evidence of a late system ripening on schedule but of a prisoner using a window: conditions tolerable, materials at hand, health not yet collapsed. Had the window come earlier or later, the corpus's balance of draft and revision would differ, and with it the whole editorial problem the critical edition solved. Contingency, in this text, goes all the way down, and the reader who has internalised the clock stops asking the corpus the systematician's questions, which presume a workshop in steady state, and starts asking the historian's, which presume a life. That exchange of questions is the final conversion the sequence performs, and it is why this guide, having begun with editions and their politics, is content to end its survey with a timetable of one man's permitted hours.

There is a sentence in the correspondence, much quoted and variously translated, in which the prisoner describes his condition as that of a man who has been given a long sentence and means to use it as a study. The editions of the letters print it; the sentiment organises the notebooks better than any preface written since. A study is what the cell became, by an act of will performed daily against the institution's intention, and the exercise books are the study's furniture. Readers who keep the timetable in view, who know at which desk, in which year, under which permissions a fragment was produced, are reading the corpus as its author inhabited it. Everyone else is reading its reputation. The distinction has been this guide's whole subject, and it is time to hand the reader over to the three audiences whose uses of the text the series serves.

## From Moscow to Turin: the archive becomes an institute

Between the manuscripts' return to Italy and their critical edition lies a quarter-century of custody that belongs in the guide, because custody determined access, and access determined knowledge. The party founded an institute bearing the author's name in the postwar years, in 1950, and the notebooks, with the rest of the papers, passed into its keeping in Rome. The institute was at once a shrine, a research centre, and a gatekeeper, and for a generation the terms on which scholars could consult the manuscripts, as opposed to the printed thematic volumes, ran through its doors. Italian scholars of the author's own tradition worked closest to the papers; foreign scholars worked, mostly, from the printed page; and the printed page, until 1975, was Platone's arrangement. The long interval between the corpus's availability in print and its availability in full is the material cause of the lag this guide has repeatedly noted, in which arguments built on the rearrangement persisted for decades after the sequence was restored, in languages and literatures where the restoration arrived as a rumour before it arrived as a book.

Valentino Gerratana's edition was the institute's own product, prepared within its walls by a scholar of the tradition, which complicates any simple story of the party's edition being corrected from outside. The correction came from inside the house, a generation on, when the institute's scholarly mission had matured past the founding generation's strategic one and the manuscripts' evidence could be allowed to overrule the founder's portrait. Editorial history at this depth is institutional history: editions change when the institutions that keep the papers change their minds about what the papers are for. The thematic edition served a party that needed a founder. The critical edition served an institute that needed, and could finally afford, a text. Readers who grasp that sequence will not be surprised by the lag, the pieties, or the timing, and will read the remaining custodial questions, of access, of digitisation, of the national edition's course, as the same history continuing by other means.

The archive's geography closes the circle the survival section opened. Cell at Turi, sister-in-law's custody, Moscow vault, Roman institute, the world's libraries: the corpus's journey outward maps exactly onto its widening circle of permitted readers, from the censor alone, to the family, to the movement, to the academy, to anyone with the protocol and a library card. Each widening deposited an edition, and each edition bears its circle's stamp. To read the notebooks responsibly is to know at which widening one's own text was made, and to read through that stamp rather than mistaking it for the paper underneath.

## When the notebooks disagree with themselves

One use of the sequence remains to be taught, because beginners meet it as a scandal and anthologies conceal it entirely: the corpus contradicts itself, in places that matter, and the contradictions are among its most instructive contents. A corpus written across six years, under changing reading and a changing Europe, by a man whose method was to test formulations against archives, could not do otherwise. Assessments sharpen and soften. The relation between state and civil society, the corpus's most debated theoretical knot, is formulated across several notebooks with shifts of scope that scholars map and contest. Judgements of persons and parties alter as news, filtered and delayed, reached the cell. The thematic edition, seating passages from different years as neighbours, manufactured agreements the author never reached and hid the disagreements he actually worked through. The sequence restores both, and with them the reader's duty.

The duty has rules, and they are the protocol's rules turned inward. Date both sides of the contradiction before weighing either. Check the stages: a C text's disagreement with its own A draft is not a contradiction but a correction, and citing the pair as evidence of confusion reverses their meaning. Ask whether the contexts differ, since a formulation aimed at Croce's idealism and one aimed at a party manual may share words and divide in intent. And where the tension survives all three checks, report it as a tension, with both poles located, rather than resolving it by favour: the secondary literature's worst pages on this author are its harmonisations, in which a scholar with a thesis irons six years of thinking into a single afternoon's consistency. The author's own practice is the rebuke. He returned to his formulations because they dissatisfied him, and the returns are the thought. A contradiction in the sequence is a place where the thinking is still warm.

This is also the point at which the guide's long argument about editions pays its final dividend, because the capacity to disagree productively, within a seminar, a literature, or a single reader's mind, is exactly what a common, numbered, stage-marked text makes possible. Two readers of the selection who dispute a formulation dispute their impressions of an arrangement. Two readers of the critical text who dispute one can open the same notebook at the same paragraph, produce the draft, consult the neighbours, and locate their disagreement in evidence that both can see. The disagreement may not settle; the best ones do not. But it has become scholarship rather than theatre. Everything this article has taught, from the censor's costume to the archive's geography, converges on that modest transformation, and the reader who carries the protocol away has the transformation in portable form: name the edition, fix the coordinates, mark the stage, decode the words, read the neighbours, and let the text, in its own order, argue with itself where it must.

## Before the cell: the pre-prison writings and the threshold of the corpus

A reader's guide to the notebooks must finally mark the corpus's threshold, because a significant traffic of error crosses it in both directions. Gramsci wrote voluminously before his arrest: journalism for the socialist press in Turin, the Ordine Nuovo writings of the factory council years, party documents, and essays of the movement's internal struggles. That earlier corpus was published, argued over, and largely available while the notebooks slept in Moscow, and it formed the world's first Gramsci, the political leader and journalist, into whose silhouette the prison writings were later fitted. The two corpora differ in every editorial particular. The journalism was printed in its author's lifetime, much of it unsigned or pseudonymous by the conventions of the movement press, with attributions since argued by scholars; the essays survive in the forms their occasions gave them; and the whole pre-prison body has its own editions, its own selections, and its own dependencies, which this guide does not map but insists the reader not confuse with the notebooks' map.

The threshold text, where the confusions concentrate, is the unfinished essay of 1926 on the southern question, written in the weeks before the arrest and left incomplete by it. The essay, which analyses the southern intellectuals, the Vatican's rural networks, and the alliance the northern workers would need with the southern peasantry, reads in hindsight like the notebooks' prospectus: the historical method, the attention to intellectuals as social organisers, the refusal of economic reductionism, all present in a text meant for a party journal. It was published, unfinished, by his comrades in exile a few years later, and it has circulated ever since in the company of the prison writings, sometimes bound with them, sometimes quoted with the notebooks' authority. The quotation is legitimate; the authority transfer is not. The essay is a free man's polemic, written for print against a deadline, in the movement's plain vocabulary, with no censor at its shoulder and no six years of archive at its back. To cite it as though it were a notebook fragment is to misgrade a load-bearing text by exactly the protocol's measures, and the error is common enough, in textbooks that introduce the prison Gramsci through his most accessible essay, to deserve naming here as the threshold's standing trap.

The reverse traffic misgrades in the opposite direction. Notebook formulations, famous in their coded dress, are projected backward onto the pre-prison leader, so that the journalist of the council years is credited with the prisoner's matured positions and the party leader is judged by the prisoner's second thoughts. The sequence discipline forbids the projection in both corpora: the author of 1920 and the author of 1934 are related as a man to his later self across a catastrophe, not as a doctrine to its own preface. Editions sustain the discipline when they keep the corpora distinct and date their contents; selections that mingle pre-prison and prison writings for thematic convenience, of which the market has produced several honourable specimens, must be read with the mingling priced in. The rule completes the protocol. Grade every text by its own conditions of production, prison or newsroom, costume or plain speech, draft or print, and let no text borrow another's grade, however convenient the loan would be for the argument at hand.

Standing at the threshold, the reader can also see the whole of this guide's subject in one view, and the survey can close. Behind the threshold lies a public writer, prolific and embattled, whose works the movement printed and the police eventually answered with a cell. Beyond it lies the cell's product: thirty-three exercise books in a substituted vocabulary, surviving by a sister-in-law's vigilance and an economist's errand, sleeping in an archive through a world war, waking into a party's portrait of its founder, and emerging, a generation later, into the numbered daylight of the critical text, from which this guide has taught its reading. The distance between the journalist's desk and the reader's screen is the longest mediation chain in the tradition this series covers, longer than the German Ideology's, longer than the Paris manuscripts', because it includes a censor at the origin and a party at the destination. That the words survived the chain at all is the archive's miracle. That they can now be checked at every link is the editions' achievement. The reader who checks them, link by link, and reads on undiscouraged, is the reader the prisoner, who had every reason to expect oblivion and wrote anyway, was writing for. The persona sections that follow are therefore not appendices but destinations: the student's card file, the teacher's seminar, and the researcher's footnote are the three places where this corpus now lives its public life, and the guide's protocols were designed backwards from those desks, so that what is checked in private, at the moment of citation, arrives in public already graded, already coordinated, already honest about the costume it wears and the hands it passed through on its way from a cell in Turi to the page in front of a stranger.

## For students and exam candidates

Your Gramsci questions are set from the selection more often than from any other source, and your first discipline is to know that about your own preparation. Learn the canonical passages in the Hoare and Nowell Smith translation, since that is the English your examiners share, but hold them with the protocol's awareness: be able to say which notebook a passage comes from, whether it is a draft or a revision, and what its coded terms decode to. That awareness converts directly into marks, because questions on this author reward candidates who handle the text as evidence rather than as a quotation bank. The cluster's full examination treatment is in the [gramsci exam answers](/2015/05/15/gramsci-exam-answers/), which builds on this guide's protocols. Build your revision cards by notebook number from the start, one card per fragment with its stage and edition recorded, and when a question turns on emphasis or development in the thought, answer from the sequence rather than from the anthology. Candidates who can write, of a famous passage, that its author reworked it two notebooks later are playing a different and better game than candidates who merely deploy it.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This corpus is the finest teaching instrument in the tradition for one transferable skill: showing students how editions think. Set the same passage twice, once from the selection and once in its notebook context with its draft, and let the class discover what the arrangement had been doing to their reading; the discovery survives long after the content is forgotten. Choose extracts by stage as deliberately as by theme, pairing an A text with its C revision at least once in any course, and require notebook and paragraph coordinates in every submitted citation, a small administrative rule that reforms quotation habits within a fortnight. The edition map reproduces well as a seminar handout, and the pedantry objection deserves a full session at its strongest, answered by the class from cases rather than by assertion from the front. Where students read in translation only, say plainly what that limits, and where the library holds Gerratana, put the Italian volume on the desk even for those who cannot read it, so the apparatus's existence is a physical fact in the room and not a rumour from a richer institution.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Cite from the critical text or not at all where the claim is load-bearing. The coordinates are notebook and paragraph, the edition is Gerratana's or Buttigieg's where his volumes reach, and the draft stage belongs in your note the first time a fragment is used, because your readers will build on your citation and inherit its grade. Arguments about the corpus's emphases, silences, or development are arguments about the sequence, and the sequence exists only in the critical editions; journalists in particular should treat thematic-table-of-contents claims, including claims about what the author never discussed, as unverified until checked against the corpus in order. The reception history that the editions produced is traced in the companion study of [gramsci reception and misreadings](/2015/05/01/gramsci-reception-and-misreadings/), which documents what each editorial regime did to the author's afterlife. Counts of notebooks and pages travel as approximations in this literature, attributed to the critical edition's presentation; repeat them in that form. Where a quotation arrives from a secondary source without coordinates, the scholarly act is to refuse it passage until it has been found in the corpus, however eminent the source.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the Prison Notebooks?

They are the thirty-three exercise books Antonio Gramsci filled between 1929 and 1935 while imprisoned by the fascist regime in Italy. Their contents are working notes, not a finished book: reading notes, translations, drafts of arguments, and analyses of politics, history, philosophy, and culture, written under censorship in a vocabulary designed to pass inspection. Gramsci never prepared them for publication, never titled them, and never arranged them into a whole. Every printed form of the notebooks is therefore an editorial construction built on the manuscripts after his death, and the constructions differ enough, in order, selection, and apparatus, that identifying which one a reader holds is the first act of reading them responsibly.

### Q: How many notebooks did Gramsci write?

Thirty-three, by the count the critical scholarship works from, comprising roughly two thousand eight hundred manuscript pages in the presentation used by the critical edition. The numbers should be carried as approximations with that attribution, since counts of pages vary with what is counted and the corpus includes translation exercises alongside the notebooks of notes. What matters more than the totals is the internal division. The sequence contains miscellaneous notebooks, where subjects mingle in the order of his reading, and, from 1932, special notebooks in which he rewrote earlier material under single themes. Tatiana Schucht's inventory, made when she took charge of the manuscripts at his death, underlies the confidence with which the corpus's extent is stated.

### Q: Why did Gramsci write in code?

Because everything he wrote on paper in prison was liable to inspection, and the vocabulary of the movement he had led would have ended the writing and worsened his position as a prisoner. The code is a substitution system rather than a cipher. Marxism is called the philosophy of praxis, Marx is designated as that philosophy's founder, Lenin appears under his patronymic, and other contemporaries are handled by initials, family names, or periphrasis. An informed reader could always decode it; the point was register and deniability, moving the work from pamphlet to scholarship in the censor's eye. Reading the substitutions literally, as though philosophy of praxis named a doctrine of Gramsci's own invention, is the standing error the code produces in unwary readers.

### Q: Which edition of the Prison Notebooks should you read?

It depends on the claim the reading has to support, and the guide's edition map is built to answer exactly this. For a first encounter in English, the selection edited and translated by Hoare and Nowell Smith remains the practical entry point: strong translation, helpful notes, the canonical passages in one volume. For any argument that turns on order, dating, emphasis, or draft status, only a critical text serves: Gerratana's Italian edition for the whole corpus, or Buttigieg's English edition for the first eight notebooks, both arranged notebook by notebook with the apparatus that marks drafts and revisions. The thematic Italian edition is read for the history of the corpus's reception, as the postwar party's portrait of its founder, not as the corpus itself.

### Q: What is the difference between the first and revised drafts?

Many of the corpus's arguments exist twice. A note was written first in a miscellaneous notebook, among whatever else that month's reading produced, and was then, from 1932 onward, liable to be taken up into a special notebook, where Gramsci rewrote it under a single theme, expanding, compressing, re-aiming, and sometimes reversing its emphasis. The critical edition marks the stages with letters: A for a first draft that was later superseded, B for a note written once and never reworked, C for the revised version in a special notebook. Quoting an A text as the author's position risks attributing to him a formulation he himself replaced, which is why the stage of a fragment belongs in any serious citation of it.

### Q: Is the English selection a reliable edition?

Reliable for what it was made for, and unreliable beyond that by design. The Hoare and Nowell Smith selection is a fine translation with valuable notes, and for conveying the doctrine of the canonical passages it has served generations well. But it is a selection drawn from the thematic Italian edition, so it inherits that edition's rearrangement and adds a further concentration of its own: most of the corpus is absent, dates and draft stages go largely unmarked, and the substitutions of the censorship code are often decoded silently into their referents. Arguments about what Gramsci argued can rest on it. Arguments about the order, emphasis, completeness, or wording of the corpus cannot, and should be verified in a critical text.

### Q: Where should you start in the Prison Notebooks?

Start in the selection, with the notes on the intellectuals and the notes on Machiavelli and the modern Prince, reading for the arguments and accepting the volume's own honest status as an anthology. Then perform the guide's central exercise: take one passage already read and meet it again in a critical edition, in its notebook, among its neighbours, with its draft stage visible, and register what changes. After that initiation, read by notebook for a stretch rather than by theme, one miscellaneous notebook entire, to acquire the corpus's native weather. The sequence matters more than any particular starting theme, because the corpus's meaning lives in its movement, and movement is only visible to a reader travelling in the author's direction.

### Q: Did the Italian party edit the notebooks?

Yes, in the specific and documented sense that the first edition, published by Einaudi between 1948 and 1951, was prepared under the political direction of the party's leader, Palmiro Togliatti, with Felice Platone as editor, and arranged the notes thematically rather than in the notebooks' order. The arrangement embodied the party's reading of its founder for the postwar strategy it was pursuing, and its selections and emphases served that portrait. This should be stated without imputing bad faith: the edition was open about its thematic titles, it created the corpus's readership, and it was an act of transmission under political conditions its makers did not choose. The scholarly consequence stands regardless of motive. Arguments resting on that edition's order or completeness rest on an editorial construction, which the critical edition of 1975 then corrected by restoring the sequence.

### Q: Are the Prison Letters worth reading too?

Yes, and for a reader of the notebooks they are close to indispensable, provided they are read with the same suspicion of surfaces that the notebooks require. More than four hundred letters survive, written under the same censorship, so their warmth and stoicism are performances for the family, for the writer's own morale, and for the official reader, all at once. They document the writing's conditions from inside, the books requested, the study plan announced in 1927, the body failing by instalments, and they train the ear for indirection that the notebooks' coded register demands. They also restore proportion: the theory was written in exercise books, between illnesses and censored parcels, by a man writing to his children about hedgehogs. Read them as the corpus's companion and timescale, in a named edition, as with everything he wrote.

### Q: Is there a complete English edition?

There is a complete critical edition in Italian, Gerratana's, and an English critical edition, Buttigieg's, published by Columbia University Press, which gives the full text with apparatus for the first eight notebooks across three volumes published between 1992 and 2007. Beyond the eighth notebook, no complete critical English text exists in that form, and the remainder of the corpus is available in English chiefly through the selection and through partial translations of individual notebooks and essays of uneven apparatus. The practical consequence is sharp: the famous special notebooks, where the best-known arguments sit, are precisely where English readers are most dependent on the selection's lineage. Readers should check the Columbia project's publication position with the publisher rather than trust any secondary description of it, this guide's included.

### Q: How does the edition dependency change what can be quoted from the notebooks?

It changes the grade of a quotation, not its words. A passage quoted from the selection can carry a claim about the doctrine it states, because the translation is strong and the passage is genuine. The same passage cannot, without verification, carry claims about where the argument sits in the author's development, how much weight the corpus gives its theme, whether the wording is a draft or a revision, or what the author never wrote, because on all of those the selection is either silent or misleading by construction. The dependency therefore sorts quotations by the work they are asked to do. Writers who name their edition, give notebook and paragraph coordinates, and verify load-bearing fragments against the critical text can quote as freely as anyone. Writers who cannot say which edition a quotation came from are quoting at one remove more than they know.

### Q: What should a reader check before citing a notebook fragment?

Five things, in order. The edition, identified by editor and publisher, with the citation's grade marked accordingly. The coordinates, notebook and paragraph number, which make the fragment findable in any library on earth. The draft stage, whether the text is a superseded first draft, a single draft, or a revised version, and, where a draft and its revision both survive, a reading of both before either is used. The code, so that the philosophy of praxis, its founder, and the initialled contemporaries are decoded before interpretation begins. And the neighbours, the notes around the fragment in its own notebook, which show whether the passage is a position or a step toward one. The checks take minutes and correspond, one for one, to the documented failure modes of the secondary literature.

### Q: Why do the thematic and critical editions organise the same text differently?

Because they were made to answer different needs under different regimes of truth. The thematic edition, prepared in the years after the war under party direction, needed a usable founder: a set of treatises on philosophy, intellectuals, the Risorgimento, and the state, teachable at once to a movement and a republic being founded. Rearranging the notes under subject titles served that need and announced itself openly on the volumes' spines. The critical edition, prepared a generation later at the Gramsci Institute, needed the corpus as evidence: what was written, when, in what order, with what revisions. Restoring the notebook sequence served that need, at the price of the treatises' readability. Neither organisation is the text's natural state, since the author published nothing. Each is an instrument, and the reader's task is to match the instrument to the claim.

### Q: How did censorship shape the notebooks' vocabulary?

At three depths. At the surface, it imposed substitutions for the forbidden centre: the theory renamed as the philosophy of praxis, its founder designated by office, living contemporaries rendered as patronymics, family names, and periphrases, so that inspected pages wore a scholarly costume. More deeply, it deformed the whole lexicon, driving forbidden distinctions into permitted words and overloading them, so that terms like intellectuals, common sense, and folklore carry charges their ordinary senses do not announce. At the deepest level, it displaced argument into historical example, where a note on Machiavelli or the Jacobins could do the work of an analysis of the author's own movement. The result is a vocabulary denser than the same author's journalism, and a standing rule for readers: where a passage's force seems to depend on a word's ordinary meaning, suspect the reading and check the code.

### Q: What separates a fragment's draft stages in the critical edition?

An act of the author's, recovered from the manuscripts. A first-stage fragment, marked A, is a note from a miscellaneous notebook that Gramsci later chose to rework; a single-stage fragment, marked B, is one he left as written; a revised fragment, marked C, is the reworking itself, found in a special notebook where the theme was gathered and the argument recast. The separation is therefore biographical before it is editorial: it records where the prisoner's attention returned and where it did not, across years in which his strength was failing. Editorially, the stages are distinguished by the manuscripts' evidence and printed so the reader can follow the crossings, with first drafts set in reduced type and cross-referred to their revisions. What separates the stages, finally, is authority: the C text is the author's later word, and citation that ignores the stages can quote a man against his own correction.
