Every field has a piece of infrastructure that its practitioners use daily and its wider audience barely knows exists, and for the study of Marx that infrastructure is an edition. Not a book, not a translation, not a selection with an editor's introduction, but the complete scholarly edition of everything Marx and Engels wrote, drafted, excerpted and annotated, printed in the languages in which they wrote it and surrounded by the apparatus that lets a reader verify every reading of every line. The edition is called MEGA2, a compressed German acronym that expands to Marx-Engels-Gesamtausgabe, and it has been in production, under three different political orders and through one execution, for the better part of a century. Readers who have never opened a volume of it still read Marx through its consequences, because the texts that reach classrooms and paperbacks descend, at one or two removes, from editorial decisions made inside projects like this one. The distance between the Marx of the anthology and the Marx of the archive is measured in editorial choices, and the purpose of the pages ahead is to make those choices visible.

![MEGA2 and the Marx manuscripts, drafts, letters and excerpt notebooks in the critical edition - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-21.webp)

The argument of this guide can be stated before any of the detail, because the detail is easier to hold once the claim is in view. The claim has a name, and the name is worth keeping: the notebook effect. The notebook effect is the pattern by which the most consequential reinterpretations of Marx across the last half century have come not from new arguments about published books but from the publication of material Marx never published himself, above all the excerpt notebooks gathered in the edition's fourth section, where he copied, condensed and commented on the work of agricultural chemists, geologists, ethnologists and mathematicians. Because that material appeared late, in instalments, according to an editorial schedule set by institutions and their budgets, the questions scholars could ask about Marx were partly decided by what editors had happened to print. A reader who grasps the notebook effect reads every confident claim about Marx's view of nature, of non-European societies, or of science itself with a standing question attached: which Marx is this built on, the author of the finished books, or the researcher whose reading kept moving after the books were done?

Three services are attempted in what follows. The first is a map: what the edition is, how its four sections divide the material, what state each section is in, and what each has changed. The second is a history, told straight: the first attempt at a complete edition, launched in Moscow in the late 1920s, destroyed in the early 1930s along with its director; the relaunch in the mid 1970s as a joint East German and Soviet enterprise, with all the institutional weight that fact carried; and the reconstitution after 1990 under an international foundation in Amsterdam and a German academy, working to a plan much reduced from the original design. The third service is practical: how a researcher actually uses this edition, how it differs from the fifty-volume English collected works that most Anglophone readers know, and what honest guidance can be given about access, language and citation. Where the record is contested, the contest is reported rather than settled. Where a claim belongs to a named scholar, it is given to that scholar and not dressed as consensus.

## MEGA2 and the idea of a complete critical edition

The phrase complete critical edition carries two promises, and each promise separates the enterprise from ordinary publishing. Completeness means that nothing is excluded on grounds of taste, doctrine or convenience: juvenilia, abandoned drafts, letters to relatives, shopping lists of the intellect in the form of reading notes, all of it belongs, because an editor cannot know in advance which fragment a later generation will need. The second promise, the critical one, concerns how each text is established. A critical editor works from the manuscripts and the earliest printings, compares every surviving version, records the variants between them, reconstructs the order in which drafts were written, and prints the evidence for those decisions in an apparatus that accompanies the text. The reader of a critical edition is never asked to trust the editor blindly. The reader is handed the editor's working, and can disagree with it on the page.

Set against that standard, the more familiar kind of edition, the collected works, makes a different and entirely honourable bargain. A collected works aims to give a reading public the author's writings in a usable, uniform form, usually in translation, usually with light annotation, and usually following texts established by earlier editors rather than re-establishing them from the manuscripts. Its virtues are coverage, price and readability. Its limitation is inheritance: where the underlying text was set from a flawed copy, or where a manuscript was misread a century ago, the collected works tends to transmit the flaw faithfully, because re-editing from the archive is not its job. Neither bargain is fraudulent, and most scholars use both kinds of edition in the same working week. The confusion this article exists to correct is the assumption that they are the same thing under two names, an assumption that quietly falsifies footnotes across the literature. A separate guide in this series on [researching marxism sources](/2021/10/01/researching-marxism-sources/) treats the wider method of which this distinction is one part.

The Marx material poses the critical editor's problems in an unusually pure form. Marx wrote in German, English and French, sometimes mixing languages inside a single notebook page. He left a small number of finished books, a large number of unfinished manuscripts, several distinct drafts of his major work, and a reading archive of excerpts that dwarfs the published writings in bulk. Engels edited, completed and in places reshaped the posthumous volumes, so that the boundary between the two men's hands is itself an editorial question; the series guide to the [marx engels authorship question](/2013/06/01/marx-engels-authorship-question/) examines what the edition's evidence has shown about that boundary. An edition of such an estate cannot be a neutral container. Every decision about ordering, dating and transcription is an interpretation, and the honest edition is the one that exposes its decisions to inspection.

### What is MEGA2?

MEGA2 is the second historical-critical complete edition of Marx and Engels, presenting all their writings, drafts, correspondence, and reading notebooks in the original languages with scholarly apparatus. It is organised in four sections, the fourth containing the excerpt notebooks that reshaped readings of Marx on nature and agriculture.

That compressed answer deserves unpacking clause by clause, because every clause marks a boundary against some neighbouring thing. Second, because a first complete edition was attempted in Moscow and destroyed; the history occupies a later section of this guide. Historical-critical, because the texts are established from the manuscripts with a full record of variants and genesis, rather than reprinted from earlier editions. Complete, because the design admits everything from the doctoral dissertation to the marginal note scribbled in the margin of another man's book. Original languages, because translation is treated as interpretation and postponed: the text volumes print German, English and French as written, while the scholarly apparatus that accompanies them is written in German, a fact with real consequences for access that the practical sections take up. Reading notebooks, finally, because the fourth section prints what Marx read and how he read it, and it is that section, the least finished and the most consequential, around which the notebook effect gathers.

The superscript numeral in MEGA2 is the first thing a newcomer trips over, and it is worth a plain explanation. Scholars write MEGA with a superscript 1 for the Moscow edition of the interwar period and MEGA with a superscript 2 for the edition launched in the 1970s, distinguishing the two projects that share a name and a ambition but not an institution, an editor or a fate. In plain text the superscript flattens, and MEGA2 and MEGA1 result. The distinction is not pedantry, because the two editions differ in scope: the first aimed at the works and letters, while the second added the manuscripts of Capital as a section of their own and, decisively, the excerpt notebooks as a fourth. When a writer cites MEGA without a numeral, the reference is usually to the later project, but the careful reader checks, since the older volumes still circulate in libraries and still anchor some citations in the older literature.

### Why does the edition print texts in their original languages?

A critical edition prints the words its author wrote, in the language in which he wrote them, because every translation silently resolves ambiguities that the original leaves open, and scholarship needs the ambiguity preserved until an interpreter, rather than a translator, chooses to resolve it. Marx compounds the ordinary case. He drafted Capital in German while filling notebooks in which a page might move from German summary to English quotation to French aside and back, following the language of whatever he was reading. To translate such a page into one language is to erase evidence about the reading itself. The cost of the policy is real: the edition addresses itself first to readers with German, the language of its apparatus, and every other public receives the material later, partially, through derivative editions and translations. That cost, and the ways scholars manage it, belong to the practical discussion of access below.

There is a further reason, less often stated, for the original-language rule. Marx's vocabulary is itself an object of study: the same German word may carry a philosophical resonance that its English equivalent lacks, and the history of his concepts is partly a history of his word choices across drafts. An edition that normalised those choices into another tongue would destroy, at the moment of preserving, part of what it exists to preserve. Editors of the first Moscow project understood this and followed the same rule, which is one of the threads of continuity between the two enterprises despite everything that separates them.

## The first MEGA and the fate of its director

The complete edition was first attempted in Moscow, and the attempt produced one of the great editorial achievements of the twentieth century and one of its emblematic catastrophes. The institution was the Marx-Engels Institute, founded in the young Soviet state to collect the literary remains of the two founders, and its director was David Ryazanov, a historian and bibliophile of formidable energy who had spent the pre-revolutionary years hunting Marx manuscripts across Europe. Under Ryazanov the institute assembled, by purchase, gift and photostat, an archive of a completeness no Western institution matched: original manuscripts, copies of letters scattered among the papers of socialists in a dozen countries, and the library's working collection of the socialist press. On that foundation the institute launched, in the late 1920s, the first historical-critical complete edition, planned on a generous scale and edited to a standard that specialists, including specialists hostile to the regime that housed it, recognised as exacting.

The edition's volumes began to appear at the end of the 1920s, and around a dozen had been published when the project met the fate of its director. Ryazanov was arrested in the early 1930s, in the widening machinery of the purges, removed from the institute he had built, exiled, later rearrested, and executed in the latter part of the decade. The institute was merged into a larger party institution, its editorial programme halted, and the edition stopped with it. The manuscripts did not vanish; many had copies held in Amsterdam, where the International Institute of Social History had acquired the largest share of the Marx-Engels papers in the later 1930s, a transaction that preserved the physical archive outside the reach of both Moscow and Berlin. But the edition as a living project, with its editor's standards and its accumulated knowledge of the papers, was destroyed, and the destruction was not a scholarly event. It belongs to the political history of Stalinism, and this guide records it in those terms and no others, because the brief of a research article is to place the fact where it belongs and to draw no rhetorical wages from a man's death.

### Why did the first edition collapse?

The first edition collapsed because its director was arrested in the purges and the political order that had sponsored the institute withdrew its protection from the project, not because scholars judged the edition a failure. Its published volumes were respected internationally, and its standards were the ones its successor later resumed.

Its halt was, in form, an administrative act of a police state against its own editor, and in substance the end of a scholarly enterprise collateral to a political terror. The dozen or so volumes it produced remain citable, and its fate is the standing proof that editorial projects of this scale live at the mercy of the institutions that fund them.

Ryazanov himself deserves a paragraph of biography rather than a sentence of martyrology, because his career explains what was lost. He was a scholar of the old European school transplanted into a revolutionary state: a collector who bought manuscripts at auction, a bibliographer who published catalogues of the archive he was building, an editor who insisted that texts be printed whole and established from originals at a time when selective, doctored publication would have been more convenient to the state that employed him. His insistence on completeness was a scholarly virtue and a political exposure, since a complete Marx includes texts that complicate any party's preferred inheritance. Historians of the edition regularly note that the project's troubles deepened as its findings complicated official pieties, though the arrest itself belonged to the general machinery of the terror rather than to any single editorial decision. After his death his name was removed from the institute's history for decades, and its restoration is part of the later story of the second edition, whose scholars worked in conscious succession to his standards while serving institutions of a very different temper.

The material legacy of the first project survived in three forms. The archive itself, divided between Moscow and Amsterdam holdings, survived physically, which is the precondition of everything later. The published volumes survived, with their texts of the early writings and letters still cited. And a discipline survived: the expectation, which the first edition had set, that Marx would eventually be edited whole or not at all, and that partial editions claiming completeness were a kind of falsehood. When the project was relaunched a generation later, it was relaunched in explicit succession to Ryazanov's design, enlarged. The enlargement, and the peculiar politics of the institution that carried it, are the next part of the story.

## The relaunch: two states and one edition

The second complete edition was a product of the Cold War's strangest cultural arrangement: a joint scholarly enterprise of the German Democratic Republic and the Soviet Union, produced by the institutes of Marxism-Leninism attached to the ruling parties in East Berlin and Moscow, and published from the mid 1970s onward. The arrangement gave the project resources that Western scholarship could not have matched, salaried editorial teams working for decades on single sections, access to the Moscow holdings, and a print programme of imposing physical quality. It also gave the project masters. The institutes that housed the editors existed to certify an official Marxism, and the edition appeared under the shadow of the suspicion, voiced often in the West, that a party edition of Marx would print what suited the party and delay what did not.

The record that accumulated complicates that suspicion in instructive ways. The second edition enlarged Ryazanov's design in two directions that no censorious logic explains. It gave the manuscripts of Capital, the drafts and preparatory works behind the published volumes, a section of their own, printing the genesis of the theory rather than only its results. And it added the fourth section, the excerpt notebooks and marginalia, material of no conceivable propaganda value, expensive to edit and read by almost no one outside scholarship. Delay, where it occurred, followed the material's difficulty more often than its content: transcribing Marx's handwriting, in his mixture of languages and private abbreviations, is slow work under any regime. Western scholars who used the appearing volumes generally treated the texts as reliable while reserving judgement on the framing essays that accompanied them, a division of trust that the edition's own structure encouraged, since its apparatus separated the establishment of the text from the commentary upon it.

None of this makes the institutional setting innocent, and the honest summary holds two facts together. The edition was produced inside party institutes whose other publications were instruments of doctrine, and scholars working on it operated within limits that were real even when they did not touch a given volume. At the same time, the edition's texts, established by editors trained in the philological tradition the first project had set, met standards that independent specialists verified and used. Both facts belong in the record, because the credibility of the material a researcher quotes from the edition depends on understanding exactly this doubleness: an apparatus built to certify a doctrine produced, in its textual work, an instrument that has repeatedly embarrassed doctrines, not least by printing the notebooks from which heterodox readings of Marx on nature and on non-European societies were later built.

Production during the joint period was steady and slow, volumes appearing year by year through the late 1970s and the 1980s, each the work of editors who expected the project to occupy careers. The plan, as designed in that period, was very large, well beyond a hundred volumes in total, reflecting the completeness principle applied without a budget constraint ever being allowed to speak in public. That scale is part of the historical record of the plan as designed, and it should be reported as a plan: the figure belongs to the project's paperwork, not to any shelf, and the rescoping after 1990, which cut the design substantially, is unintelligible except against the original ambition.

## After the sponsoring states fell: the foundation, the academy, the reduced plan

The collapse of the sponsoring states in 1989 and after left the edition an orphan with an archive. The party institutes that had housed it were dissolved or transformed, the Soviet partner vanished with the Soviet Union, and an editorial project measured in decades faced funders who now thought in grant cycles. That the edition survived at all is the second surprise of its history. Custody passed to an international foundation created for the purpose, the International Marx-Engels Foundation, based in Amsterdam, which took responsibility for the project in association with scholarly bodies, and the editorial work in Berlin continued in association with the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences, the successor institution in the academy tradition of that city. Publication continued under academic auspices, with international editorial commissions replacing the party institutes as the project's governors.

Reconstitution came with rescoping. The new custodians reviewed the inherited design and reduced it substantially, concentrating the plan, retiring some projected subdivisions, and accepting explicitly what the old plan had concealed, that the edition would be the work of generations and that its completion lay far ahead. Volumes have continued to appear under the new dispensation, prepared increasingly by international teams and reviewed in the ordinary scholarly journals, a normalisation of the project's governance that matters for how its texts are received: the edition is now judged as other great editions are judged, volume by volume, in the open. The lesson the whole history teaches, and the reason this guide spends its length on institutions, is that an edition is not a natural object. It is a century-long act of maintenance, and every fact about it, its scope, its speed, its very existence, is an institutional fact before it is a scholarly one.

A reader meeting references to the edition's state should therefore handle all numbers with the suspicion this history justifies. Counts of published volumes date instantly, plans have been revised more than once, and the only durable statements are structural: four sections, original languages, German apparatus, a second section whose completion was announced in the early 2010s, a fourth section still appearing, and a governing foundation in Amsterdam with academy partnership in Berlin. Anything more precise belongs to the publisher's own listings, which the final practical section directs the reader to consult directly. This discipline, refusing to freeze a moving target, is not evasion. It is the only accuracy available about a project whose defining trait is that it outlives its describers.

## The section map

The edition divides its material into four sections, and the division is itself an interpretation of the estate. The first section gathers the works, articles and drafts, everything Marx and Engels composed for publication or left as free-standing manuscripts, arranged chronologically. The second gathers Capital and its entire preparatory archive, the drafts, plans and manuscript versions from which the published book emerged. The third gathers the correspondence, the letters of both men to each other and to the world. The fourth gathers the excerpt notebooks and the marginalia, the record of their reading. Works, masterpiece, letters, reading: the map that follows sets out what each section contains, what state it is in, what it has changed, and what a reader of English can use instead.

Study the map as a whole and a shape emerges that the notebook effect names. The sections nearest completion are the ones whose material was already famous, the works and the masterpiece. The section with the longest road still to travel is the one whose material was invisible for a century, the reading. Interpretation, meanwhile, has been moving in the opposite direction to editorial completion: the freshest claims gather where the edition is least finished. A student who understands that inversion understands the modern literature on Marx better than many of its participants, who sometimes write as though the notebooks were a settled possession of scholarship rather than an instalment plan still being delivered.

## Section One: works, articles and drafts

The first section is where most readers' business with the edition begins, because it contains the texts that made the reputation: the early philosophical manuscripts, the polemics, the journalism, the historical writings, in chronological order and established from the manuscripts where manuscripts survive. Its volumes have been appearing since the edition's launch, and its particular gift to scholarship has been correction at the level of the individual famous text. Again and again, a work that generations had read in a form arranged by an earlier editor turned out, under critical editing, to have a different internal order, a different date, or a different authorial status from the one tradition assigned it.

Two cases stand for the many. The manuscript known to the world as The German Ideology, written with Engels in the middle 1840s and abandoned, in Marx's later phrase, to the criticism of the mice, reached print in the twentieth century in arrangements imposed by editors who stitched a book out of disordered manuscript bundles. The critical edition's treatment rearranged the material according to the evidence of the manuscripts themselves, distinguishing layers of writing and revision, and in doing so changed what the text can be quoted as saying: arguments that a stitched arrangement had fused into one chapter stand apart in the critical text as separate attempts, written at different moments, with different targets. The series guide to the [german ideology readers guide](/2010/03/01/german-ideology-readers-guide/) works through the consequences for readers of that text. The second case is the set of manuscripts from 1844, known as the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts, whose order and completeness had been editorial constructions since their first publication in the early 1930s. Printing the notebooks as notebooks, in their manuscript state, changed the standing of famous passages on alienation, which readers had encountered as chapters of a treatise that Marx never assembled; the guide to the [economic philosophic manuscripts guide](/2011/04/15/economic-philosophic-manuscripts-guide/) takes up the reading consequences.

Beyond the famous cases, the section performs a quieter service across hundreds of smaller texts: journalism recovered and dated, pamphlets disentangled from Engels's hand or restored to it, drafts that show a published argument's discarded beginnings. Attribution questions, which older editions settled by convention, are reopened wherever the manuscripts permit, and the findings feed directly into debates about who wrote what within the partnership. Dating work of this kind rarely makes headlines, yet every chronology of Marx's development rests on it, and every claim that he changed his mind between one text and another silently presupposes an editor's decision about when each text was written.

### What does a critical apparatus actually do?

A critical apparatus does four jobs at once. It reports the variants, every place where a draft, a corrected proof or a later printing differs from the text the editor prints. It reconstructs the genesis from paper, ink, hand and internal reference. It dates and attributes where no title page survives. And it explains its own decisions openly, for audit.

The explaining is the heart of the matter, because it converts editorial authority into editorial accountability: a reader who rejects a dating or a reading can see exactly which piece of evidence to contest, and the contest can proceed on documents rather than on reputations. The apparatus is printed separately from the text it serves, in this edition in companion volumes or sections of volumes, and it is written in German. A text without its apparatus asks for trust; a text with one invites audit, and that difference, more than any single finding, is what the word critical in the edition's name purchases.

Learning to use an apparatus is a skill with a learning curve, and it is fair to say plainly who needs it. A reader who wants the argument of a finished book needs the text, well translated, and nothing else. A researcher whose claim depends on a sentence's exact wording, on the order of two manuscripts, or on whether a passage is Marx's or an editor's bridge, needs the apparatus and cannot responsibly proceed without it. Between those poles lies most scholarly practice, which cites the critical text for precision and consults the apparatus when a dispute makes precision expensive. The edition's design serves all three readers, but it refuses to pretend they are the same reader.

## Section Two: Capital and the manuscripts behind it

The second section is the edition's monument and its first completed territory. It assembles, in chronological order, the entire archive of Capital: the economic manuscripts of the late 1850s, including the vast draft known as the Grundrisse; the manuscripts of the early 1860s, including the long critique of political economy's categories from which theories of surplus value were later quarried; the drafts of the three books from the middle 1860s; the published first volume in its successive editions and its French version, which Marx revised so heavily that he claimed for it an independent standing; and the manuscripts Engels inherited and worked into the second and third volumes after Marx's death. The section's completion was announced in the early 2010s, and the announcement marked a genuine milestone in the project's history, the first of its four territories fully delivered.

What completion bought scholarship is best described as the conversion of a literature of speculation into a literature of inspection. Before the section appeared, claims about how Capital was composed rested on Engels's prefaces, on the correspondence, and on inference from the published texts. After it, the claims can be checked against the drafts themselves, printed in sequence with their variants. The picture that inspection sustains is of a theory built by repeated demolition and reconstruction across two decades: categories tried, abandoned, and retried; a plan announced in six books and narrowed in execution; a first volume polished through edition after edition while its sequels remained in states that no single description fits. Scholars continue to dispute what the genesis implies for the theory's coherence, and the dispute is now conducted on documents, which is the difference the section made.

The section also settled, as a by-product, the most consequential attribution question in the whole estate: the extent of Engels's hand in the posthumous volumes. With Marx's drafts and Engels's editorial papers both printed, the joins became visible. Engels emerges from the comparison largely as he described himself, a faithful editor who assembled, ordered and occasionally bridged, whose interventions can now be located page by page and weighed rather than suspected in bulk. Interpretations that require a systematic Engels distortion of Marx must now specify where, in a fully printed record, the distortion sits. Some of the older polemic on that question has not survived the requirement.

A caution belongs here, because the section's completeness can mislead in the opposite direction. A full archive of drafts is not a higher form of the book. The drafts were working papers, written to be superseded, and reading them against the published text requires the discipline of asking what status Marx assigned each layer. The edition gives the documents; the judgement about their authority remains the reader's work, and the strongest scholarship on the genesis is careful to let the published first volume remain the theory's public form while using the manuscripts to explain how its arguments were reached and where they were heading when work stopped.

## Section Three: the correspondence

The third section prints the letters, and letters are the connective tissue of the whole estate. Marx and Engels wrote to each other, across the decades of their collaboration, with a frequency and candour that make their exchange the single richest source for the partnership's inner history: drafts announced, money begged and sent, publishers cursed, rivals assessed with a freedom no printed page was offered, and the theory itself argued out in provisional form years before it reached a manuscript. Around that central exchange the section gathers the letters to and from the wider world, editors, political allies, family, and the scientists and public figures whose work Marx followed.

The scholarly yield of the correspondence is disproportionately chronological. Letters date manuscripts. A draft that floats undated in the archive can be fixed by a sentence in a letter announcing its dispatch, its abandonment, or its author's disgust with it, and hundreds of editorial datings in the first two sections rest on evidence the third section prints. Letters also correct the public record of positions: a doctrine attributed to Marx on the strength of a pamphlet reads differently beside a letter, written the same month, qualifying it for a correspondent he trusted. Biography before the full correspondence was a genre exposed to memoir and myth; with the letters available, the myth-making must at least quarrel with documents.

For the general reader the section holds a different pleasure, though pleasure must be rationed by the same access facts that govern the whole edition: the letters appear in their original languages with German annotation, and the English collected works prints a generous selection in translation rather than the whole. The selection is the working tool of most Anglophone scholarship, and it is a good one, but the researcher whose question turns on what Marx wrote to a particular correspondent in a particular week will end, as all roads in this territory end, at the critical text or at the archive behind it.

## Section Four: excerpts, notebooks and marginalia

The fourth section prints what the other three presuppose: the reading. Throughout his working life Marx read by excerpting. In notebook after notebook he copied passages from the books and journals in front of him, condensed arguments into his own formulations, interleaved comment, calculation and abuse, and returned to the notebooks years later as a quarry for his drafts. Engels excerpted too, in his own manner. The resulting archive, notebooks by the score across four decades, is the largest single component of the literary estate by bulk, and for most of the twentieth century it was the least known, consulted by a handful of archive visitors and described at second hand in the literature as a working habit rather than read as a body of evidence.

Editing such material is the hardest work the edition attempts. A fair copy challenges an editor's Latin; a notebook challenges everything at once. The hand is rapid and often close to illegible. Languages alternate as the source changes. Excerpt and comment share the page without consistent marking, so that establishing where the copied author ends and Marx begins is a judgement renewed line by line. Dating depends on paper, on the bibliography of the sources excerpted, and on cross-reference to letters. The section was a late addition to the edition's design, its volumes have appeared in instalments across the project's life, and it remains far from complete, a state of affairs that the reader should hold firmly in mind, because the interpretive excitement the notebooks have generated rests on a foundation still under construction.

The marginalia belong with the notebooks and are printed in the same section: the notes Marx wrote in the margins of books he owned, from his own published works, which he annotated for revisions he never made, to the volumes of economists, historians and naturalists that crowded his shelves. Marginalia are the most compressed form of his reading, sometimes a single word of endorsement or contempt, and their evidentiary value is correspondingly double-edged: they record reactions at the moment of reading, unmediated by any thought of publication, and for that very reason they tempt interpreters to build more on a pencilled exclamation than an exclamation can bear. The discipline of the section, in its apparatus, is to present the note, its placement and its context, and to let claims about its significance be made by interpreters who can then be checked against the presentation.

### How is an excerpt notebook different from a draft?

A draft aims at a text of the author's own and moves, however slowly, toward something publishable, while an excerpt notebook aims at mastery of other men's texts and moves toward nothing beyond the reader's own readiness to write. The distinction sounds tidy, and the manuscripts refuse to keep it perfectly.

Excerpts shade into paraphrase, paraphrase into comment, comment into passages that reappear, transformed, inside drafts of Capital, so that the notebook is at once a record of reading and the first stratum of composition. Editors mark the strata as the evidence allows, distinguishing the copied, the condensed and the original on each page. The interpreter's rule follows directly: never quote a notebook as Marx's opinion until the strata on that page have been checked.

Held at that standard, the notebooks are evidence of a kind no other section supplies. The published works show what Marx concluded. The drafts show how his conclusions were built. The notebooks show what the building consumed: which sciences he followed and in what depth, which authorities he trusted and which he fought line by line, how a reading of one decade resurfaced in the theory of another. A scholar who wants to know what Marx made of a question he never treated in print has exactly one place to look, and it is this section. That monopoly is what gives the fourth section its importance and its danger in equal measure.

## The notebook effect

The claim at the centre of this guide can now be stated with the evidence assembled. The notebook effect holds that the most significant reinterpretations of Marx in recent decades have come not from new arguments but from newly published material he never published himself, and that the sequence of interpretation in this field is therefore partly a function of editorial scheduling. Consider the shape of the modern literature. The great twentieth-century contests over Marx, the contests of parties and schools, were fought over the published books, above all Capital and the early writings once those appeared. The contests that have redrawn the map since the notebooks began to appear in bulk are of a different kind: they are contests over what the reading shows, opened by the arrival of documents, and their turning points are publication dates in an edition's catalogue as often as they are publication dates of interpretive books.

Three demonstrations carry the claim. The ecological reconstruction of Marx's thought, examined in its own section below, exists in its strong form because notebooks on agricultural chemistry and geology became available to be read. The account of the late Marx as a student of anthropology and of the Russian commune, a scholar whose interests in his final decade ranged far beyond the industrial Europe of his famous books, rests on the ethnological excerpts and the correspondence around them. The mathematical manuscripts, Marx's sustained private engagement with the calculus, revised the picture of his scientific ambitions when they circulated. In each case the interpretive move was enabled by an editorial event. Scholars of the first rank made the arguments, but the arguments were waiting on the mail from the archive, and a different editorial schedule would have produced a different sequence of Marxist debates. That dependency is the notebook effect, and naming it is a protection: it teaches the reader to ask of any sweeping claim about Marx's views on a subject he treated mainly in his reading, when did the evidence for that claim become available, and to whom?

The standing caveat follows, and it is the practical edge of the whole argument. Claims built only on the published works carry an exposure that claims built on the notebooks do not, because the published works are a selection Marx made under the pressures of his lifetime, polemical, financial and political, from a research process that continued after them and ranged wider than them. The caveat does not void the older claims. Capital remains the theory's public form, and the notebooks revise at the margins of most questions and at the centre of a few. But on the questions where the reading was the research, on nature, on pre-capitalist societies, on the sciences of his day, a claim that ignores the notebooks is not a conservative claim. It is an uninformed one, and the edition's fourth section is what makes that verdict possible to reach and to check.

## The notebooks by subject: what Marx was reading

Open the fourth section at its full spread and the first impression is of range. The notebooks of the 1850s, kept in London exile, run heavily to political economy, the history of economic doctrine, technology and the money market, the reading that fed directly into the manuscripts of Capital. Alongside them stand notebooks on world history and diplomacy, on the politics of the European states, and on languages, which Marx pursued with a amateur's energy and a scholar's system. The excerpting was never random accumulation. It followed the drafts in progress and anticipated the drafts to come, which is why the notebooks can be read as the theory's supply lines, visible at last.

The natural science reading forms the stratum on which the ecological interpretation rests. From the 1860s Marx excerpted the agricultural chemistry of Justus von Liebig and the German agronomic literature that followed him, following the debate over soil exhaustion and the robbery of nutrients from the land that industrial farming entailed. Geology enters through works such as those of Jukes and the literature on the earth's crust and its transformation. The physical sciences appear in notebooks on chemistry and electricity, and the biological literature of the period, Darwin above all, is present in the reading and the correspondence alike. None of this was the hobby of a man of letters. The excerpts are dense, technical and sustained across years, and they track the sciences at the points where those sciences touched his theory of production: the soil, the mine, the machine, the labouring body.

A second stratum, belonging chiefly to the final decade of Marx's life, has redrawn the map of his interests. The ethnological notebooks of the late 1870s and early 1880s excerpt the anthropology of the period at length: Lewis Henry Morgan on the communal institutions of the Iroquois and the evolution of the family, Henry Sumner Maine on early law and the village community, John Lubbock on prehistoric society, Maxim Kovalevsky on communal landholding, whose work Marx excerpted with particular closeness and whose Russian materials fed his study of the peasant commune. Beside them stand notebooks on Russian agrarian conditions, assembled as Marx learned Russian and read the statistical literature of the zemstvos, and the historical notebooks in which he compiled a chronology of world history from the sources available to him. This is the reading of a man in his sixties whose curiosity had moved outward from the factory districts of his famous subject to the communal forms of societies on several continents.

The mathematical notebooks form a third stratum, stranger to the legend of Marx than either. Across many years he worked privately at the foundations of the differential calculus, filling notebooks with his own attempts to ground the derivative historically and algebraically, reading the standard authorities and arguing with them in the margins of his exercises. The manuscripts circulated in the twentieth century and were published in Russian and in English translation, and they changed the assessment of his scientific self-understanding: the co-author of a doctrine sometimes accused of borrowing the prestige of science appears in them as a working student of its hardest contemporary discipline, pursuing the problem for its own sake in notebooks no one was meant to see.

Other strata reward the specialist: notebooks on aesthetics and literature in the early years, on the history of religion, on Spanish and on Oriental history in the journalism years, the excerpting of parliamentary papers and factory reports that fed the published chapters on the working day. The point of the catalogue is not abundance for its own sake. It is that the fourth section, read whole, replaces the portrait of a system-builder refining one doctrine with the record of a researcher whose reading programme was wider than his publishing programme by an order of magnitude, and that every interpretation of Marx silently chooses which of those two figures to interpret.

### What can a notebook prove, and what can it not prove?

A notebook proves reading, attention and sequence: that Marx encountered a claim, in a stated source, at an establishable time, and thought it worth the labour of copying. It can prove engagement where a published text is silent, and it can date an interest that later surfaces in a draft of his own.

It cannot by itself prove agreement, since excerpting was his way of interrogating opponents as well as authorities, and the copied passage sits in the notebook whether he meant to use it or to destroy it. It cannot prove influence in the strong sense without a bridge into his own writing, a phrase carried over, a draft that takes the material up. And it cannot prove a doctrine, because a researcher's reading is a workshop, not a creed. The interpreters who respect those limits build the strongest claims the notebooks sustain; the ones who breach them supply the sceptics with easy targets.

This evidential discipline matters beyond pedantry, because the notebook literature contains both kinds of work and the reader needs a way to tell them apart. The test to apply is the presence of the bridge. Where a scholar shows the excerpt, shows the comment Marx attached to it or the draft that absorbed it, and only then draws the conclusion, the claim stands on the documents. Where a scholar moves from the single fact of excerpting to a settled position in Marx's system, the claim stands on enthusiasm. The edition itself, through its apparatus, enforces the first procedure and exposes the second, which is one more reason the slow publication of the fourth section, apparatus and all, is worth more to scholarship than a rapid dump of transcriptions would be.

## The ecological reading and the notebooks

No case shows the notebook effect at work more clearly than the ecological reading of Marx, and no case requires more careful attribution, so this section proceeds by giving each claim to its maker. The reading begins from a concept in the published work: Marx's account, in Capital, of the metabolic relation between human labour and nature, and of the rift in that metabolism when capitalist agriculture exhausts the soil while concentrating population in towns. In the closing years of the twentieth century the sociologist John Bellamy Foster built on that account, in his book Marx's Ecology and in related work with Paul Burkett, an interpretation of Marx as a thinker of ecological crisis grounded in materialist science. That interpretation was constructed from the published texts and the manuscripts then available, and it was contested from the start by readers who held that the ecological passages were marginal to the theory or inconsistent with its productivist main current.

The notebooks changed the evidential basis of that dispute. As the fourth section's volumes on the natural science excerpts appeared, scholars could inspect the agricultural chemistry reading whole: the Liebig excerpts and the literature around them, the geology, the sustained engagement with the science of soil fertility across the very years of Capital's composition. The Japanese scholar Kohei Saito built his prize-winning study of Marx's ecological notebooks directly on this material, reconstructing from the excerpts a research programme in which Marx's critique of capitalist agriculture developed alongside, and partly through, his reading of the natural sciences, and extending the argument to the late notebooks on communal forms and the limits of growth in the theory's horizons. Saito's work is cited here as its author presents it, a reconstruction offered to judgement, and its reception has included both strong endorsement and the objection that a reading assembled from notebooks risks promoting a private research interest into a doctrine Marx never published.

That objection deserves its full weight, because it generalises. The published works were the works Marx chose to stake his name on, and an interpretation that subordinates them to the reading notebooks inverts the author's own hierarchy of commitment. Defenders of the ecological reconstruction reply that the notebooks are not being used to override Capital but to explain it, that the metabolic argument sits in the published text itself, and that the excerpts merely show how deep its scientific roots ran. The dispute is live, it is conducted on documents the edition supplied, and the reader who wants the interpretation itself argued at length should go to the series treatment of [was marx an ecological thinker](/2017/08/15/was-marx-an-ecological-thinker/), which owns that question. What belongs here is the structural observation: a debate that could not have taken its present form before the notebooks were printed took that form within years of their printing. The scheduling of an edition set the agenda of a field. That is the notebook effect, photographed mid-operation.

The same pattern, at lower intensity, repeats wherever the fourth section has delivered a coherent body of material. The late notebooks on India, on communal landholding and on the Russian commune fed a reconsideration of Marx's views on colonialism and on societies outside the capitalist core, a reconsideration whose documentary base widened each time a relevant volume appeared. The history notebooks altered biographical accounts of his last years, replacing the picture of a thinker in decline with the record of a compiler of world chronology working at full stretch. None of these revisions required a new theory of interpretation. They required paper, print and patience, supplied by an institution in Amsterdam and an academy in Berlin working to a plan drawn up before many of the interpreters were born.

## The late ethnological reading and its disputes

The final decade of Marx's life has become, thanks chiefly to the notebooks, the most contested territory in his biography. The established picture, built from the published works and the correspondence long available, showed the author of Capital in his last years as a man of failing health, working intermittently at the unfinished volumes, travelling for cures, his creative period behind him. The ethnological and Russian notebooks, as they became known, complicated that picture past repair. They show a reader working through the anthropology of his day at a scale that implies a project, excerpting Morgan and Maine and Kovalevsky with the same density he had once given to Ricardo, and corresponding about the Russian peasant commune as a possible point of departure for a development that might not repeat the Western path.

The interpretive claims built on this material are substantial and are attributed here to their makers. The historian Teodor Shanin, editing a famous collection on the late Marx and the Russian road, argued that the Marx of the final years had moved toward a multilinear view of historical development, in which the sequence of social forms he had described for Western Europe was one path among others. The drafts of Marx's reply to Vera Zasulich, the Russian revolutionary who had asked him directly whether the commune must dissolve, supply the correspondence evidence most often cited: in draft after draft, four in number, Marx worked at an answer that refused to convert his account of Western development into a universal fatality. Scholars differ on how far the drafts, which were never sent in their long forms, revise the theory and how far they state an exception for Russian conditions. The notebooks discipline that difference, because they show the reading that stood behind the drafts, and they leave it open, as honest evidence does, whether the late Marx was rebuilding his system or exploring its edges.

Scepticism about the late-Marx literature takes two forms, and both should be heard. The first is evidential: excerpting is not concluding, and a sick man's reading, however dense, may document curiosity rather than a new position; against this, the sheer scale of the ethnological notebooks, and their convergence with the Zasulich drafts and the late correspondence, is the evidence offered in reply. The second is editorial: the picture depends on the order and dating the edition assigns to notebooks that Marx left undated, and if the apparatus errs, the edifice leans. That second scepticism is the healthy kind, because it is checkable against the printed apparatus, and it illustrates again the symbiosis this whole article describes: interpretation advances exactly as far as the edition's verifiable labour allows, and not one step further.

Engels stands at the edge of this territory in a way the edition has clarified. His book on the origin of the family, published the year after Marx's death, drew openly on Morgan and on Marx's excerpts, and the critical printing of those excerpts allows the reader to compare the use with the source, approving here, simplifying there, bending the material toward a scheme in places where the notebooks themselves are more tentative. The comparison has fed the wider argument about the partnership's asymmetries, and the documents are now available for it to proceed on evidence rather than on loyalty. Readers following that argument will find the series guide on the authorship question the natural next step, since the same editorial method, printing hands separately and dating layers, governs both debates.

## Two editions, two purposes: the critical edition and the Collected Works

The single most consequential confusion in this territory, the one this guide was commissioned to dissolve, is the belief that the English Collected Works and the critical edition are the same enterprise in two languages. They are not, and the difference is not a nuance of cataloguing but a difference of kind that shows up in footnotes. The Marx Engels Collected Works, fifty volumes published in English between the middle 1970s and the opening years of the following century, was a joint undertaking of publishers in Moscow, London and New York, and it gave the Anglophone world, for the first time, a uniform edition of the writings on a scale approaching completeness. Generations of English-speaking scholars were formed on it, and its volumes remain the first resort of any reader whose German is unequal to the critical text. Nothing in this guide should be heard as diminishing that achievement, without which the Anglophone literature on Marx would be poorer by an order of magnitude.

The difference lies in the textual base. The Collected Works was edited as a reading edition: its texts were taken, in the main, from the best editions then available, including the Russian collected works and the early volumes of the second critical edition as they appeared, and they were translated, introduced and annotated for the general scholarly reader. Where no satisfactory text existed, its editors worked closer to the sources, and some of its volumes, particularly among the early writings and the economic manuscripts, represented real editorial labour. But the enterprise did not set out to re-establish every text from the manuscripts with a full critical apparatus, and where the texts it inherited rested on pre-critical foundations, the Collected Works transmits those foundations, silently, in excellent English. A translation can be faithful to a flawed copy with complete integrity. The flaw is upstream, in the copy.

The practical consequences sort themselves by the kind of claim a scholar is making. For the argument of a published work, the architecture of the theory, the biography in outline, the Collected Works serves perfectly, and its translations are the medium in which most English-language debate has been conducted. For a claim that turns on exact wording, on the dating of a manuscript, on whether a passage stood in a draft or was supplied by a later editor, or on anything in the notebooks, the critical edition is the court of appeal, and citing the translation for such a claim is a methodological fault that referees in the field are trained to catch. The two editions also differ in coverage: the critical edition's design includes the excerpt notebooks entire, while the Collected Works, true to its period and purpose, printed the works and a selection of letters, leaving the reading archive aside. The researcher who needs the notebooks in English will not find them in either place, a gap taken up in the access discussion below.

### How do the two editions divide the work between them?

In use, the separation is a separation of questions. The collected works answers the question what Marx argued, in a form a reader can absorb and a seminar can discuss. The critical edition answers the prior questions: what exactly he wrote, in which order, with what revisions, and on the basis of which manuscripts any printed page claims to know.

The first of those questions is the one most readers bring, most days, and the collected edition that serves it is no one's idea of a fraud. The second set becomes unavoidable the moment two interpreters disagree about a passage, a date or an attribution, because at that moment the argument moves from the text to the evidence for the text. Scholars keep both editions within reach because scholarship lives at both levels, sometimes within a single footnote.

One further difference deserves emphasis because it is so often missed: the two editions embody different relationships to completeness. The Collected Works is finished. Its fifty volumes stand complete on the shelf, and a citation to it will mean the same thing in fifty years. The critical edition is unfinished by design and by budget, its fourth section especially, and a citation to it is a citation to a moving frontier. Neither condition is a defect. A finished edition freezes the state of knowledge at its closing date, which for the Collected Works means, in places, the state of knowledge before the notebooks were edited. An unfinished edition keeps improving the base and keeps embarrassing the claims built on its earlier state. The wise researcher cites the finished edition for stability and checks the unfinished one for truth, and the tension between those two virtues is simply the condition of the field.

## The English question and the online dispute

It follows from everything above that the English-language situation is one of productive incompleteness. The reader without German has the Collected Works for the writings, selected translations of individual manuscripts as scholars produce them, the Grundrisse and the published volumes of Capital in established translations, and, for the notebooks, the partial publications and the quotations embedded in the secondary literature. That is a great deal, and it is not the whole. No complete English MEGA exists or is planned as such, and the honest answer to the frequently asked question whether the critical edition is available in English is no, with the rider that its findings reach English readers continuously through the work of the scholars who use it. Guidance on choosing among the translations that do exist belongs to the companion guide on [marx translations which edition](/2013/09/15/marx-translations-which-edition/), which owns that question; the point here is structural, that translation follows the critical edition at a distance measured in decades, and that the distance is itself one of the notebook effect's mechanisms, since interpretations reach the Anglophone public in the order their documentary bases are translated.

The politics of access produced one public quarrel that a factual account must include. In the mid 2010s a dispute arose over the presence of the English Collected Works, in electronic form, on a well-known free online archive of socialist texts. The publisher holding rights in the English edition asserted its copyright and required the archive to remove the texts; the archive complied while protesting, and commentators divided between those who held that a commercial publisher was entitled to control a property it had maintained and those who held that writings of this public importance should circulate freely. Petitions were organised, statements issued, and the texts were in due course absent from that archive while remaining available through libraries and the second-hand market. This guide reports the dispute as what it was, a copyright matter between a rights holder and an archive, notes both positions as their holders stated them, and takes no side, because the rights and wrongs of the case are a question of publishing law and of political sentiment on which a research guide has no verdict to sell. The durable lesson is narrower and more useful: access to editions is governed by law and money as well as by scholarship, and any research plan that depends on a particular electronic text should verify that the text is still there before the plan hardens around it.

A parallel point concerns the critical edition's own electronic prospects, which are often overstated in casual reference. Portions of the project's work are described in digital terms by its custodians, samples and catalogues circulate, and scholars reasonably expect electronic access to widen over time. The rule this guide follows, and recommends, is to describe no specific digital holding as available unless the custodian's own listings confirm it at the moment of need. Editions outlive announcements. The reader who plans archive work in Amsterdam, Berlin or Moscow should treat every statement in print about holdings, including this one, as a draft to be checked against the institution's catalogue before travel is booked.

## How a researcher actually uses the edition

Practical guidance about this edition must begin by lowering one expectation and raising another. The expectation to lower is that of the reading room fantasy, the scholar alone with the manuscript. The edition exists precisely so that almost no one needs the manuscript: the printed volumes, with their apparatus, are the working instrument, held by university and national libraries in the cities where research on Marx is done, and reached by interlibrary loan where they are not. The expectation to raise is that of preparation. A volume of the fourth section is not a book to be browsed. It is a dossier to be interrogated, and the researcher who arrives with a defined question, a knowledge of the notebook's dating and sources, and a system for recording what the pages show will extract in a week what the unprepared reader misses in a month.

The working sequence used by scholars in this field runs in a consistent order, and setting it out plainly is the most useful service this guide can perform. A question is first located in the published record: what do the finished works and the established manuscripts say, and where does their testimony run out? The correspondence is then used to fix the chronology of the relevant period, since a notebook's meaning depends heavily on when in the sequence of drafts it was filled. Only then does the researcher go to the notebooks themselves, reading the excerpts against the sources excerpted, distinguishing the copied from the commented, and noting every bridge into the drafts. Findings are recorded with the edition's own coordinates, section, volume and page, together with the apparatus evidence for dating and attribution, so that another scholar can retrace the path. The series guide on [citing marx and engels](/2021/12/15/citing-marx-and-engels/) sets out the citation conventions that make such retracing possible, and it should be read alongside this section by anyone preparing work for publication.

Two companion tools earn their keep in exactly this work, and they are named here because the workflow, not the product, is what recommends them. For the long discipline of keeping one's own excerpts, cross-references and reading notes in order across a project that may run for years, the study-notes companion at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) serves the same function for the modern researcher that the notebook served for Marx, a single durable place where excerpts are gathered with their sources and can be searched when the draft that needs them is finally written. For working with the documentary material itself, scans, transcriptions and the accumulated paper of an edition-based project, the document tools reached through [ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) support the handling of manuscript and edition material in the ordinary formats of scholarly work. Neither tool replaces the edition. Both serve the habits that edition-based research demands, the habits of exact recording on which everything else depends.

Language is the gate through which every user of the edition must pass, and candour about it serves the reader better than encouragement. The texts themselves are in the languages of composition, German predominating, with English and French throughout, and a reader with schoolroom French and a dictionary can follow more of Marx's own pages than might be feared. The apparatus, the part that justifies the edition's authority, is in German, and the researcher who cannot read scholarly German will be dependent on the kindness of colleagues and the partial translations of the secondary literature at exactly the points where independence matters most. This is not a counsel of despair. It is a budgeting fact, of the same kind as the cost of travel to an archive, and scholars plan around it by building language work into projects, by collaborating across the language line, and by using the growing body of scholarship in English that reports the apparatus findings for the texts it discusses. What no one should do is cite the apparatus unseen.

### What must be noted down before a notebook passage is quoted?

Before quoting a notebook, a researcher should record the edition's coordinates for the passage, the dating the apparatus assigns and its evidence, the source being excerpted, the stratum of the page the words belong to, the language of the passage, and any bridge into a draft or letter where the material is taken up.

Each element of that record guards against a characteristic accident of notebook scholarship. Coordinates guard against the quotation that no later reader can find. The dating and its evidence guard against building a development story on a notebook assigned to the wrong year. The source guards against crediting Marx with the words of the author he was copying. The stratum guards against the same error at closer range, where copy and comment share a page. The language guards against a translation hardening, unnoticed, into a term of art. The bridge guards against the leap from reading to doctrine that the previous sections marked as the field's standing temptation. A quotation supported by such a record can be audited by any reader. A quotation without it asks to be believed, and in notebook scholarship belief is the one currency that has been debased too often.

The record-keeping discipline sounds clerical and is in fact the substance of the field's credibility. Notebook findings enter the literature through chains of quotation, one scholar quoting a passage from another's article, a third quoting the second, and errors of transcription or attribution compound along the chain unless the first link was forged carefully. The edition's apparatus exists to break such chains by giving every quotation a fixed address. Scholars who supply the address make their claims corrigible, which in this field is the highest compliment available, since the history of Marx interpretation is littered with incorrigible claims resting on passages no one could find.

## Delays, scheduling and the shape of the literature

The edition's pace deserves a section of its own, because the pace is not an accident of the story but one of its causes. A project conceived in the 1920s, destroyed in the 1930s, relaunched in the 1970s, orphaned in the 1990s and rescoped by its new custodians has delivered its material in an order dictated by institutional history, by the survival of editors, and by the difficulty of the material, never by the order in which interpreters wanted it. The works came first because the works were the point, for the party institutes as for everyone else. The Capital manuscripts followed, their section completed under the reconstituted project. The notebooks, the hardest material and the least useful to any doctrine, waited longest and are still arriving. The literature's development mirrors that order with embarrassing fidelity: the century's Marxology was a debate about the works, then about the masterpiece's genesis, and has become, as the notebooks arrive, a debate about the reading.

Delay also worked on scholarship through a quieter channel, the expectation of completeness. Because the edition promised everything, scholars learned to write as though the archive were a known quantity, a fixed body that a sufficiently diligent reader could master. The notebooks broke that composure. Each new volume of excerpts has carried the possibility that a settled claim, about Marx's indifference to a science, about the limits of his late interests, about the sources of a concept in Capital, would meet a page of excerpts that complicated it. Mature scholars in the field now write with a visible provisionality on notebook-adjacent questions, dating their claims to the state of the edition, and the habit is one of the healthiest the long project has taught. Claims in this field carry an implicit edition date, and the reader who asks for it is not being pedantic. That reader is applying the field's own hardest lesson.

There is a counterfactual worth a paragraph, provided it is labelled as one. Had the first edition survived its director, the notebooks might have been in print by the middle of the twentieth century, and the debates of that century, about Marx and nature, about the young and the old Marx, about the completeness of the theory of history, would have been fought on the full record. The ecological reading might be an old orthodoxy by now, or might have been refuted young and forgotten. Counterfactuals prove nothing, and this one is offered only to expose what the actual history did: it rationed the evidence, releasing the works to one century and the reading to the next, and it thereby decided, without intending to, which Marx each century would argue about. Editorial history is intellectual history, conducted by other means and at a slower speed.

## The papers themselves: how the archive survived

Behind the edition stands a paper trail with its own dramatic history, and a researcher benefits from knowing it, because the archive's accidents explain the edition's gaps. Marx died leaving his papers in London, in the keeping of his daughters and then of Engels, who worked through them as editor and executor, ordered parts of them for his editions of the posthumous volumes, and passed what remained into the custody of the German socialist movement's archive and the family. The papers travelled as the movement travelled: to Berlin, into the party archive, and then, as the Nazi seizure of power made Germany lethal for them, out again along the routes of exile. The largest share was sold in the later 1930s to the International Institute of Social History in Amsterdam, a transaction arranged to put the manuscripts beyond the reach of the regime that would have destroyed them, and the Amsterdam holdings survived the occupation of the Netherlands hidden from the occupiers. A second great body of material, copies, photostats and originals gathered by Ryazanov's institute, remained in Moscow. The estate of the most international of thinkers is thus itself divided between cities and institutions, and the edition's editors have always worked across that division, collating in one capital what is held in another.

Engels's papers followed a parallel path into the same repositories, and the two men's surviving libraries, the books with the marginalia, came to rest substantially with the archive holdings as well. What did not survive is as instructive as what did. Papers were lost in moves, in wars and in the ordinary entropy of a movement that expected history to preserve what it valued and learned otherwise. Some notebooks known from references in letters have never been found. Some material survives only in the copies made by the first institute's photographers, so that the edition prints, for those items, from a photostat whose original is gone. The apparatus records these conditions item by item, and the researcher citing a passage should know which condition stands behind it, since a text printed from a lost original's photograph carries a different order of certainty from a text printed from a manuscript that any sceptic can re-examine.

The division of the archive also explains a feature of the edition that puzzles newcomers: the sigla and repository notes that stud the apparatus, recording for every manuscript where the original lies and on what basis the printed text was established. Those notes are the edition's honesty about its own foundations. They let a reader distinguish a reading verified against an Amsterdam original from a reading inherited from a Moscow copy, and they map the strange geography, part scholarly, part the residue of Europe's catastrophes, on which the whole enterprise rests.

## The editor's craft: transcription, dating and the illegible page

A word about the labour itself is owed, because the edition's slowness becomes intelligible the moment the labour is described. Marx's handwriting is notorious among editors: a rapid, compressed script, in several languages, thick with abbreviations of his own invention, written often in pencil or fading ink on paper that has not always aged kindly. A single notebook page can require hours of decipherment, and the decipherment is only the beginning. The editor must then identify the source being excerpted, which may be a book, a journal article, a parliamentary paper or a statistical return, and which Marx rarely cites in a form a catalogue recognises. The excerpt must be collated against that source to establish where copying ends and comment begins. The page must be dated, from the source's publication, from cross-references in letters, from the physical evidence of the notebook itself. Multiply that labour by the bulk of the fourth section and the decades explain themselves. The wonder is not that the edition is unfinished. The wonder is that anyone undertook it twice.

Dating deserves its own paragraph because so much interpretation hangs on it and so little of it is simple. Marx dated almost nothing in the notebooks. The apparatus therefore dates by triangulation: a notebook cannot precede the publication of the newest work excerpted in it; a letter may mention the reading in progress; a draft may take up material from a determinate notebook, fixing that notebook before the draft. Each inference is printed for inspection, and each carries a margin. Interpretations that require a notebook to belong to a particular season of Marx's life stand or fall with those margins, and the field's more careful writers cite the dating evidence, not merely the date. Where the evidence permits only a span of years, honest interpretation works with the span and declines the false precision of a single year.

Transcription has its politics too, conducted at the level of the comma. An editor decides whether to reproduce Marx's spelling and punctuation exactly or to regularise silently, whether to expand his abbreviations in the text or in the notes, how to mark a deletion, an insertion, a passage underlined once or three times. The critical edition's conventions are stated in its editorial guidelines and applied uniformly, which is what makes its texts quotable: two scholars citing the same page are citing the same decisions, visibly made. Reading editions make different choices for different purposes, and silently, which is proper to their purpose and fatal to certain kinds of argument. The researcher who has once watched a celebrated interpretation wobble because it rested on a punctuation mark that a reading edition had supplied will never again treat the distinction between the edition types as a librarian's nicety.

## Reading one notebook: a guided pass

Abstract talk of excerpts becomes concrete if the reader follows a single notebook through the questions a scholar puts to it, and this section performs that pass in general terms, on the kind of natural science notebook that fed the ecological debate. The notebook opens, as Marx's notebooks do, without ceremony, in the middle of a source: page after page of condensed agricultural chemistry, the doctrine of soil nutrients in the form in which the German literature of the 1860s presented it. The first scholarly question is identification: which book, which edition, which pages stand behind the excerpts, and the apparatus answers it, because the editors have done the collation. With the source fixed, the strata separate. The long stretches of faithful condensation tell the scholar what Marx wanted at his command. The departures tell more: a calculation in the margin converting a source's figures, an exclamation at a claim about the exhaustion of English soils, a cross-reference, in Marx's shorthand, to a chapter of his own draft where the material is destined to work.

The second pass follows the bridges. The scholar takes the marked passages and searches the manuscripts and the published text for their reappearance, transformed from excerpt into argument: the robbery of the soil, in the notebooks a chemist's doctrine about nutrients shipped to the towns and never returned, emerging in Capital as an element of the critique of capitalist agriculture, phrased now in the theory's own vocabulary of metabolism. Where the bridge exists, the interpretation may speak of the notebooks as the theory's research base. Where a notebook passage has no bridge, the scholar records a reading interest and stops. The third pass checks the dating, because the force of the whole construction depends on the excerpting preceding or accompanying the drafting, and the apparatus evidence for the notebook's place in the sequence is examined with the same care as the text.

A pass through an ethnological notebook runs the same questions to different answers. The sources are the anthropologists; the strata show Marx excerpting kinship systems and communal property forms with a lawyer's attention to the property material; the comments grow longer and more argumentative than in the chemistry notebooks, because here the reading is testing positions rather than gathering data; and the bridges lead outward, to the Zasulich drafts and the late correspondence, rather than inward to a famous published chapter. The same discipline, differently rewarded: in the one case the notebooks underwrite a published doctrine's scientific depth, in the other they document a research front that publication never reached. A reader who has performed both passes possesses the notebook effect not as a slogan but as a pair of worked examples, and can judge new claims in the literature by asking which pass the claimant performed and which was skipped.

## What the edition does to biography

Every serious biography of Marx written since the edition began to appear is, whether it advertises the fact or not, a post-edition biography, and the differences from the older life-writing are structural. The older biographies were built from the published works, the memoirs of family and comrades, and the selection of letters then in print, and they share the shape that evidence imposes: strong on the public career, thin and anecdotal on the inner workshop, prone to fill the workshop's silence with the subject's own later mythology. The edition's documents have been dissolving that shape volume by volume. The correspondence fixes the chronology of composition to the week. The drafts show the theory's false starts, which no memoir records. The notebooks reveal the reading that the memoirs never mention, because the family remembered a father and a political leader, not a man spending his nights on agricultural chemistry.

The most visible biographical casualty is the tidy division of the life into a young humanist and a mature economist, a division the early publication history had imposed by printing the works of the 1840s decades late and the notebooks hardly at all. With the reading record available, continuity and change both become more precise and less theatrical: the philosophical interests of the young excerptor of Hegel persist as habits inside the economist's method, while the late turn to anthropology and agrarian Russia extends the life past the point where the older story ended it. Biographers dispute, as they should, the weight to give each document, but the dispute's terms are now documentary. A life of Marx written from the edition cannot be the life that was written before it, and the difference is the clearest measure, outside technical scholarship, of what the long project has bought.

There is a corresponding effect on the biography of Engels, whose standing the edition has quietly revised in both directions at once. The printed correspondence shows the partnership's daily reality, the money, the editorial labour, the management of the Marx household's crises from Manchester, at a density no selection conveyed. The Capital manuscripts show his editing to have been, in the main, the faithful work he claimed for it. And the notebooks and the comparison of his syntheses with Marx's excerpts show the divergences, of temperament and sometimes of doctrine, that the partnership's public face concealed. Hagiography and its mirror image, the debunking life, both find the documents uncooperative. What the documents support is a working relationship of unusual durability between two unlike men, and the edition's achievement is to have made that unglamorous conclusion the one the evidence keeps returning.

## The edition among editions: a comparison that clarifies

Readers sometimes ask why this author, alone among political thinkers, receives an editorial enterprise of this scale, and the comparison with the editing of other major figures supplies the frame. Historical-critical editions are the standard instrument by which national scholarly cultures preserve their central writers: philosophers, poets and composers of the first rank have them, produced by academies over generations, at costs that legislatures approve because the alternative is the slow corruption of the texts a culture reads itself by. Judged in that company, the Marx edition is large but not anomalous, distinguished less by its scale than by its biography, the execution, the two states, the orphaning and rescue, which no edition of a safer author has had to survive. Its subject's political afterlife made his papers a prize of states in a way that the papers of a poet are not, and the edition bears the marks of that contest in every phase of its history.

The comparison clarifies the objection considered later in this guide, that the enterprise is a poor use of scholarly resources. The objection, to be consistent, must be an objection to critical editing as such, to the whole academy system of preserving texts by establishing them, since the Marx edition consumes resources of the same kind and order as its peers. It is possible to hold that position, but it should be held openly and applied evenly, to the editors of the philosophers and the poets as to the editors of Marx. What cannot be sustained is the selective version, in which the editing of every other major figure counts as civilisation and the editing of this one counts as indulgence. The selective version is a political judgement wearing a budget's clothing, and the record of what the edition has produced, the corrections, the datings, the notebooks and the debates they opened, is the answer to it on the merits.

A second comparison runs in the opposite direction, against the editions of figures whose papers were smaller and whose editors finished. Completion changes an edition's moral character. A finished edition can be judged as a whole, its principles assessed across the entire corpus, its remaining errors catalogued as errata. An unfinished edition must be judged in motion, its later volumes correcting its earlier ones, its principles evolving as editors retire and are replaced, its users asked to trust a process rather than a product. The Marx edition's users have developed the habits appropriate to that condition: citing volume and year, watching for revised printings, treating the newest volume on any text as superseding the project's earlier word. Those habits, learned under necessity, are good scholarly hygiene in any field whose sources are still being established, which is to say, in most fields that touch the past at all.

## Questions the notebooks have opened and not answered

An honest map of the fourth section should mark not only the territory it has delivered but the questions its deliveries have raised faster than its scholarship can settle them, because those open questions are where the next decades of interpretation will be spent. Marx's engagement with the natural sciences is now documented in a depth that his published work never suggested, and the field has not finished deciding what that documentation means for the standing of his theory of production: ornament, foundation, or a parallel research life that touched the theory only at points. The ethnological notebooks have made the late Marx a problem again, and the problem will not close until the remaining notebooks of the final years are edited and the chronology of the last reading is secure. The mathematical manuscripts continue to provoke disagreement about whether their author was resolving a genuine foundational difficulty or pursuing a private satisfaction at the edge of his competence, a disagreement the documents inform but cannot end, since it turns on judgement of the work's quality and not merely on its contents.

Other open questions are quieter and more strictly editorial, and therefore more likely to be settled. The dating of particular notebooks will be refined as the correspondence volumes advance. The identification of sources excerpted without attribution will proceed item by item. The marginalia will be more fully printed, and with them the map of Marx's running arguments with the authors on his shelves. Each settlement will be small, and their sum will be a different Marx again, differing from the Marx of this decade's debates in ways no one can predict, which is precisely the condition the notebook effect describes. The field has learned to expect its subject to change under it, slowly, at the speed of an academy's publication schedule. There are worse things for a scholarly field to learn, and the learning of it may be the edition's most durable gift to the study of any thinker whose papers survive in bulk: the permanent reminder that interpretation is downstream of preservation, and that what a generation can think about its author was decided, in part, by what the previous generation of editors managed to print.

## The strongest objection: cost, scale and the size of the payoff

Every claim made in this guide must survive the objection that the whole enterprise is disproportionate, and the objection should be given in its strongest form rather than in the weak form that is easy to answer. In its strongest form it runs as follows. A vast editorial project, consuming the careers of scores of trained scholars across two generations and the funds of institutes, foundations and academies, has been devoted to establishing the complete text of a nineteenth-century writer whose major works have been available, in serviceable editions, for a century. The same scholarly labour spent elsewhere, on neglected archives, on the history of the movements Marx influenced rather than on the minutiae of his reading, would arguably have produced more knowledge. And the celebrated payoff, the ecological reinterpretation and the late Marx, amounts on inspection to a handful of debates in a specialised literature, refinements of the portrait of a thinker whose historical role was settled by his published books, not by his notebooks. On that accounting the edition is a monument industry, admirable as craftsmanship and indefensible as priorities.

The answer begins by conceding the objection's one solid point. The payoff has been concentrated in a small number of areas. Most volumes of the edition refine rather than revolutionise, most notebooks confirm the picture of a voracious reader rather than overturn it, and no honest advocate should sell the enterprise as a perpetual machine for reinterpretation. The defence rests instead on two grounds, the first of which is the comparison already drawn: the standard being applied, that textual preservation must justify itself by reinterpretations per decade, is one that no critical edition of any figure could meet, and the civilised practice of establishing the texts of major writers has never depended on meeting it. The texts are preserved because a culture that reads an author for centuries owes him, and itself, a reliable text, and the debt does not fluctuate with the fashion of debate.

The second ground is that the concentrated payoff, in the few areas where it fell, fell on load-bearing ground. The ecological reconstruction bears on whether the most influential critique of industrial capitalism contains the resources for thinking about that system's collision with the natural world, a question whose importance does not need arguing to any reader of this century. The late notebooks bear on whether the theory of history in the famous books was a universal sequence or a regional account, a question at the foundation of the doctrine's claims about the non-European world. Two debates of that weight, opened by documents, conducted on evidence, are not a modest return on an edition; they are more than most editions of any author produce in a century, and they arrived as by-products of an enterprise whose primary product is quieter: a text that can be trusted, sentence by sentence, by every future reader who will ever need to know what Marx wrote. The objection is answered, finally, by the archive's own history. The alternative to preservation is not a different scholarship. It is the Moscow outcome, an editor shot and a project halted, with the papers surviving by luck in another country's institute. Measured against that alternative, the slow, expensive, unfinished edition is what success looks like.

## Misreadings this guide corrects

Certain errors recur in otherwise respectable writing about this material, and gathering their corrections in one place serves the reader who has absorbed them from the literature. The first is the belief that the English Collected Works and the critical edition are the same thing in two languages. They differ in purpose, in textual base and in coverage, and the difference decides real arguments. The second is the belief that Marx's complete writings have long been available and that the edition merely reprints them handsomely. The notebooks, the drafts in their full sequence and much of the correspondence were unavailable, in any language, until the edition printed them, and some remain unavailable still. The third error is the belief that the notebooks are marginal, a scholar's garnish. The ecological and late-Marx debates stand as counterexamples: marginal material does not reorganise two fields. The fourth is the belief that the first project was abandoned for scholarly reasons, merged away or superseded. It was destroyed with its editor, and the destruction is a political fact. The fifth is the belief that the edition is finished, an error encouraged by the announcement that one of its four sections is complete. The fourth section, the one on which the liveliest claims depend, is still appearing, and every statement about the edition's contents should be heard with that incompletion in mind.

Underlying all five errors is a single habit of mind that this guide has argued against from its first page: the habit of treating an edition as a transparent window, through which an author's work is simply seen, rather than as a built structure, with a history, an institution, a budget and a schedule, through which the work is mediated. Windows do not get their editors shot. Structures have histories, and the histories have consequences for everything seen through them. The reader who finishes this guide with that one correction installed, who asks of any edition of any author the questions asked here of this one, what does it contain, who made it, what did it cost, what is still missing, has taken from the article the thing it most wanted to give.

## The young Marx in the edition: an afterlife arranged by editors

The first section's reordering of the early writings deserves a fuller treatment than the two famous cases given earlier, because the young Marx is where the edition's editorial decisions have had their widest public consequences. The writings of the 1840s reached the twentieth century late and in pieces: the Paris manuscripts unpublished until the first Moscow edition printed them in the early 1930s, the great polemic against the young Hegelians unpublished until the same enterprise recovered it, the journalism scattered in newspapers that few libraries held complete. A generation of interpreters therefore met the early work after the mature work, in editions whose arrangements reflected the recoverers' sense of what a book should look like. The celebrated division between a young humanist Marx and an old scientific Marx, over which so much ink was spilled in the middle of the last century, was in part a genuine development in the author and in part an artefact of that publishing sequence, since the evidence for continuity and for rupture alike arrived out of order and through other men's editing.

The critical edition's contribution has been to return the early writings to their manuscript condition and their calendar. Notebooks that tradition printed as treatises stand again as notebooks, with their discontinuities visible. Texts that tradition dated by guesswork are dated by evidence, and some famous sequences turn out to run in the opposite direction from the one the older literature assumed, with consequences for any story about which idea came first. The doctoral dissertation and the newspaper editing of the earliest years, once treated as a preface to the real career, can now be read in the density the documents support, since the section prints the juvenilia with the same completeness it grants the masterpieces. None of this settles the interpretive quarrel about the young Marx. It relocates the quarrel onto ground where both sides must cite the same documents in the same order, and relocation of that kind is what editions are for.

A subtler service of the same volumes is the recovery of the first excerpt notebooks as part of the young Marx's record. The excerpting habit began early: the student at Bonn and Berlin filled notebooks with the reading for his dissertation, the Kreuznach notebooks of the early 1840s gathered the historical and political reading behind his critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, and the Paris notebooks stand immediately behind the famous manuscripts of 1844. Printing reading and writing together, in one chronological fabric, dissolves the picture of a thinker who first philosophised and later researched. The young Marx of the edition is a researcher from the start, and the continuity between the Kreuznach excerpts and the London notebooks of the 1850s, four decades of one method, is among the edition's quietest and most consequential demonstrations.

## Engels as an author inside the edition

An edition of two names must do justice to the second, and the treatment of Engels within the project has its own instructive history. The first section prints his works entire, from the journalism of his Bremen and Barmen youth through the great syntheses of his later years, and the printing has not always flattered the legend of the self-effacing partner. His Condition of the Working Class in England stands on its own documentary base in Manchester observation, which the apparatus can now separate from the mythology of collaboration. His popularisations, above all the polemic against Eugen Dühring that became the sourcebook of a doctrine, are printed with their genesis, including the chapters Marx contributed and the readings the two men gave the manuscript together. The unfinished manuscripts on the dialectics of nature, withheld from publication in his lifetime and printed in Moscow in the 1920s, appear in the critical text with their fragmentary character honestly displayed, neither inflated into a system nor apologised into silence.

Engels the excerptor is the edition's other revelation about him. His notebooks, military, scientific and linguistic, show a reader as systematic in his domains as Marx in his, and the correspondence between the two excerpting programmes, visible only when both are printed, explains the partnership's division of intellectual labour better than any memoir. He followed the natural sciences and military science as the partnership's correspondent in those fields, and his judgements reached Marx's drafts through the letters. Scholars who write about either man now work with both archives or explain why not, and the older genre in which Engels appears as a mere echo or a mere corrupter has lost its documentary basis in both directions. The edition did not intend a rehabilitation or a demotion. It printed the papers, and the papers support a working intellectual of independent range whose strengths and limits are both more visible than the legend allowed.

## The plans that failed: the archive of intentions

Among the manuscripts the second section prints, a peculiar class deserves separate notice: the plans. Marx was a compulsive planner of books he did not write. The 1857 introduction sketches a work in five structural parts; the letters of the following years announce a critique of political economy in six books, on capital, landed property, wage labour, the state, foreign trade and the world market; the executed work covers a fraction of the design, and the fraction's relation to the whole has been debated ever since. The critical edition prints the plans, the draft tables of contents, the lists of materials assembled and abandoned, in their places in the sequence, so that the architecture of intention stands beside the architecture of achievement and neither is confused with the other.

The scholarly stakes of printing intentions are real. A large literature reads Capital as the torso of the six-book design and interprets its silences, on the state, on foreign trade, on the world market, as systematic absences awaiting their volumes. Another literature reads the narrowing of the plan as the theory's own development, the design of 1858 superseded by the design the executed work embodies. The documents cannot decide between those readings, since a plan's abandonment is ambiguous evidence, but they discipline both, fixing what was planned when, and what materials toward the unexecuted books survive among the excerpts. The Russian reading belongs here too, for the projected treatment of landed property and of the commune drew Marx toward the agrarian materials of his last decade, and the line from a plan of the 1850s to the notebooks of the 1880s is one the edition's chronological fabric makes it possible to trace for the first time.

There is also a human document embedded in the archive of intentions, and biographers have not been slow to use it. The repeated announcement of imminent completion, across twenty years of letters, beside drafts that show the work continually reopening itself, is the record of a researcher whose standards of finish kept rising with his material. The edition, by printing promises and performance in the same chronological order, replaces the moralising question of why Marx never finished with the documentary answer: the work kept growing at the joints, because the reading kept feeding it, and a man who excerpted agricultural chemistry in the middle of composing a chapter on machinery was never going to reach the end of a plan drawn before the reading began.

## The edition and the Russian thread

No thread in the later archive is more instructive, or more dependent on the edition's combined sections, than the Russian one. Marx followed Russia across his whole career: the diplomacy and history notebooks of the 1850s, the correspondence with Russian scholars and revolutionaries that thickened after the appearance of Capital in Russian translation, the first translation the book received in any language, and in the final decade the sustained study of Russian agrarian conditions, the statistical literature of the zemstvos, and the commune. The correspondence section prints the exchange with Nikolai Danielson, his Russian translator and informant, through which books, statistics and questions flowed to London for a decade. The fourth section prints the reading that the correspondence delivered. The first section prints the drafts of the Zasulich reply and the late journalism where the Russian questions touched print.

Assemble the three sections and a research programme appears that no single section reveals: a thinker testing, in his last years, whether the account of capitalist development he had built on English evidence described a universal road or a regional one, and using Russia, with its communal landholding and its late, state-driven industrialisation, as the test case. The scholars who have made the late Marx a subject, Shanin prominent among them, built their case by assembling exactly this cross-section evidence, much of it available only as the edition delivered it. The point for the reader of this guide is the method on display. The edition's sections are not four separate books but one instrument with four registers, and its characteristic discoveries happen at the joins, where a letter dates a notebook and a notebook explains a draft. Researchers who work in one register only, works without letters, letters without reading, systematically miss the class of finding the instrument was built to produce.

## Working across languages: names, scripts and the friction of the archive

A practical word about the archive's linguistic friction will save researchers real time. Marx read Russian in his last decades and excerpted in it; his notebooks include passages in the Cyrillic script, transliterated and annotated by the editors according to stated conventions. Names travel badly across the edition's languages: a Russian statistician appears in the correspondence under one transliteration, in the notebooks under another, in the secondary literature under a third, and the apparatus indexes exist partly to prevent the three from being mistaken for three men. Scholars citing Russian material from the edition should cite the apparatus identification of persons alongside the passage, since the identification, not the spelling, is the stable element. The same counsel applies to the periodical literature Marx excerpted, whose titles he abbreviated past recognition and whose identification is among the apparatus's most-used services.

Translation friction compounds the script friction at exactly the points of highest theory. The German vocabulary of the manuscripts carries distinctions, between kinds of labour, kinds of value, kinds of subsumption of the worker under capital, that English renders by families of terms whose boundaries do not match the original's. The critical text cannot remove that friction, since it arises between languages and not within the manuscripts, but it localises it: the scholar who knows the precise German sentence, in its draft state, can see which distinctions a translation was forced to spend and which it preserved. Debates in the Anglophone literature that present themselves as disagreements about Marx's theory repeatedly resolve, on inspection of the critical text, into disagreements between translations of a passage whose draft history complicates both. That resolution is available only to those who check, and the checking is the edition's daily justification in the seminar rooms of countries whose language it does not print.

## The notebooks in sequence: four decades of one habit

The fourth section rewards being read as a biography of a habit, and a synoptic pass across its decades gives the reader a possession no single volume supplies. The notebooks of the 1840s are a student's and a young journalist's: philosophy and law at the universities, history and politics at Kreuznach, political economy at Paris and Brussels and Manchester, where in 1845 both men read economics in the libraries of the cotton metropolis during the weeks that also produced their joint manuscripts. The excerpting is already systematic, already argumentative, already interleaved with the drafting it feeds. Nothing in the later method would have surprised the young men of Manchester except its scale.

The London notebooks of the 1850s are the habit at industrial strength. Settled in exile, working in the reading room of the British Museum, Marx excerpted in great organised sweeps: the history of economic doctrine, the technology of production, the money market and the crisis that closed the decade, alongside history, diplomacy and the politics of the European states. These notebooks are the direct supply lines of the economic manuscripts, and their printing has allowed scholars to watch categories being assembled from sources, tested against authorities, and carried into the drafts. The 1860s notebooks narrow toward the composition of Capital and widen, at the same moment, into the natural sciences, as the soil and the machine claim their places in the argument. The notebooks of the final decade, ethnology, Russia, history, mathematics resumed, show the habit outliving the publishing programme it was built to serve, the reading continuing under its own momentum after the books it fed had stopped appearing. Read in sequence, the four decades make the edition's deepest biographical claim without a word of commentary: that the author of the famous volumes was, before and after everything else, a reader, and that the reading, not the system, is the continuous thread of the life.

That synoptic view also corrects a distortion the instalment publication itself creates. Because the fourth section appears volume by volume, scholars meet the notebooks in editorial order, one cluster at a time, and the literature's attention jumps from cluster to cluster as each appears. The sequence disciplines that attention. A notebook of the 1870s on communal property means one thing as an isolated revelation and another, richer thing as the fourth decade of a practice that began with a student excerpting Roman law. The edition prints the practice whole, in the long run, and interpretation matures exactly as the whole comes into view, which is the notebook effect stated from the inside.

## What a finished section makes possible: the Capital scholarship since

The completion of the second section, announced in the early 2010s, changed the working conditions of Capital scholarship in ways worth specifying, because they illustrate what any section's completion does. With the drafts entire in print, arguments about the theory's development could be carried on by citation rather than by archive travel, and a generation of scholars without access to Amsterdam or Moscow entered debates from which the geography of the manuscripts had excluded them. The successive versions of the first volume, the German editions Marx revised, the French edition he reworked, the text Engels standardised after his death, could be compared line by line from printed pages, and the comparison has fed a literature on the value form and on the opening chapters that works from the variants rather than from a single received text. Questions that sounded metaphysical in the older literature, about whether the theory's presentation was finished or provisional, became in part documentary questions with documentary answers.

Completion also disciplines the citing habits of adjacent fields. Economic historians, sociologists and political theorists who use Capital as a source can now be asked, reasonably, which text of it they are using, the author's final revision or his editor's standardisation, the draft or the book. Most will go on citing the standard translation for most purposes, properly, but the existence of the completed section has raised the cost of carelessness at the points where the variants matter. That is the generalisable lesson. An edition does not need to be read by everyone to govern everyone's standards. It needs to exist, complete and checkable, so that any claim can in principle be taken back to the documents, and the fields that cite its author adjust their manners to that possibility. The first, third and fourth sections will exert the same governance as they approach completion, each in its territory, and the fourth, when its remaining decades of notebooks are in print, will do for the study of Marx's reading what the second has done for the study of his masterpiece.

## Criticism of the edition itself

An account that praised the edition's apparatus for inviting audit would fail its own standard if it exempted the edition from the audit, and the scholarly record contains criticisms that a balanced guide must register. Editorial decisions in the first section, above all the presentation of the manuscripts behind The German Ideology, have been contested by scholars who argue that the edition's arrangement, for all its documentary scruple, still shapes a book out of materials whose bookhood is the very thing in question, and that rival arrangements, including digital presentations that let readers assemble the fragments in different orders, expose choices the printed volume naturalises. Transcription conventions have drawn criticism from editors of other projects, on the handling of deletions, the expansion of abbreviations and the treatment of Marx's punctuation, the standing quarrels of the philological guild, conducted here at the level of expertise where they belong. The pace of the fourth section has been criticised by scholars whose fields wait on its volumes, and the criticism is sharpened by the knowledge that editorial generations are mortal in a way that projects are not.

The edition's defenders answer, in the main, from the apparatus itself: contested decisions are printed with their evidence, rival arrangements can be built from the published record, and the alternative to the project's conventions is not a convention-free text but a different set of conventions, less visible than these. On pace, the defence is the labour itself, described earlier in this guide: the handwriting, the source hunting, the dating by triangulation. Both the criticism and the defence belong in a research guide, because the user of an edition should know its contested points as a surveyor knows the contested boundaries of a map, not to distrust the map but to use it at full intelligence. What no serious criticism alleges is bad faith or incompetence in the textual work, and that negative fact, across a century of political temptation, is the edition's most remarkable institutional achievement. The project that began with an editor shot by the state that commissioned him has ended, in its reconstituted form, as a byword for the opposite condition, scholarship whose enemies must attack its conclusions because its methods will not give them purchase.

## Before the critical edition: the partial editions it replaced

The critical edition did not enter an empty field, and its achievement is clearer against the succession of partial editions it superseded. Engels himself was the first editor, shaping the posthumous volumes of Capital and issuing the writings he judged ripe, with an editor's freedoms that the later standard would not have permitted him. After his death the literary executors of the movement published the Nachlass in selections: Franz Mehring's edition of the literary remains in the opening years of the twentieth century gave the early writings and the correspondence of the 1840s their first wide public, in arrangements governed by a biographer's sense of narrative. Karl Kautsky's edition of the manuscripts on the history of economic theory, published as Theories of Surplus Value, reordered its material thematically and presented as a book what its author had written as a working critique, a presentation generations of readers took for Marx's own design until the manuscripts could be inspected in sequence.

The 1930s then delivered the strange double publication that dramatised the need for a critical standard. The manuscripts of 1844 appeared both in the first Moscow edition and, in the same period, in a rival edition prepared by Siegfried Landshut and Jacob Peter Mayer for a commercial publisher, the two texts differing in arrangement and in the completeness of what they printed, so that the scholarly world received the young Marx twice in two shapes at once. The German Ideology likewise reached print through the Moscow enterprise in a form assembled by its editors. For half a century, arguments about the early Marx were arguments conducted across these divergent presentations, often without the disputants noticing that their texts differed. The second critical edition's re-editing of the whole territory from the manuscripts is the permanent settlement of that condition, and the older editions now survive as documents in the history of reception, valuable for what they show about their own decades, superseded as authorities on what Marx wrote.

This prehistory carries a lesson that generalises beyond the one author. Texts do not reach readers; editions reach readers, and every edition is an argument made by its makers about what matters in the material. The partial editions were arguments by disciples, biographers and parties, and their selections taught generations a Marx composed by his followers' priorities. The critical edition is also an argument, but of a different and corrigible kind, since it prints its evidence and its exclusions are, by the completeness principle, designed to be none. The reader who has understood the prehistory will never again ask whether a text is available without asking the further question, available in whose edition, on what textual base, with what silently normalised.

## The journalism and the International: attribution at scale

One territory of the first section deserves separate mention because it shows critical editing solving a problem no reading edition could have cracked: the journalism. For a decade Marx earned his living chiefly as the London correspondent of the New York Tribune, and the hundreds of articles that appeared under his name are a principal source for his views on the politics, wars and economies of the 1850s. The byline, however, conceals the workshop. Engels wrote a substantial share of the articles that appeared as Marx's, particularly the military correspondence, at Marx's request and to Marx's deadlines, while Marx's English was still unequal to the pace; Marx meanwhile kept the notebooks, the reading on India, Spain, Turkey and the diplomacy of the powers, from which both men's articles were built. The critical edition, working from the correspondence, the notebooks and the surviving drafts, assigns the articles between the two hands on documentary evidence, article by article, where the older collections had assigned them by byline and convenience.

The consequences reach into live debates. Arguments about Marx's view of India, of colonial progress, of the Eastern Question, rest heavily on the Tribune correspondence, and the reassignment of articles between the partners has moved particular famous judgements from one man's account to the other's, or into the shared workshop where simple attribution fails. The documents of the International Working Men's Association present the same problem in institutional form, minutes, drafts and addresses passing between hands in a collective that wrote as a committee, and the edition's treatment of them models how attribution should behave when the truth is plural. Scholars who cite the journalism now cite it with its editorial history attached, which is to say they cite it as the edition has taught the field to cite everything: as a document with a provenance, not as an oracle with a byline.

## A worked itinerary: one question through all four sections

The sections of this guide have praised the edition as one instrument with four registers, and the praise should be cashed in a worked example. Take a question of the kind a doctoral researcher actually brings: did Marx, in his final decade, change his mind about whether the Russian peasant commune could serve as the starting point of a development that avoided the capitalist road he had described for the West? The itinerary begins, counterintuitively, not with the famous draft letters but with the correspondence, where the chronology of the Russian engagement can be fixed: the arrival of the Russian translation of Capital, the opening of the exchange with Danielson, the requests for statistical material, the reading programme the letters announce. The correspondence gives the question its dates, and dates are what turn a mood into a position.

The itinerary proceeds to the fourth section, to the reading the correspondence delivered: the excerpts from Kovalevsky on communal landholding, the zemstvo statistics, the ethnological notebooks in which the Russian material sits inside a comparative frame of communal forms from several continents. Here the researcher applies the notebook discipline in full, strata, dating, bridges, and finds the reading dense, technical and sustained across years, the profile of a research front rather than a courtesy interest. From the notebooks the itinerary moves to the first section and the drafts of the reply to Zasulich, four attempts in which a public answer was being composed, never sent in its long forms, each draft more carefully hedged than the last against converting the Western account into a universal fatality. The published record, the preface to the Russian edition of the Manifesto that Marx and Engels signed together, states the public minimum of the position. Assembled, the four registers support a conclusion of a specific and limited kind: that the late Marx treated the commune's fate as an open empirical question, contingent on the Russian state's fiscal pressure and on the survival of communal institutions, and refused, on the record, both the fatalism of his Westernising Russian readers and the romance of the commune's admirers. Note what the itinerary did not do. It did not find a doctrine of the Russian road, because the documents do not contain one. It found a research programme arrested by death, visible only because an edition prints reading, letters and drafts in one chronological fabric. A researcher who has walked this itinerary once possesses the method for every other question of the same shape, and the method, not the conclusion, is the edition's transferable gift.

## Registers, indexes and the unglamorous apparatus

Praise of the apparatus usually fastens on its glamorous functions, the variants and the datings, and neglects the instruments through which the edition is actually navigated day by day: the registers. The volumes carry indexes of persons, identifying the hundreds of contemporaries who pass through the correspondence and the notebooks, from ministers and generals to the landladies and booksellers of the exile years, with the biographical minimum that makes a reference usable. They carry indexes of the literature excerpted and cited, the map of the reading itself, from which a scholar can reconstruct, for any year, exactly what stood open on Marx's desk among the printed works of his century. They carry subject indexes and lists of the manuscripts with their repositories and shelfmarks, the finding aids of the archive translated into print.

These instruments are easy to mock as pedantry and impossible to replace in practice. The person index alone embodies thousands of small identifications, any one of which, got wrong, would send a biographer's chapter into error, and got right, allows the correspondence to be read as the social document it is. The literature index is the nearest thing in existence to a catalogue of a great reader's mind, and historians of reading as a discipline have begun to use it as such, comparing Marx's excerpting practice with the commonplace traditions behind him and the research technologies ahead of him. When the history of this edition is written at proper length, the registers will deserve their chapter, as the place where the project's collective labour most exceeds what any individual scholar could have contributed, and where its volumes stop being editions of texts and become the reference infrastructure of a field.

## The project's future, stated with the guide's own discipline

This guide has refused throughout to state as fact what a moving project has not finished, and its closing prospect obeys the same rule. What can be said durably is structural. The edition continues under its Amsterdam foundation and its academy partners, with the second section complete, the first and third far advanced in their sequences, and the fourth appearing volume by volume, the hardest material last, as it was always going to be. The plan under which the custodians work is the reduced plan adopted after the project's reconstitution, and it measures the remaining work in decades rather than years, a timescale the project's friends state openly and its critics quote as though it were a confession rather than a description of philology at this scale. Electronic forms of access are developing under the custodians' direction, and the researcher should consult the custodians' own listings for their state rather than rely on any printed description, this one included.

What the completion of the remaining sections will do to interpretation can be predicted in form though not in content, because the form has repeated three times already. A territory of claims now held provisionally will harden into checkable form. Some settled judgements will meet documents that complicate them, most severely where the claims were built loudest on the least evidence. New questions, of the kind the ethnological notebooks posed when no one was asking them, will arrive with the remaining notebooks of the early and middle years. And the notebook effect will run its course to the end, the schedule of an academy's publishing programme having quietly governed, for a century and a half after its subject's death, the order in which the world was permitted to know what Karl Marx read, wrote and thought. The edition's last volume, whenever it comes, will not close the subject. It will complete the evidence, which is a different and better thing, and the arguments will proceed on it, as they should, indefinitely.

## Who cites the edition, and how citation changed

The edition's influence can be measured in the footnotes of fields that rarely acknowledge it, and tracing that influence completes the account of the notebook effect. Historians of economic thought cite the second section when the argument concerns the development of the theory of value, because the drafts are now the common evidence. Historians of science cite the fourth section's science notebooks in revising the picture of how a nineteenth-century social theorist consumed the research of his day. Anthropologists meet the edition through the ethnological notebooks, which document an early, critical engagement with their discipline's founding texts from the receiving end. Geographers and environmental historians meet it through the soil and metabolism materials. In each case the pattern repeats: a neighbouring field discovers that its questions about Marx were being answered from a selection, and that the selection has been superseded by documents. The citation of the edition across those fields is the quiet index of how far the consequences of an editorial project travel beyond the guild that produced it.

Citation practice inside Marx scholarship changed in step, and the change is a useful marker of scholarly seriousness for any reader learning to judge the literature. The older practice cited the works in whatever edition lay nearest, by the pagination of a translation, with the result that two scholars quoting the same sentence might be quoting different texts of it. The practice the edition has imposed cites the work in its documentary identity, the manuscript or the version, with the edition's coordinates where precision is needed, and reserves translation pagination for the argument rather than the evidence. Literature reviews in the field now routinely sort claims by their documentary base, pre-edition and post-edition, notebook-based and works-based, and the sorting is not pedantry. It is the field's immune system, developed after a century in which confident systems were built on texts their builders had never seen established.

## Three cautions for readers of the notebook literature

The notebook literature's growth makes a short code of cautions worth stating in one place, gathered from the failures the field has already committed and corrected. The first caution concerns selection. A notebook corpus of this size contains passages that can be marshalled for almost any portrait of its author, the ecologist, the productivist, the Eurocentric theorist, the multilinear pluralist, and the marshalled passages are all genuine. The defence against portrait-shopping is the whole-corpus question: not whether the passage exists, but what its weight is in the reading as a whole, its date, its recurrence, its fate in the drafts. Interpreters who answer the whole-corpus question earn their portraits. Those who cannot, or will not, are quoting a quarry.

The second caution concerns second-hand quotation. Notebook passages circulate in the secondary literature detached from their strata and their dating, translated at second hand, trimmed at third, until a sentence attributed to Marx may be a source's sentence, a translator's compression and an interpreter's gloss in layers no one has audited. The remedy is the one this guide has repeated: return to the edition's coordinates, check the stratum, check the date, and quote from the page or not at all. The third caution concerns the romance of the unpublished. Unpublished material carries an aura of suppressed truth, as though what an author did not print must be what he really thought. The notebooks invert the romance. They are, in the main, what a researcher read on the way to thinking, and their authority over the published works is the authority of the workshop over the building, real for questions of construction and void for questions of design. Readers who hold all three cautions together can take from the notebook literature its genuine riches, which are considerable, without buying its occasional counterfeits.

## What the edition cannot do

A balanced guide should end its survey by marking the instrument's limits, since an edition oversold is an edition distrusted. The critical edition cannot recover what is lost. Notebooks known from references and never found will not be printed, and the gaps in the record are permanent features of the evidence, to be reasoned around rather than filled. It cannot make its author consistent. Where the documents show Marx changing his mind, hesitating between formulations, or pursuing incompatible lines in the same season, the edition prints the inconsistency in its dated order and leaves the interpretive pain where it belongs, with the interpreters. It cannot settle the disputes it enables, because evidence disciplines argument without replacing it: the ecological dispute and the dispute over the late Marx will be conducted better because of the notebooks and will not be ended by them. And it cannot compel its own use. Scholars will go on citing the translation on the desk for most purposes, properly, and the edition's governance of the field will remain what it has been, a standing possibility of correction rather than a police force.

There is a final limit of a different order, and it belongs to the historical record rather than to method. The edition cannot give back the century it might have had. The project destroyed in Moscow in the 1930s would have delivered its texts to a world that argued about Marx with states and movements at stake in the argument, and what that world would have made of the notebooks in the 1940s rather than the 2000s is a question history does not answer. The edition that exists is the one the twentieth century permitted, belated, rescued and unfinished, and the scholarship it serves is conducted in full knowledge of the belatedness. That knowledge, the sense that the evidence arrived late and might not have arrived at all, is written into the field's best work as a kind of gratitude with an edge to it. An instrument that teaches its users how contingent its own existence was has done something for scholarship beyond scholarship: it has made the preservation of the record itself visible as an achievement, fragile and political and worth defending wherever records are kept.

## One manuscript, three decisions: the editorial guidelines in practice

The edition's editorial guidelines run to a forbidding document, and their meaning is best grasped by watching them work on a single recalcitrant page, of a kind the fourth section supplies by the thousand. Imagine a notebook page of the middle 1860s: a condensed excerpt from an agricultural chemistry text occupies the top two thirds, in German, following the source's order; the bottom third carries a calculation, two sentences of comment in English, and a reference, abbreviated to near invisibility, to a draft chapter. The editors' first decision concerns the text itself: the excerpt is collated against the printed source, and the collation reveals that the condensation silently alters a figure and drops a qualification. Both facts go to the apparatus, because a future interpreter building on the figure must know that it reached Marx altered, and that the alteration may be his. The second decision concerns the strata: the two English sentences are Marx's own, but the first of them begins as a paraphrase so close to the source that the boundary has to be argued, and the apparatus argues it, citing the source's wording and the habits of the surrounding pages. The third decision concerns dating and placing: the source's publication gives the earliest possible day, the draft reference gives a relation to the composition sequence, and the letters of the season either confirm the reading or leave the span open, in which case the apparatus prints the span and not a year.

Three such decisions on one page, repeated across tens of thousands of pages, constitute the edition's real content beyond the texts themselves. They also explain the otherwise puzzling fact that the edition's volumes are reviewed, in the scholarly journals, as contributions to knowledge in their own right rather than as containers. A dating argued in an apparatus can redirect a subfield. A source identified for an anonymous excerpt can connect Marx's reading to a scientific controversy no one knew he had followed. The guidelines exist to make those contributions auditable: every decision of the three kinds is rendered in a uniform notation, so that the reader who wishes to quarrel with a dating knows exactly which evidence the editors weighed and which they may have missed. Scholarship that exposes itself to correction at that granularity has earned the authority the edition is usually accorded, and the accord is renewed, volume by volume, on the evidence.

## The library on the shelves: marginalia and the annotated books

The marginalia printed in the fourth section derive from a physical survival as improbable as the manuscripts themselves: a substantial part of the private library Marx and Engels assembled, the working books of two households, preserved through the same exiles and sales that scattered the papers and coming to rest, in the main, with the Amsterdam institute. The books matter because of what their owners did in them. Marx annotated as he read, underlining in pencil and ink, scoring passages with vertical strokes, and filling margins with the compressed judgements, agreements, objections and occasional jeers, that his fuller comments expand in the notebooks. A reader who works through an annotated volume of an economist Marx fought for decades watches an argument conducted at the speed of reading, years before the polemic reached print.

Two classes of marginalia carry particular weight. The first is Marx's annotation of other men's systems, the political economists, historians and naturalists whose pages supplied him with both his materials and his opponents, where the margin records the exact point at which assent stopped. The second is his annotation of his own books: the copies of his published works, above all of Capital, in which he entered corrections, expansions and second thoughts for editions he planned and sometimes made. Those annotated copies stand behind the variant history the second section prints, and they give the lie, in documentary form, to the picture of a doctrine delivered complete and thereafter defended. The author's own margins kept moving after publication, like the reading, like the drafts, and the fourth section's printing of them completes the edition's central portrait of a mind whose work was a process the books only intermittently surfaced from.

Handling marginalia as evidence requires the most delicate version of the notebook discipline, and the edition's apparatus models it. A mark is dated, where dating is possible, by the edition of the book and the evidence of the hand; a note's placement is described, since a comment in the margin of a page argues with that page and not with the book entire; and the temptation to treat a jeer as a doctrine is refused by the presentation itself, which prints the jeer among its neighbours, assents and queries alike. The scholarly literature has not always matched that delicacy, and more than one sweeping claim about Marx's view of a contemporary rests on a marginal word whose neighbours reverse its apparent sense. The field's rule, learned the hard way, is the one this guide has applied to every class of document in the estate: context first, quotation second, doctrine last of all, if at all.

## How a notebook finding travels: from apparatus to textbook

Between a notebook's appearance in a volume of the fourth section and its arrival in an undergraduate textbook there runs a relay with recognisable stages, and knowing the stages teaches a reader how much confidence to place in a finding at any moment of its journey. The first stage is the edition itself: text and apparatus, the finding present but buried, one item among thousands in a volume few will open. The second stage is the specialist article, in which a scholar who has done the strata and dating work announces what the notebook shows and submits the chain of evidence to referees who can check it against the printed page. The third is the monograph, where findings accumulate into an interpretation, the ecological reconstruction being the type case, with the strengths and the exposures that accumulation brings. The fourth is the review literature and the conference corridor, where the interpretation is stress-tested by scholars with rival commitments and equal access to the documents. The fifth, arriving years later, is the textbook and the syllabus, where the survivor of the earlier stages is compressed into a paragraph and taught as the state of knowledge.

The relay matters because confidence should be calibrated by stage. A claim at the second stage is a proposal with its evidence attached. A claim at the fifth stage is a consensus with its evidence forgotten, and the forgetting is precisely what the next volume of notebooks may punish, since the edition is still delivering stages one and two while the textbooks are teaching stage five. The mature reader of this field holds the textbook paragraph in one hand and the edition's schedule in the other, and treats the distance between them as the measure of the paragraph's risk. That habit, generalised, is the notebook effect converted from a fact about Marx scholarship into a piece of intellectual self-defence usable anywhere that interpretation rests on an archive still being published.

## The two hands: telling Marx from Engels on the page

One of the edition's most delicate crafts deserves a closing word, because it underlies every attribution claim in the literature and is invisible when done well. The two men whose names the edition bears wrote, for stretches of their collaboration, in hands that contemporaries could confuse and that the surviving secretaries, family copyists and later transcribers confused in fact. Manuscripts passed between them for correction; drafts were copied by wives, daughters and comrades; a fair copy in a third hand may embody either man's text. The editors therefore work as palaeographers before they work as interpreters: hands are distinguished by their letter forms, their ink, their habits of abbreviation and correction, and the distinguished hand is then weighed with the internal evidence, the vocabulary, the argument, the state of the author's known projects at the document's date.

The yield of that craft is a slow correction of the canon at its edges. Articles migrate between the partners. A manuscript long printed as a joint work resolves into one man's draft with the other's marginal corrections, a distinction that changes the weight the text can bear in arguments about doctrine. The famous cases, the division of labour in the manuscripts of the 1840s, the editing of the posthumous volumes of Capital, are only the visible peaks of a correction that proceeds item by item through thousands of pages. Readers who have never considered how an editor knows whose hand holds the pen are like readers of a map who have never considered surveying: the product looks like a simple representation until the labour that guarantees it is described. The edition's guarantee, printed in its apparatus for any sceptic to test, is what separates the attributions the field now relies on from the attributions of convenience that filled the older literature, and the separation is among the strongest answers available to anyone who asks what the long project's money and decades actually bought.

## A first visit: what to do with a volume in your hands

Practical guidance in this guide has so far concerned projects and citations, and one gap remains: the physical encounter. A researcher who has ordered a volume of the fourth section to a library desk, and who opens it without preparation, faces a forbidding object: hundreds of pages of transcribed notebooks, in three languages and a thicket of editorial signs, with the apparatus in German ranged behind or beside the texts. The first visit succeeds or fails on a handful of decisions made in the first hour, and they are worth stating for the newcomer this guide may send to the shelves.

Begin with the volume's own introduction and editorial report, in which the editors describe the manuscripts the volume contains, their physical state, their provenance and the principles applied to their dating. Skip it and the texts float; read it and every page that follows has a frame. Proceed next to one notebook entire, rather than grazing across many, and read it against the grain of the questions brought to it: first for its sources, letting the apparatus name them, then for its strata, then for its bridges outward. Keep the record described in the working section of this guide from the first page, coordinates onward, because reconstructing a trail across a volume once read is the classic beginner's tax, paid in a second visit that a good record makes unnecessary. Use the registers as they were built to be used, the literature index to discover what else in the volume, or in its companion volumes, comes from the same season of reading, the person index to stabilise the names that swim in the correspondence of the same months. And end the first visit by writing, before memory edits it, a page of orientation for the second: what the volume actually contains against what the catalogue promised, which notebooks reward a full pass, and which questions the material has refused. That page is the researcher's own apparatus, and the habit of writing it is the point at which the edition's method stops being the editors' property and becomes the reader's.

The same orientation, compressed, serves the reader consulting a single volume for a single fact. Find the text through the registers, read it with its apparatus notes and not around them, note the dating evidence beside the passage in the citation, and record which manuscript, in which repository, stands behind the printed page. Ten minutes of that discipline converts a quotation into evidence. Its absence converts evidence into decoration, and the literature of this field, at its worst, is decorated exactly so.

## A note on this guide's own discipline

A research guide about an edition owes its readers the same accounting it demands of interpreters, and this closing section pays the debt briefly. The account given here is structural and historical rather than enumerative: it names the edition's sections, its custodians, its phases and its effects, and it deliberately declines the two kinds of statement that decay fastest in print, the running total of published volumes and the schedule of promised ones. Readers who need those figures need them fresh, from the custodians, at the moment of use, and a guide that printed them would be selling its readers a false currency within a few seasons. The attributions made here follow the same rule: the ecological reconstruction is given to Foster, Burkett and Saito, the multilinear reading of the late Marx to Shanin and the scholars around his collection, the editorial findings to the edition's apparatus, because claims in this field belong to their makers and a guide that absorbed them into a voiceless consensus would be committing the offence it exists to correct. Where the record is disputed, the dispute is reported and left standing. Where this guide has generalised, it has generalised about method, the one territory where its evidence, the documents and their history, is complete enough to bear the weight. The rest it leaves, as the edition leaves so much, to the next volume and the reader who checks.

One habit, finally, is worth passing on as the guide's last practical word, because it costs nothing and protects everything built on this material. Whenever a statement about the edition is about to do work in an argument, a footnote, a syllabus or a piece of journalism, let the statement be sorted into one of two piles before it is used. In the first pile go the durable facts: the four sections and their contents, the original languages and the German apparatus, the fate of the first project, the succession of custodians, the completion of the second section, the existence and the character of the notebook debates. Those facts may be used freely and will not rot. In the second pile go the perishable facts: counts, schedules, availability, the state of any section's frontier. Those may be used only with a date attached and a fresh check behind them, against the custodians' listings or the holding library's catalogue. A literature that sorted its facts that way, as a matter of reflex, would be embarrassed by its sources far less often than the literature of this field has historically been, and the sorting takes less time than a single embarrassed correction. The edition has spent a century teaching that lesson by example. This guide ends by simply saying it.

## For students and exam candidates

For students meeting this material in a syllabus, the edition itself is rarely the set text, but its findings stand behind the questions examiners ask about the late Marx, about Marx and nature, and about the reliability of the texts in translation. The profitable approach is to learn the structural facts as a map: four sections, original languages, the Collected Works as the English reading edition of fifty volumes, the notebooks as the fourth section and the source of the newest interpretations. Examination answers gain credit by attributing notebook-based claims to the scholars who built them and by registering the standing caveat, that a claim resting on the published works alone is exposed where the reading was the research. Know the first project's fate in one sentence, Ryazanov arrested and executed and the edition halted, because it is the fact examiners use to test whether a candidate's knowledge is documentary or decorative. Above all, avoid the two symmetrical errors: treating the edition as a rumour, and treating any single notebook finding as a new doctrine. The material rewards precision about what kind of evidence a notebook is, and the H3 question on what a notebook can prove is the one to internalise before any examination on sources.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teaching this material works best backwards, from a live dispute to the documents that made it possible. Set a seminar the ecological question or the question of the late Marx's multilinear development, let the strongest published claims be stated from the secondary literature, and then put the class to the evidential question: what would have to exist, in print, for this claim to be checkable? The section map on the page above is the teaching instrument for the second hour, because it converts an abstract editorial project into four territories whose states of completion explain why the debate has the shape it has. Students who grasp that the fourth section is unfinished grasp why the literature's claims arrive with dates attached. A productive exercise closes the unit: give the class a notebook-based claim from a journal article and require a one-page audit of its evidential chain, source, stratum, dating, bridge, before any discussion of whether the claim is attractive. Teachers pressed for time can compress the whole topic into that single exercise, since it teaches the edition, the method and the scepticism in one motion, and leaves students with a transferable habit for every archive-based literature they will meet.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, this article's counsel reduces to a discipline of citation and a discipline of expectation. Cite the critical edition for anything that turns on wording, dating, attribution or the notebooks, cite the Collected Works for the published arguments where its text is sufficient, and record the apparatus evidence whenever a notebook is quoted, coordinates, dating, source, stratum and bridge, as the working section of this guide sets out. Expect the field to move: check the custodians' listings before building on any statement about the edition's contents, including the statements in this guide, and date your own notebook-dependent claims to the state of publication you actually used. Historians of the project itself will find the institutional thread the richest unworked seam, the succession from Ryazanov's institute through the party institutes to the Amsterdam foundation being a compact history of twentieth-century scholarship's entanglement with power. Journalists face the narrowest brief and the easiest errors: newly published Marx material is news only when a scholar has built a checkable claim on it, the edition's schedule is measured in years rather than news cycles, and no announcement about the project should be reported without the section and the volume being named. A claim that cannot name its document is not a story. It is a rumour with a famous man attached.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is MEGA2?

MEGA2 is the second historical-critical complete edition of the works of Marx and Engels, the successor to the Moscow project destroyed in the 1930s and the enterprise under which their writings are established from the manuscripts. It prints everything the two men wrote, drafted, excerpted and annotated, in the languages of composition, with a scholarly apparatus in German recording variants, dating and genesis. Its four sections cover the works, the Capital manuscripts, the correspondence and the excerpt notebooks. Custody rests with an international foundation in Amsterdam, working in association with a Berlin academy, and the edition remains in progress.

### Q: What is the difference between MEGA and the Collected Works?

The difference is one of purpose and of textual base. The Collected Works is a reading edition in English, fifty volumes, giving the writings in translation for the general scholarly public, with texts taken in the main from the best editions available at the time. MEGA is a critical edition: it establishes every text afresh from the manuscripts, prints the original languages, records every variant in a German apparatus, and includes the excerpt notebooks entire, which the Collected Works does not print. For the published arguments, the English edition serves most readers well. For exact wording, dating, attribution and anything resting on the notebooks, the critical edition is the court of appeal.

### Q: What happened to the first MEGA project?

The first complete edition was launched in the late 1920s by the Marx-Engels Institute in Moscow under its director, David Ryazanov, and around a dozen volumes had appeared to international scholarly respect. In the early 1930s Ryazanov was arrested in the purges, removed from the institute he had founded as an editorial enterprise, exiled, later rearrested and executed in the latter part of the decade. The institute was absorbed into a larger party body and the edition was halted. Its destruction was a political act against its editor, not a scholarly judgement on its work, and the second edition later resumed its standards under a much enlarged design.

### Q: How many volumes will MEGA2 have?

No final count can be stated responsibly, and any source that offers one without qualification should be distrusted. The project as designed in the 1970s was planned on a very large scale, well beyond a hundred volumes. After 1990 its new custodians reviewed the inherited design and reduced it substantially, and the plan has been administered as a living document rather than a contract. What can be stated durably is the structure: four sections, of which the second, containing Capital and its preparatory manuscripts, has been completed, while the fourth, the notebooks, continues to appear. For the state of publication at any moment, the custodians' own listings are the only reliable source.

### Q: What is in the fourth section of MEGA2?

The fourth section contains the excerpt notebooks and marginalia of Marx and Engels: the notebooks in which, across four decades, they copied, condensed and commented on their reading, together with the notes written in the margins of their own books. Marx's notebooks dominate by bulk and range across political economy, agricultural chemistry, geology, ethnology, mathematics, history and the agrarian conditions of Russia, among much else. Because excerpt and comment share the page, the section's apparatus works line by line to distinguish the copied from the condensed and the original. It is the least complete section of the edition and the one on which the most consequential reinterpretations have been built.

### Q: Has MEGA2 changed how Marx is read?

Yes, in identifiable places, and the changes arrived through documents rather than manifestos. The first section rearranged famous manuscripts whose familiar order proved to be an earlier editor's construction, changing what those texts can be quoted as saying. The second section made the twenty-year genesis of Capital inspectable and located Engels's editorial hand in the posthumous volumes page by page. The fourth section supplied the evidence for the ecological reconstruction of Marx's natural science reading and for the account of his late ethnological studies. Against those cases stands the larger truth that most volumes refine rather than overturn. The edition's deepest effect is methodological: claims in this field now carry an implicit date, the state of the edition when they were made.

### Q: Are Marx's excerpt notebooks worth studying?

They are worth studying for any question on which Marx's research outran his publishing, and not otherwise, which is a more precise answer than either enthusiasm or dismissal offers. On nature, on pre-capitalist societies, on the sciences of his day and on the sources of Capital's categories, the notebooks are primary evidence of a kind that exists nowhere else. On questions the published works settle, they add depth rather than correction. The discipline they demand is the condition of their value: the strata of each page must be checked, the dating evidence weighed, and a bridge into his own writing found before a notebook entry is quoted as his view. Studied at that standard, they repay the labour as few archives do.

### Q: How do you access MEGA2 material?

Access runs through libraries rather than bookshops. The volumes are held by national and university libraries in the centres where research on Marx is conducted, and interlibrary loan reaches most scholars who lack a local set. The custodians, the International Marx-Engels Foundation in Amsterdam and its academy partners, publish the listings against which the state of any section should be checked before a project is planned around it. The original manuscripts themselves are held chiefly in Amsterdam and Moscow and are consulted, where consultation is granted, by arrangement with the holding institutions. Because the project is alive, every statement about holdings, including the ones in this guide, should be verified against the institution's catalogue at the moment of need.

### Q: Is MEGA2 available in English?

No, and the honest answer matters because the question is asked so often. The critical edition prints its texts in the languages of composition, German, English and French as written, and its scholarly apparatus in German, and no complete English version exists or is planned as such. What English readers have is the fifty-volume Collected Works for the writings in translation, established translations of the major works, and a growing body of scholarship that reports the critical edition's findings, including translations of individual manuscripts and notebook materials as scholars produce them. The gap is real and shapes the field: interpretations reach the Anglophone public in the order their documentary bases are translated, which is one of the mechanisms behind the notebook effect.

### Q: What did the notebooks reveal about Marx and nature?

They revealed the depth and the technical character of a reading that the published works had only hinted at. The notebooks on agricultural chemistry, above all the excerpts from Liebig and the German agronomic literature, together with the geology and the wider natural science reading, show Marx following the soil science of his day closely across the years of Capital's composition. On that evidence the Japanese scholar Kohei Saito built a prize-winning reconstruction of Marx's ecological research programme, extending Foster's earlier metabolic reading, which had been constructed from the published texts. Other scholars caution that excerpts record reading rather than doctrine. The dispute continues on documents the edition supplied, which is itself the revelation's most durable part.

### Q: How does the notebook effect change a claim built on the published works alone?

It changes its status from settled to exposed. A claim about Marx's view of nature, of non-European societies or of science, built only on the works he published, rests on a selection he made under the pressures of his lifetime from a research process that continued after those works and ranged wider than them. The notebook effect names the pattern by which such claims have repeatedly been revised when the relevant excerpts appeared. The claim is not thereby refuted; on most questions the published works remain the theory's public form and the notebooks adjust the margins. But the burden shifts. The claimant who ignores available notebook evidence must now explain the omission, where once the notebooks' absence explained everything.

### Q: What should a researcher record from an excerpt notebook before quoting it?

Six things, recorded at the moment of consultation rather than reconstructed later. The edition's coordinates, section, volume and page, so the passage has a fixed address. The dating the apparatus assigns and the evidence offered for it. The source being excerpted, identified from the apparatus or the notebook's heading. The stratum of the page the words belong to, copy, condensation or the excerptor's own comment. The language of the passage, with any translation marked as the researcher's own. And the bridge, the draft, letter or published passage where the material is taken up, if one exists. A quotation carrying that record can be audited by any reader; a quotation without it travels on trust, and notebook scholarship has been damaged too often by trust to extend it further.

### Q: Why does the fourth section matter to scholars of Marx's science?

Because it is the only place where the science can be studied as a working input rather than admired as a finished allusion. The published works cite the sciences sparingly and at the point of use; the notebooks show the reading whole, its sequence, its depth and its dead ends, across chemistry, geology, physiology and mathematics. Scholars of Marx's science can therefore ask questions the published record cannot support: which authorities he trusted, where his understanding was that of his decade and where it ran ahead of it, how a scientific doctrine changed as it passed from excerpt to draft. The mathematical manuscripts alone revised the assessment of his scientific ambition. Without the fourth section, Marx's science is a matter of inference from allusions; with it, the subject has documents.

### Q: How did the edition's long delays shape what scholars could argue?

The delays rationed the evidence by century. The first project's destruction postponed the whole enterprise by a generation; the relaunch delivered the works first, the Capital manuscripts next and the notebooks last, because that order suited the institutions and the difficulty of the material rather than the curiosity of interpreters. Scholarship followed the deliveries. Twentieth-century debate was a debate about the published books because the books were what existed in reliable form; the genesis debate followed the second section; the nature and late-Marx debates followed the notebooks and could not have preceded them. Whole positions were thus held, defended and abandoned in ignorance of evidence that existed in an archive the whole time. Remembering that history is the best protection against repeating it, since the fourth section is still arriving and the next rationing is in progress.

### Q: What separates a critical edition from a collected works edition in use?

In use, the separation is between two kinds of question a reader brings to a page. The collected works edition answers the question of what the author argued, giving a reliable reading text, in this case in translation, that a seminar can discuss and a scholar can cite for the argument. The critical edition answers the questions underneath: what exactly was written, in what order, with what revisions, and from which manuscripts the printed text claims its authority, recording the evidence so that its answers can be audited. The separation becomes practical the moment an argument turns on a wording, a date, an attribution or an unpublished text. At that moment only one of the two editions can help, and knowing which one, before the footnote is written, is the whole of the distinction in daily use.
