The 1991 reform in Indian politics is habitually filed as an episode in economics, a technocratic answer to a balance of payments emergency, and the filing conceals the event. Between 21 June 1991, when P. V. Narasimha Rao took office at the head of a minority government, and 24 July 1991, when Finance Minister Manmohan Singh presented his first budget and the government tabled its Statement on Industrial Policy, a political order was renegotiated. The men who carried out the renegotiation held no electoral instruction for it. Rao was a member of neither House of Parliament when he became Prime Minister. Singh had never fought an election. The Congress manifesto had promised fiscal discipline and modernisation in general language and had placed neither devaluation, nor the abolition of industrial licensing, nor majority foreign equity before the voters of May and June 1991. Every measure of that July therefore had to be authored, defended and paid for in political currency, and the record of how it was done (the budget speech, the industrial policy statement, the correspondence with the International Monetary Fund, the revolt inside the Congress Parliamentary Party) is a record of politics conducted through economic instruments.

![Manmohan Singh presenting the budget of 24 July 1991 and the 1991 reform as a political settlement - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-107.webp)

The claim defended across this pillar hub is stated plainly. Indian economic policy reads primarily as political-architectural content: economic decisions perform political work, are shaped by political conditions, and furnish the material out of which parties build their public identities. The July 1991 reform is the paradigmatic case for this reading, which the master framework for the series calls Framework 9, Reform as Politics. July 1991 is paradigmatic not because it was the largest economic event of the republic's first seven decades, but because its political machinery lies exposed. A government too weak to survive a routine vote rewired the commanding heights of the economy, and it survived. Explaining that survival requires the political reading; the economic reading alone cannot say why the patient lived.

The pillar that this article opens applies the same reading to the planned economy Jawaharlal Nehru built as a political compact, to the nationalisations Indira Gandhi wielded as political grammar, to the sanctions response of 1998, to the Right to Information settlement of 2005, to the crisis management of 2008, to demonetisation in 2016, to the Goods and Services Tax in 2017 and to the corporate tax cut and production-linked incentive scheme of 2019 and 2020. Each of those moments is treated in its own article in this pillar. The task here is to establish the frame, adjudicate the rival accounts of 1991 on the primary documents, and show why the settlement struck in July 1991 was inherited, rather than reversed, by every government that followed Rao's. A companion frame in this series, [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), performs the same operation on external policy; the two frames meet in 1991, when a foreign exchange crisis made the boundary between domestic and external policy impossible to police.

## Two Readings of Indian Economic Policy and the Case for a Third

The technical-economic-decision reading treats the measures of July 1991 as answers to an optimisation problem. Jagdish Bhagwati and Arvind Panagariya, in India's Tryst with Destiny (2012), write the reforms as the moment Indian policy finally aligned incentives with growth: licensing had suppressed entrepreneurship, trade protection had taxed exports, and the 1991 package removed distortions whose costs economists had documented for decades. On this reading, the content of the reform is its explanation. The measures were correct, their correctness was knowable in advance from the literature Bhagwati himself had helped build since the nineteen sixties, and the crisis merely opened a door that argument had already unlocked. Politics enters as friction, the resistance of lobbies and habits that a determined finance minister had to push through.

The policy-trajectory reading distributes the same event across a decade. Vijay Joshi and I. M. D. Little, in India's Economic Reforms 1991-2001 (1996), reconstruct the sequence measure by measure: stabilisation first, then trade reform, industrial deregulation, financial reform, each step dated and graded. The trajectory reading has the virtue of accuracy about pace. Reform in India was not a single explosion; the rupee reached current account convertibility only in August 1994, capital account questions stayed open, and disinvestment crept where the 1991 statements had gestured at strides. Yet the trajectory reading, like the technical reading, treats the political setting as scenery. It can say what happened between 1991 and 2001. It is quieter on why a Lok Sabha in which Congress held 232 of 521 seats voted, abstained and manoeuvred in ways that let the sequence continue.

The standard survey of the event, of which the encyclopaedia treatment of economic liberalisation in India is the most read specimen, shows the limits of both readings in their popular form. The survey proceeds by chronology and taxonomy: a crisis paragraph with the reserve figures, a Rao and Singh paragraph, the measures sorted under liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation, a growth table for the decades after, a criticisms section in which inequality and agriculture appear. Every fact in it can be correct and the event still goes missing, because the survey's categories cannot hold the questions this article is built from. Under which heading does the survey file Rao's refusal to let his finance minister resign to the party's anger, or the decision to table the industrial policy on budget day, or the fertiliser rollback that saved the programme by wounding it, or the funeral arrangements of December 2004? These are not colour around the reform. They are the reform's load-bearing members, and a taxonomy of measures has no shelf for them. The pillar's wager is that readers who meet the event first as politics will understand its economics better than readers who meet it as a list of measures and never learn why the list survived contact with a Parliament elected to oppose most of it.

The third reading, defended here, begins from the authorship of decisions. Every economic instrument in the Indian record was signed by a named officeholder, announced to a named audience, and priced in a currency of votes, party cohesion and institutional survival. The licence that a joint secretary in the Ministry of Industry granted or withheld between 1951 and 1991 was an economic fact and simultaneously a political resource. The budget that a finance minister reads to the Lok Sabha is an accounting document and simultaneously a speech act that distributes honour, blame and expectation. Reading economic policy as politics does not deny that reserves, deficits and exchange rates obey arithmetic. It insists that arithmetic never selected its own government, wrote its own speech, or absorbed its own blame.

### Why does the reform as politics framework treat the budget as a political document?

The budget is treated as a political document because it allocates more than money: it assigns honour and blame, sequences pain, and addresses creditors in Washington and voters in Nandyal at once. The budget speech of 24 July 1991 carried a programme no manifesto had announced, so its rhetoric, its omissions and its targets were themselves acts of government.

Three features of the Indian record force the political reading on any honest account. The first is the identity of the decision-makers. The decisive economic turns of the republic were taken by politicians facing elections, rivals and party factions: Nehru defending planning against his own finance ministers, Indira Gandhi nationalising banks in the middle of a party split in July 1969, Rao and Singh governing without a majority in 1991. The second is the reversibility test. Measures that were economically available for decades, documented in official committees from the Hazari report of 1967 to the Narasimham committee of 1985, became law only when a political configuration permitted them; the delay is a political fact, not an economic one. The third is the settlement effect. The 1991 measures outlived their authors, their party and their century's turn, surviving the defeats of 1996, the coalitions of 1996 to 1999, the Bharatiya Janata Party governments of 1998 to 2004 and the return of Singh himself as Prime Minister in 2004. A merely technical correction does not acquire that durability. A political settlement does.

The gold shipments of 1991 show the three readings at work on a single episode. The technical reading files the pledge of 47 tonnes in July 1991 as a financing operation: reserves were short, gold was the asset a creditor would accept, the operation raised hard currency and the metal was bought back within the year. The trajectory reading places the shipments in a sequence of expedients between the January drawings from the Fund and the October stand-by, one bridge among several. The political reading starts from the fact that the shipments had to be flown in secrecy, that the May sale by the State Bank of India was arranged under a caretaker government, and that for thirty years afterwards the phrase for the episode in political speech was not financing but pawning: the family silver carried to London. A financing operation does not enter a nation's rhetoric as a wound. The gold episode entered rhetoric because every participant understood that the state's honour was the collateral, and it is the political reading alone that can explain why a repaid loan still argues in elections.

The technical reading has a further difficulty it rarely faces: it must explain the variance among governments facing the same arithmetic. The Chandra Shekhar government of November 1990 to March 1991 read the same reserve figures, drew the same Fund facilities and deferred the structural decisions; the Rao government, weaker in seats and led by a man who had not contested the election, took the decisions within five weeks. If arithmetic selected policy, the sequence would run the other way, the stronger caretaker mandate of a fresh Fund drawing funding delay and the minority government buying time. The record runs opposite. What differed between the two governments was not information but political construction: one was a caretaker arrangement on Congress sufferance whose licence ended at stabilisation, the other a party government that had decided, at its summit, to convert the crisis into a founding act. The political reading treats that difference as the explanation; the rival readings must treat it as noise.

## The Settlement 1991 Inherited: Planning as a Political Compact

The order that Rao and Singh renegotiated in 1991 had itself been built as politics. The planned economy was the Nehruvian settlement's economic wing: the Planning Commission created by cabinet resolution in March 1950, the Industrial Policy Resolution of 30 April 1956 reserving seventeen industries in its Schedule A to the state, the Avadi resolution of January 1955 committing Congress to a socialistic pattern of society, and the Mahalanobis strategy of the Second Five-Year Plan directing investment toward heavy industry. Read through [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/), these instruments formed a compact among the Congress organisation, the industrial houses that accepted a protected home market, the bureaucracy that administered licences, and the electorate that received the state's promise of development in exchange for patience. The licence was the compact's everyday token. To build a factory, expand a line or import a machine, an entrepreneur required the state's permission, and permission created relationships that outlasted any single file.

Rajni Kothari's celebrated account of [the Congress system](/2019/11/18/kothari-congress-system-post-independence/) described a party of consensus that absorbed its oppositions by internalising their programmes in diluted form; the licence system was that system's economic plumbing. Because the state decided who could build, borrow abroad and import machinery, every interest in the economy had business at the Congress high command's door, and the party's factions competed to be the door's keeper. Planning's allocations gave the centre its leverage over state governments of its own party, and the public sector's postings gave legislators their patronage. This is why the settlement's renegotiation in 1991 could not be a mere policy change: it cut the plumbing of the Congress system itself, and the wonder of July 1991 is not that Congress backbenchers revolted but that their party's leadership persuaded them to saw through the branch the system sat on, by persuading them the tree was falling anyway.

The political function of planning explains its longevity better than its economic results explain anything. Growth through the nineteen fifties, sixties and seventies averaged close to the rate critics later mocked, and shortages were chronic; yet the compact held because it distributed recognition. State governments bargained for steel plants and dams as trophies. Industrial families invested in Delhi liaison offices because access was a factor of production. Congress legislators treated public sector postings and licences as the patronage that bound districts to the party. An arrangement that inefficient in output and that efficient in loyalty does not persist by accident. It persists because it is a settlement, a standing answer to the question of who may decide, who must ask, and who gets seen to give.

The compact also carried a warning that every politician of Rao's generation had memorised. In June 1966, under pressure from the World Bank and the United States and facing a foreign exchange shortage after two wars and a drought, Indira Gandhi's government devalued the rupee by 36.5 per cent. The promised aid did not flow at the expected scale, imports of food under PL-480 became a national humiliation in the telling of the opposition, and Congress lost ground heavily in the general election of February 1967, surrendering power in eight states. Devaluation entered Congress folklore as the reform that punished its authors. For twenty-five years after 1966, Indian governments liberalised in whispers (the delicensing of twenty-five industry groups in 1985, the tax cuts of Vishwanath Pratap Singh's 1985 budget, the computer and electronics openings under Rajiv Gandhi) and retreated whenever a measure acquired a public name. The lesson of 1966 was not that reform failed economically. It was that reform, visibly owned, failed politically.

### Why did the 1966 devaluation fail politically where July 1991 held?

The 1966 devaluation was read as foreign dictation: aid promised by Washington and the World Bank arrived short, drought and food imports framed it as humiliation, and Congress paid for it in the 1967 election. In 1991 the authors sequenced the measures, clothed them in nationalist language, and spread the pain, so the programme survived its first Parliament.

The settlement's third feature was its personnel. The Indian state of 1991 was staffed at its economic heights by men formed inside the compact: Singh had served as Chief Economic Adviser, Finance Secretary, Reserve Bank Governor from 1982 to 1985 and Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission from 1985 to 1987. Rao had administered Andhra Pradesh as Chief Minister from 1971 to 1973 and held the external affairs, home and defence portfolios in Delhi during the nineteen eighties. The reform of 1991 was not an invasion of the settlement by outsiders. It was a renegotiation conducted by the settlement's own senior partners, using its own institutions (the budget, the industrial policy resolution, the Reserve Bank) as their instruments. That genealogy matters for the adjudication later in this article: whatever else July 1991 was, it was an inside job.

## Nationalisation as Political Grammar: The Indira Precedent

If planning supplied the settlement's architecture, Indira Gandhi supplied its grammar lesson. On 19 July 1969, four days after Morarji Desai was stripped of the finance portfolio, an ordinance nationalised fourteen banks holding the bulk of the country's deposits; the story is told in the account of [bank nationalisation in 1969](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/). The ordinance's official reasoning spoke of aligning credit with plan priorities. Its political work was done in the open: the nationalisation split the Congress, isolated the party's old guard, and let Indira Gandhi present herself as the champion of the poor against the syndicate of bosses. The Garibi Hatao slogan of the 1971 election converted the bank seizure into a mandate, and a sequence followed the same grammar: general insurance nationalised in 1972, coal mines in 1973, the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973 tightening the state's grip on foreign firms. Each measure had an economic rationale available in committee reports. Each was timed, announced and defended as a political act against named enemies.

The precedent matters for 1991 because it establishes the direction of causation the political reading claims. In 1969 the economic instrument did not drag politics behind it; the political requirement selected the instrument, set its date, and wrote its justification. A generation of Congress politicians learned the grammar at first hand, and Rao, who served in Indira Gandhi's cabinets through the nineteen seventies and eighties, was among the pupils. When his own government reached for economic instruments in 1991, it reached with the same understanding, pointed the other way: where Indira Gandhi had used seizure to prove the state could command capital, Rao and Singh used release to prove the state could command the crisis. The instruments were opposites. The grammar (announce decisively, personalise the authorship, convert an economic file into a verdict on the government's character) was identical.

The Janata interlude of 1977 to 1980 supplied the precedent's mirror image and proved the grammar ran in every direction. The Janata government's industrial policy statement of December 1977 re-centred the small-scale and cottage sector as the regime's identity, and Industries Minister George Fernandes enforced the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act's equity dilution demands against the two most visible foreign firms in the country: Coca-Cola and IBM both left India in 1977 rather than dilute their equity and share their formulae and technology on the statute's terms. The expulsions were defended as swadeshi and attacked as vandalism, and both descriptions agree on the framework's point: an equity percentage in a foreign exchange statute had become the terrain on which a government's character was judged, by its supporters as purity and by its critics as folly. When Coca-Cola returned to the Indian market in 1993, under the Rao government's settlement, the return was reported as a verdict on the new order exactly as the departure had been a verdict on the old. Between 1977 and 1993 the same bottle of cola carried two settlements' signatures.

The licence system's political economy completes the prehistory. By the nineteen eighties the permit structure had generated its own constituency of beneficiaries and brokers, and expert opinion inside the state had turned against it in print: the Dagli committee on controls and subsidies reported in 1979, the Narasimham committee on the financial system would report in 1991 itself, and a paper on restructuring industrial policy prepared under Montek Singh Ahluwalia's authorship circulated within the government in 1990. The ideas that July 1991 enacted were therefore neither new nor imported at the moment of crisis. They were a standing internal brief awaiting a political configuration willing to sign it. The crisis of 1990 and 1991 supplied the configuration; it did not write the brief.

## The 1991 Reform as Politics: The Framework Stated

Framework 9, Reform as Politics, reads an economic-policy decision through four components, and the hub's claim is that the four together explain what the technical and trajectory readings leave unexplained. The first component reads each decision for its political function: what a measure does for the authority, cohesion and survival of the government that issues it. The second component runs the causation the other way, from political conditions to economic choices: minority status, factional balance, electoral calendars and creditor deadlines shape which measures are chosen, in what order, at what size. The third component treats economic content as a party's public identity: a settlement tells citizens who the governing party is, as Garibi Hatao told them in 1971 and as the promise of a self-reliant, modernising India told them in 1991. The fourth component examines the mechanics of reform moments themselves: sequencing, timing, the pairing of pain with compensation, the selection of which decisions are dramatised and which are buried in notifications.

The four components are summarised in Table 1, the pillar's citable artifact, with the July 1991 evidence entered against each. Later articles in this pillar cite the table's rows when they read the planned economy, the nationalisations, the sanctions response, the information settlement, the crisis management of 2008, demonetisation, the Goods and Services Tax and the tax and production measures of 2019 and 2020.

Table 1 rewards a slow reading. Component Two explains why the same measures were impossible in 1989 and unavoidable in 1991: the menu had not changed, the configuration had. Component Four explains the fertiliser exception. A government that concedes nothing invites a coalition of the injured; a government that concedes one item, publicly and early, converts the concession into proof that it listens, and the exception was priced accordingly. The framework's discipline is that none of the four components is decorative. Remove the first and 1969 becomes inexplicable; remove the second and the timing of July 1991 becomes arbitrary; remove the third and Singh's peroration becomes ornament rather than instrument; remove the fourth and the survival of a minority government through five budgets becomes luck. The hub claim is that the components travel: they were built on 1991 and they read the rest of the pillar. A rehearsal on the older settlement's founding document shows the portability. The Industrial Policy Resolution of 30 April 1956 performed political function for the Nehru government by converting the Avadi resolution on a socialistic pattern of society, adopted in January 1955, into administrative fact, proving the party's socialist credentials to its left wing in a single instrument. Political conditions shaped its content: the seventeen reserved industries of Schedule A were as many as a Congress dependent on private capital for plan finance could reserve without frightening the investment the Second Plan required, a calibration produced jointly by P. C. Mahalanobis's strategy and the industrial houses' lobbying. Its content furnished identity: the public sector's commanding heights became the visible body of the Congress idea of India, steel plants and dams raised as the temples of the new state. And its mechanics were engineered for survival, with Schedule B's industries left open to progressive state expansion and the private sector expressly invited into the remainder, so that no constituency received a total defeat in 1956. The framework reads 1956 and 1991 as the same species of act signed in opposite directions, which is the hub's wager in miniature.

## The World in the Room: The Cold War's End and the Creditor's Calendar

No reading of 1991 is complete without the external configuration, which Component Two treats as a political condition and not as a command. The Soviet Union, India's largest trading partner within the rupee payment arrangement and its strategic counterweight since the treaty of August 1971, was dissolving through the very months of the reform: the failed Moscow coup of August 1991 fell between Singh's letter of intent and the Fund's board meeting, and the Union itself ceased to exist in December 1991. The rupee trade area's collapse removed an export market and a payments cushion in the same season that the Gulf War raised the oil bill and emptied the Gulf of remitting workers. The United States stood as the sole superpower, the Fund and the World Bank were applying structural adjustment templates from Latin America to Africa, and India negotiated inside a world where the alternative patron of the Nehru decades no longer existed. Foreign policy and economic policy, the subjects of two sister frameworks in this series, fused in 1991 into a single domestic problem: how a government re-prices its external dependencies without surrendering the authorship of its internal order.

The creditor's calendar disciplined the sequence without writing it. The Fund's staff required stabilisation arithmetic (deficit paths, reserve floors, monetary ceilings) as the price of the stand-by approved on 31 October 1991, and the World Bank's structural adjustment credit of December 1991 attached conditions on trade and industrial policy that the July documents had already enacted. The documentary order is the evidence: conditions that ratify decisions taken before the loan is signed are not dictation, they are a creditor purchasing a programme it prefers after the programme exists. India's negotiators understood the distinction and used it; Singh's correspondence with Camdessus through the autumn of 1991 reads as a negotiation between a Fund that needed a success in a large democracy and a government that needed the Fund's seal to unlock bilateral and commercial credit. The Left's sovereignty critique, recorded in the debate section of this article, answered that the seal itself was the surrender, since it bound successors; the government's answer was that the seal bound the creditors too. Both answers are intelligible only inside the political reading, where an international agreement is a domestic instrument fought over by domestic actors for domestic stakes.

## A Standing Brief Awaiting a Signature: Reform Ideas Inside the State, from 1967 to 1991

The conversion reading gains its strongest evidence from a paper trail that predates the crisis by a generation. In 1967 the industrialist R. K. Hazari, commissioned to review the licensing system, reported that licensing had failed to channel investment as the plans intended and had instead entrenched the large houses it was meant to discipline; the Monopolies Inquiry Commission of 1965 had already documented concentration under the permit regime. In 1970 Jagdish Bhagwati and Padma Desai published India: Planning for Industrialization, a systematic indictment of the control system's economic costs, written from inside the Indian policy conversation rather than against it. The state's own committees kept adding pages to the brief: the Dagli committee reported on controls and subsidies in 1979, the Abid Hussain committee on trade policies reported in 1984 recommending a turn toward export promotion and import liberalisation, the Chakravarty committee reviewed the monetary system in 1985, the Narasimham committee on the financial system was appointed in 1991 and reported that November, and the Long Term Fiscal Policy statement of December 1985 conceded the fiscal arithmetic in print. By the time Rao took office, the diagnosis of the control regime existed in official prose at every level of the state.

Governments had even rehearsed the remedies in miniature. The budget of 1985, presented by Finance Minister Vishwanath Pratap Singh in Rajiv Gandhi's government, cut income and corporate tax rates and declared war on black money; the industrial policy measures of 1985 delicensed twenty-five broad industry groups and raised the asset ceiling that defined a monopoly house; broadbanding of licences followed in 1986, letting firms vary product mixes within broad categories without fresh permission. Each rehearsal stopped at the boundary the configuration of the moment imposed: the Bofors scandal consumed the Rajiv Gandhi government's authority from 1987, the 1989 election replaced it with a coalition whose economic energies ran toward farm loan waivers and Mandal, and the reform brief returned to the files. The men of 1991 had read the files. Ahluwalia's 1990 paper on restructuring industrial policy, prepared inside the government during the National Front months, supplied the immediate draft from which the Statement of 24 July 1991 was adapted; its existence, established in the record, is the standing refutation of the claim that the July programme was composed in Washington in the weeks after the gold flights.

This prehistory disciplines both rival readings. Against compulsion, it shows that the content of reform was Indian, dated and fought over for decades before the Fund's 1991 conditions existed; the Fund's stand-by of October 1991 asked for stabilisation and endorsed a direction the Indian state had already chosen, and India's negotiators, Singh among them, wrote the programme documents in Delhi. Against a simple conversion story, it shows that belief alone had been insufficient for twenty years: the brief lost every time its sponsors faced an election, a faction or a drought, in 1966, in the late nineteen seventies and in 1987. What the believers lacked was never argument. It was a configuration: a crisis that repriced delay, a Prime Minister who treated the programme as his government's reason to exist, a finance minister whose person certified the programme to its creditors, and an opposition divided among three incompatible objections. July 1991 is the date those four elements first coincided, which is why the framework dates the settlement to a political moment rather than to the publication of any report.

The prehistory also fixes the meaning of the framework's second component for the rest of the pillar. Political conditions shaping economic choices is not a claim about 1991 alone; it is the pattern of the whole record from 1948 onward. The planned economy's design answers to the Congress coalition of the nineteen fifties, the nationalisations to the Congress split of 1969, the tentative openings of 1985 to a young Prime Minister's majority of 404 seats, and the settlement of 1991 to a minority government's need to found itself on an act no rival could easily repeal. Economic policy in India has a history because Indian politics has one, and the two chronologies are the same chronology read at different speeds.

## The Crisis Ledger, from August 1990 to June 1991

The configuration that made July 1991 possible was assembled by disaster, and its figures belong to the primary record. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in August 1990 doubled the price of oil within weeks, raised India's import bill, and cut the remittances of Indian workers in the Gulf. The Economic Survey of 1991 and 1992 records the reserve position with bureaucratic calm: foreign exchange reserves fell from 3.11 billion US dollars at the end of August 1990 to 896 million dollars on 16 January 1991. The central government's fiscal deficit had reached 8.4 per cent of gross domestic product in 1990 and 1991. Wholesale price inflation climbed through the year and touched 16.7 per cent in August 1991. Credit rating agencies downgraded Indian paper, non-resident depositors withdrew funds, and the state that had never defaulted in four decades began to price default as a real possibility.

Two governments burned through the interval. The National Front government of Vishwanath Pratap Singh fell in November 1990, and Chandra Shekhar governed from 10 November 1990 on Congress sufferance, with a party of roughly five dozen members in the Lok Sabha. In January 1991 his government drew about 1.81 billion US dollars from the International Monetary Fund, combining a compensatory financing drawing of about 1.03 billion dollars with a first credit tranche drawing of about 789 million dollars, and deferred the structural decisions the Fund's staff were urging. Congress withdrew its backing in March 1991. Elections followed; Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated by the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam at Sriperumbudur on 21 May 1991, between the first and second rounds of polling. In May 1991, with the country mid-election and no government commanding a majority, the State Bank of India sold twenty tonnes of gold to the Union Bank of Switzerland under an arrangement that allowed repurchase. The gold transactions of 1991 began, that is, under a caretaker dispensation and a dead leader, before Rao's government existed.

The caretaker months deepened the lesson in what an unlicensed government cannot do. Finance Minister Yashwant Sinha presented an interim budget in February 1991 that promised fiscal correction and a new industrial policy in the direction the Fund was urging, and the promises died with the arrangement that sustained him: in March 1991 Congress withdrew its backing after two Haryana police constables were found keeping watch outside Rajiv Gandhi's residence, an espionage row that gave the withdrawal its public reason. From March to June 1991 India was governed by a caretaker administration through an election campaign, with the reserves thin, the rating agencies circling and the structural file unopened. The gold sale of May 1991 belongs to this interregnum, authorised by a government that could finance a bridge and could not sign a settlement. The contrast with July is the sharpest natural experiment the record affords on the framework's second component: same officials, same figures, same Fund staff, and the difference lay entirely in the political form of the government reading them.

### How much foreign exchange did India hold when Rao took office?

The Economic Survey of 1991 and 1992 records reserves falling from 3.11 billion US dollars at the end of August 1990 to 896 million dollars on 16 January 1991. On 21 June 1991 the stock stood near 1.2 billion dollars, and Singh priced it in his budget speech at roughly 2,500 crore rupees, a mere fortnight of imports.

India had stood at the Fund's window once before, and the memory governed the second visit. In November 1981 the Fund approved an Extended Fund Facility of 5 billion Special Drawing Rights for Indira Gandhi's government, then the largest arrangement in the Fund's history; India drew about 3.9 billion, stabilised its accounts on the back of the Bombay High oil discoveries and a good monsoon, stopped drawing in 1984 and treated the early exit as a certificate of competence. Officials of Singh's generation, and Singh himself at the Reserve Bank from 1982, had run that operation and learned its lesson in both directions: the Fund could be used without disgrace, and the disgrace attached only when the borrowing looked like drift. The difference between 1981 and 1991 was not the window but the purpose. The 1981 arrangement financed the old settlement through a difficult decade-opening; the 1991 arrangements financed the settlement's replacement. That is why the second visit, smaller in real terms, carries the larger history: the money was similar, the politics were opposite, and the men signing knew it, which is why the letters of 1991 were drafted with a care for their future readers that the technical reading mistakes for boilerplate.

The ledger's political meaning lies in its audience. Every actor in the drama of July 1991 could read the same numbers: the Congress leadership deciding whether Rao or another claimant should lead, the opposition deciding how hard to press a new government, the Fund deciding its terms, industrial houses deciding whether to hoard or invest, and Singh himself, who had watched the reserves as an official through the nineteen eighties. The crisis did not dictate a single response; India could have chosen import compression, default brinkmanship or a slower adjustment, and each option had advocates inside the state. What the crisis dictated was the price of delay. Component Two of the framework names this precisely: conditions do not author decisions, they discipline the menu from which authors choose, and they punish hesitation in a currency (reserves measured in days) that every participant could count.

## A Prime Minister Without a Seat: 21 June 1991 and the Choice of Manmohan Singh

The general election of May and June 1991 returned Congress as the largest party with 232 seats of the 521 then being contested, ahead of the Bharatiya Janata Party at 120 and the Janata Dal at 59, and returned no majority. The Congress Working Committee had elected Rao party president on 29 May 1991, after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, as a consensus figure acceptable to the factions that a contested succession might have split. Rao had announced his retirement from electoral politics before the assassination, had not contested the election, and took the oath as Prime Minister on 21 June 1991 as a member of neither House, constitutionally required to enter Parliament within six months. He entered the Lok Sabha through the Nandyal by-election in November 1991. The government he formed was, on paper, among the weakest of the republic's history: a minority administration, led by a man without a seat, governing a party in mourning and in factional suspense, in the middle of a payments crisis.

The mandate question deserves precision, because the adjudication turns on it. The Congress manifesto of 1991 promised price stability, fiscal discipline and a modernising continuation of Rajiv Gandhi's economic direction in broad terms; it did not present devaluation, delicensing, majority foreign equity or subsidy withdrawal as proposals on which voters were asked to rule. No campaign speech of that spring priced a 51 per cent foreign equity route or an end to the licence system. The reform of July 1991 was therefore not the execution of an electoral instruction. It was a governing decision taken after the election by men who then had to manufacture its legitimacy retrospectively, in Parliament, in the party and in print. Recognising this is not a criticism of Rao or Singh. It is the framework's central observation: the politics of 1991 was not the backdrop to the reform; manufacturing consent for an unmandated programme, under deadline, was the reform's first and hardest component.

The choice of Singh was the first manufactured signal. Singh was sworn in as Finance Minister with the rest of the cabinet on 21 June 1991. His curriculum vitae was the message: Chief Economic Adviser in the nineteen seventies, Finance Secretary, Governor of the Reserve Bank from 1982 to 1985, Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission from 1985 to 1987, Secretary-General of the South Commission in Geneva, and briefly adviser on economic affairs to Prime Minister Chandra Shekhar. To creditors and to the Fund, the appointment announced that the finance ministry would be run by a man whose signature they already trusted. To the Congress organisation, it announced something subtler: the economic programme would be fronted by a non-politician, a Sardar with a Cambridge doctorate and no faction, so that its costs could be attributed to expertise and its authorship shared upward to Rao alone. Sitapati's biography, examined later in this article, documents Rao's deliberateness in the selection. Component One of the framework reads the appointment as the reform's first political function performed before a single measure existed: the finance minister was himself the opening measure.

Rao's own positioning completed the authorship structure. He held the industries portfolio in his own charge, so the Statement on Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 issued from the Prime Minister's hand rather than from a ministry that could disown it. He kept public silence as his instrument, letting Singh absorb the argument of July and August, and intervened at chosen moments: defending Singh inside the party, calibrating the single retreat on fertiliser, and owning the programme in his Independence Day address of 15 August 1991 from the Red Fort, where he presented the new economic direction as national necessity rather than partisan choice. The division of labour (the expert speaks economics, the Prime Minister speaks survival, the party is managed in private) was the minority government's substitute for a majority.

## Thirty-Three Days in July: The Sequence as Method

The measures came in an order that repayments of attention still reward. On 1 July and again on 3 July 1991 the Reserve Bank under Governor S. Venkitaramanan devalued the rupee in two steps totalling roughly 18 per cent against the major currencies; Sanjaya Baru's account in The Accidental Prime Minister (2014) records that Singh and Deputy Governor C. Rangarajan nicknamed the two-step adjustment a hop, skip and jump. On 4 July 1991 Commerce Minister P. Chidambaram announced a trade policy that replaced replenishment licences with eximscrips, tied import entitlements to export earnings, and stripped away layers of import licensing for capital and intermediate goods. Within the same weeks the Reserve Bank airlifted 47 tonnes of gold to London, pledged with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan; together with the May sale to the Union Bank of Switzerland, the gold operations raised on the order of 600 million US dollars and bought the government its summer.

The set piece arrived on 24 July 1991. Singh's budget speech opened with the ledger: reserves, in his words, in the range of 2,500 crore rupees, sufficient to finance imports for a mere fortnight, a situation without parallel in independent India's history. The speech set a fiscal target of reducing the deficit to 6.5 per cent of gross domestic product within the year, abolished the cash compensatory support for exports, raised fertiliser prices by about 30 per cent on average, and promised a new industrial and trade architecture. The Statement on Industrial Policy, tabled the same day, confined licensing to eighteen industries on security, strategic and environmental grounds, cut the industries reserved for the public sector from seventeen to eight, removed the asset threshold that had subjected large houses to the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices regime, opened automatic approval for foreign equity up to 51 per cent in high-priority industries, and announced disinvestment of government equity in public enterprises. Singh closed the speech with the passage that attached the programme to national resurgence. Borrowing Victor Hugo's sentence that no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come, he named the emergence of India as a major economic power as one such idea, told the world to hear it loud and clear, declared India awake, and promised that his countrymen would prevail and overcome. The speech circulates in archives and in teaching decks alike, and a reader opening a presentation copy of it can do so through a [browser-based slide viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html).

Read the sequence as Component Four prescribes. The exchange rate moved first, before Parliament sat on the programme, and its pain could be billed to the crisis and to the previous caretaker months. Trade followed within days, industrial policy rode on the budget's authority, and the structural statutes (the measures that dismantled the settlement's architecture rather than merely pricing its failures) were announced when opposition benches had no agreed alternative text. The government's own later chronology confirms the deliberateness: the formal letter to the Fund requesting a stand-by arrangement was written by Singh to Managing Director Michel Camdessus on 27 August 1991, after the July measures were facts, and the Fund's board approved a twenty-month stand-by of 1,656 million Special Drawing Rights, about 2.2 billion US dollars, on 31 October 1991. A 500 million US dollar structural adjustment loan and credit from the World Bank followed in December 1991. The sequence matters to the adjudication: the decisive domestic measures preceded the binding international commitments, which is not the chronology of a programme dictated from outside. The July documents also carried their compensation machinery in the same covers, a detail the technical reading treats as welfare garnish and the framework treats as load-bearing. The budget promised a safety net for labour displaced by restructuring, and the National Renewal Fund was constituted in February 1992 to finance retraining and voluntary retirement in sick and surplus-staffed units; the exit policy that would have let firms close was in the same years diluted into the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction's slow procedures. Pain in this programme was never announced without its paired analgesic, and the pairing was not kindness but method: each constituency asked to bear a loss was shown its compensation in the same document that imposed the loss, so that opposition had to argue against the package whole. Component Four again, and the fertiliser rollback of August 1991 was its emergency application when the pairing failed at the farm gate.

## The Officials: Personnel as the Settlement's Transmission Belt

Component Two names the mechanism abstractly; the personnel of 1991 give it faces. Around the two principals stood a small cadre whose careers had been spent inside the old settlement's economic ministries and whose convictions had turned: A. N. Verma, Principal Secretary to the Prime Minister, who ran the coordination from Rao's office; Montek Singh Ahluwalia, Commerce Secretary, whose 1990 paper supplied the industrial policy's draft and who would serve every subsequent formation up to the deputy chairmanship of the Planning Commission under Singh's premiership; S. Venkitaramanan at the Reserve Bank, who signed the devaluation adjustments of 1 and 3 July; his deputy C. Rangarajan, who succeeded him as Governor in December 1992 and carried the exchange and monetary reforms through the decade; Rakesh Mohan in the industry ministry's economic division, a hand in the industrial policy's drafting; and Jairam Ramesh across the planning and finance apparatus, chronicler and participant at once. The cast list matters because it is the same cast that had administered the control regime, in several cases for decades. The settlement of 1991 was not staffed by converts imported for the occasion. It was executed by the old order's own senior clerks, whose authority inside the machine let the programme move at the speed it did.

The arrangement between the principals became the stuff of reported anecdote because it summarised the method: Rao, in the recollection of those who served both men, assured Singh that the economics was his province and the politics would be the Prime Minister's. The anecdote's precise wording varies across memoirs, and the framework does not need the wording. The division of labour is documented in the behaviour: Singh never campaigned for the programme in the country, never addressed a public meeting in its defence, and confined his advocacy to Parliament, the budget and the interview; Rao never explained a single instrument in public and confined his advocacy to the party forums, the Red Fort address of 15 August 1991 and the management of votes. When the programme needed a technocrat's signature it received Singh's; when it needed a politician's protection it received Rao's silence breaking at the chosen moment, as in the Congress Parliamentary Party meeting where the Prime Minister's defence ended the backbench revolt of July 1991. Personnel was policy in the most literal sense available to a minority government: the men were the majority the government did not have.

The transmission belt ran forward as reliably as it had run in. The officials of 1991 became the governing cadre of the following three decades across parties (Ahluwalia advising Chandra Shekhar, serving Rao, planning under Vajpayee's National Development Council assignments and presiding as Deputy Chairman under Singh; Rangarajan chairing the Prime Minister's Economic Advisory Council under Singh; the reform cadres staffing the finance ministry, the Reserve Bank and the regulators through the alternations of 1996, 1998, 2004 and 2014). A technical reading sees continuity of expertise and is not wrong. The political reading sees the settlement reproducing itself through careers: to rise in the Indian economic state after 1991 was to be formed by the settlement, whatever party held office, and the cadre's migration across regimes is the administrative form of the succession test the inheritance section applies to governments.

## August to December 1991: Consolidation Under Fire

The settlement's first winter tested whether July had been a spasm or a founding. Inflation, the index on which households grade governments, peaked at 16.7 per cent in August 1991 before the stabilisation bit, and every opposition speech of the winter session quoted it. The government answered with the sequence's second instalment rather than with retreat: the budget of 29 February 1992, Singh's second, cut the peak customs duty from the 150 per cent ceiling set in 1991 to 110 per cent, widened delicensing, and held the fiscal course while the stand-by's reviews proceeded; the budget of 1993 would take the peak rate to 85 per cent. Reserves rebuilt through the winter on the Fund drawing, the World Bank credit, non-resident deposits courted with new instruments and the first trickle of the foreign investment the July statement had invited, and the pledged gold was repurchased within months of the worst passing, closing the pawnshop chapter of the crisis before it could harden into permanent metaphor. The government's parliamentary position, absurd on paper, proved workable in practice because the three critiques cancelled, as the debate had already shown; the winter session ended with the settlement intact and its authors still in office.

The planning machinery itself was re-founded in the same season, the surest sign that the change was constitutional in the small-c sense. The approach to the Eighth Five-Year Plan, framed through 1991 and approved with the Plan by the National Development Council in May 1992, re-described the Planning Commission's task as indicative: the state would signal, facilitate and invest in infrastructure and human development while the market allocated the rest. The institution Nehru had built to command the economy was retained, renamed in function and set to opposite work by the same cabinet method that had built it, a resolution rather than a statute. Read through the framework, the Eighth Plan's approach paper is the settlement's second founding document, less quoted than the budget speech because it was designed to be: Component Four reserves the theatre for the moments that need it and lets the deep changes travel in committee prose.

## Who Was Not in the Room: Party, Parliament and the Organised Critics

The programme of July 1991 was written by a small circle (Rao, Singh, and a handful of officials including Principal Secretary A. N. Verma and Commerce Secretary Montek Singh Ahluwalia), and its exclusions were structural rather than accidental. The Congress Parliamentary Party had not debated delicensing before it was announced. State governments, whose consent the old system had courted through plan bargaining, learned of the industrial policy from the same day's newspapers. Organised labour, whose federations Congress itself had built, received no seat at the drafting table. The exclusions bought speed, and the bill for them arrived within weeks. At a meeting of Congress members after the budget, as Vinod Jose's reporting in Caravan recorded from participants, as many as 63 backbenchers spoke against Singh and the programme, with fertiliser prices at the centre of the anger; Rao defended his finance minister in the room and the revolt was contained. In August 1991 the government partly withdrew the fertiliser price increase. The single retreat taught every observer the location of the electoral nerve: measures touching the village would be priced by the party's own members before the opposition needed to move.

The organised critics supplied the three languages in which 1991 would be contested for a generation. The Left parties, holding 49 seats between the Communist Party of India (Marxist) and its allies, argued that the government had pawned sovereignty: the gold shipments were their exhibit, and the letter of intent of 27 August 1991, with the stand-by approved on 31 October, gave the argument a dated document to cite. Their alternative (import compression, default brinkmanship rejected, mobilisation of non-resident deposits) was stated in Parliament and in print; it was not a refusal to count the reserves, it was a rival reading of what the reserves obliged. The nationalist right spoke a different language. The Swadeshi Jagran Manch was founded at Nagpur on 22 November 1991 under Dattopant Thengadi, drawing the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad and sister organisations into a platform against economic imperialism, and it opened its first national campaign on 12 January 1992. The Bharatiya Janata Party, divided between its trading constituency's swadeshi instincts and its economists' approval of deregulation, attacked the government for surrendering to the Fund and praised the burial of the licence system in adjacent speeches.

Capital itself asked for politics rather than merely for markets. In 1993 a group of established industrialists, with Rahul Bajaj of Bajaj Auto as their most visible spokesman and labelled the Bombay Club by the press, pressed Singh for a level playing field: protection in transition, time to adjust, a hearing for the houses that the licence system had raised and the open market threatened. The Bombay Club's memorandum is Component Three evidence of the purest kind. The incumbents of the old settlement did not dispute that a settlement existed; they petitioned for their place inside its successor. Between the Left's sovereignty, the Manch's swadeshi and the Bombay Club's level field, the contest of 1991 was never between politics and economics. It was among three political economies, each with named holders, dated documents and seats in Parliament or on the street.

The excluded constituencies answered on their own calendars. Trade union federations, including the Congress-affiliated Indian National Trade Union Congress in an agony of divided loyalty and the Left-led Centre of Indian Trade Unions without one, mounted all-India strikes against the new economic policies in 1992 and repeatedly thereafter, protesting the exit policy for sick industries and the threat to public sector employment. The strikes did not stop a single clause of the July programme, and their failure is itself framework evidence: labour's organisations could impose costs in the organised enclaves of the public sector and the mills, but they could not assemble a parliamentary majority for reversal, and the government had chosen its ground, in Component Four's calculation, precisely where the defenders of the old order were fewest relative to the beneficiaries of the new. The one arena where the injured were numerous and electorally concentrated, the farm gate and its fertiliser bag, had already received the August concession.

### What did the dismantling of licensing do to the market for political access in Delhi?

Under licensing, a file in a ministry could decide whether a factory existed, so access to Delhi was a factor of production and liaison offices were industrial assets. Abolition devalued that access overnight and shifted competition toward markets, credit and land, arenas where discretion survived in new forms rather than disappearing from economic life.

The exclusions also shaped what the programme could not do. Labour law reform, the one structural item that would have required open combat with organised workers and their parliamentary patrons, was absent from the July documents and from every subsequent budget of the Rao government; the exit policy for sick industries was promised, diluted and finally deferred. The framework reads these silences as data. A minority government rationing its political capital spent it where returns were quick and opposition was disorganised (licences, equity caps, trade lists) and declined the fights where opponents were concentrated and its own party was divided. The shape of the 1991 reform, celebrated for its sweep, is equally a map of the fights its authors calculated they could not win.

## The Dunkel Winter: Trade Diplomacy Comes Home, from 1991 to 1994

The settlement's hardest early fight arrived through the trade door rather than the budget door, and it confirmed the framework's claim that external commitments are domestic instruments. In December 1991 Arthur Dunkel, Director-General of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, tabled the draft final act of the Uruguay Round, carrying texts on agricultural subsidies, patents and services that reached into Indian fields and pharmacies. The draft's Indian opponents read it as the Fund programme's second volume: seeds patented by foreign firms, drug prices surrendered to product patents, farm subsidies capped by Geneva arithmetic. The Karnataka Rajya Raitha Sangha under M. D. Nanjundaswamy built a campaign against the draft that treated seed saving as sovereignty, and the Bharatiya Kisan Union's Mahendra Singh Tikait mobilised the western Uttar Pradesh countryside in the same years on the same fear in a different idiom. Parliament debated the Dunkel texts through 1992 and 1993 with the opposition parties, so divided on the budget, briefly united in suspicion of the trade round.

The government's handling repeated the July method at a slower tempo. It negotiated amendments, publicised the flexibilities India had won, signed the Marrakesh agreement in April 1994 and carried India into the World Trade Organization at its founding on 1 January 1995, while the predicted catastrophes in seeds and medicines were deferred by transition periods and later fought again over the Patents (Amendment) Act of 2005. The episode matters to the hub because it shows the settlement's operating system running on a file the July documents never mentioned: a global negotiation converted, by farmers' organisations and opposition parties, into the decade's loudest domestic argument, and answered by the government with the same sequence of negotiation, dramatised concession and fait accompli. Economic policy as politics was not a reading imposed on 1991 from outside. By the Dunkel winter it was the idiom in which every participant, governmental and insurgent alike, had learned to speak.

## The Debate in Parliament, July and August 1991

The programme's first public trial was the budget debate, and its transcript is a mandatory source for the framework because it shows the three languages of contest competing on the record. The general discussion on the budget in the Lok Sabha in the last days of July and the first days of August 1991 drew the Left's sovereignty argument in its parliamentary form: Communist members, with Somnath Chatterjee among the CPI(M)'s leading voices in the House, charged that the government had accepted the Fund's dictatorship and carried the nation's gold to London. Bharatiya Janata Party speakers, Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Jaswant Singh among them, split their fire, condemning the surrender to foreign pressure and the fertiliser and subsidy provisions at home while declining to defend the licensing system the budget had buried. Janata Dal members attacked the programme as a betrayal of the poor by a party that had governed in the name of Garibi Hatao. Congress backbenchers, fresh from their internal revolt, largely held their peace in public and extracted their price in the August rollback of the fertiliser increase.

Singh's replies in the House across 1991, the second mandatory primary source for this article, repay attention as statecraft. He refused the surrender frame without conceding the opposition's arithmetic: the reserves figures he had himself placed before the House set the boundary of the possible, and inside that boundary he claimed the programme as the government's own authorship, drafted in Delhi, exceeding in places what any creditor had asked. He coupled the defence with a distributive promise, that adjustment would be carried with a human face and that the poor would be shielded as revenues recovered, a formulation that entered the vocabulary of every successor government including the United Front's common minimum programme of 1996. The demands for grants were voted, the Finance Bill completed its passage in September 1991, and in October 1991 Singh entered the Rajya Sabha from Assam, regularising a finance minister who had presented the republic's most consequential budget as a member of neither House. The settlement, by the winter of 1991, had its documents, its votes and its minister in constitutional order.

The debate also fixed the opposition's structural problem, which the inheritance section will trace. The three critiques could not be summed: a House that accepted the Left's sovereignty argument had to reject the deregulation the BJP's business constituency wanted; a House that accepted the Bombay Club's plea for shelter had to reject the Left's demand to expel foreign capital altogether. Rao's minority survived the decade's turn because its opponents' alternatives cancelled one another, leaving the government's programme the only text on the table. Parliamentary management of that kind is not arithmetic luck. It is Component Four practised on the floor of the House, and the budget debate of 1991 is its textbook.

## Compulsion, Stealth, Conversion: Three Readings Adjudicated

Three scholarly readings compete to explain July 1991, and the hub's duty is to adjudicate among them on the documents rather than to anthologise them. The compulsion reading, carried by Joshi and Little's India's Economic Reforms 1991-2001 (1996) and by much official memory, holds that the crisis authored the reform: with reserves at a fortnight of imports, any government would have devalued, borrowed and deregulated, and the personalities were interchangeable. The stealth reading, named by Rob Jenkins in Democratic Politics and Economic Reform in India (1999), holds that the method is the story: Indian democracy punishes visible reform, so reformers learned to move by stealth, depoliticising measures, diffusing blame across committees and levels of government, and presenting structural turns as technical adjustments. Ashutosh Varshney's companion argument in Mass Politics or Elite Politics (1999) adds the insulation mechanism: reform touched elite consumption and production first and mass entitlements late or never, so the mass electorate whose verdict Jenkins feared was never directly billed. The conversion reading, carried by Bhagwati and Panagariya (2012) on the side of ideas and by Vinay Sitapati's Half Lion (2016) on the side of authorship, holds that the reform happened because identifiable men had come to believe identifiable propositions: the policy elite's conversion to market instruments across the nineteen eighties, and Rao's deliberate decision to spend his premiership on the programme, shielding Singh, timing measures and treating reform as his government's defining project.

Each reading seizes a real component and mistakes it for the whole. Compulsion explains the deadline and cannot explain the content: the Fund's programmes in other countries did not typically include the abolition of an industrial licensing system or a 51 per cent automatic equity route within five weeks of a government's formation, and the chronology already established (devaluation on 1 and 3 July, trade policy on 4 July, industrial policy on 24 July, the letter of intent written on 27 August) shows the domestic measures outpacing the international commitments they were supposedly dictated by. The ideas for those measures had circulated inside the Indian state since the reports of the nineteen seventies and the Ahluwalia paper of 1990; the Fund's arrival changed their price, not their authorship. Stealth explains the presentation and cannot explain the theatre: a government practising concealment does not close its budget speech with Victor Hugo and a declaration that India is wide awake, does not table the industrial revolution on budget day, and does not let its Prime Minister own the programme from the Red Fort on 15 August. Varshney's insulation point survives better: the sequencing protected mass entitlements (food subsidy survived, fertiliser was partly restored, labour law untouched) precisely because the authors knew which bills the electorate would collect. Conversion explains the authorship and cannot explain the arithmetic: Singh's beliefs were formed by 1985, Rao's caution was a lifetime's habit, and neither man's convictions moved a single vote in a Lok Sabha of 232 Congress members; the programme passed its tests because abstentions were arranged, opponents were divided among three incompatible critiques, and the one organised internal revolt, over fertiliser, was bought off with a partial rollback in August.

The adjudication the framework delivers is compositional. Compulsion names the Component Two condition: the crisis disciplined the menu and the calendar. Stealth, corrected by the Hugo peroration and the Red Fort ownership, names half of Component Four: the mechanics were theatrical where theatre was cheap and quiet where quiet was necessary, notifications carrying what speeches could not. Conversion names Component One: the programme performed the political function of giving a leaderless-looking government a defining act, and its authors knew it. The three readings are therefore not rivals to be ranked but partial sightings of the single event the framework describes whole. The strongest evidence for the composite reading remains the event's strangest feature, which no single-cause account touches: a government that should have fallen instead founded a settlement. Settlements are founded by political work, and the documents of 1991 are saturated with it. Readers who want the government's own day-to-day record of that work will find it in [the post-1991 institutional-political context](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/); the present article's business is the frame that record sits inside.

### Why did the same July 1991 measures look like surrender to one camp and liberation to another?

The Left read the Fund correspondence and the gold shipments and saw sovereignty pawned; liberalisers read the licensing clauses and saw the state retreat from daily command over production. Both camps fastened on real clauses of the same documents, which is why the meaning of 1991 stayed contested long after its measures became routine.

The insider chronicle supports the composite reading against its rivals. Jairam Ramesh, who served in the planning and finance apparatus through the period, reconstructed the crisis year in To the Brink and Back: India's 1991 Story (2015) from the files: the January drawings under Chandra Shekhar, the gold operations, the drafting of the programme inside the Indian government, the management of the Fund as a counterpart to be negotiated with rather than obeyed. Ramesh's narrative is a participant's and carries a participant's loyalties, and the framework discounts it accordingly; what survives the discount is the documentary sequence, dates and instruments that compulsion cannot reorder. A dictated programme does not precede its dictator's signature by two months. A stealth programme does not announce itself from the Red Fort. A mere conversion of elites does not explain why the converted waited for a minority government and a payments crisis to act on beliefs documented since 1970. The composite reading is the only one of the four that assigns every document of 1991 to its place without remainder.

One further witness deserves a hearing before the inheritance is traced: the Prime Minister's own retrospective framing. Rao, in his Foreword and interviews around the novel he published as The Insider (1998), and in the recollections Sitapati assembled, presented the reform as compelled by circumstance and executed by consensus-seeking; Singh, in his Lok Sabha interventions of 1991 and after, presented it as India's overdue entry into a world economy it could no longer afford to refuse. The two framings differ in the way the framework predicts: the politician stresses necessity and consensus (Component Two, the conditions), the economist stresses opportunity and design (Component One, the function). Neither principal described the event as a technical adjustment. The principals, on the record, read their own reform as politics.

## The Reform's Vocabulary: Words Chosen, Words Avoided

The settlement rewired the language of Indian politics, and the vocabulary is evidence a word counter can audit. The Rao government almost never said privatisation: the budgets and statements of 1991 to 1996 spoke of disinvestment, a word that described a sale of shares while declining to announce a transfer of the state's commanding role, and the choice was deliberate, remembered by the officials who drafted the documents as the price of carrying the party. Liberalisation and globalisation entered the decade as the programme's twin banners in English and as twin abuses in the bazaar and the union hall, where globalisation named the feared arrival of the foreign firm and liberalisation named the retreat of the protective state. The Swadeshi Jagran Manch countered with swadeshi, a word with a freedom movement pedigree that the Manch's founders, Thengadi above all, wielded as a claim that the settlement of 1991 was a second colonisation. Each side of the argument reached instinctively for vocabulary as its first weapon, because each understood the framework's third component from the inside: a settlement stands or falls by the words in which citizens are asked to describe their own lives.

The successor vocabulary records the settlement digesting its critics. Reform with a human face, Singh's debate phrase of 1991, became the United Front's treaty language in 1996 and the standing formula by which coalition governments promised the settlement and its softening in one breath. Second generation reforms, the phrase of the Vajpayee years, conceded that the first generation was a completed founding whose authors were no longer in office. India Shining, the slogan of the government's 2004 campaign, attempted to convert the settlement's growth into ruling party property and was answered by the Congress slogan promising the common man his share, after which inclusive growth became the Eleventh Plan's title language under Singh's own premiership. A technical adjustment does not generate a thirty-year succession of slogans competing to rename it. A political settlement does, because the slogans are the settlement's continuing negotiation with the electorate, conducted in the only currency elections accept.

## The Human Face Ledger: Distribution and the Reform's Social Account

The settlement's legitimacy rested on a promise its authors repeated from the budget speech onward: that adjustment would be carried with a human face, and that growth would finance the protection the budget had trimmed. The social account of the nineteen nineties is where the rival readings fight their longest war, and the hub must enter the figures with the holders named. Growth in gross domestic product, which had averaged roughly 3.5 per cent a year across the nineteen seventies (the rate Raj Krishna christened the Hindu rate of growth), rose to an average near 6 per cent in the years from 1992 to 1997, and exports and software services expanded in ways the licence era had not produced. Bhagwati and Panagariya read this record as vindication and press the argument further in India's Tryst with Destiny (2012): growth is the first instrument of poverty reduction, and the reforms' defect was timidity, a second track of health, education and redistribution left unbuilt beside the first track of growth. Their distinction between Track I and Track II reforms is itself a Component Three artefact, an identity claim about what the post-1991 state should be seen doing.

The counter-account holds the same decade up to a different light. Amartya Sen and Jean Drèze, in India: Economic Development and Social Opportunity (1995) and in their later work, argued that India's failure was not reform but the neglect of public action in schooling, health and nutrition, fields where market opening could not substitute for the state and where the 1991 settlement, in their reading, changed too little. Poverty measurement itself became a political battleground: the official headcount ratios showed poverty falling through the nineteen nineties, from roughly 36 per cent in 1993 and 1994 by the Planning Commission's Lakdawala method, while critics disputed the survey design of the National Sample Survey's fifty-fifth round in 1999 and 2000 and warned that employment growth lagged output growth. The framework does not need to settle the statisticians' quarrel to use it. What the quarrel shows is Component Two in social form: the settlement's terms set the terrain on which even its welfare critics had to fight, disputing the measurement of a programme whose existence none of the disputants proposed to end.

The electorate sent its own ledger entry in 2004. The Vajpayee government, having administered the settlement competently and campaigned on the India Shining slogan of early 2004, lost the general election of April and May 2004 to a Congress-led alliance whose slogan promised the common man a share in the growth story. Economists and psephologists disputed then and later how far the verdict was a bill for the settlement's social account and how far state-level verdicts and alliance arithmetic explained it; the dispute itself is recorded here without a verdict. What is not disputed is the consequence: the United Progressive Alliance governments of 2004 to 2014 kept the 1991 architecture and layered the second track upon it by statute, enacting the National Rural Employment Guarantee Act in 2005 and the Right to Education Act in 2009, an attempt to complete the settlement's social half from inside its economic frame. The human face had begun as a finance minister's reassuring phrase in the debate of 1991. By 2005 it was the governing coalition's legislative programme, which is what phrases become when a framework's third component does its work across a generation.

## The Inheritance: Every Successor Governed Inside the 1991 Settlement

The test of a settlement is succession. On 28 July 1993 the Rao government survived a no-confidence motion by 265 votes to 251, fourteen months of scandal and secession threats notwithstanding, and served its full term to May 1996, the first minority government in the republic's history to do so. The opposition that had predicted collapse instead began to plan its own tenancy of the settlement. The general election of 1996 removed Congress from office; the United Front governments of H. D. Deve Gowda and I. K. Gujral, dependent on Congress backing and staffed by parties that had denounced the Fund programme, kept the programme. Finance Minister P. Chidambaram's budget of 28 February 1997 cut personal and corporate tax rates, widened the tax net and deepened the trade opening, and was celebrated by the same business press that had lionised Singh. A coalition whose common minimum programme promised reform with a human face delivered reform with continuity; the human face was the rhetoric, the continuity was the settlement.

The Bharatiya Janata Party's tenancy made the point across the aisle. The governments of Atal Bihari Vajpayee from 1998 to 2004 created a disinvestment ministry in 1999 under Arun Shourie, sold a controlling stake in Bharat Aluminium in 2001, opened insurance to foreign equity through the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act of 1999, and passed the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act in 2003. The party whose economic affiliate, the Swadeshi Jagran Manch, had been founded to resist the programme administered its extension, confining its swadeshi inheritance to rhetoric and to chosen battlegrounds such as foreign investment in retail. In 2004 the electorate returned Congress, and the author of the budget of 24 July 1991 became Prime Minister for a decade. A settlement whose finance minister can return as head of government, whose opponents extend it while denouncing its parentage, and whose repeal no manifesto has ever proposed, has passed the only test political settlements recognise: the succession test.

The settlement's disciplines bound even the majority government of 2014, which is the inheritance's sternest evidence because a single-party majority is the configuration the framework would expect to escape them. The government elected in May 2014 with the first Lok Sabha majority in thirty years carried the settlement's programme forward in taxation, foreign investment and monetary framework, yet its attempt in 2015 to amend the land acquisition law of 2013 by ordinance met the same calculation Rao had made in 1991: land, like fertiliser and labour before it, sat on the electorate's nerve, and the ordinance was allowed to lapse in August 2015 after the Prime Minister announced the retreat himself. A majority of 282 seats did not repeal the lesson of the minority of 232. The configuration had changed beyond recognition; the menu's discipline, Component Two's whole claim, operated unchanged, which is what a settlement means when it is fully inherited: it no longer needs its authors' weakness to explain its rules.

The later moments of this pillar supply the framework's forward evidence, and each receives its own article. The Goods and Services Tax that took effect on 1 July 2017, examined as [the cooperative federalism test of 2017](/2019/07/25/gst-2017-cooperative-federalism-test/), was negotiated through a council in which the Union and the states voted together, Component Two machinery built into a constitutional amendment. The demonetisation of 8 November 2016, examined in [the politics of the November 2016 announcement](/2019/07/20/demonetisation-november-2016-politics/), was Component One in pure form: an economic instrument whose first function was a verdict on the government's character. The post-2014 renegotiation of the founding tensions, mapped in [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), ran through these economic acts, and the administrative centralisation charted in [the post-2014 PMO architecture context](/2019/08/11/pmo-centralisation-question-india/) decided who authored them. Even the decade's institutional contests obeyed the pattern: the Election Commission under T. N. Seshan, whose tenure is read in [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/), showed in parallel that the nineteen nineties were a decade in which every Indian institution, economic or electoral, was being re-priced in public.

Why did no successor reverse the settlement? The framework's answer assembles the components. Reversal would have required a successor to own the consequences of re-licensing an economy whose firms, consumers and state governments had reorganised around openness (Component Two, the conditions had shifted). The beneficiaries of 1991, exporters, consumers of formerly scarce goods, state governments competing for investment, were dispersed and unorganised, exactly as Varshney's insulation argument predicts, and no election from 1996 to 2019 was fought on a promise to restore the licence system. The costs of reform had been front-loaded onto its first authors; successors inherited the asset without the founding bill. Component Three completes the explanation: by the late nineteen nineties every major party's public identity required a dialect of the 1991 language (growth, investment, modernity) even when the same party's speechwriters reached for swadeshi or for a human face. The settlement had become the vocabulary in which alternatives had to be phrased.

## The Second Generation: States, Statutes and the Widening Settlement, from 1993 to 2004

A settlement proves itself by metabolising its own crises, and the first arrived within a year. The securities scandal that broke in April 1992, when the scale of Harshad Mehta's operations in the government securities market became public, handed the opposition a narrative in which liberalisation and fraud were synonyms; a Joint Parliamentary Committee under Ram Niwas Mirdha examined the scam through 1992 and 1993. The government's response is the framework's evidence: it did not reimpose the controls the scandal seemed to indict, it built the regulator the new market required, giving the Securities and Exchange Board of India statutory authority by the Act of 1992 and pressing on with the National Stock Exchange, incorporated in November 1992 as a screen-based rival to the Bombay Stock Exchange's floor. Crisis, in the settlement's grammar, was converted into institution-building by the same method as 1991: the political decision to answer a market failure with a market institution rather than with a restored licence.

The states became the settlement's second authors. The Eighth Five-Year Plan, approved by the National Development Council in May 1992 for the years 1992 to 1997, restated planning as indicative rather than imperative, and the arena of economic competition shifted downward to the state capitals. West Bengal's Left Front government under Jyoti Basu issued a new industrial policy statement in 1994 that welcomed private and foreign capital into sectors the Left's national rhetoric still treated as suspect, a state-level renegotiation conducted by the very formation that led the sovereignty critique in Parliament. Odisha enacted an electricity reform act in 1995, the first state to restructure and move toward privatising power distribution, after decades in which electricity boards had been the purest form of state patronage. Andhra Pradesh under N. Chandrababu Naidu, Chief Minister from September 1995, built a governing identity on courting investment, marketing Hyderabad as an information technology destination and publishing a Vision 2020 document that read like a state-scale draft of the 1991 grammar: an economic instrument, the investor summit and the single-window clearance, doing the political work of defining a government's character.

The statutes of the second generation codified the settlement across party lines. The rupee became convertible on the current account in August 1994, closing the exchange question the devaluation had opened. The Foreign Exchange Management Act of 1999, enacted by the Vajpayee government to take effect in June 2000, replaced the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973 and rewrote the state's posture toward foreign exchange from policing to management in its very title. The Electricity Act of 2003 generalised the Odisha experiment. Tax reform proceeded on the lines the Chelliah committee had laid down in its reports of 1992 and 1993, under finance ministers of three different formations. By the time the phrase second generation reforms entered budget speeches at the turn of the century, it conceded the framework's point in its grammar: the first generation was a founding act whose sequel every government was obliged to write in its own hand, and no government proposed instead to repeal the founding.

## Where the Framework Bends: Technical Content and Its Political Licence

The strongest counter to the reform as politics reading is the one the brief for this article itself insists on facing. Some decisions in the record are dense with technical content that no political reading dissolves: the Reserve Bank's management of the exchange rate through the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System of March 1992 and the unified market rate of March 1993; the September 1994 agreement between the government and the Reserve Bank to phase out automatic monetisation of the deficit through ad hoc treasury bills, completed by 1997; the monetary targeting debates of Governor C. Rangarajan's tenure from 1992 to 1997. These episodes were argued in the grammar of economics (reserve money, sterilisation, real effective exchange rates) among officials who shared a professional formation, and a citizen reading the documents finds little party colour in them. If any part of the record belongs to the technical-economic-decision reading, this part does.

The framework's answer is a distinction between the instrument and the licence. Technical reasoning selected the instruments: the two-step structure of the 1992 exchange reform, the calendar of the treasury bill phase-out, the width of the band the rupee was allowed. Political authorisation selected the moments, the officeholders and the ownership of consequences: a finance minister signed the 1994 agreement knowing it surrendered a fiscal convenience his predecessors had guarded, a Prime Minister accepted the exchange-rate regime knowing its failures would be billed to his government, and Parliament funded the transition without a vote on its design. The technical content operated through political licence rather than independently of it, and the proof is the counterfactual the record supplies. The same instruments were available to the Chandra Shekhar government in the spring of 1991, staffed by many of the same officials, and that government deferred them because its political licence, a caretaker mandate on Congress sufferance, did not extend to founding a settlement. Technique waits for licence. The framework claims the licence, and leaves the arithmetic to the economists with respect.

The Asian financial crisis of 1997 and 1998 supplied the bend's hardest test and its clearest verdict. The Tarapore committee on capital account convertibility, appointed by the Reserve Bank, reported in May 1997 with a phased three-year road map toward fuller convertibility, signed by the finest technical hands in Indian finance. Within months the Thai baht, the Indonesian rupiah and the Korean won had collapsed, and the road map was shelved by governments of two different formations without a parliamentary battle or a public campaign. The shelving was a political decision about a technical instrument, taken in the light of foreign wreckage, and its consequence (India's relative insulation in 1997 and 1998, purchased by the very controls the reform had retained) was claimed by every party as wisdom. Technique had drawn the map; politics chose the year not to travel; and the credit, as always in this record, was collected in the political currency. The episode is the framework's whole argument in miniature, conducted by the settlement's own graduates.

The bend in the framework is real, and it marks the pillar's honest boundary. Where a decision's content is exhausted by its arithmetic and its authorship carries no distributional or identity consequence, the political reading thins to the observation that someone signed it. The pillar's claim is not that such decisions are common. It is that in the Indian record the consequential economic decisions (the ones that reorder who may produce, who may borrow, who may trade, and who is seen to rule) have consistently carried the four components in readable form, and that July 1991 carries them in their most legible hand.

## The Credit War: Authorship Contested After the Fact

A settlement's authorship is an asset, and the struggle over the credit for 1991 confirms the framework from an unexpected direction. In the first years the programme had two names attached, the Prime Minister who owned it and the Finance Minister who announced it, and the division suited both. Across the later nineteen nineties the Congress party's memory turned selective: defeated in 1996 and reorganised under new leadership, the party celebrated Singh's stewardship and grew reticent about Rao, whose tenure it associated with the Babri Masjid demolition of December 1992 and the defeats that followed. When Rao died in December 2004, the arrangements for his farewell were widely reported as a slight: his body was not received inside the Congress headquarters at Akbar Road in Delhi, and the cremation took place in Hyderabad. Whatever the intentions of the organisers, the episode was read across the press as the party declining to claim the settlement's senior author, an act of memory management that makes no sense in a technical reading of 1991 and perfect sense in a political one: the party wanted the reform's credit without the reformer's liabilities.

Singh, elevated to the premiership in 2004, used his authority to restore the partnership in the record, naming Rao in speeches as the leader under whom the reforms were launched and whose political protection made them possible; in his later years he described Rao as the father of the economic reforms in terms his party's reticence had avoided. Sitapati's Half Lion (2016) pressed the restoration into biography, arguing from the papers that Rao was the programme's political author and Singh his chosen instrument, a thesis that irritated admirers of each man in turn. The quarrel's terms are the framework's vindication. Nobody contests the credit for a technical adjustment a generation later. Credit is contested for founding acts, and the ferocity with which 1991's parentage was fought over, in memoirs, biographies and funeral arrangements, is primary evidence that the participants themselves understood the event as the political kind.

The credit war also disciplines the conversion reading one final time. If the reform had been the simple expression of its finance minister's beliefs, the credit would have settled on him uncontested and the party's embarrassment would be unintelligible. If it had been the Fund's dictation, there would be no credit to fight for. The fight exists because the authorship was real, joint and consequential: a Prime Minister who supplied the protection and the timing, a Finance Minister who supplied the design and the credibility, and a party that supplied, withheld and later rationed the applause. Framework 9's first component, decisions read for the political function they perform for their authors, extends naturally to the decades after the decision, when the function becomes inheritance.

## 1991 Among the Settlements of the Republic

The series that this pillar belongs to is organised around renegotiated settlements, and placing 1991 among them fixes its rank. The founding settlement of 1950 distributed citizenship, franchise and federal authority in a constitutional text. The basic structure judgment of 1973 renegotiated the boundary between Parliament and Constitution in a courtroom. The settlement of July 1991 renegotiated the boundary between the state and production in a budget speech and a policy statement, instruments with no constituent ceremony attached. Yet of the three, the economic settlement touches the citizen most often: every licence not sought, every import not queued for, every interest rate set by a market the state licensed into being, is the 1991 settlement operating at the level of daily life. Its invisibility is the measure of its success. Settlements that work disappear into the ordinary, and by the second decade of the twenty-first century the ordinary Indian economy, its scarcities abolished and its new scarcities debated, was unintelligible except as the successor of the order Singh's budget retired.

The comparison sharpens the adjudication one last time. Constitutional settlements survive because courts, parties and citizens organise around their texts; the 1991 settlement survived without a text anyone could cite in a courtroom, through the weaker instruments of expectation, vocabulary and sunk reorientation. That it survived at all, across six changes of governing formation between 1996 and 2014, is the fact the technical and trajectory readings cannot carry and the political reading was built to carry: the settlement was never only a set of measures, it was a redistribution of who may decide, who must ask and who is seen to give, and redistributions of that kind are defended by their beneficiaries in every capital and state capital where they took root.

## The Paradigmatic Case, Restated

Return to the claim with the evidence assembled. Indian economic policy reads primarily as political-architectural content, and the July 1991 reform is the paradigmatic case for Framework 9, the reform as politics framework, because in 1991 the components stand in the open. The decisions performed political function: they gave a seatless Prime Minister and a minority government a defining act and a route to a full term. Political conditions shaped the decisions: minority arithmetic, a frightened party, a creditor's calendar and a fortnight of reserves disciplined the menu, the sequence and the single advertised retreat. The content furnished identity: from Hugo in the budget speech to swadeshi at Nagpur and the level playing field in Bombay, every actor of the decade described himself in the vocabulary the reform had set. The mechanics were engineered: hop, skip and jump on the exchange rate, trade on 4 July, industry on budget day, the Fund's signature solicited after the facts, the fertiliser nerve cauterised in August.

The founding generation would have recognised the species of event. The republic's first settlement, read through [the founding three-tensions framework](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), was likewise a political construction wearing institutional dress, and the constitutional moments that followed it, surveyed in [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), show the same pattern of text doing political work. Economic settlements belong in that company. The articles that follow in this pillar apply Table 1 row by row: to the Nehruvian planned economy re-read as a compact rather than a blueprint, to the nationalisations read as a class of political act, to the reforms of 1991 in their documentary detail, to the sanctions response of 1998, to the information settlement of 2005, to the crisis management of 2008, to demonetisation and the Goods and Services Tax re-read under this frame, and to the tax cut and production incentives with which the pillar closes. A claim that cannot lose is not a claim, and the hub states its exposure plainly. The reform as politics reading would be defeated, or gravely thinned, by three findings the record does not contain: a successor government that reversed the settlement's core (licensing restored, the equity route closed, the trade lists rebuilt) without paying a political price for the reversal; a July 1991 documentary trail showing the measures drafted outside the Indian government and transmitted as conditions before they were announced; or a decade after 1991 in which the programme's content carried no party identity, so that manifestos, slogans and credit claims ignored it. The record holds the opposite of each: no reversal was ever proposed by a formation that won office, the drafting trail runs through Indian committees from Hazari in 1967 to Ahluwalia in 1990, and the credit for 1991 was fought over at funerals and in biographies for thirty years. Inside those limits the framework claims its ground. Economic decisions in the Indian record are political acts with authors, audiences and prices, and the archive of 1991, its speeches, statements, letters and votes, is the archive of a political founding that happened to be denominated in rupees.

The pillar's remaining articles spend the frame in order. The Nehruvian planned economy is re-read as the compact whose terms 1991 renegotiated, and the nationalisations as the grammar lesson that taught the renegotiators their trade. The reforms of 1991 are then reopened in documentary detail, the budget and the industrial policy read clause by clause against the politics this hub has mapped. The sanctions response of 1998 tests the frame under external punishment rather than external credit. The information settlement of 2005 and the crisis management of 2008 test it on transparency and on finance, the two fields where the technical reading claims its strongest ground. Demonetisation and the Goods and Services Tax are re-read as the settlement's heirs acting in its grammar, and the corporate tax cut of September 2019 with the production-linked incentive scheme closes the arc, a reform moment conducted in the open, by announcement, in the manner July 1991 invented for Indian economic policy. The frame stands citable. The pillar will spend it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does economic-policy-as-politics mean?

Economic-policy-as-politics is the reading defended across this pillar: an economic decision is analysed first for the political work it performs, the political conditions that produced it and the public identity it builds for its authors, and only then as a technical answer to an economic problem. The reading does not deny that deficits, reserves and exchange rates obey arithmetic. It insists that arithmetic never chose its own government, wrote its own budget speech or absorbed its own blame. Applied to the budget of 24 July 1991, the reading asks who authored the measures, which audiences they addressed, what they cost the Congress party internally, and why they survived five changes of government, questions the technical reading of the same document does not pose.

### Q: Why is the 1991 reform the paradigmatic case for reform-as-politics?

July 1991 is the paradigmatic case because its political machinery lies exposed rather than buried. The government that carried it held 232 of 521 Lok Sabha seats, its Prime Minister sat in neither House, its Finance Minister had never fought an election, and its manifesto had not proposed devaluation, delicensing or majority foreign equity to the voters. Every element of the reform therefore had to be manufactured politically after the fact: authorship asserted in a budget speech, consent assembled in Parliament, a backbench revolt bought off with a single fertiliser concession, creditors answered with a programme drafted in Delhi before their conditions were signed. Where a stronger government might hide the machinery, the weakness of Rao's government displays it, and a framework proven on the exposed case can then be carried to the concealed ones.

### Q: What is the four-component economic-policy-as-politics framework?

The framework reads an economic decision through four components, summarised in Table 1 of this article. The first component reads the decision for its political function: the authority and defining character it creates for the government that issues it. The second reads the causation in reverse, from political conditions to the choice itself: minority arithmetic, factional balance and creditor deadlines discipline which measures are chosen and in what order. The third treats the decision's content as the governing party's public identity, the material of its slogans and self-description. The fourth examines the mechanics of the reform moment: sequencing, the pairing of pain with compensation, the decision of what to dramatise and what to bury in notifications. A decision fully read exhibits all four, as the budget and industrial policy of 24 July 1991 do.

### Q: How is reform-as-politics different from technical-economic policy reading?

The technical-economic reading asks whether a measure was correct: whether the exchange rate was set at the right level, the deficit path at the right slope, the tariff schedule at the right height. Reform-as-politics asks the prior questions the correctness test cannot answer: why the measure became possible in July 1991 and not in 1985 when its drafts already existed, why a minority government chose the largest available version of it, why one item, fertiliser, was conceded and the rest defended, and why no successor reversed it. The two readings also weigh evidence differently. The technical reading's archive is the measure and its outcomes; the political reading's archive is the speech, the party meeting, the vote, the letter of intent and the funeral, the record of who decided and what the decision did for them.

### Q: What was the post-1991 political-architectural shift?

The shift was a redistribution of who may decide, who must ask and who is seen to give. Before 1991 the state's permission stood between an entrepreneur and production, between a citizen and foreign goods, between a state government and its investment; the licence, the import list and the plan allocation were the architecture of that permission, and the Congress system was its political tenant. After 1991 permission was progressively replaced by regulation: firms entered and expanded by right in most industries, the state's role moved to refereeing markets it had licensed into being, and competition among states for investment replaced petitioning at the centre as the economy's political geography. The architecture outlived its builders: governments of the United Front, the National Democratic Alliance and the United Progressive Alliance all governed inside it, amending its rooms and never restoring the house it replaced.

### Q: What is Framework 9 (Reform as Politics)?

Framework 9, Reform as Politics, is the master framework under which this series reads moments when a government remakes an economic settlement, and this pillar applies it to India from the planned economy to the production-linked incentive scheme. The framework's working rule is that a reform moment is a political event with economic instruments, legible through the four components set out in Table 1: political function, shaping conditions, identity content and engineered mechanics. Its companion frameworks in the series perform parallel operations on founding settlements, foreign policy and constitutional doctrine, reading texts for the political work they do. Framework 9's distinctive demand is evidentiary: no reform is explained until its authors, its audiences, its excluded parties and its price in votes or cohesion have been named from the dated record.

### Q: How does economic policy operate as domestic-political content?

Economic policy operates as domestic-political content because its instruments distribute the things domestic politics is about: permission, livelihood, honour and blame. A licence granted in Delhi decided which family's factory a district would host and which legislator could claim the credit; a bank nationalised in 1969 announced which India the ruling party claimed to defend; a fertiliser price raised in a budget and partly withdrawn within weeks taught every farmer the location of the government's nerve. The content is also rhetorical: budgets are speeches, policy statements are manifestos in office, and slogans from Garibi Hatao to swadeshi to India Shining are economic documents in campaign form. To read the instruments without the politics is to keep the ledger and lose the account it was kept in.

### Q: Why did Narasimha Rao choose a career official, Manmohan Singh, to front the reforms?

The appointment was itself the programme's first measure. Singh, a former Governor of the Reserve Bank, Finance Secretary and Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission, certified the government to its creditors by his mere presence: the Fund, the World Bank and the rating agencies were being asked to trust a signature they already knew. Domestically the choice performed the opposite service. A finance minister with no faction, no seat and no electoral past let the programme be attributed to expertise rather than to any Congress camp, concentrated its authorship in Rao's hands alone, and gave the party a front man it could defend as a national necessity rather than a partisan preference. Vinay Sitapati's biography of Rao records the deliberateness of the selection: Rao wanted the reforms and wanted them carried by a man whose credibility would substitute for the majority the government lacked.

### Q: What did the Swadeshi Jagran Manch oppose after November 1991?

The Swadeshi Jagran Manch, founded at Nagpur on 22 November 1991 under Dattopant Thengadi with the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad and sister organisations on its platform, opposed the settlement of 1991 as economic imperialism in modern dress. Its targets, stated in its campaigns from 12 January 1992 onward, were foreign direct investment and the entry of multinational firms, the Dunkel Draft's patent and agriculture texts in the Uruguay Round, and the government's recourse to the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. Its argument, drawn from the swadeshi tradition of the freedom movement, held that economic sovereignty surrendered to foreign capital would recreate political dependence, and that Indian small industry and the Indian farmer would pay for the settlement. The Manch's pressure worked on the Bharatiya Janata Party from inside its own family of organisations for the rest of the decade.

### Q: What did the Bombay Club ask the Finance Minister for in 1993?

The Bombay Club, the press label for a group of established Indian industrialists with Rahul Bajaj as their most visible spokesman, pressed Finance Minister Manmohan Singh in 1993 for a level playing field in the transition to open competition. Their petition did not ask for the licence system back. It asked for time and terms: protection phased so that Indian houses, raised under four decades of shelter and saddled with high domestic interest rates and costs, would not be swept aside by multinational entrants before they could restructure. The episode is prized by the reform as politics reading because it shows the old settlement's chief beneficiaries negotiating their place in the new one rather than denying its legitimacy. Even capital, in 1993, asked the state for politics, a managed passage, instead of asking merely for markets.

### Q: Why was the July 1991 devaluation carried out in two steps?

The Reserve Bank adjusted the rupee downward on 1 July 1991 and again on 3 July 1991, a cumulative fall of roughly 18 per cent, in a sequence Sanjaya Baru's account records Singh and Deputy Governor C. Rangarajan calling a hop, skip and jump. The two-step design spread a single shock across two announcements, letting the first move test the market and the political reaction before the second completed it, and it kept the government ahead of its critics: by the time Parliament assembled for the budget debate, the exchange rate was a fact to be discussed rather than a proposal to be defeated. The design also carried a negotiating use. A devaluation completed before the letter of intent of 27 August 1991 could be presented to the Fund as India's own opening measure, evidence of seriousness authored in Delhi rather than a condition extracted in Washington.

### Q: How did Congress MPs react to the 1991 budget inside the party?

The party's anger surfaced in the Congress Parliamentary Party's own meeting rather than on the floor of the House. As Vinod Jose's reporting in Caravan recorded from participants, as many as 63 backbenchers spoke against Manmohan Singh and the programme at the meeting held after the budget, with the increase in fertiliser prices at the centre of the complaint: members calculated, in the open, that the budget's farm prices would be collected from them at the next election. Rao defended his finance minister in the room and refused the demand for retreat on the programme as a whole. The settlement of the quarrel was surgical: the fertiliser increase was partly withdrawn in August 1991, the single advertised concession, and no other clause of the July package was reopened. The episode fixed the reform's internal frontier for the rest of the Rao years.

### Q: Why did the Rao government present the Industrial Policy on the same day as the budget?

The same-day presentation of 24 July 1991 was sequencing as strategy. The budget carried the government's fiscal authority and its crisis narrative, the reserves at a fortnight of imports; the Statement on Industrial Policy, tabled under its cover, inherited that authority for the structural half of the programme, the end of licensing, the cut in public sector reservation from seventeen industries to eight, and automatic foreign equity up to 51 per cent. Presented separately, the industrial policy would have faced its own debate, its own headlines and its own lobby campaigns, with weeks for the injured interests to organise. Presented inside the budget's day, it arrived as one clause of a national emergency settlement, debated in the budget's shadow and voted in the budget's train. Rao, who held the industries portfolio himself, signed the arrangement personally.

### Q: What role did the 27 August 1991 letter to the IMF play in the reform sequence?

The letter of 27 August 1991, in which Finance Minister Manmohan Singh wrote to the Fund's Managing Director Michel Camdessus requesting a stand-by arrangement of 1,656 million Special Drawing Rights, is the document on which the dictation theory of 1991 rests, and its date is the theory's refutation. By the time the letter was written, the devaluation of 1 and 3 July, the trade policy of 4 July, the budget and the industrial policy of 24 July were already facts of Indian law and administration. The letter therefore functioned as a ratification market: it converted decisions already taken into a programme the Fund could endorse and finance, unlocking the stand-by approved on 31 October 1991 and the World Bank's structural adjustment credit of December 1991. In the sequence the letter is the hinge between authorship and credit, which is why the framework insists on its date.

### Q: Why did no later government restore industrial licensing?

Restoration failed on arithmetic before it failed on ideology. By the time any successor took office, firms, banks, consumers and state governments had reorganised production, supply chains and careers around entry by right; reimposing licences would have required a government to own the shortages, queues and corruption stories that the old system's victims remembered and its beneficiaries could no longer defend in public. Electorally, no formation from 1996 onward, including the Left-backed United Front and the swadeshi-tongued Bharatiya Janata Party in office, placed restoration in a manifesto, because the proposal's costs were concentrated and immediate while its nostalgic constituency had dissolved into the new economy's stakeholders. The deepest reason is the framework's: licensing had been the Congress system's plumbing, and no successor party possessed either the machine that had run it or an interest in rebuilding a machine whose valves its rivals might one day hold.

### Q: How did the states become arenas of reform politics after 1991?

Delicensing moved the geography of economic competition from the Union ministries to the state capitals. With entry freed at the centre, the contest for investment turned on what states controlled: land, power, labour administration and speed of clearance. West Bengal's Left Front government issued an industrial policy in 1994 welcoming private and foreign capital its national party still criticised in Parliament. Odisha restructured its electricity sector by statute in 1995, the first state to do so. Andhra Pradesh under N. Chandrababu Naidu from September 1995 built a governing brand on investor summits, single-window clearance and the marketing of Hyderabad to the information technology industry. Chief ministers became investment ambassadors, state budgets became reform documents, and the federal system acquired an economic rivalry the plan era's bargaining for steel plants had only rehearsed.

### Q: What is the difference between reform by stealth and reform by consensus in the 1991 debate?

The two phrases name rival accounts of the reform's democratic method. Reform by stealth, Rob Jenkins's formulation of 1999, holds that Indian democracy punishes open reform, so the Rao government advanced by indirection: measures depoliticised as technical adjustments, blame diffused across committees and levels of government, the programme's true scale visible only in retrospect. Reform by consensus was the government's own counter-description, used by Rao and Singh in and after 1991: the claim that the programme was argued in Parliament, defended in the party and adjusted where consent failed, as the fertiliser rollback showed. This article's adjudication divides the honours: the mechanics were stealthy where stealth was cheap, in notifications and committee prose, and theatrical where theatre was needed, in the budget speech's peroration and the Red Fort address, which is a combination neither slogan names alone.

### Q: Did the 1991 reform have an electoral mandate?

No mandate for its content was sought or given. The Congress manifesto of 1991 promised fiscal discipline, price stability and a modernising continuation of Rajiv Gandhi's direction in general language; it did not place devaluation, the abolition of industrial licensing, foreign equity at 51 per cent or subsidy withdrawal before the electorate of May and June 1991, and no campaign of that spring was fought on them. The election itself was dominated by the Mandal and Mandir questions and interrupted by Rajiv Gandhi's assassination on 21 May 1991. The reform's legitimacy was therefore manufactured after the vote, in the budget debate, the Congress Parliamentary Party's containment and the survival of the 1993 no-confidence motion, rather than inherited from it. The distinction matters to the framework: 1991 shows a settlement can be legitimate in retrospect, ratified by successors and electorates, without having been mandated in prospect.

### Q: How did the 1993 no-confidence motion test the reform government?

The no-confidence motion of 28 July 1993 was the settlement's first full parliamentary audit under scandal conditions, moved against a government battered by the securities scam inquiry and the aftermath of the Babri Masjid demolition of December 1992. The Rao government survived by 265 votes to 251, assembling its majority from the abstentions and defections its managers had cultivated across the smaller parties. The vote's meaning for the reform was larger than survival: a House that would not dismiss the government in 1993 had, by the act of sustaining it, acquiesced in the programme the government existed to execute, and the remaining Rao budgets proceeded without a comparable challenge. The framework reads the margin as the settlement's ratification instrument, the moment the political system recorded that 1991 would be administered, amended and inherited, but not repealed.

### Q: How quickly did India buy back the pledged gold?

The gold was repurchased within months, as the reserves recovered through the winter of 1991 and 1992. The 47 tonnes airlifted to London in July 1991 and pledged with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan were redeemed first, and the twenty tonnes sold to the Union Bank of Switzerland in May 1991 under a repurchase arrangement were bought back by the end of 1991, closing the episode inside the calendar year that had opened it. The speed of the buyback was itself a political objective: the shipments had entered opposition rhetoric as the pawning of the nation's honour, and the government understood that every month the metal sat in foreign vaults the metaphor compounded. Redeeming the gold closed the crisis's most quotable chapter and let the settlement of July 1991 be argued on its measures rather than on its collateral.
