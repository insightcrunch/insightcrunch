## A Scene Built to Be Heard: The Sound of Mourning in Act Four

The sound of mourning in Act Four is the most deliberately composed noise in the play, and the verdict of this reading is plain: the stylized lament over the seemingly dead Juliet is neither failed realism nor empty rant, but a piece of scored communal music, patterned because communal grief in Verona has a score, and the comic musicians who follow it do not mock that music from outside. They complete the chord from below, adding the trade sounds of hired instruments, wage talk, and appetite that the ritual cry leaves out. Heard whole, the scene is a dissonance with a design: formal lament above, prose haggling below, and between them a silent girl who makes no noise at all and around whom every other noise in the act arranges itself. The hollowness some listeners hear in the cries is real, but it belongs to particular voices inside the pattern, to a suitor performing a grief he has purchased with a ring and a priest performing a consolation he knows to be false. The pattern itself is not hollow. Pattern is how a household that has never rehearsed sorrow performs sorrow together on the spot, each mourner taking up a shared stock of cries the way singers take up a burden they all already know.

![The patterned lament over the seeming corpse of Juliet and the silent hired musicians of Act Four - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That claim needs the whole act, not the single famous scene. Act Four is a crescendo engineered in stages: a hushed cell where a desperate plan is whispered into being, a kitchen in uproar as a wedding feast is cooked at speed, a girl alone in a chamber imagining shrieks that exist only inside her fear, a house at three in the morning ringing with orders and a crowing cock and a curfew bell, and then the wedding morning itself, where the noise of preparation stops dead against a body that will not answer when called. The lament of Act 4 Scene 5 lands with its full weight only because the act has trained the ear for an hour beforehand, raising the volume scene by scene so that the silence at the centre of the mourning can be felt as a physical drop. The musicians then lower the register again, from verse cry to prose quibble, and the scene ends with three hired men deciding to wait for dinner. Between the first call into the still chamber and that last flat prose sentence, the play runs its entire acoustic range, and the range is the meaning.

The evidence throughout comes from René Weis's Arden Third Series edition of the play, cited by act, scene, and line, with quotation kept brief as the subject demands: this is an article about brief cries, repeated phrases, and the spaces between them. What follows listens to the act in order, then takes the lament apart cry by cry, then gives the musicians their full hearing, and only then settles the old quarrel between listeners who find the mourning moving and listeners who find it hollow. Both camps are hearing something true. The task is to say precisely what each is hearing, and why the scene was built so that both hearings are possible at once.

## Before the Body: Act Four Raises Its Volume by Stages

Act Four opens in a near whisper, and the quiet is conspiratorial. In Friar Laurence's cell, Paris speaks the smooth public language of a sanctioned suitor while Juliet answers in doublespeak that only the audience and the Friar can fully hear, and the scene's most eloquent noise is a door: Juliet begs that the door be shut and not opened until the Friar has heard her, because the plan about to be spoken exists only inside a sealed acoustic space (4.1). The potion plot is, among other things, a sound plot. A girl who cannot afford to be overheard arranges to be mistaken for a corpse, which is the one condition in which a Capulet daughter cannot be made to speak, answer, or consent aloud. The Friar's cell is the quietest room in the act, and its quiet is the sound of secrecy working.

The next scene slams the volume up. Act 4 Scene 2 is a kitchen and a counting house in full cry: Capulet, reconciled to the marriage and drunk on his own recovered authority, issues orders in every direction, sending Peter out with a guest list, hiring cooks by the score, promising that the wedding will now happen a day early. His satisfaction expresses itself as command, and command is the loudest speech register the household owns. Where the cell scene whispered a plan for a false death, the kitchen scene shouts a timetable for a real wedding, and the collision between those two volumes, the whispered potion and the shouted feast, is the act's whole mechanism stated in sound. The audience hears the trap being built at full volume by a man who believes he is building a celebration.

Act 4 Scene 3 drops the volume to one voice and fills the silence with imagined noise. Juliet, alone with the vial, conducts an entire acoustics of fear inside her own head: she hears, in prospect, the shrieks of mandrakes torn from the earth, a sound that in the period's lore drove hearers mad, and she populates the vault in advance with the rattling of bones and the stench and clamour of a charnel house (4.3). None of these sounds exists. The scene is a solo for a voice describing sounds that terrify it, and its climax is not a cry but a swallow: she drinks, and the voice stops. The act's pattern is now clear in miniature. Sound in Act Four is always either performed for hearers, as the lament will be, or suffered alone where no hearer can answer, as here. Juliet's imagined shrieks are the private rehearsal for the public wailing that her still body will shortly provoke, and the contrast between the two is the act's cruellest joke in acoustics: the girl who feared the vault's noises will lie at the centre of the loudest scene in the play and hear none of it.

Act 4 Scene 4 is the noise of a great house waking too early, and Shakespeare scores it in small exact signals. The second cock has crowed. The curfew bell has rung three. Capulet orders lights, spices, baked meats, logs for the fire; the Nurse scolds him toward bed and calls him a cot-quean for fussing over the pantry; Lady Capulet moves through with keys in her hand, asking for more spices, for dates and quinces in the pastry (4.4). It is the sound of wealth at work, dozens of servants implied offstage, a feast being assembled in the dark by people who do not yet know that the bride at the centre of the noise has drunk herself into a deathlike sleep in the next movement of the act. The scene ends with a handover of noise that the next scene collects on. Capulet, who has refused bed and played the housewife for one night, sends the Nurse to wake Juliet and help deck her up, transferring the morning's first sound from his own commanding voice to hers. The transfer matters. The act's loudest man delegates the discovery to the act's most intimate voice, and the chamber scene opens, fittingly, not with an order but with a call, the household's chain of command dissolving at the bedroom door into a servant's pet names. Authority has shouted its last shout of the act; what follows belongs to love, rank, and grief, in ascending order of volume and descending order of control. The cockcrow and the bell matter beyond their naturalism. They are communal sounds, the sounds by which a city tells itself what time it is, and they place the household's private uproar inside a larger civic soundscape that will go on crowing and ringing whatever happens in the Capulet chamber. That is the soundscape Bruce R. Smith maps in The Acoustic World of Early Modern England, a world in which bells, cries, and hired music carried social meaning before they carried anything else, and in which a household's noises announced its standing to the street. Act Four keeps glancing at that larger acoustic world, the world of curfew bells and morning cocks, precisely so that the chamber scene can seal itself against it: the mourning of 4.5 happens indoors, in a room the city cannot hear, and its sounds are for the family and the audience alone.

So the act arrives at its fifth scene with the ear fully trained. The audience has heard secrecy whisper, authority shout, terror hallucinate, and industry clatter. What it has not yet heard is grief, and grief, when it comes, will come in the most artificial register of all, more patterned than the kitchen orders and more choral than the cockcrow, and the artificiality is the first thing to understand about it.

### Why does the Nurse call Juliet by name after name before any wailing starts?

The Nurse calls Juliet by a climbing series of names, mistress, lady, lamb, love, sweetheart, bride, before she understands that the girl will not answer, and the series is the sound of a search being conducted by voice alone. Each name is a different key tried in the same lock.

The household names come first, the names of service and routine, then the pet names of intimacy, then the newest name of all, bride, the name the whole house has been shouting toward for two scenes. The escalation measures her dawning knowledge more exactly than any narration could, because a call that changes its terms is a mind changing its guess about what it faces: a girl asleep, a girl sulking, a girl hiding, a bride late for her own wedding. Only when every name has failed does the voice change its nature, from calling to crying, and the famous wail begins. The names are therefore not decoration on the discovery. They are the discovery, happening at the speed of sound.

The calls also establish the scene's governing acoustic fact, which is that sound in this chamber goes out and nothing comes back. A call is a social instrument; it presumes an answerer. The Nurse's first lines are all presumption, spoken into a room she believes contains a sleeper, and each unanswered name makes the room quieter in retrospect, because the audience re-hears every earlier call as having been addressed to a body past answering. Shakespeare had staged discovery through soliloquy often enough; here he stages it as failed address, and the choice turns the whole scene into an essay on its own medium. The mourning that follows will be address on a grander scale, apostrophe piled on apostrophe, all of it directed at a girl who cannot hear a word, and the Nurse's little ladder of names is where that pattern is set, quietly, before the choir assembles.

There is a further precision in the sequence worth hearing. The Nurse's final name for Juliet in the calling series is bride, and it is the name that kills the routine, because bride is the one identity the day was built around and the one the body on the bed has most completely vacated. The calls that follow it are no longer addressed to Juliet in any expectation of reply. "Help, help! My lady's dead!" (4.5) is addressed to the house, and the shift of addressee is the hinge of the scene's first movement: sound stops travelling toward the girl and starts travelling outward, summoning hearers, and the lament, properly speaking, is nothing but that outward travel organized into pattern.

## Calls Into Silence: The Discovery as Failed Address

The discovery movement of 4.5 is built from three entrances, and each entrance is announced by sound before it is completed in sight. Lady Capulet enters to the Nurse's noise and contributes the scene's first verse, a couplet of pure exclamation: "O me, O me! My child, my only life! / Revive, look up, or I will die with thee!" (4.5). The couplet is worth pausing over for its brevity alone. The mother gets two lines where the Nurse will later get six in a single speech, and the proportion is not an accident of composition but an entry in the scene's ledger of who is licensed to make how much noise. Lady Capulet's grief arrives already rhymed, already shaped, as if the feeling had a form waiting for it; the couplet's click of closure is the sound of a woman whose emotional vocabulary was always going to be the household's approved one, correct, balanced, and over almost before it began. Her imperative verbs do the scene's most poignant acoustic work: revive, look up. They are commands issued to a corpse, the mother's lifelong register of instruction meeting the one pupil who will never again obey an instruction, and the commands' failure is the discovery restated in grammar.

Capulet enters still in wedding time, complaining that the bridegroom has arrived and the bride is not ready, and his conversion from impatience to grief is the act's finest piece of sound engineering. He looks, and the verse slows. "She's cold. / Her blood is settled, and her joints are stiff" (4.5): the lines itemize the body the way his kitchen orders itemized the feast, cold, settled, stiff, a list of properties replacing a person, and the list is recognizably the same mind at work, the great household manager taking inventory, now of a daughter. Then the inventory breaks into figure, the famous frost simile, death lying on her like an untimely frost upon the sweetest flower of the field, and the line lengthens and softens as it goes. The detailed reading of that frost figure belongs to the frost article in this series, and its place here is narrower: it is the moment Capulet's sound changes key, from the staccato of inspection to the long breath of lament, and a listener can hear the exact line where management ends and mourning takes the voice over. Few discoveries in the period's drama are scored with this precision, a man's register collapsing in stages inside a dozen lines.

What the two parents and the Nurse then exchange, before the Friar and Paris arrive, is the scene's first fully choral moment, and it lasts only two lines. The Nurse cries "O lamentable day!" and Lady Capulet answers, "O woeful time!" (4.5). The exchange is tiny and complete: two women, neither of them inventing, passing a formula between them like a response in a liturgy, the second cry tuning itself to the first. This is the lament in embryo. Before the set speeches begin, the play shows the mechanism bare, grief as antiphon, one voice supplying the phrase the next voice needs. Everything the scene later does at length, the shared adjectives, the circling repetitions, the mourners finishing one another's emotional sentences, is present in these two exclamations, and their smallness is what makes them diagnostic. Strip the lament of imagery, narrative, and argument, and what remains is this: a cry, and another voice taking the cry up.

Capulet then gives the scene its thesis in a single paradox. "Death, that hath ta'en her hence to make me wail, / Ties up my tongue and will not let me speak" (4.5). The loudest man in Verona, the order giver of two bustling scenes, announces that grief has struck him dumb, in a line he is in the middle of speaking, inside a scene where he will speak more than anyone. The paradox is not a slip. It is the play naming its own acoustic subject from inside: lament is what a voice does when it experiences speech as simultaneously compulsory and impossible, and the formal patterning of everything that follows can be heard as the household's solution to that paradox. When feeling claims to have no words, it borrows words that already exist, formulas, refrains, other people's phrases. The tied tongue is untied by the common stock. That is why the laments sound borrowed, because by the scene's own account, they are: they are the sound of speakers whose private vocabularies have failed, reaching for the public one.


## The Anatomy of a Cry: How the Patterned Lament Is Built

Take the lament apart and its construction is almost brazenly simple, which is the point: the scene's grief is built from a small kit of parts, and the kit is audible. The first part is the open vowel that begins nearly everything, the cry of "O". Lady Capulet opens with it doubled. The Nurse's great speech is strung on it like beads: "O day! O day! O day! O hateful day!" (4.5). Paris opens his lament with it twice inside one line, "O love! O life!" Capulet opens his with it twice in four words, "O child! O child!" The vocative O is the least verbal sound in the language, a vowel shaped into address, closer to a note than to a word, and its dominance is the lament's way of announcing that it operates at the border between speech and music. A cry of O carries no information. It carries only pitch, volume, and duration, which is to say it carries exactly the things music carries. The mourners keep retreating to it because it is the one utterance in the scene that cannot be wrong, cannot be argued with, and cannot fail to be understood by any hearer in any language. It is grief's minimum viable sound, and the scene builds cathedrals on it.

The second part is the shared adjective stock. Woeful, lamentable, hateful, miserable, accursed: the epithets circulate from mouth to mouth like coin from a common purse. The Nurse calls the day lamentable, then woeful three times in a single line, then hateful. Lady Capulet calls it accursed, unhappy, wretched, hateful. No mourner guards a private vocabulary of sorrow; each spends from the common fund, and the effect on a listener is of a single grief speaking through several throats rather than several griefs coinciding. This circulation is the strongest evidence for hearing the lament as choral. Choirs are defined not by simultaneous singing but by shared material, the same words in the same order from every mouth, and by that definition the Capulet chamber, once the set speeches begin, is a choir that assembles itself in real time, each new entrant handed the song sheet on arrival. Paris, who has never mourned in this house before, is singing from the sheet within seconds of entering it.

The third part is antiphony, mourners answering mourners in matched shapes, and its clearest display is the paired drumbeat at the scene's centre. Paris laments in a volley of past participles: "Beguiled, divorced, wronged, spited, slain!" (4.5). Four speeches later Capulet answers in the identical figure: "Despised, distressed, hated, martyred, killed!" (4.5). The echo is too exact to be accident and too functional to be mere decoration. Capulet, the host of this grief, takes up the guest's rhythm and returns it amplified, as a host should, and the matching volleys bind the two men into a temporary unit, the father and the cheated bridegroom drumming in time. Heard as sound, the participles are percussion: five stressed monosyllables and disyllables in a row, each one a blow, the line's meaning almost secondary to its beat. What is being counted in both speeches is damage, damage done to the speaker by the death, and the drumbeat counts it the way a bell counts hours, in repeated identical strokes. The full account of how echo works between speakers belongs to the companion study of [Echo and Repetition in the Lovers' Dialogue](/2025/07/25/echo-repetition-lovers-dialogue/), where the same device in the lovers' mouths builds intimacy; in the mourning chamber the device builds something else, solidarity in loss, and the difference between the two uses is a measure of how neutral, and how powerful, the instrument is.

The fourth part is the refrain, and its fullest form is the Nurse's. Her set speech is constructed like a song with a burden: the phrase woeful day returns and returns, gathering the interjection O around it, until the speech's six lines strike the word day ten times and toll the word woeful six. Nothing in the speech advances. No new fact arrives, no image develops, and that refusal to advance is the refrain's whole function: a burden exists to be returned to, and returning is what communal singing does with grief, holding the company together on ground nobody has to invent under pressure. The speech is regularly singled out as the scene's most excessive, and by the standards of information it is; by the standards of sound it is the scene's most necessary, the moment where the chamber's noise organizes itself into the shape of a song that a whole room could join. A lament that can be joined is a lament that is working, socially, whatever a literary listener thinks of its repetitions.

### What makes five separate mourners sound like a single choir?

Five mourners sound like a choir because the scene progressively removes every marker of individual voice and replaces it with shared material: the same opening vowel, the same circulating adjectives, the same rhythms answered back and forth, and the same refrain taken up by whoever is speaking.

Individuality in speech comes from invention, from saying what only that speaker would say, and the lament's speakers invent almost nothing after their first lines. They quote the common stock, they answer one another's figures in kind, and they converge on identical formulas for the day, the hour, and the girl. The convergence is audible as unity. By the time Capulet matches Paris participle for participle, the chamber is no longer five people grieving in proximity; it is one instrument with five stops, and the grief has become, in the strictest sense, choral: a single utterance distributed across bodies, which is what ritual mourning in a great household was for.

Underneath the four parts sits a fifth, less visible: the rhymed couplet, the lament's default container. Speech after speech closes its thought with a rhyme, and the rhymes give the noise a pulse of completion, tiny cadences of closure arriving every two lines in a scene whose emotional content is that nothing can be closed. The verse form of the mourning, and its eventual surrender to the prose of the musicians, is the ground covered by the series study of [Act 4 Scene 5: Mourning the 'Dead' Juliet](/2023/11/17/act-4-scene-5-mourning-juliet/), which tracks the scene's structure whole; the concern here is only what the couplets do to the ear. They make the grief sound finished at regular intervals, minted and sealed, and that finish is precisely what divides listeners. To one ear, the cadences are the dignity of ritual, grief given a shape strong enough to be borne in company. To another, they are the click of a mechanism, proof that the feeling has been prefabricated. Both ears are responding to the same acoustic property, the couplet's closure, and the scene's greatness lies in its refusal to suppress either response: it lets the form be beautiful and suspect at once, and then sends in three musicians with unplayed instruments to make the suspicion impossible to ignore.

A word belongs here to the page as well as the stage, because most readers meet this scene silently and its sound reaches them through punctuation. The early texts point these speeches sparsely; the thicket of exclamation marks that bristles across a modern edition's lament, every cry capped with its little vertical shout, is editorial, supplied by centuries of editors, Weis among them, making decisions about volume on the reader's behalf. The decision is unavoidable and consequential. An exclamation mark is a volume instruction, and a page dense with them instructs the inner ear to keep the scene at a scream throughout, which flattens exactly the dynamics the verse builds, the climb from call to cry to chorus to the Friar's level cadence. Read with the pointing held lightly, the scene's dynamics return: the discovery is quiet before it is loud, the set speeches swell rather than start at full voice, and the prose of the musicians drops the register by contrast rather than by editorial announcement. The sound of mourning in Act Four is thus partly an editorial artefact, and hearing it well means hearing past the marks to the metre, where the true volume markings are written in stress, line length, and the alternation of verse and prose.

## Five Voices, Five Registers of Wailing

Within the shared pattern, each mourner is still given a distinct acoustic signature, and the signatures amount to a small sociology of who may make what kind of noise in a great house. The Nurse wails in bulk. Hers is the longest single cry in the scene, the most repetitive, and the least adorned with imagery, and its proportions are exactly those of her station and her claim: she has no rhetorical education to perform and no public dignity to protect, but she has the longest intimacy with the body on the bed, having washed it, dressed it, and told it stories for fourteen years, and the scene pays that intimacy in the only currency she trades in, volume and duration. Her wail is also the one that stays closest to the border of prose. Where the parents and Paris produce shaped verse units, the Nurse's refrain keeps collapsing toward the rhythm of a work song or a street cry, grief in the metre of labour. The play's wider system of mourning, its rites, its hired and inherited forms, and its final refusal of lament in the tomb, is mapped in [Grief and Mourning Rituals in Verona](/2024/11/08/grief-and-mourning-analysis/); within that system, the Nurse's cry is the sound of the servant's hall admitted, for once, to the family's central rite, and admitted on the strength of love rather than rank.

Lady Capulet wails in miniature, and the miniature is eloquent. Two lines at the discovery, then one set speech of six lines built on a pile of adjectives and a single reiterated structure, one child, one thing to rejoice in, one loss. Her grief is real and it is rationed, and the rationing is characterization by acoustics: this is a woman who experiences even catastrophe inside the forms of household management, grief as an inventory of the nursery, But one, poor one, one poor and loving child. The counting is the sound to notice. Where the Nurse repeats a phrase, Lady Capulet repeats a number, and the difference between refrain and tally is the difference between the two women's whole lives, one spent in the body's service, the other in the house's accounts. It would be easy to call the mother's brevity coldness, and the scene does invite the thought; but brevity in this scene is also the mark of a grief that has been struck at the root of identity, a mother whose entire social self was invested in the single counted child. The tally stops because there is nothing left to count.

Capulet wails twice, in two different voices, and the doubling is the scene's portrait of a man discovering that his two registers, manager and father, produce two different griefs. The first Capulet is the inspector of the cold body, the frost speech, grief arriving through the senses. The second Capulet, arriving after the Friar has entered, is the public mourner, the one who answers Paris's drumbeat with his own, who pronounces the son-in-law figure of Death, and who closes the whole lament with the great inventory speech that renames the wedding as a funeral. That closing speech is, for the purposes of sound, the most remarkable in the scene, because it is itself a soundmap in verse: "Our instruments to melancholy bells, / Our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast, / Our solemn hymns to sullen dirges change" (4.5). Capulet hears his own household's noise turning into its opposite and lists the conversion item by item, instruments to bells, hymns to dirges, cheer to feast of burial. The man who ran the act's noisiest scenes on command now stands inside the noise and describes it changing key around him. The speech's fame rests on its imagery of inversion, but its acoustic content is prior: it is the only speech in the scene that is about the scene's own sound, a mourner pausing to hear the mourning. In that pause, the pattern briefly becomes self-aware, and the self-awareness is what keeps the speech from hollowness on any hearing: a grief that can listen to itself is not an empty grief, however borrowed its phrases.

Paris wails in borrowed polish, and his is the sound on which the hollowness charge fastens most justly. His lament is the most perfectly made in the scene, a balanced structure of participle volley, apostrophe to Death, and closing paradox, "O love! O life! Not life, but love in death!" (4.5), and its perfection is the problem. Paris has known Juliet, by the play's accounting, barely at all; he has courted her father, admired her in a crowd, and kissed her hand in a corridor of doublespeak where she told him, in terms he never decoded, that she loved another man. His grief therefore has the acoustics of an anthem sung by a man reading the words for the first time and singing them beautifully. Everything is correct: the figures, the timing, the antiphonal courtesy with which he supplies Capulet a rhythm to answer. What is missing is any sound that could belong only to him, any crack, any prose, any unseemly excess like the Nurse's. The scene needs one mourner whose grief is entirely formal, to stand as the control sample against which the others' mixtures of form and feeling can be measured, and Paris, courteous to the last, volunteers.

His entrance line sharpens the measurement. "Have I thought long to see this morning's face, / And doth it give me such a sight as this?" (4.5) is a question about seeing, posed by the one mourner whose relationship to Juliet was always conducted at the distance of the eye, in crowds, across rooms, through a father's negotiations. Paris grieves for a face, and his lament keeps faith with that limitation: it is a connoisseur's grief, beautifully framed, addressed to abstractions, Death and love and life, rather than to the girl, and it contains no memory, because there is barely a shared past to remember. Set his speech beside the Nurse's, which is nothing but memory, fourteen years of it tolling in a refrain, and the scene's acoustics complete their sociology: the closer the mourner stood to the body in life, the less shaped and the more repetitive the sound; the farther away, the more polished. Polish, in this scene, is the sound of distance.

The Friar does not wail at all, and his silence within the pattern is the fifth register. He enters with a question tuned to the wrong occasion, "Come, is the bride ready to go to church?" (4.5), hears the answer, and moves directly into the scene's most finished verse, the consolation, without contributing a single cry of his own. Every other voice in the room pays the toll of an exclamation; his pays none. The exemption is legible two ways, and the scene's listeners have always divided on it: it is the professional's exemption, the priest who buries other people's children weekly and whose office forbids him the family's noise, and it is the conspirator's exemption, the one man in the room who knows the corpse is a sleeper and whose grief would be perjury if he performed it. The two readings coexist in the sound itself. His couplets are the room's most regular music and its least felt, a metronome set going among the mourners, and the acoustic judgment and the moral judgment arrive together: what keeps time for everyone else's grief is the one voice that has no grief to keep time with.

Two things stand out when the scene is tabulated this way. The first is how much of the lament's material is repetition with variation rather than statement: of the eighteen events, at least twelve are built on a phrase, rhythm, or figure that another voice has already used or is about to use. The second is the direction of travel. The rows move steadily downward in social register and steadily outward in honesty about what sound costs: the top of the table is a servant calling a girl's names for love, the middle is a family singing its sorrow from a shared sheet, and the bottom is three tradesmen pricing their silence and staying for a meal. The soundmap does not show a scene that begins in feeling and ends in farce. It shows a single continuous inquiry, conducted in noise, into what grief sounds like at every level of a household that has to produce it on demand.

## The Friar's Couplets: Consolation as a Lid on Noise

The Friar's consolation speech is usually discussed for its content, the brisk doctrine that the parents have got what they prayed for, a daughter advanced above the clouds, and the content has been fully weighed in the series account of the play's grief system. Heard as sound, the speech does something prior to doctrine: it lowers the room. "Peace, ho, for shame!" (4.5) is not an argument; it is a hush, the verbal equivalent of a hand raised over a choir, and everything that follows is engineered to keep the volume down. The couplets arrive in balanced pairs, each thought closed by its rhyme before the next begins, each sentence built on antithesis, heaven set against the household, the parents' part against heaven's part, tears against merriment. Antithesis is the acoustic opposite of the lament's method. The lament accumulates; the consolation divides. The lament repeats a phrase until it becomes a burden; the consolation splits every proposition into two halves that cancel into calm. A listener does not need to accept a word of the doctrine to feel the speech working, any more than a listener needs to believe a lullaby. The form is the sedative.

That is also why the speech fails, as consolation, even as it succeeds as sound control. The mourners do not answer it. No voice in the scene says amen, or weeps more quietly on cue, or takes up the Friar's cadence as they took up one another's. Capulet's response is to deliver the inventory speech, in his own register, at his own length, as if the consolation had been an interval rather than an intervention. The lid is set on the noise and the noise simply continues underneath it until it has finished its own course, and the scene's structure delivers the judgment: the lament ends not because it has been answered but because it has been completed, the way a song ends. The Friar can conduct the ending, ordering the body borne to church and converting noise into arrangements, but he cannot shorten the song by a bar. Set beside the series account of [Music Cues and Instruments in the Play](/2031/07/25/music-cues-and-instruments-in-the-play/), which catalogues the drama's scored sounds whole, the consolation reads as one more cue that does not produce the effect it calls for: music prescribed, like the musicians' pipes, and never quite played.

### What happens to the room's noise the moment the Friar begins to speak?

The room's noise changes government the moment the Friar begins: wailing that had been self-organizing, passed from voice to voice by imitation, comes under the control of a single regular metre that nobody else adopts. His couplets do not join the pattern; they stand over it, balanced, antithetical, and unexclaimed.

They are the first sustained stretch of verse in the scene that no other mourner echoes, and the hush he commands is real but shallow. Underneath his cadence the lament completes itself on its own schedule, in Capulet's answering inventory, and the practical orders that end the movement, the rosemary, the bearing to church, succeed where the doctrine failed because they give the mourners tasks instead of thoughts. The scene thus separates two kinds of authority over sound: the authority that can lower a volume, which the Friar has, and the authority that can answer a grief, which nobody in the room has.

There is a final refinement in the consolation's sound that rewards attention, and it concerns what the Friar does not say. Every mourner before him has addressed the body or the day; the Friar addresses the mourners, and his speech contains no apostrophe to Juliet at all. The girl who has been called by a ladder of names, apostrophized as flower, child, soul, and bride, disappears from the scene's address system at the exact moment the professional takes over, replaced by doctrine about her. The acoustics state the fact cleanly: lament speaks to the dead, consolation speaks about them, and the change of preposition is the change of genre happening inside a single room. When the musicians later speak about nobody at all, only about tunes and wages, the trajectory completes itself. The scene's sound travels from to, through about, down to mere noise about things, and the girl at its centre is addressed less and less as the noise around her grows more organized. That trajectory, more than any single cry, is what the hollowness hearing is detecting, and it is a designed trajectory, not a failure of feeling.

## The Silent Centre: A Girl Who Makes No Sound at All

Every account of the scene's noise must end at its centre, where there is no noise. Juliet lies through the loudest scene in the play without a syllable, and the silence is not an absence in the design but its load-bearing element. Consider what the audience knows and hears simultaneously: the body at the centre of the chorus is warm, breathing, and under a timed enchantment of the Friar's own manufacture, due to wake in roughly two days, and the wailing that fills the chamber is addressed to a girl who is, in the fiction's own terms, the only person in the play who will ever be in a position to have heard her own funeral lament and lived. She cannot hear it; the potion has seen to that. But the audience hears it on her behalf, and the double hearing, grief performed at full volume around a sleeper who will survive it, is the scene's deepest acoustic effect. The lament is a rehearsal the protagonist sleeps through, for a funeral the play will later stage in earnest with almost no lament at all.

The silence also corrects a lazy assumption about where the scene's power sits. It is tempting to credit the pattern, the refrains and drumbeats, with the scene's effect on an audience, but pattern around a genuinely dead girl would produce a simpler and more comfortable scene, a household rite correctly performed. What makes the chamber scene ache is the mismatch between the noise and its object: maximum sound, zero reception. Every cry lands nowhere. The Nurse's ladder of names was the miniature of this; the full lament is the same figure at scale, a roomful of voices discovering, without knowing it, that address itself has failed. Shakespeare had already run the experiment once in the act, in Juliet's soliloquy, where a voice in terror addressed gods, parents, and the Friar and received no answer either. The two scenes are acoustic rhymes: one girl alone making all the sound and hearing none returned, then the same girl making none while a household makes it all around her. Act Four is built on that exchange of positions, and the mourning scene is unintelligible, as sound, without the soliloquy before it.

It follows that the scene's famous question, whether the grief is moving or hollow, may be miscast. Grief addressed to someone who cannot hear it is neither: it is exposed, shown in the act of being produced, its machinery audible because its purpose has been quietly removed. A machine heard running with its load disconnected makes a different sound from the same machine at work, thinner, faster, more evidently mechanical, and the chamber scene's laments have exactly that quality for an audience in possession of the secret. The hollowness is the sound of the mechanism unloaded. The movingness is the sound of the mechanism itself, the real skill and real love with which these people operate it. Both are present in every line, and the scene's construction ensures that no listener can have either one without the other.

## Instruments That Never Play: Peter, the Musicians, and the Bathos

The mourners leave, the body is borne away, and the scene does something no lament can survive unchanged: it stays in the room. The First Musician's opening remark is a masterpiece of deflation precisely because it is practical: "Faith, we may put up our pipes and be gone" (4.5). In one flat prose sentence the instruments that the wedding hired and the funeral silenced are packed away, and the scene's whole economy of sound is inverted. Verse has been about sound that means too much; prose begins with sound being put back in its box. The Nurse, exiting, gives the musicians the scene's last shaped utterance, a couplet's worth of verse about putting up and pity: the case is pitiful, she says, and the First Musician, alone with his fellows, picks the word up in its other sense, "Ay, by my troth, the case may be amended" (4.5). The pun is the hinge of the scene's acoustics. Case means the instrument's box and the household's plight at once, and in passing from the Nurse's mouth to the musician's it changes meaning without changing sound, which is exactly the operation the entire scene has been performing in reverse: the lament kept one sound, woe, and changed its meaning from voice to voice; the prose keeps one word, case, and splits its meaning in two. The scene's last rhyme is replaced by its first quibble, and the quibble is a small exact model of the lament, pattern surviving the fall from verse into prose.

Peter then enters with a demand that completes the scene's inquiry into what music is for. He wants a tune, by name: Heart's Ease, and he wants it as a remedy, on the stated ground that his own heart is playing a grief tune of its own and needs a merry counterweight. The request's logic is medical, and it is also the lament's logic stated without dignity: patterned sound, applied to feeling, to alter it. The mourners used pattern to express grief; Peter uses pattern to treat it, and the nakedness of the transaction is what makes the moment funny and faintly shocking at once. A tune is ordered in a house of mourning the way a physic would be ordered, by name, from tradesmen, with an expectation of effect. The musicians refuse, and their refusal is the scene's most quietly important sound event: no note is played. The instruments that Paris brought through the dark to serenade a bride are never heard in the act at all, and the wedding's music, the act's great expected sound, simply does not occur. What the scene offers instead of played music is talked music, music as a topic, and the substitution is the bathos in its purest form: the sublime of the lament succeeded not by silence, which would be tragic, but by shop talk, which is comic, and the shop talk is about the very art the sublime displaced.

Peter presses the point by singing, or quoting, the scene's only actual song. "When griping grief the heart doth wound, / And doleful dumps the mind oppress, / Then music with her silver sound" (4.5): the lines are the opening of a known song, a lyric about grief and its musical cure, and Peter gets three lines out before the argument swallows the performance. The fragment matters out of proportion to its length. It is the only moment in the scene where music stops being discussed and starts, briefly, to happen, and it happens in the mouth of a servant, as a citation, inside a quarrel about wages. Everything the scene has kept at bay, performed song, arrives in its lowest available form, quoted, partial, and instrumentalized as a debating point, a text Peter deploys to win an argument about what musicians are for. He then puts the song's key phrase to each musician in turn like a catechism: why silver sound, Simon Catling; why silver sound, Hugh Rebeck; why silver sound, James Soundpost. The catechism form is the lament's antiphony returned as farce. Mourners took up one another's phrases to build a chorus; Peter takes up his own phrase and deals it round a panel of tradesmen, collecting answers, and the answers descend the same ladder the whole scene has descended: the first musician gives the poetic answer, silver has a sweet sound; the second gives the professional answer, musicians sound for silver; the third, the singer, gives no answer at all, and Peter supplies the punchline himself, that musicians have no gold for sounding. Pattern, again, is doing all the work. The comedy is not an escape from the scene's method but the method itself, run at prose speed, on mercenary material.

The quarrel's insult exchange deserves its beat of attention before the rhythm takes over, because it shows the scene's two sound worlds colliding in vocabulary. Peter, losing the argument about the tune, reaches for the feud's own idiom and threatens the First Musician with a name and a dagger: the man is a serving-creature, a nobody, and the dagger will be laid on his pate if the impudence continues. It is the language of the street brawl, demotion by naming, violence as the full stop at the end of a failed sentence, shrunk to a servant's spat over a song. The musicians answer without heat, and Peter's dagger, drawn and brandished to no effect, becomes the scene's last weapon and its most harmless, a prop in a quarrel about music. The feud's sound, the insult that in Act One and Act Three summoned swords in earnest, here summons only a punchline, and the demotion of that sound is the act's final acoustic judgment: in the Capulet house on this morning, even violence has been reduced to talk about talk, threat as patter, the sword's music gone the way of the pipes, unplayed.

### Why do the musicians refuse to play, when playing is what they were hired for?

The musicians refuse because the occasion that hired their music has been cancelled by the death, and playing without an occasion would convert their art into noise and their fee into a gift. Their contract, as they understand it, was with a wedding: serenade the bride, play the feast, be paid.

A funeral has supervened that nobody has hired them to score, and their professional code, expounded with great clarity in the haggle that follows, is that sound is labour and labour is priced. The refusal also protects something subtler, the boundary of their trade. Peter asks for music as a private medicine, a merry tune to comfort one servant's heart, and the musicians decline to let their art be used as a household remedy dispensed free at the back door of a tragedy. Their dignity is real, however comic its expression. In a scene where grief has been produced by amateurs from a shared stock of phrases, the professionals alone insist that their sound is not a common stock, and the insistence, heard aright, is the lament's mirror image: the family gives its cries away freely because they cost nothing, and the musicians charge for their notes because notes are all they have.

The haggle's rhythm deserves a final hearing of its own. Peter and the musicians trade short prose units, question and answer, insult and counter, in exchanges that keep threatening to become the thing they are discussing: patter, the music of the music hall that does not yet exist. Peter draws his dagger, too late and to no purpose, in the scene's last spasm of the feud's idiom, violence as punctuation, and the musicians are unmoved; their exit line abandons even the quarrel. They will go in, they decide, tarry for the mourners, and stay dinner (4.5). It is the flattest sentence in the act, and its flatness is earned. The act began with a whisper in a cell and rose through bustle, hallucination, uproar, and choral lament to the highest pitch of formal grief the play ever reaches, and it exits on appetite, on three men calculating that a funeral, like a wedding, ends with a meal for those who worked it. The world, the line says in prose, goes on being hungry inside other people's catastrophes, and any honest soundscape of mourning has to include the sound of that hunger. The bathos is not a lapse in the scene's tone. It is the scene's bass note, the one that makes the treble of the lament audible as treble, and productions that cut the musicians to protect the lament's dignity invariably discover that they have protected it out of existence: unironic, unbathos'd, the pattern curdles into the very hollowness its defenders deny.

## Moving or Hollow? The Critics on the Lament, the Musicians, and the Sound

The disagreement the scene provokes is old, structured, and worth stating through its strongest advocates rather than its weakest. Harry Levin, in his account of form and formality in the play, gives the defence its most rigorous shape: the tragedy is built on formal patterning at every level, set speeches answering set speeches, ritual answering ritual, and the lament of 4.5 is the pattern system at its most exposed, a formal lamentation in which the mourners' convergence on shared formulas is not a failure of invention but the design itself, grief stylized because the play's whole method is stylization. On Levin's reading, to find the scene hollow is to mistake the play's native idiom for an accident, like finding a fugue repetitive. The pattern is the content, and the antiphonal participles, the circulating epithets, and the burden of woeful day are its voices entering on cue.

Susan Snyder, reading the play through its comic matrix, accepts the pattern and relocates its meaning. In The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies, the comic materials of the early acts are not discarded when the tragedy darkens but persist inside it, transformed, and the chamber scene is a prime exhibit: the most ritualized grief in the play is followed, without a scene break, by clowns haggling over a tune, because in this play's construction the comic and the tragic are not alternating concessions but a single fabric under changing light. On Snyder's account the musicians do not relieve the lament and do not parody it; they reveal that the lament was always made of the same stuff as their patter, patterned utterance produced by people performing a social rite, and the recognition is meant to unsettle rather than amuse. Where Levin hears a fugue, Snyder hears a mechanism that tragedy and comedy share, and the difference between the two hearings is the whole disagreement in miniature.

The editors stand behind both critics with the material facts. Weis's Arden Third Series text, the edition quoted throughout this article, prints the scene whole from the fuller early text: the full sequence of laments, the consolation, and the complete musicians' exchange, with the instrument jokes glossed into legibility, Catling from the catgut of violin strings, Rebeck from the rebec the second musician carries, Soundpost from the small wooden post inside a viol that carries its sound. The editorial tradition has not always been so hospitable. Performance texts across the centuries cut the musicians freely and sometimes trimmed the laments themselves, treating the scene's second half as expendable padding after the emotional climax, and the cutting tradition is itself a critical verdict in action: generations of practitioners heard the haggle as an embarrassment to be removed and the pattern, shorn of its bass note, then struck other generations as the hollow thing the cutters had feared. The text's fullness and the stage's scissors have been arguing about this scene for as long as it has been played, and the argument is data: a scene that can be heard both ways, at full length, by professionals, is a scene built to be heard both ways.

Performance bears the editors out in a way print cannot. Played whole, with the lament allowed its full pattern and the musicians allowed their full haggle, the scene reliably produces the double response its construction predicts: audiences are moved by the chorus and then, in the prose, hear themselves being moved, the laughter at Peter arriving with a small shock of self-recognition in it. Played cut, with the haggle trimmed to a tasteful exit, the lament curdles within minutes into the monotone its detractors describe, pattern without contrast registering as rant. The scene is thus an unusually pure case of a theatrical structure that exists only at full length, like a chord that becomes a different chord when one note is removed. The scissors do not shorten the scene's meaning; they abolish it, and the long performance tradition of cutting it stands as the largest body of evidence for the verdict this article defends, since the hollowness reliably appears exactly where the dissonance has been removed.

Bruce R. Smith's acoustic history supplies the frame that lets the disagreement be settled rather than merely staged. In the world Smith reconstructs, sound was social before it was expressive: bells apportioned the day, cries advertised wares and losses, waits and hired musicians marked the thresholds of weddings and funerals with sound that announced an event to a community, and private feeling conducted itself, when it became public, through inherited forms precisely because the forms were the public medium. Within that world, the question moving or hollow, posed as a question about the sincerity of individual mourners, is an anachronism. Communal lament was a technology for distributing an unbearable noise across many throats, and its patterning was its function, as the patterning of a bell's peal is its function. The chamber scene stages that technology at work and then, in the musicians, stages its price list, and the two stagings belong to one inquiry.

The adjudication follows from the frame. Levin is right about the design and wrong only in stopping there: the pattern is the content, but the scene knows it, and builds the knowledge in, in Capulet's tied tongue, in his self-hearing inventory, in the Friar's exemption from the chorus he conducts. Snyder is right about the continuity and wrong only in emphasis: the musicians do not expose the lament as mechanism, because the lament has already exposed itself; they extend its inquiry downward, to the people for whom patterned sound is not expression but rent. The hollowness, where it genuinely exists, is local and characterized: it is in Paris, whose perfect lament has no private sound in it, and in the Friar, whose perfect consolation has no grief in it, and the scene places those two polished emptinesses inside a chorus of noisier, messier, unmistakably felt grief to make the measurement possible. The verdict on the soundscape is therefore double and precise. As ritual sound, the mourning of Act Four is moving, because pattern is how this world grieves in company, and the pattern here is executed with a skill that keeps revealing love inside formula. As individual expression, it is hollow exactly where it is meant to be, in the mouths of the two men performing offices, and the bathos of the musicians is the scene's own device for telling the audience so, a drop in register that recalibrates the ear and lets the whole chord be heard at once.


## A Ladder of Volumes: The Act Heard Whole

Step back from the chamber and the act's acoustics describe a single long shape, a ladder of volumes climbed and then descended, and the shape is worth tracing rung by rung because it explains why the mourning lands as it does. The first rung is the cell's whisper, speech designed not to travel. The second is the kitchen's shout, speech designed to travel everywhere at once, orders flung across a great house to cooks, servants, and a daughter. Inside that shout one small sound deserves its own notice: laughter. Capulet in 4.2 laughs at the servingman's confusion over the guest list and at his own jest, a master briefly delighted with his household's machinery, and the laugh is the last unshadowed sound the act permits anyone. It lasts a line. After it, the act's noises darken in sequence, a girl's whispered terror, a father's orders shouted at servants who are dressing a bride for a funeral neither knows about, a chorus of wailing, a haggle. The laugh's placement is the act's quietest cruelty: joy, in this act, is a sound effect of the trap being set, audible only to an audience that knows what the laughter is building. The third rung is a paradox, a voice alone raising imaginary multitudes, Juliet's soliloquy, where the volume is zero and the described noise is the loudest in the act, shrieking mandrakes, rattling bones, a vault full of unquiet dead. The fourth is the house at work in the dark, cockcrow, curfew bell, commands, scolding, the ordinary sublime of a rich family's logistics. The fifth is the lament, public noise at maximum organization. The sixth is prose at the noise floor, men discussing dinner. No other act in the play is so thoroughly organized as a sequence of listening experiences, and the organization is not decorative: the plot of Act Four is a plot about a sound that will be misread. A girl takes a potion so that her silence will be mistaken for death, and the act spends its whole length teaching the audience how this household makes and hears noise, so that when the misreading comes, the audience can measure exactly how loud a mistake can be.

Two smaller sound motifs stitch the ladder together. The first is laughter, which the act permits itself exactly where the audience least wants it. Capulet in 4.2, swollen with recovered paternal authority, laughs at his own jest about loggerheads and calls for the wedding a day early, and his mirth is the sound of the trap being sprung by its victim's father. The second motif is the bell. The curfew bell of 4.4 is a real civic sound, heard by everyone on stage and everyone in the theatre, marking three in the morning; the melancholy bells of Capulet's inventory speech are imagined ones, wedding instruments converted in a figure of speech. Between the real bell that opens the wedding day and the imagined bells that close its mourning, the act measures the distance between the city's time and the family's time: the city rings its hours regardless, while the household's instruments change meaning in a single morning. Bells in the acoustic world Bruce Smith describes were the loudest public voice a community had, the sound of collective time itself, and the play's quiet joke is that the Capulets' catastrophe, for all its volume, never reaches bell scale. It stays indoors, in a chamber, priced in a servant's quarrel, while the curfew bell rings on over the city, indifferent. The mourning is enormous to its makers and inaudible to its world, and the act's soundscape keeps both facts in the ear at once.

Hearing the act whole also settles a question of proportion that single-scene readings often fumble: the lament is not the act's climax of noise but its climax of organization. The kitchen scenes are louder in implication, dozens of servants, a feast for a city's worth of guests, orders bouncing off stone; the soliloquy is louder in imagination. What 4.5 adds is not volume but form, noise given a score, and the distinction is the article's whole subject in one sentence. Grief enters the act as the moment noise submits to pattern, and the musicians exit it as the moment pattern dissolves back into noise, appetite, and talk. Between those two thresholds the play conducts its inquiry, and everything outside them, the whisper, the shout, the hallucinated shriek, is the control group against which the inquiry's results can be read.

## Echoes Forward: The Tomb Hears What the Chamber Taught

Act Four's soundscape is also a training programme for Act Five, and the training pays off in negative space. When the real deaths come, the play withholds almost everything the chamber scene supplied. Balthasar brings Romeo the news of the real death in a report, not a lament, and Romeo's answer is the anti-lament in five words of defiance, no refrain, no chorus, no answering voice: the pattern of 4.5 has been spent on a rehearsal, and the event itself must make do with fragments. At the tomb, Paris performs the play's last private mourning, strewing flowers and weeping in a speech pitched for no hearer, grief as a solo with the volume turned down, and it is interrupted not by musicians but by a fight: noise in Act Five is violence, where noise in Act Four was rite. The tomb scene thereafter runs on testimony, explanation, and official record, voices accounting rather than crying, and the Prince closes the play in the flattened register of a report. The audience that has heard 4.5 knows what is missing, and the missingness is the meaning: Verona's great machine for communal grief was used up, at full power, on a girl who was not dead, and when the deaths are real the machine does not start again. The series account of the play's mourning rituals takes that distribution as its subject; as sound, the point is simpler and colder. The loudest grief in the play is lavished on the false death, and the true ones get paperwork.

There is one more echo, quieter and worse. The musicians do not return. No hired sound attends the real funeral, no pipes are put up at the tomb, no Peter asks for a tune to comfort anybody, and the absence completes the bathos argument from the other side. In the chamber scene, the tradesmen's presence proved the world continued; in the tomb scene, the world's absence, its cooks and fiddlers gone home to dinner and forgotten, proves the catastrophe has passed beyond the household's management entirely. Act Four's dissonance, lament above and haggle below, was the sound of a household still in control of its disaster, still able to staff it, feed it, and score it. Act Five's thinner sound, solos and statements and a prince's summary, is the sound of a disaster that has outgrown staffing. The ear trained by Act Four hears the difference without being told, which is what the training was for.

The Friar's own voice completes the pattern. In Act Four his couplets were the most finished sound in the chamber, consolation as cadence; in Act Five he returns with the play's longest single speech, the narrative of the whole plot delivered to the Prince and the families in a flattened, sequential register, names, dates, doses, hours, a deposition rather than an utterance of feeling. The man who scored the false funeral with balanced verse now reads the true catastrophe like a clerk entering evidence, and the demotion of his music is the measure of what has happened to every voice the mourning trained. Where the chamber scene turned statement into song, the tomb scene turns song back into statement, and the Friar, its conductor in both directions, ends the play's acoustic story as he began the mourning: the most regular voice in the room, and the one with the least grief in it.

## What the Dissonance Achieves

The dissonance, then, achieves three things, and they can be stated as plainly as the verdict they support. First, it makes the lament audible as music. A pattern unbroken for a hundred lines stops being heard as pattern; it becomes weather. The musicians break the pattern at exactly the point of satiation, and the break retroactively organizes everything before it, letting the audience hear, in memory, that what it took for spontaneous overflow was scored. Second, it prices the grief. The lament costs the family nothing but breath; the musicians introduce money into the scene's acoustics, and the introduction reframes the family's abundance of feeling as an abundance of a household that can afford feeling, that keeps a choir of mourners the way it keeps cooks. Grief in this house is partly a function of staffing, and the scene is honest enough to say so in the hearing of the corpse. Third, it preserves the scene from the single hearing that would kill it. Pure lament, unrelieved, asks to be judged as sincerity and dies under the question; lament followed by haggle asks to be judged as a soundscape, a whole social world making its noises at a death, and under that question it flourishes. The scene's dissonance is its defence mechanism and its thesis at once: mourning is what a household sounds like, all of it, the wailing and the wage dispute and the dinner, and a play that wants the truth of mourning has to record the whole band of frequencies.

That is why the cliche this series exists to correct, the notion of Act Four as a soft hinge between the balcony and the tomb, collapses the moment the act is listened to rather than summarized. Restoring its sound restores its argument. The mourning of Act Four is the play's fullest statement of how Verona processes loss: communally, formally, expensively, and with the machinery showing, and the Friar's plot depends on that processing being exactly as loud and exactly as hollow as it is, a performance convincing enough to free a girl and empty enough to leave her, in the end, to a silence no lament will ever fill again.

## The Serenade That Never Sounds: The Wedding's Expected Music

One sound haunts the mourning scene by its absence, and it arrived at the door before dawn. Paris comes to the Capulet house in the small hours with musicians in attendance, because a bridegroom of his standing wakes a bride with music: the morning serenade, the aubade in reverse, is part of the ceremony's expected soundtrack, as scripted as the feast and the church. The musicians who haggle at the scene's end are therefore not an intrusion from another play or a bit of groundling fodder bolted on. They are the wedding's own band, present from the scene's first minutes, waiting in the chamber's edge of the action with their instruments ready, hired to make the sound of a marriage and standing idle through a funeral. Their silence during the lament is not an oversight of staging. It is the scene's most eloquent stage direction: the professionals of sound, the only people in the room whose business is music, contribute nothing to the most musical scene in the play, because the music they were hired for has no occasion left. The family sings its own grief, amateur and patterned; the band keeps its counsel and its pipes cased.

Hearing the scene with the expected serenade in mind changes its proportions. The lament can then be heard as the wedding music's substitute, the sound the household produces when the hired sound fails to materialize: where the morning was to open with instruments saluting a bride, it opens with a servant calling her names, and the chorus that assembles is the serenade's stand-in, voices doing the work strings were paid to do. The substitution had a social grammar that the play's first audiences would have read at once. Great households and city companies alike kept music on retainer for thresholds, weddings, funerals, feasts, mayoral processions, because a threshold crossed in silence announced poverty or disgrace; the waits and household bands of the period were, in effect, a family's public voice, the sound by which its standing travelled down a street. A Capulet wedding morning without music would have been a contradiction in terms, and the play exploits the contradiction: the band is present, the standing is intact, the instruments are cased, and the family's public voice has to be improvised out of its women, its master, and its priest, in that order. The lament's amateur patterning, so often heard as a flaw in the grief, is on this hearing its most realistic feature. It is the sound of insiders doing, badly and movingly, a job that existed precisely because insiders were never meant to do it. The substitution also explains the musicians' otherwise puzzling prominence at the scene's close. They are not a coda. They are a debt coming due: the scene owes the audience the wedding band's performance, and it pays the debt in talk about performance instead, the haggle standing where the serenade should have stood, prose patter in place of the pipes. Every element of the expected soundtrack is present in the scene, and every element arrives transformed: the serenade becomes a lament, the lament becomes a negotiation, and the instruments, the only honest objects in the room, stay in their cases and say nothing at all.

## Counting the Cries: Repetition by the Numbers

The lament's patterning can be counted, and the counts, crude as any counting of poetry must be, expose the design with unusual clarity. Take the Nurse's set speech, the scene's refrain engine. In six lines she strikes the word day ten times: three times in a bare volley of exclamation, twice inside her closing burden, and the rest distributed through the lines between, while the word woeful tolls six times across the same six lines, three of them in a single breath. No other speech in the scene repeats at anything like this density, and the density is the characterization. The Nurse's grief has the fewest resources and the deepest claim, and repetition is how a speaker with a small stock makes a large sound: say the same words until the saying becomes the scale.

Lady Capulet's set speech counts differently. Her governing repetition is the word one: one child, one poor and loving child, one thing to rejoice and solace in, the numeral struck four times in two lines, grief reckoned as arithmetic. Paris counts in participles, five blows in one line, and Capulet, answering, counts in the same coin, five more. The Friar counts in antitheses, heaven and earth, part and part, tears and merriment, every proposition twinned. Each mourner, that is, has a private arithmetic, a preferred unit of repetition, and the units are fingerprints: the servant repeats a phrase, the mother repeats a number, the bridegroom and the father repeat a rhythm, the priest repeats a structure. The choir effect described earlier is real, but it is a choir of distinguishable voices whose parts can be separated by their counters, and the separation is the answer to listeners who hear only machinery in the scene. Machinery does not vary its repetitions by the soul of the operator. This scene does, measurably, voice by voice, and the variation inside the pattern is the strongest single piece of evidence that the pattern is a medium for feeling rather than a substitute for it.

The counts also locate the scene's hollow spot with numerical precision. Paris's five participles and Capulet's answering five make a perfect exchange, ten blows, evenly matched, and the perfection is the tell: real antiphony drifts, overlaps, mistimes; this exchange is machined. Where the Nurse's repetitions pile up beyond symmetry, ten days in six lines, an excess no designer would balance, Paris and Capulet achieve an exact formal courtesy, grief as a well-played set of tennis. The scene lets a listener hear, in the numbers themselves, the difference between repetition as overflow and repetition as etiquette, and it needs no commentary to make the point, only an ear willing to count.

One more count belongs to the mother. Lady Capulet's set speech opens by spending four epithets in a single line, accursed, unhappy, wretched, hateful, a pile-up of adjectives that spends the common stock faster than any other mourner dares, as if grief could be outbid. The pile is her version of the Nurse's refrain: where the servant repeats one word until it becomes a burden, the mistress stacks synonyms until the stack itself becomes the statement, quantity doing the work that duration does for the Nurse. Heard together, the two techniques bracket the scene's whole method, repetition across time and repetition across the dictionary, and both are ways of making a sound too large for the feeling any single word could hold. The lament's speakers, for all their shared material, never stop competing in volume of pattern, and the competition is itself a kind of love: nobody in that room is willing to be out-grieved.

### What sound was the wedding morning supposed to begin with, and what begins it instead?

The wedding morning was supposed to begin with hired music: Paris arrives before daybreak with musicians in attendance, the bridegroom's serenade being part of the ceremony's expected sound, and the bride was to be woken, dressed, and conducted to church inside a frame of professional music.

What begins it instead is a servant's voice climbing a ladder of pet names outside a door, then a cry of help, then a household teaching itself to wail. The substitution governs the whole scene's acoustics. Every formal sound the chamber produces, the antiphons, the refrain, the drumbeat of participles, is amateur work covering for the professionals standing silent at the edge of the room, and the professionals' eventual emergence into prose, unplayed pipes in hand, merely states openly what the lament had been concealing: on this morning, all the music is improvised, and the only scored sound in the house is the one nobody performs.
