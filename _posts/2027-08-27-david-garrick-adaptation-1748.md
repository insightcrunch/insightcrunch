David Garrick's adaptation of Romeo and Juliet is the most consequential act of editing in the play's stage history, and the verdict of the documentary record is sharper than the familiar story allows. The familiar story credits Garrick with a pure restoration: Shakespeare driven off the stage by Thomas Otway's Roman rewrite, Shakespeare rescued by the actor-manager of Drury Lane in 1748, Verona recovered, the text made whole. The record shows something more interesting and less clean. Garrick restored the repertory, not the text. He made Shakespeare's tragedy, in Shakespeare's city, with Shakespeare's plot and the greater part of Shakespeare's verse, the standard possession of the English stage for ninety-seven years, and that achievement is real. But the version through which three generations knew the play was a hybrid built on Theophilus Cibber's scaffold, carrying Otway's tomb device, Garrick's own cuts, Garrick's own pathos, and a funeral procession invented in the heat of a commercial war between two theatres. Restoration and adaptation were not two phases of Garrick's work on the play. They were the same gesture, and the article that follows separates what he restored from what he kept changing, change by change, until the ledger balances.

![David Garrick's adaptation of Romeo and Juliet on the eighteenth-century stage - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The stakes are not antiquarian. For roughly a century, to see Romeo and Juliet was to see Garrick's Romeo and Juliet, and to read the play in a Garrick-derived acting edition was a common experience well into the Victorian period. Berg's study of the play's stage fortunes, quoted in Banner's analysis, calls Garrick's script the most enduringly successful production of the play, and the superlative is earned by the simplest evidence: no other single version of the tragedy, before or since, has held a national stage as its standard text for anything like ninety-seven years while simultaneously colonizing the printed editions through which readers met the work. The lovers that the eighteenth century wept over met in a Verona from which Rosaline had been erased. Their final meeting in the tomb lasted through a long waking conversation that Shakespeare never wrote. Between the fourth and fifth acts, a procession of friars, choristers, nuns and mourners carried the heroine to the Capulet monument while a dirge composed for the occasion filled the theatre. Anyone who wants to understand what the play meant to the audiences who canonized it has to understand the version Garrick built, because that version, and not the quarto text, was the play for the age that fixed its reputation.

A preliminary correction governs everything that follows, because the date in the cliché does double duty. "Garrick's adaptation of 1748" names two different objects fused by fame. The first is a production: the Drury Lane staging of the 1748-49 season, Barry and Cibber in the lovers' parts, Garrick managing, the text substantially the arrangement Theophilus Cibber had prepared in 1744. The second is a document: the printed text of 1750, issued under Garrick's name with his alterations and his additional scene, in which the version's signature operations, the permanence of the Rosaline erasure, the enlarged death scene, the procession, stand fully formed. The adaptation as a public fact dates from the production; the adaptation as an authored text dates from the revision two years later, forced into being by the rivalry that made Garrick play Romeo himself. Keeping the two objects separate is not pedantry. It is the difference between repeating Garrick's publicity, which dated the rescue to the first triumph, and following the documents, which show the rescue assembled in stages, by several hands, under commercial pressure. The brief for this article carries the standard date, and the standard date stands, with the two-stage structure of the thing it names made explicit.

## What Garrick Claimed to Restore, and What the Record Shows

Garrick's public claim was restoration, and he made it in the language of title pages. When his version reached print at the end of 1750, the title page announced "Romeo and Juliet. By Shakespear. With Alterations, and an Additional Scene: As it is Performed at the Theatre-Royal in Drury-Lane." Every word of that formula works. The play is "By Shakespear", the author's name promoted to the largest claim on the page. The alterations are admitted but subordinated, a small plural noun between the author's name and the theatre's. The one addition considered worth advertising, the "Additional Scene", is the funeral procession, spectacle rather than poetry. The formula is honest in its proportions and evasive in its silences: nothing on the page mentions Cibber, whose altered text stood underneath the whole enterprise, and nothing mentions Otway, from whom the version's most famous scene descended.

The billing matched the manager's larger campaign. Elaine M. McGirr, in the study "'What's in a Name?': Romeo and Juliet and the Cibber Brand" published in the journal Shakespeare in 2017, shows that Garrick chose the play as a star vehicle for Susannah Cibber and as a stage in his own campaign of "restoration of Shakespeare from the taint of Cibberian adaptation", following his celebrated Richard III of the early 1740s and his King John of 1745, both mounted in direct competition with Cibber versions. Restoration, in other words, was Garrick's brand before it was his method. He sold himself as the man who gave Shakespeare back to the English stage, and the claim was commercially potent because it contained a large measure of truth. Measured against what had held the stage since 1679, his Verona was recognizably Shakespeare's tragedy in a way that Otway's Rome, with its senators and its Roman politics, had never tried to be.

Yet the textual record underneath the brand is layered. Olive Baldwin and Thelma Wilson's study of the funeral procession, examining the playbills and editions of the period, concludes that when Garrick staged the play at Drury Lane in the 1748-49 season, with Spranger Barry and Susannah Cibber as the lovers, "he almost certainly used Theophilus Cibber's version, as is claimed on the title-page of Cibber's 1748 edition." Cibber's claim on his own title page was naturally self-interested, but the surviving evidence has not displaced it, and George Winchester Stone Jr., the editor of The London Stage and Garrick's biographer, built the standard documentary account of the production on records that treat the first Garrick staging as a mounting of the play in the form Cibber had prepared in 1744. Garrick's distinct adaptation, the text that can properly be called his, belongs to the autumn of 1750, when he took the part of Romeo himself for the first time and published his alterations before the year ended.

### Did Garrick restore Shakespeare, or only replace Otway?

Garrick replaced Otway outright and restored Shakespeare in bulk: Verona, the families, the scene order and most of the verse returned. The text as written did not. Rosaline, the full wordplay, the sexual candour and Shakespeare's ending stayed altered in every Garrick printing, so the recovery was real but partial, achieved by continuing adaptation.

The distinction is between the play's public form and its letter. What returned in 1748 was Verona, the feud of the two households, Shakespeare's sequence of scenes and most of his lines. What never returned, in any Garrick printing, was Rosaline, the play's wordplay at full strength, its sexual candour, or its ending. The accurate word for the Garrick version is neither restoration nor travesty but a working compromise: Shakespeare's tragedy re-engineered by the century's greatest actor-manager to hold a stage whose taste he knew better than any man alive, and branded, with deliberate skill, as a rescue. The compromise, not any theory of deception, explains the version's longevity. Garrick was not cheating his audience of a Shakespeare they demanded whole. His audience's Shakespeare was already a playwright improved by his interpreters, in the tradition of Tate's happy-ending Lear and the musical Macbeth, and Garrick's alterations were designed to remove what the age considered the play's defects: the punning it called jingle and quibble, the indecency it blushed at, the supposedly cold perfection of a hero whose heart changed tenants between the first act and the second. Where Garrick misleads posterity is in the word "restoration" itself, which promises an end to alteration. The printings tell the truer story. His text appeared as an acting version, was revised across his career, and was then adopted, with his deathbed conversation intact, by editions of 1794, 1814, 1819 and 1874, as the Library of Congress's survey of its Garrick holdings records. For more than a century the altered text did not merely serve the stage. It competed with Shakespeare's text in print, and for long stretches it won.

## The Rome Garrick Inherited, and the Haymarket Version Beneath His Own

To see what Garrick restored, the baseline has to be named. From 1679 the stage Romeo and Juliet was, in substance, Thomas Otway's The History and Fall of Caius Marius, a Roman tragedy into which Otway had poured large portions of Shakespeare's play, renaming the lovers Marius and Lavinia, moving the feud to the politics of republican Rome, and reworking the whole into the idiom of Restoration heroic tragedy. Otway's version was no stopgap. It held the stage for over half a century, with performances recorded as late as 1735, a run that testifies to how completely the age preferred its Shakespeare digested into another writer's design. The companion study of [Otway's Roman rewrite of the lovers' story, Caius Marius](/2027/08/20/otway-caius-marius-roman-rewrite/) gives that strange, durable play its full hearing. One Otway device matters here above all others, because every later version inherited it: in Otway's closing movement, Lavinia wakes from her deathlike sleep before Marius dies of his poison, and the lovers learn the mistake together. The stroke descends from the older Italian novella tradition behind the story, and it converts Shakespeare's ending, in which the lovers die in ignorance of each other, into a recognition scene of excruciating mutual knowledge.

Before Otway, the record holds one strange flare of the play itself. In 1662 Davenant's company revived the tragedy, and James Howard's alteration turned it into a tragi-comedy that pursued different conclusions on alternating nights, the lovers dying on one night and surviving on another, so that both tastes in the house could be served in rotation. Samuel Pepys, who saw the first performance since the Restoration, left the age's most quoted review of the experiment in his diary: the piece was "the play of itself the worst that ever I heard in my life, and the worst acted that ever I saw these people do". The experiment did not take. John Dover Wilson, editing the play for Cambridge in 1961, calculated that no rendition of Shakespeare's original was performed for over eighty years after the 1660s, and he dismissed what filled the silence as a strange hotch-potch of garbled Shakespeare matter and new invention. The eighty years are the fact Garrick's publicity fed on: whatever he restored the play from, Rome or the Cibbers or neglect, he restored it from an absence long enough that three generations of playgoers had never seen Shakespeare's Verona at all.

The stage history between Otway and Garrick runs through the Cibber family, and the names are a small lesson in how the eighteenth-century theatre worked. Theophilus Cibber, son of Colley Cibber and estranged husband of Susannah, brought Shakespeare's play back to the London stage in his own altered version at the Haymarket Theatre on 11 September 1744, playing a Romeo in his forties opposite his fourteen-year-old daughter Jenny. His alterations, as the Yale Shakespeare editors summarized them in 1917, were to retain Otway's feature of the waking Juliet and to omit the references to Rosaline. Cibber's venture was closed under pressure from the patent theatres, but the version survived him as a going concern. He revived it in 1748 precisely to compete with Garrick's Drury Lane production, in which Theophilus's own estranged wife played Juliet, and he published his text that year with an appended Serio-Comic Apology praising his daughter, condemning Susannah, and slighting William Warburton's edition of Shakespeare. The 1748 editions thus present a bibliographical brawl: Cibber in print claiming that Garrick's staging was Cibber's text, Garrick in the theatre building a triumph on a vehicle prepared by the rival he was publicly dedicated to displacing.

This inheritance matters for every judgement that follows, because it redistributes the credit and the blame that the cliché assigns to Garrick alone. The two most famous strokes of "Garrick's adaptation", the erased Rosaline and the waking Juliet, stand in Cibber's 1744 version before they stand in any Garrick document, on the Yale editors' account. Baldwin and Wilson, weighing the same evidence, credit the cutting of the Rosaline references specifically to Garrick's 1750 adaptation of Cibber's version, and the lengthening of the death scene to the same revision. The responsible conclusion, and the one this article adopts, is that the omissions and the device are Cibberian in origin and Garrickian in permanence: whoever first struck Rosaline from the acting text, it was Garrick's print that fixed the striking for a century, and Garrick who expanded the inherited waking scene into the seventy-line pathos machine that audiences refused to surrender. Attribution in the theatre belongs less to the first hand than to the hand that makes the change unrepealable, and by that measure the changes are Garrick's, with the caveat permanently attached.

The Cibber inheritance arrived wrapped in scandal, and the scandal shaped how the versions competed. Theophilus and Susannah Cibber's marriage had ended in 1739 in a public trial for criminal conversation, a suit Theophilus himself had engineered against his wife's lover, as McGirr's study recounts, and the wreckage of the family was common knowledge in every playhouse in London. When Theophilus revived his Romeo and Juliet in 1748 against Garrick's production, the contest was therefore legible to its first audiences as something more than repertory scheduling: the discarded husband's text, played by the discarded husband and his daughter, against the same text burnished by the era's rising manager and played by the wife who had left him. Cibber's printed edition of that year, with its Serio-Comic Apology praising Jenny, condemning Susannah and sneering at Warburton's Shakespeare, tried to fight the battle in the bookstalls, claiming the Drury Lane staging as his property. Garrick never answered in kind. He answered with receipts, and then, in 1750, with a printed text of his own that quietly dropped the Cibber name from the play's future while keeping the Cibber machinery inside it.


## 1748: The Production That Made the Play a Fortune

The Garrick Romeo and Juliet opened at Drury Lane at the end of November 1748, with the bills by 1 December already advertising its ornaments. Two facts about the casting overturn the picture most accounts leave. Garrick did not play Romeo. The lover was Spranger Barry, the tall, silver-voiced tragedian whose tenderness in the part became a byword, and Juliet was Susannah Cibber, the age's most admired tragic actress, whose gift for pathos Garrick had been building productions around since his Richard III triumphs. Garrick the manager, Garrick the Shakespeare campaigner, stood behind the production; Garrick the actor waited two years to claim the part. The choice was commercial intelligence of a high order. Barry and Cibber were the draw, the play was the vehicle, and Garrick's name on the enterprise did the ideological work of branding a Cibber-prepared text as the Restoration campaign's next victory.

The receipts vindicated the calculation. Fiona Ritchie, in Women and Shakespeare in the Eighteenth Century, calculates that among the forty-eight mainpieces staged at Drury Lane in the 1748 season, Romeo and Juliet was the theatre's most performed piece, taking a twelve per cent share of the overall receipts. A tragedy that had been effectively absent from the repertory for decades, displaced by Otway's Roman dress, returned not as a novelty but as the house's leading earner. Garrick dressed the return in the theatre's full machinery: the bills promised "a New Masquerade Dance proper to the Play" at the Capulet ball, weaving the dance culture of the patent houses into Shakespeare's feast, and the production asked its audience to receive the play as an event, part tragedy, part spectacle, part national celebration of the recovered author. George Winchester Stone Jr.'s study of the production details the weaving. Playbills for Garrick's Romeo and Juliet often note a minuet performed by Juliet and the leading dancer of the company, and Stone describes the lovers' first meeting staged so that the pair unfolded their growing, youthful love by pantomimic action in a place of prominence, against a background of dancers and soft music. The observation matters beyond choreography. The meeting that Shakespeare writes as a shared sonnet, fourteen lines of interlocked verse, became in Garrick's theatre a danced and mimed tableau framed by music: the adaptation's whole method in one stage picture, the poetry retained as occasion while the feeling was carried by spectacle, gesture and score. Pantomime and dance were not decorations on the Garrick version. They were load-bearing members of its emotional engineering, and the cutting of dialogue in their favour, which Banner notes as a feature of the text, was a deliberate redistribution of the play's means from the ear to the eye.

The season also set the terms of the play's stage life for the next century: Romeo and Juliet was now property valuable enough to fight over. By 23 November 1749, as Stone's research established, Covent Garden had bought the rights in Garrick's version, which meant that John Rich's house intended its own mounting of the prize. Within Garrick's own company the part of Romeo had become an irritant and a magnet, with Barry's success in it feeding the rivalry that would send Barry across to Covent Garden for the autumn of 1750. The 1748 production, in short, did three things at once. It re-established the tragedy as a repertory staple, it attached the play to Garrick's restoration brand, and it created the asset over which the two patent theatres would stage the most famous acting contest of the century. The text underneath all this remained, in 1748, substantially the Cibber arrangement. Fame first, authorship later: the Garrick adaptation as a public fact dates from the production; the Garrick adaptation as a document dates from 1750.

### Why did Garrick wait two years to play Romeo himself?

Because Barry and Susannah Cibber were the stronger hand in 1748, and the production had to succeed as a Shakespeare event before it succeeded as a Garrick performance. Garrick managed the house and its receipts; displacing a winning Romeo served nothing until Barry's defection in 1750 turned the part into a weapon of rivalry.

Casting Barry opposite Susannah Cibber was also the stronger artistic hand. Barry's Romeo pleased from the first season, Garrick's campaign needed the play established in the repertory before the manager pressed his own claim on its hero, and the receipts rewarded the patience. When Barry defected to Covent Garden in 1750, Garrick took Romeo for the first time and converted another man's success into the defining contest of his career. The episode makes the adaptation's founding point plain: it began as theatrical strategy, the rebuilding of a property, rather than as an actor's vanity project, and Garrick's subordination of his own performance to the production's success is the earliest evidence of the managerial intelligence that governs everything else in the version's history.

## Garrick's Juliet: The Sentimental Heroine Takes the Stage

Any account of the adaptation that stops at its cuts misses half its work, because Garrick's version rebuilt the heroine as carefully as it pruned the hero. Jessica Banner's study of Juliet's costuming and characterization on the eighteenth-century stage argues that Garrick's text, which cuts Shakespeare down partly in favour of added pantomime and dance, recasts Juliet in the trappings of the sentimental heroine: an opinionated, self-motivated young woman whose actions are driven by her own desires, presented in the visual and emotional language that Samuel Richardson's Pamela, published in 1740, had made the age's dominant grammar of feminine feeling. The point is not that Garrick modernized Juliet in a modern sense. He dressed her, in cloth and in dramaturgy, for the audience of the sentimental novel, an audience trained to read a heroine's worth in her suffering, her constancy and her articulate distress, and impatient with the wilfulness, speed and bodily candour of Shakespeare's girl.

Performance made the refashioning visible in two contrasting Juliets. McGirr's study distinguishes the Cibber family's two readings of the part. Jenny Cibber's Juliet of 1744 was the blooming youth, the naïve daughter whose marriage might be expected to heal a divided house, a figure McGirr compares to an Elizabeth of York turned tragic, her meaning carried by her youth and by her status as her father's instrument. Susannah Cibber, who took the part in Garrick's Drury Lane production of 1748, transformed that daughter into another in her line of doomed and tragic wives, a fair penitent figure: a Juliet always already doomed, in whose love, as McGirr puts it, there is no hope from the first. The difference is the adaptation's difference. Shakespeare's Juliet is a girl whose choices outrun every adult in the play; the Garrick-era Juliet, in its most admired impersonation, was a woman marked for suffering before she chose anything, her tragedy legible as fate wearing the dress of feeling. Garrick's text, with its softened language and its feud frame indicting the parents, supports exactly that reading, and Susannah's art gave the reading a body.

The period's own reviewing confirms that the refashioning was deliberate and was noticed. The Dramatic Censor of 1770, Francis Gentleman's critical journal, praised Garrick for having taken "very unusual, and very successful pains with his female character", and described the resulting Juliet as "a most amiable lady; she is tender, affectionate and constant; possessed of liberal sentiments and delicate feelings". The review's emphasis falls where Banner's analysis falls, on articulation and agency: this Juliet openly expresses her affection at the first meeting, is impatient to be married, delivers a passionate soliloquy, holds an expressive and affecting discussion with the Friar, and is given, above all, the chance to react to Romeo's death. A heroine who reacts is a heroine who feels in public, in sequence, at length, and the Garrick text's restructuring of the last act exists to give its Juliet that privilege. The two great Juliets of the rival houses divided the new heroine's virtues between them in the Censor's famous comparison: one excelled in amorous rapture, the other called every power of distress and despair to her aid; Bellamy was an object of love, Cibber of admiration; Bellamy's execution was more natural, Cibber's more forceable. Rapture and distress, nature and force: the sentimental heroine had become a part with schools of interpretation, a generation after Shakespeare's girl had been a part in a Roman imitation's source material.

The refashioning was worn as much as written, and the visual record lets it be seen. Anthony Walker's etching of the lovers, published in 1754, and Ignatius Joseph van den Berghe's scene of the lovers in the Friar's cell of 1794, separated by forty years of the version's reign, dress Juliet alike: a light-coloured gown of natural silhouette flowing loosely around the body, a modest collar, a small belt at the natural waist, the chemise-style dress that the period's fashion plates associated with virtuous, feeling femininity. Banner sets these images beside Joseph Highmore's celebrated paintings of Richardson's Pamela, whose heroine is clad in the same white flowing style, and the comparison is the argument in visual form: by the time Garrick's Juliet reached the print shops, she was being drawn from the same pattern book as the novel's great pattern of persecuted virtue. The character escaped the theatre into the culture's common stock of feeling. A bashful correspondent of the Lady's Magazine in 1773 could address his beloved simply as his dear charmer, Juliet, confident that every reader would parse the allusion, and the heroine's name survives in the language of dress itself, in the Juliet cap of later bridal fashion that Banner's study traces back to the century's favourite headwear. Shakespeare's Juliet had been a character. Garrick's became a reference.

The refashioning had a long iconography. George Anne Bellamy, Garrick's Juliet in the Drury Lane production of 1750 and after, was painted with him by Benjamin Wilson in 1753 in the tomb scene at the very moment of the waking, the adaptation's signature invention fixed as the play's representative image: the heroine rising from the bier into her lover's astonishment. A Shakespeare scene that Shakespeare never wrote became, in the most reproduced image of the mid-century play, the scene by which the tragedy was known. That is the measure of the Garrick Juliet's success, and of its cost. The sentimental heroine was splendidly actable, morally legible and dressed for the age's tears, and she displaced, for the better part of a century, the faster, funnier, more dangerous girl of the quartos, whose desire is an action rather than a condition.

## 1750: Garrick Takes the Part, and the Text Becomes His

The version the world calls Garrick's dates from the autumn of 1750, and it was forged in competition. With Barry and Susannah Cibber installed at Covent Garden, Garrick acted Romeo for the first time at Drury Lane, opposite the young George Anne Bellamy, and the two houses ran their rival productions in the head-to-head contest that the season-by-season record preserves. The contest itself belongs to the companion account of [the 1750 stage war between the two patent theatres' lovers](/2027/09/03/1750-romeo-and-juliet-stage-war/), and one passage here is enough: the rivalry forced Garrick to make the production his own, textually as well as histrionically, because a manager fighting for the play could not fight with a borrowed script. Before the end of the year his text was in print under the title already quoted, "By Shakespear. With alterations, and an additional scene", printed for the Tonsons and destined to be reprinted, with the alterations intact, for generations.

The printed book repays attention as a physical piece of evidence. Garrick's Advertisement in the 1750 edition is dated 27 September, the day before the rival productions opened, which places the text's preparation squarely inside the contest that produced it: the adaptation was typeset as a weapon before it was read as a book. The imprint names J. and R. Tonson and S. Draper, the trade's most prestigious Shakespeare house, so the acting version entered the world with the bibliography of respectability. Inside, the additional scene is no rumour: the edition prints full stage directions for the funeral procession and the words of Garrick's dirge as the opening scene of the fifth act, fixing in type the ceremony the bills had advertised. A Tonson printing of 1758, recorded in the Bodleian Library, runs to sixty-nine pages, a playing text of an evening's length whose bulk is recognizably the author's play, scene by scene, however planed its surface. John Bell's acting edition of 1773 carried the version forward as the Drury Lane text, and the prompt copy of that edition preserved in New York, annotated with its procession list, shows the book still working in the theatre a generation after its making. Print was never the version's afterlife. It was one of its two native media from the first autumn, and the editions' sequence is the adaptation's biography in its most durable form.

In that printed text the adaptation's signature operations stand fully formed. The references to Rosaline are cut, so that Romeo's opening melancholy hangs on no named mistress and his love for Juliet stands as the single, uncontaminated passion of his life. The death scene of the lovers, inherited in outline from Cibber and Otway, is made longer and more pathetic, expanded into a set piece of mutual recognition and farewell. The funeral procession of Juliet, the "additional scene" of the title page, takes its place between the fourth and fifth acts. The wordplay that Garrick's publicity called jingle and quibble is thinned throughout. The sexual joking that ran through Mercutio's speeches and the Nurse's is cut or softened to the standard of a decorous house. And the whole is framed by the feud, the two households of the Prologue's "two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, 1), so that private love suffers inside a public quarrel and the parents' folly, not the hero's fickleness, carries the tragedy's indictment.

The print history that followed matters as much as the text. Garrick's Romeo and Juliet did not freeze in 1750; acting texts rarely did, and the play was reprinted across his lifetime and beyond, in editions that kept the alterations as features rather than corruptions. The Library of Congress's examination of its holdings counts no fewer than seven printings that include the added deathbed conversation, and notes that subsequent editions of 1794, 1814, 1819 and 1874 adopt Garrick's alterations to the original text. Prefaces to these editions defended the changes, some on grounds of taste, some on the bolder ground that Garrick's ending was more faithful than Shakespeare to the Italian sources of the story, since the novella tradition had indeed let the lovers meet awake. Only in the later nineteenth century did print turn back: an edition of 1882 restored Rosaline and let Romeo die, as Shakespeare wrote it, immediately upon the kiss. The stage had surrendered the Garrick version decades earlier. Print, the slower medium, kept faith longest.

## Garrick's Romeo: The Making of the Century's Lover

The adaptation's deepest work was done on the hero, and scholarship on the eighteenth-century essays about the play explains why the work was necessary. Love, as a tragic subject, stood under moral suspicion in the period: it was widely treated as immoral and antisocial, a private appetite unfit to carry the public weight of tragedy, and the play's hero was vulnerable on exactly that ground, a young man whose passion could be read as appetite changing its object. The study of Garrick's adaptation and the making of "Romeo as a lover", published in the journal of the Shakespeare Society of Japan, sets out the textual strategy by which Garrick answered the suspicion. His play-text drastically cut the jingle and quibble, simplified and clarified the plot and the characterization, and eliminated many sexual allusions, solving at a stroke the three problems the age perceived in the play: its diction, its structure and its ethics. Around the cleansed love he set the frame of the feud, clarifying the sufferings Romeo bears because the feud forbids his love, so that the passion is purified and elevated into a heroic act, and the foolishness of the stubborn parents who will not end the quarrel becomes the tragedy's true indictment.

The result was a new cultural type. Before Garrick, Romeo could be played as a warning; after Garrick, he was the lover, the standard against which faithful passion measured itself, and the adaptation's long popularity made the type permanent. When Garrick finally took the part himself in 1750, he was not merely succeeding Barry in a successful role. He was embodying a character his own editing had invented, and the concentration of his acting style, its fire and its nervous intensity, fused with the purified text to produce the century's definitive image of the hero: single-hearted, persecuted, eloquent in grief, and absolved in advance of every charge the Friar's churchyard of reproaches could bring. The same study notes the sequel with fairness: the adaptation has been underestimated since the end of the nineteenth century precisely because of its drastic eliminations and alterations, the very operations that had made its hero possible. Its closing argument asks for the account to be reopened: the acceptance and applause that greeted Garrick's Romeo as a lover, and with him Shakespeare's play itself, in the eighteenth century would not have been possible without Garrick's ingenuity, and the adaptation's contributions to the stage history deserve fresh appreciation. Coming from a close analysis of the adapted text rather than from theatrical nostalgia, the revaluation carries weight, and it aligns with this article's ledger from the opposite direction: the cuts were the creation, and judging the version requires pricing the creation and the cuts in a single sum. Judgement of Garrick's Romeo has to hold both truths. He did not discover the lover inside Shakespeare's character. He manufactured the lover by excision, and the manufacture was so complete that audiences forgot there had ever been another Romeo to discover.

Read as a whole, the ledger shows a pattern no single change reveals. The cuts all move one way, toward gravity, purity and pathos. The additions all move the same way, toward spectacle and sentiment. Garrick's Shakespeare is Shakespeare with the laughter drained, the desire chastened, the hero absolved, and the grief amplified to the size of a cathedral procession. That is not a failure of intelligence; it is a coherent reading of the play, executed by a man who understood his audience's nerves better than its library. The ledger's pattern is the adaptation's meaning, and the sections that follow weigh the two cuts the brief of this article names as the crux, the Rosaline erasure and the smoothed language, before the tomb and the procession receive their due.

Two features of the ledger deserve notice before the weighing begins. The first is how little of it is invention from nothing. Of its twelve rows, the majority are operations of removal, retention or inheritance: Garrick's originality lies in selection and permanence, in choosing which of the available alterations to fix in print and defend, rather than in devising novelties. The second is the asymmetry between the columns. The gains the ledger records are immediate and theatrical, receipts, tears, moral clarity, a procession no rival could outbid for long. The losses are deferred and literary, irony, register range, the hero's instability, the wit's intelligence, and they fell due only when the culture changed around the version and readers returned to the quartos with fresh eyes. An adaptation is a loan taken against the future of taste, and Garrick, who borrowed more successfully than any adapter before or since, also left the largest repayment schedule. The ledger's final column is where that schedule is written.


## The Rosaline Cut: What the Century Gained and What the Play Lost

No single stroke of the adaptation repays examination like the erasure of Rosaline, because it changed not a scene but the hero. In Shakespeare's text Romeo enters in the grip of a declared, unreturned love for a woman who never appears, and the whole first movement of the play is built on the audience's knowledge that the great passion is the second one. The Friar states the design in rebuke when Romeo confesses the exchange of mistresses: the old love is dismissed, and the swiftness of the dismissal is itself evidence. Juliet's own line at the feast, "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5), carries its famous shock partly because the audience has watched the supposedly inconsolable lover transfer his heart within a day. Shakespeare's Romeo is a study in the velocity of desire, and Rosaline is the control measurement against which the velocity is read.

Garrick's text abolishes the measurement. With the references cut, Romeo's opening melancholy floats free of any named object, and he meets Juliet as a heart unencumbered, loving once and wholly. The gain, for the eighteenth century, was moral legibility. A hero whose passion changed objects overnight risked reading as a rake or a fool, and the age's criticism had long worried the point; a Romeo who loved only Juliet could be offered as a pattern of constancy destroyed by other people's hatred. The adaptation's scholarship has a name for the result: the making of "Romeo as a lover", the hero re-founded as the type of the faithful, suffering bridegroom, his sufferings under the feud clarified until opposing his love becomes the parents' crime rather than his own instability. On stage the gain was practical as well. Barry's tender gravity, and later Garrick's own concentrated fire, played more cleanly without an earlier mistress trailing behind the performance in the audience's memory.

The machinery Garrick dismantled can be watched working in two short passages of the uncut text. The Chorus that opens the second act announces the transfer in so many words: "Now old desire doth in his death-bed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0, lines 1-2), a burial and an inheritance compressed into a couplet, with Rosaline as the corpse. The Friar then conducts the autopsy in the cell scene, marvelling at the speed of the exchange and drawing its moral in the play's most quoted piece of psychology: "Young men's love then lies / Not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3, lines 67-68). His sentence for the offence is exact, "for doting, not for loving, pupil mine" (2.3), a distinction between appetite and love that the rest of the tragedy keeps testing. Both passages exist to mark Romeo's first love as a fact of the design, the Chorus by announcing its death, the Friar by prosecuting its shallowness. An acting text that erases Rosaline must mute the Chorus's couplet into decoration and leave the Friar scolding a young man for an offence the audience never saw committed.

The losses are structural, and they compound. Without Rosaline, the Friar's chiding loses its factual basis and shrinks toward ceremony. The theme of haste, the play's most persistent self-commentary, loses its first and clearest instance, for Romeo's overnight transfer is the pattern the marriage, the killings and the tomb all repeat. The comedy of the opening acts loses its engine, since Mercutio's mockery and Benvolio's counsel presuppose a lovesick friend pining for a specific, unseen woman. And the irony that powers the ending is weakened: an audience that has watched desire change its object in a day watches the tomb with a colder eye than an audience assured the passion was single and fated. Garrick's cut bought a hero the century could admire at the price of the hero Shakespeare wrote. The ledger records the transaction fairly: purity gained, meaning lost, and the loss falling on exactly the element, the hero's mutability, that makes the tragedy a tragedy of youth rather than a martyrdom of love.

### Why does the Rosaline cut matter more than any other change Garrick made?

Because the other changes alter scenes, language or spectacle, while this one alters the tragedy's design. It redefines the hero, disables the Friar's rebuke, removes the first instance of the haste pattern, and softens the ending's irony all at once, and it held for the better part of a century, so its restoration became the marker of the text's return.

The cut alters the causal and moral design of the whole tragedy. Processions can be omitted and wordplay restored by later hands, and both were. The erasure reached into the hero's character, the Friar's function, the haste theme and the ending's irony at once, redefining what kind of story the audience believed itself to be watching. A production can survive a cut scene. A cut premise rewrites the play, and this premise was missing from the stage's Romeo and Juliet for the better part of a century, which is why its restoration became the marker of the text's return.

## The Smoothed Language and the Silenced Bawdy

Garrick's second great operation was on the play's tongue. His announced policy, in the phrase his publicity fixed on, was to clear the play of its jingle and quibble: the punning exchanges, the rhyming fencing, the verbal games that the age's criticism treated as a young playwright's indiscipline. Around that policy gathered a wider programme. Sexual allusion was eliminated in quantity, as modern analyses of the adapted text record, because the patent theatres played to a decorum that Shakespeare's innuendo repeatedly breached. Mercutio, the chief carrier of both the wit and the indecency, had his part reduced, as the Library of Congress survey summarizes the printed version: the role shrinks, the Queen Mab brilliance is trimmed within it, and the character who in Shakespeare threatens to steal the play becomes an attendant lord of the love plot. The Nurse's earthy comedy underwent the same discipline. What Garrick called smoothing reached down to single words in famous places, as the tomb scene will show: even a line every schoolchild would later know was adjusted to fit the new design.

Garrick was executing a critical consensus, not defying one. The mid-century's most authoritative voice on Shakespeare's faults, Samuel Johnson in the Preface to his edition of 1765, fixed the charge against the quibble in a sentence the whole age quoted: a quibble, Johnson wrote of the playwright, "was to him the fatal Cleopatra for which he lost the world, and was content to lose it." The pun, in this account, is not a blemish but a seduction, the thing for which Shakespeare sacrificed sense, propriety and plot. A manager preparing the play for the stage in 1748 and 1750 worked inside that climate of opinion, in which the wordplay marked the author as a genius with a disfiguring habit, and in which an adapter who cut the habit away could present the surgery as loyalty. Modern analysis of the adapted text confirms the programme's completeness: diction, structure and ethics were treated as a single problem with a single solution, and the sexual allusions were eliminated as part of the same operation that thinned the puns, since both marked the play as the product of a ruder age than the one buying the tickets.

Weighing this operation requires honesty about its context. The eighteenth-century stage version of any Shakespeare play was a performing text, cut and adjusted as a matter of professional routine, and Garrick's smoothing was modest beside Otway's wholesale recomposition or the operatic Macbeths of the same repertory. His admirers could point out that he kept the speeches audiences loved, kept the verse recognizably the author's, and delivered a text playable at full tragic force in an evening. Against that stand two losses the record makes plain. The first is the play's register range: Shakespeare's tragedy lives by its oscillation between bawdy comedy and high lyric grief, the Porter-like pressure of the servants' and Mercutio's world against the lovers' poetry, and a smoothed text raises the floor and lowers the ceiling until the play sits at one dignified temperature. The second is Mercutio himself, whose reduction costs the tragedy its intelligence about its own hero: the friend who sees through Romeo's postures is the audience's tutor in doubting them, and a smaller Mercutio means a Romeo less examined, which serves the absolved hero of the Rosaline cut exactly. The operations interlock. Smoothing the tongue and shrinking the wit are the Rosaline policy continued by other means: remove every voice that ironizes the love, and the love stands alone, gleaming and unexamined, ready for the procession to carry it to the tomb.

## What Garrick Kept: The Case for the Restoration

The case against Garrick writes itself from the ledger's cut column, and it has been written so often that the opposing evidence needs stating at full strength. Begin with the baseline. The adapter's normal product in Garrick's century was not a pruned Shakespeare but a replacement Shakespeare: Tate's Lear ends with Cordelia alive and marrying Edgar, Otway's lovers die in republican Rome, and the same repertory offered a Macbeth furnished with singing and dancing witches as a musical entertainment. Against that baseline, Garrick's Romeo and Juliet is an act of comparative fidelity without parallel in the period. The action stands in Verona. The families are Shakespeare's families, the scenes follow Shakespeare's sequence, the plot is Shakespeare's plot from the street brawl to the tomb, and the verse, however thinned and planed, is in its bulk the author's own, spoken by his characters in his order. The Prologue still opens the play with its two households. The feast, the garden scene, the cell, the street fight, the banishment, the potion, the tomb: the architecture a modern audience recognizes is the architecture Garrick's audience saw, and it had not been visible on the London stage, in that completeness, within living memory.

The retention was not passive. Garrick's campaign across the repertory, Richard III and King John before Romeo and Juliet, was a sustained argument that the English stage's proper business was Shakespeare, and that an actor-manager's authority derived from serving the national author rather than from displacing him. His version of the tragedy put that argument into practice with a manager's concreteness: the play was cast with the company's best tragedians, dressed with a masquerade dance and a procession, advertised on the author's name, and printed by the author's own publishers. If the text was altered, the institution was restored, and the institution was the thing Otway's Rome had actually destroyed: a living stage tradition of Shakespeare's play, in which each generation's performers measured themselves against the same scenes. Barry, Cibber, Bellamy, Kemble and the long line that followed did not become the play's interpreters by accident. They inherited a stage on which Romeo and Juliet existed as a classic to be interpreted, and Garrick built that stage.

Honesty about proportions completes the case rather than weakening it. No trustworthy line-by-line census of the Garrick text against the quarto survives in the accessible record, and this article invents none. What the record does support is the qualitative verdict every class of evidence repeats: editions, bills, memoirs, prompt copies and a century of performance all treat the Garrick version as Shakespeare's play adapted, where they treat Otway's as Otway's play derived. The distinction is the adaptation's identity. Garrick kept the play the way a great restorer keeps a building: by replacing the timbers that would not bear weight, altering the plan where the traffic demanded, and leaving the facade, the address and the name exactly where the city remembered them. Whether the city was right to call the result the original house is the question the verdict must answer, and the answer cannot be a simple yes. It also cannot be a simple no, which is why the cliché of pure restoration and the counter-cliché of vandalism both fail the record.

## The Tomb Scene: Shakespeare's Irony Traded for Recognition

One passage must serve for the adaptation's most famous scene, since the companion study of [Garrick's tomb scene, where Juliet wakes in time](/2027/09/10/garrick-tomb-scene-juliet-wakes/) examines the alteration at the length it deserves. Shakespeare's ending is built on averted knowledge: Romeo dies believing Juliet dead, Juliet wakes to find him dead, and the audience's agony is the agony of a door closing seconds too early. Otway had broken that design by waking Lavinia in time, the Cibber version kept the break, and Garrick inherited, expanded and immortalized it. In his text Juliet wakes after Romeo has taken the poison but while life remains, and the lovers share a final conversation that the Library of Congress counts at sixty-seven added lines: recognition, explanation, reproach, forgiveness and farewell, all the satisfactions Shakespeare withheld, delivered in full.

Contemporary criticism did not merely tolerate the change; it crowned it. The Dramatic Censor, reviewing the adaptation in 1770 with two decades of performances behind it, described the waking moment in the language of satisfied feeling: nature brought to its most critical pitch as Juliet wakes and her husband's transports fill the audience with a cordial sympathy of satisfaction, soon dashed, her behaviour after his death passing from grief to distraction in a masterly variation. Its verdict deserves quotation because it is the age speaking without embarrassment: "no play ever received greater advantage from alteration than this tragedy, especially in the last act; bringing Juliet to life before Romeo dies is undoubtedly a change of infinite merit. The new dying scene does Mr. Garrick great credit." A modern reader, formed by the restored text, reads that sentence as a period curiosity. It should be read as evidence. The tomb conversation was not a vulgarity smuggled past a discerning public. It was the considered preference of the era's criticism, defended in print as the adaptation's supreme improvement, and any history that treats the century's Garrick as a failure of taste has to explain away the taste's own articulate spokesmen.

The adjustment reached into the verse itself. Shakespeare's Romeo, drinking, speaks the most famous exit line of the scene: "O true apothecary! Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3). Quickness is the point; the poison outruns every rescue. Garrick's Romeo, who must linger through a conversation, says instead that the drugs are not so quick. Three words carry the whole philosophy of the adaptation. Where Shakespeare's text enacts a world in which timing is fate and speech comes too late, Garrick's text slows the world down until speech can happen, because his audience's deepest demand on tragedy was not irony but feeling exchanged and acknowledged before death. Judged as dramaturgy the change is a trade of structure for pathos, and the century ratified the trade every time it wept. Judged as editing it is the clearest proof in the version that Garrick's hand, however reverent its branding, never stopped adapting: at the play's most sacred moment he rewrote the author's sentence to serve his own design, and audiences defended the rewrite as an improvement for a hundred years. The defence had stamina. At the Stratford Shakespeare Festival of 1864, held for the tercentenary of the author's birth, the London Princess's Theatre company performed the play using the old Garrick version expressly, as The Times reported, "on account of the extra opportunity given to the lovers in the last scene". Ninety-four years after Cushman's restoration was supposed to have closed the question, and a century and more after Garrick built the scene, a festival company celebrating Shakespeare reached for the altered ending because actors wanted the farewell Garrick had written. The tomb scene is therefore the adaptation's hardest case for its critics: an interpolation that performers kept choosing, against the text, against the scholars, against the century's turn, because it gave the two people on the stage more to do with their grief than silence and a corpse allowed.

## The Funeral Procession: A Scene Born of Rivalry, Kept for a Century

The adaptation's largest addition was not verse at all but ceremony. In the autumn of 1750, as the rival productions faced each other across London, the funeral procession of Juliet entered the play: an additional scene set between the fourth and fifth acts, in which the supposedly dead heroine was carried to the Capulet monument by a train of friars, choristers, torch-bearers, flower-strewers, nuns and mourning parents, accompanied by a solemn dirge. John Rich at Covent Garden advertised his procession in advance, his dirge set by Thomas Arne. Garrick at Drury Lane advertised his only from the third night, with words he had written himself and music composed by William Boyce, sung by seven of the company's leading singers; the Boyce setting of "Rise, rise heart-breaking sighs" survives in manuscript in the Bodleian Library. Which house conceived the spectacle first became a small war of its own: Tate Wilkinson, who as a boy haunted Covent Garden, remembered that Garrick promised no procession in his bills yet sprang the effect as a surprise, while the first-night Drury Lane bill's warning against gentlemen on the stage, on account of the scenery and music in the play, hints that Garrick's procession was ready from the opening performance and merely unadvertised. Baldwin and Wilson weigh espionage, preparation times and bills, and their balanced verdict is that both managers knew the other's intentions and both mounted the scene within days of each other, an invention of rivalry as much as artistry.

The scene's celebrity can be dated by its parodies. In the summer of 1754, four years after its invention, Christopher Smart's burlesque entertainment at the Haymarket included a solemn procession to the monument of the Lunns, a mock funeral for John Rich's own Harlequin persona, accompanied by a Jews-harp and a hurdy-gurdy in place of Boyce and Arne. A theatrical device is not burlesqued until every spectator can be trusted to recognize it, and Smart's joke is documentary proof that by 1754 the funeral procession had passed from novelty to institution. Bellamy's memoir preserves the arms race behind the institution from inside Drury Lane: Garrick, attempting to rival the success of Rich's procession, purchased a new and very expensive bell for his funeral scene, found that the purchase did not increase his audience, and consoled himself by reusing the bell to toll at the execution of Pierre in Otway's Venice Preserv'd. The greatest manager of the age, beaten at spectacle by the master of pantomime, recycling his investment into another tragedy: the anecdote catches the procession's economics exactly, in which the scene was a capital asset whose returns were measured nightly at the door.

What matters for the adaptation is what happened next. The procession, dismissed by later taste as barn-door spectacle, became for a hundred years an essential part of the tragedy, so necessary that the play was staged with its procession and dirge in every season of the century's second half at one or both patent theatres, and so portable that it reached Edinburgh by January 1751 and provincial companies soon after. John Rich, asked by George Anne Bellamy in later years whether her acting explained a run's success, took snuff and answered, "Yes Mistress! but it is owing to the procession." The anecdote comes from Bellamy's own Apology, and its point survives its polish: the manager credited the scene, not the star. At the furthest edge of the profession, John Bernard remembered strolling companies in the early 1770s so small that Juliet sang her own dirge while Romeo, needed alive in the next scene, tolled the funeral bell offstage. A device invented to outbid a rival house had become, within a generation, the play's folk property.

Eyewitnesses let the scene be measured rather than imagined. Count Kielmansegge, a German visitor who saw the play at Drury Lane on Boxing Day 1761, recorded an entire funeral represented on the stage, bells tolling and a choir singing, the heroine lying on a state bed under a canopy, guarded by girls strewing flowers and by torch-bearers, with clergy in vestments walking before and the parents following behind; he found the ceremony profane in feeling and beautiful in execution, and marvelled at a stage that could field fifty performers in a night with decorations and machinery provided regardless of cost. A prompt copy preserved with John Bell's edition of 1773, now in the New York Public Library, lists the procession's order in a manager's hand: the bell tolls four times, the chorus begins on the fourth bell, servants in black with white cockades and tapers lead, incense boys follow, the chorus in surplices sings the airs, Friar Laurence walks alone, and the bier is carried by four of the theatre's carpenters, with a substitute lady lying for Juliet so that the actress need not be borne in and out of her own funeral. Thirty-three named participants marched, plus the chorus. The scene was not an embellishment sprinkled over the play. It was a second production mounted inside the first, with its own personnel, its own music and its own stage management, and it grew as the patent theatres grew: reset by Thomas Linley senior at Drury Lane in 1788 under John Philip Kemble's management, sung by fourteen named voices, amplified at Covent Garden in the 1790s until Arne's dirge needed extra instrumental parts written for the larger house.

Contemporaries argued about the procession in terms that map the whole culture war of the Garrick version. Francis Gentleman, in The Dramatic Censor of 1770, defended it with a candour managers must have envied: though not absolutely essential, he wrote, nothing could be better devised than a funeral procession to render the play thoroughly popular, since three-fourths of every audience are more capable of enjoying sound and show than solid sense and poetical imagination. The defence concedes everything the prosecution alleged, that the scene was pageantry for the many rather than poetry for the judicious, and calls the concession a virtue. By 1797 the Monthly Mirror's "Theatricus" was attacking the grand raree-show at the end of the fourth act as absurd farce, and in September 1845 George Fletcher devoted thirty-eight double-column pages of the Westminster Review to demolishing Garrick's alterations wholesale, the product, he wrote, of "the overweening vanity and meretricious taste of a mere actor, though a great one", dismissing the "paltry, sentimental dirge scene" in a sentence. The year of Fletcher's attack is the year of Cushman's restoration, and the coincidence is the turning point made visible: the critical culture that had licensed Garrick's improvements for a century withdrew the licence in print and on stage within months of each other. The procession also outlived its century in one spectacular descendant. When Charles Kemble's company carried the play to Paris in 1827, Harriet Smithson's Juliet conquered the city, and Hector Berlioz, who fell in love with the actress, built his dramatic symphony Roméo et Juliette of 1839 around the memory; its funeral convoy for Juliet, with its choral dirge bidding the mourners strew flowers for the dead virgin, is the Drury Lane procession transposed into the concert hall, flower-strewers and all.

The music tells the same story of reach and adaptation. Arne's Covent Garden setting was written for a three-part chorus with trumpets, drums, flutes and strings; Boyce's Drury Lane setting used a four-part chorus with strings, trumpet and two oboes; and the scores that travelled were the ones companies could actually perform. Pasquali's setting of the Covent Garden words, built for two soloists, a two-part chorus, violins, bass and a bell, was published and survives in a British Library copy, while Charles Avison's setting of Garrick's words for a Newcastle touring company in 1755 rests in manuscript at Burghley House. The dirge was thus re-composed wherever the procession went, each theatre shrinking or swelling the music to its forces, which is why the scene could live simultaneously in the patent houses with their dozens of singers and in strolling companies with an iron pot for a bell. Garrick's words survived every resetting; the procession was a format, not a score, and formats are the most durable inventions the theatre knows.

Within the adaptation's economy the procession is the Rosaline cut translated into spectacle. Garrick's version relocates the tragedy from irony to ceremony: grief is no longer a private recognition in a tomb but a public liturgy, choral, musical and processional, that enrols the whole city of Verona and the whole audience in mourning before the catastrophe even completes itself. The scene also solved a practical dramaturgical problem of the inherited text, giving the house a climax of feeling at the interval between the acts at the moment when Shakespeare's plot pauses on Juliet's bier, and it gave the singing company, the scene painters and the costumiers their share of the evening. That it has vanished from the modern stage while the play it adorned survived is the final evidence about its nature: it belonged not to the tragedy but to the theatrical culture that Garrick ruled, and when that culture died the procession died with it, leaving its hundred years as the measure of his power over what audiences thought Romeo and Juliet was.


## A Century of Garrick: Dominion, Drift, and the Long Return

The adaptation's reign is the best-attested fact in its history. Scholarship on the version reckons its popularity at ninety-seven years on the British stage from the first performance of 1748, a span that ends, with suspicious neatness and real accuracy, in 1845, when Charlotte Cushman restored Shakespeare's conception of the ending. Within those years Garrick's text functioned as the play's authorized form. It travelled: the tragedy reached America with the first English company in 1754, in the version the London stage had settled, and its famous additions enjoyed an American afterlife that outlasted their British one, the deathbed conversation still played in an American production as late as 1899 according to the Yale editors' survey. It settled into the provinces with its procession attached, as the Edinburgh and Hereford advertisements show, and into the toy theatres of children, whose cut-out funeral processions of 1823 preserved the staging in miniature. The Hodgson prints of that year, six sheets issued across ten days in June and now viewable through the Folger Shakespeare Library, itemize the procession as the Covent Garden staging of November 1822 with Fanny Kelly and Charles Kemble had fixed it: seven named characters and sixty-one other figures in twelve groups, flower-strewers and incense boys, grey friars and an abbot in scarlet, gold and white, choristers, nuns, the confessor, the bier, the mourning parents, Paris with his handkerchief, the Nurse weeping with Peter. A scene Garrick and Rich had improvised against each other in a fortnight of 1750 had become, seventy years on, a child's paper reconstruction of how the tragedy was supposed to look, its personnel countable like the figures of a nativity. It colonized print, as the sequence of editions adopting Garrick's alterations across 1794, 1814, 1819 and 1874 demonstrates, with the added conversation carried in at least seven printings held by the Library of Congress.

The dominion's texture is captured in the assessment Banner quotes from Berg's 1989 study of the play's stage fortunes: Garrick's text "exerted enormous influence on the stage presentation of Romeo and Juliet for over a century and was used, with only very slight variations, as the standard text in performance until the 1840s; some of its innovations were still in use in the 1880s". Standard text is the operative phrase. A standard does not compete nightly for its life; it is inherited by each new management as the default, altered at the margins, and defended by the accumulated authority of every great performance given in it. Barry's tenderness, Cibber's pathos, Bellamy's nature, Kemble's grandeur and the young Edmund Kean's fire were all delivered inside Garrick's architecture, and each success re-consecrated the frame. The version's innovations outliving its text, in use into the 1880s, is the dominion's final refinement: by the time the frame itself was dismantled, its habits had passed into the stage's unconscious, the procession instinct, the absolved hero and the expanded farewell surviving as instincts in productions that believed themselves free of Garrick altogether.

Dominion did not mean immobility, and the ledger's pattern continued under other hands. Nineteenth-century productions that thought themselves post-Garrick kept his instincts while shedding his name: Henry Irving, the Yale survey notes, followed eighteenth-century precedent in cutting most or all of the action after Juliet's death, keeping the sentimental shape of the evening inside a professedly Shakespearean frame. The adaptation had succeeded so completely that its habits could survive its text. What finally displaced it was not a rival adaptation but a change in the culture of editing itself: the nineteenth-century scholarly editions, the antiquarian stage movement, and actresses and managers who built reputations on restoration rather than improvement. Cushman's 1845 Romeo, in which she played the lover herself, restored Shakespeare's conception of the catastrophe, the silent tomb and the unshared deaths, and the century's Garrick began the retreat that the 1882 edition completed in print by restoring Rosaline and the immediate death.

The dominion's closing decades show the version dying by instalments, and the pattern of the instalments is evidence in itself. At Covent Garden in March 1840, under Madame Vestris's management, the play was staged with Shakespeare's version of the death scene restored, yet the procession remained, the Theatrical Observer praising the funeral dirge between the fourth and fifth acts as magnificently executed: the house surrendered Garrick's words before it surrendered his spectacle, because the spectacle was what the audience had paid for across four generations. Dublin in April 1850 advertised a production terminating according to the original text of Shakespeare and still included the funeral procession and solemn dirge at the end of the fourth act, the interpolation by then so detached from the question of the text that it could be billed alongside a claim of textual purity without any sense of contradiction. By January 1854 the Era could report the dirge scene as generally omitted in the major London theatres while remaining always very attractive in the country, and the last London appearance the record gives the dirge is at Sadler's Wells in October 1868, where the reviewer described the whole ceremony for readers assumed to be strangers to it. A scene that had been the play's chief attraction within living memory had become, in eighteen years, a provincial survival requiring explanation. That is how theatrical centuries end: not by refutation but by embarrassment, the device first retained from habit, then apologized for, then dropped, then forgotten so completely that its hundred years read as an error of the ancestors rather than as the taste of a civilization.

The turn can be heard earliest in the voice of a performer who had to be carried through it. Fanny Kemble, who first played Juliet in 1829 at nineteen, came to detest the scene she inherited, calling it in her Record of a Girlhood "that very dreadful piece of stage pageantry". Her diary for August 1831, quoted in the memoir, is blunter: she hated the lugubrious funeral dirge, had always hated the stage funeral business, and never saw it without a cold shudder at its unfitness. Her objection was professional rather than antiquarian, and it exposes the scene's practical secret: its pretext, as she understood the stage management, was to buy time for setting the churchyard scene and for Juliet to change her dress, although the text directs that the heroine be buried in her finest attire, while the production arrayed the substitute corpse in white muslin draperies and veils, corpse fashion. When the leading Juliet of the age experiences the play's most famous scene as an interval for a costume change, the scene's century is already ending from inside the profession that sustained it.

Baldwin and Wilson, closing their study of the procession, itemize the material causes beneath the embarrassment, and the list is worth setting beside the simpler story of recovered taste. A growing belief that Shakespeare's plays should be produced as written, without additions by lesser hands, withdrew the ideology that had licensed the scene. The Theatres Act of 1843 ended the patent monopoly and exposed the two great houses to competition from theatres newly free to mount spoken drama, weakening the institutions whose scale the procession required. The main companies no longer maintained their own resident singers capable of giving the dirge its due, and the traditional music, re-set and re-set again across the century, had come to feel dated in the ears that remained. Once the London houses dropped the scene, the provinces followed in time, as they had followed its invention. The Garrick century, on this accounting, was held up by an ecology: a duopoly of great theatres, resident musical companies, a licensed culture of adaptation and an audience trained to weep in public. The ecology failed piece by piece across the 1840s, and the version failed with it, which is the final reason to resist the language of exposure. Garrick's adaptation was not found out. Its world was dismantled.

### Why did print keep Garrick's alterations after the stage let them go?

Because print was the version's last stronghold. Acting editions had carried Garrick's text as the play's standard form for generations, their prefaces defended his alterations as improvements and even as fidelity to the Italian sources, and reading habits change more slowly than stage practice. Editions adopted his conversation into the 1870s, surrendering Rosaline only in 1882.

Charlotte Cushman's 1845 restoration of Shakespeare's conception of the ending is the conventional closing date for the stage, ninety-seven years after 1748, and what ended with it was the theatre's governing licence to improve the author in performance. The waking Juliet, the expanded farewell and eventually the procession lost their standing as improvements and were reclassified as interpolations. Print followed more slowly, holding Garrick's conversation into the 1870s and surrendering Rosaline only in 1882, so the era closed in two stages: the stage recovered Shakespeare's ending first, and the reading text recovered Shakespeare's heroine a generation later.

The dominion also had a cost accounting that the verdict must face. For ninety-seven years the English-speaking world's image of the tragedy was formed by a text without Rosaline, with a diminished Mercutio, with the bawdy laundered, with a heroine refashioned toward sentiment, with an ending rebuilt for mutual farewell and a funeral liturgy interpolated by a theatrical war. Generations of playgoers were not wrong to love that play; by every account it worked, nightly, at the highest level of craft its century possessed. But the Shakespeare who was canonized as the national poet during those years was in part Garrick's co-production, and the criticism written about the play in the period, much of it engaged with the adapted text, inherited the adaptation's reading: love pure and persecuted, parents indicted, irony muted, pathos enthroned. Understanding Garrick's version is therefore not an appendix to the play's history. It is the medium through which the play's modern reputation was manufactured, and every later restoration, from Cushman onward, defined itself against its choices.

## The Critics Weighed: Restoration, Brand, and Text

The modern argument about the Garrick version runs between three positions, and adjudicating them settles the article's verdict. The first is the theatre historian's account. George Winchester Stone Jr., whose The London Stage 1660-1800 remains the documentary spine of the period and whose critical biography of Garrick, written with George M. Kahrl, set the standard life, established the hard chronology on which all discussion stands: the 1748 production and its receipts, the sale of rights to Covent Garden by November 1749, the rival productions of 1750, the publication of the altered text. On Stone's evidence the adaptation is a datable professional artefact, the work of a manager rebuilding a property with the materials his theatre had in stock, Cibber's text included. The second position is the editor's. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of the play, whose introduction traces the performance history across four centuries, presents the Garrick version within the long continuum of acting texts: an important, documentable stage in the transmission of the play, to be recorded precisely and judged as theatre rather than as scripture, neither the rescue the brand claimed nor the vandalism later purists alleged.

Between the historian and the editor stands an older scholarly verdict that the modern debate has had to digest. The Yale Shakespeare editors of 1917, summarizing the stage history in their appendix, concluded flatly that the version Garrick used in 1750 was substantially that of Cibber. The sentence is the high-water mark of the deflationary reading, and it is half right in a way that matters: it correctly identifies the 1748 staging's dependence on Cibber's preparation, and it incorrectly extends that dependence over the printed adaptation of 1750, whose distinguishing operations, the permanence of the Rosaline erasure, the enlarged death scene and the advertised additional scene, are not Cibber's property in the same sense at all. A verdict that cannot distinguish the vehicle Garrick inherited from the text he published cannot explain why the century performed, reprinted and fought over the one and let the other vanish.

Wilson's Cambridge preface supplies a useful test of editorial temperaments. His eighty-year calculation is sound, and Banner's study accepts it while refusing his conclusion: Wilson was technically correct that no rendition of the original was performed until the 1740s, and wrong to dismiss Otway's play as a hotch-potch, since the Roman version drew large crowds and held its popularity for more than fifty years, while Cibber's 1744 version was, in Berg's phrase, similarly considered a great success. The exchange matters because it isolates the recurring error in Garrick criticism, the conversion of a true premise into a false scale of values. From the premise that Shakespeare's text was absent, Wilson infers that what replaced it was worthless; from the same premise, Garrick's publicity inferred that what replaced it was Garrick. Both inferences serve institutions, the scholarly edition in one case and the patent theatre in the other, and the record resists both: the play survived its absence inside other men's versions, some of them excellent theatre, and its return was a trade negotiation among those versions rather than a resurrection.

The third position is the sceptical one, and it has sharpened the debate. Elaine McGirr's re-centring of the Cibber brand argues that Garrick's restoration rhetoric was a competitive weapon: the 1744 and 1748 revivals were a Cibber family enterprise before they were a Garrick one, Susannah Cibber's Juliet was the draw that made the play's return possible, and Garrick's pose as the cleanser of Cibberian taint rewrites as personal virtue what was actually a takeover of a rival's going concern. Jessica Banner's study of Juliet's costuming and characterization on the eighteenth-century stage adds a cultural dimension: the Garrick Juliet, clad and rewritten as a sentimental heroine in the age of Richardson's Pamela, was an active refashioning of the heroine for the market in feminine feeling, not a recovery of anything Shakespeare wrote. Fiona Ritchie's work supplies the economics that discipline both claims: whatever the branding, the 1748 production was Drury Lane's most performed piece at twelve per cent of receipts, and by 1750 the play, in Garrick's form, was the only show in London. The numbers describe a triumph no theory of mere marketing explains, because rival houses do not buy rights in, and fight wars over, properties the public has not demanded.

The adjudication runs as follows. The Yale editors were right about 1748 and wrong about the whole, for the reason given above: inheritance explains the first staging and cannot explain the printed text's century. McGirr is right about the origin and the rhetoric: the 1748 staging stood on Cibber's text, Cibber's wife was its star, and "restoration" named a brand before it named an act, so any account that begins with Garrick alone in a empty theatre rescuing a forgotten play is repeating his publicity. She is wrong, if the argument is pressed to its limit, about the consequence: by the end of 1750 there existed a printed Garrick text, distinguishable from Cibber's by the Rosaline erasure's permanence, the enlarged death scene and the procession, and it was that text, not Cibber's, that the next century performed, printed and fought over. Stone's chronology and Levenson's continuum frame the synthesis. Garrick's adaptation is best described as an appropriation executed with genius: he took the Cibber vehicle, stamped it with his name, his taste and his theatre's machinery, and made his version so completely the play's public form that recovering Shakespeare required, a century later, an act of restoration as deliberate as his had been. The full story of that later recovery is told in the companion study of [the long campaign to restore Shakespeare's text to the stage](/2027/10/08/restoring-shakespeare-text-stage/), whose endpoint this article takes as its own closing boundary.

For readers assembling the evidence themselves, the materials are scattered across playbills, memoirs, editions and modern studies, and a research dossier kept in a single ordered place, of the kind [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) provides for a scholar's own library, repays the labour: the bills fix the dates, the memoirs of Bellamy and Wilkinson fix the anecdotes to their tellers, and the printings fix the text's drift, and only a reader holding all three classes of record at once can see where Garrick ends and the Cibbers begin.

## The Verdict on Garrick

Garrick's place in the play's stage history is that of its second author and its first modern producer, and the two halves of that title must be held together. As a producer he stands without rival in the record: he took a tragedy displaced since 1679 by a Roman imitation, restored its city, its families, its plot and the greater part of its verse, built it into his house's leading earner, and fixed it so firmly in the repertory that it has never since left. As an author his work is the compromise the ledger records: Rosaline erased and the hero with her, the tongue smoothed and the wit reduced, the heroine sentimentalized, the ending converted from irony to recognition, and a liturgy of grief bolted between the acts. Every element of the compromise served the taste of his audience, and every element purchased the play's survival in his century at a price in meaning that later centuries itemized and repaid.

The cliché his publicity coined, Garrick the pure restorer, should be retired in favour of the record's more demanding formulation. Garrick restored Shakespeare to the stage by the only means his stage understood, which was adaptation, and the adaptation was good enough, faithful enough in bulk and brilliant enough in effect, to carry the play across the century in which its modern fame was made. Shakespeare's text survived Garrick because print preserved it and because the version's success kept the title alive until taste revolved; the play survived Otway because Garrick ended Rome. That double debt is the balanced verdict. He was not the play's rescuer from alteration. He was its most consequential alterer, whose alterations happened, for ninety-seven years, to be the form in which the world agreed to love it.

The century framed itself between two critical sentences that this article has already quoted, and they make a fitting close. In 1770 Francis Gentleman could defend the funeral procession as the device that made the play thoroughly popular, trusting that three-fourths of every audience preferred sound and show to sense and imagination, and the defence was reasonable because the receipts agreed with it. In 1845 George Fletcher could dismiss the whole Garrick enterprise as the meretricious vanity of a mere actor, and that was reasonable too, because the culture had changed its mind about what an actor was for. Between those sentences lies the adaptation's real biography: not a text corrupted and then purified, but a settlement between a great play and a great theatrical culture, honoured while the culture lasted and dissolved when it ended. Michael Burden's work on the procession's influence finds its patterns alive as late as Garrick's own Shakespeare Jubilee pageant of 1769, the festival that crowned the author's national cult. The cult and the adaptation were the same project, and posterity kept the cult while discarding the adaptation that had built it. Recovering the ledger, row by row, is the price of understanding what the cult cost and what it bought. The adaptation's afterlife in the material record, from Bell's annotated prompt copy in New York to the Folger's toy-theatre sheets, keeps supplying fresh evidence by which that price and that purchase are audited.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Garrick ledger, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Garrick ledger is the table in this article that sets out the adaptation's operations in one view: the restored Verona, the erased Rosaline, the cut wordplay and bawdy, the reduced Mercutio, the smoothed verse, the tightened feud frame, the sentimentalized Juliet, the added masquerade dance, the expanded tomb scene, the sixty-seven added lines, and the funeral procession with its dirge. The pattern the rows expose is directional. Every cut moves toward gravity, purity and pathos, removing laughter, desire and blame from the lovers, while every addition moves toward spectacle and sentiment. Garrick did not alter at random. He re-engineered the tragedy to a single emotional specification, and the ledger's columns of evidence let a reader test that claim change by change rather than take it on trust.

### Q: What words did Garrick place on the title page of his printed Romeo and Juliet, and what do they admit?

The title page of the version printed at the end of 1750 reads "Romeo and Juliet. By Shakespear. With Alterations, and an Additional Scene: As it is Performed at the Theatre-Royal in Drury-Lane." The wording admits alteration openly while framing it as a subordinate detail between the author's name and the theatre's, and the single addition it advertises is the funeral procession scene rather than any rewritten verse. The formula never mentions Theophilus Cibber, whose altered text underlay the enterprise, or Thomas Otway, from whom the waking Juliet descended. The title page is therefore both a disclosure and a piece of branding: honest that the text was altered, silent about whose hands had altered it before Garrick's.

### Q: Who played Romeo and Juliet in the first Garrick production at Drury Lane in 1748?

Spranger Barry played Romeo and Susannah Cibber played Juliet when Garrick staged the play at Drury Lane in the 1748-49 season. Garrick managed the house and directed the enterprise but did not take the lover's part himself until the autumn of 1750, after Barry had defected to Covent Garden. The casting matters to the version's history because the production that restored the play was built on two other performers' fame: Barry's tender, silver-voiced lover and Cibber's Juliet, the age's most admired instrument of pathos. Garrick's first Romeo and Juliet was a managerial triumph before it was an actor's one.

### Q: What did Theophilus Cibber's 1744 version change before Garrick staged the play?

Theophilus Cibber's version, staged at the Haymarket Theatre on 11 September 1744 with Cibber as Romeo and his daughter Jenny as Juliet, kept Otway's device of Juliet waking before Romeo dies and omitted the references to Rosaline, as the Yale Shakespeare editors summarized its alterations in 1917. Cibber's venture was closed under pressure from the patent theatres, but the text survived as a going concern, and the evidence indicates that Garrick's 1748 Drury Lane staging used Cibber's version, as Cibber himself claimed on the title page of his 1748 edition. The two most famous strokes of the later Garrick adaptation thus stand in a Cibber document first, whoever sharpened them afterward.

### Q: How many lines does the lovers' added conversation in Garrick's tomb scene run to?

The added conversation runs to sixty-seven lines, according to the Library of Congress survey of its Garrick printings. In Garrick's closing scene Juliet wakes after Romeo has swallowed the poison but before he dies, and the lovers exchange recognition, explanation and farewell across those sixty-seven lines before Romeo dies and Juliet turns the dagger on herself. The passage is the version's most durable interpolation: the Library of Congress counts no fewer than seven printings that include it, and editions of 1794, 1814, 1819 and 1874 adopted Garrick's alterations, carrying the conversation in print long after the stage had begun to surrender it.

### Q: Why does Garrick's Romeo say that the apothecary's drugs are not so quick?

Because in Garrick's ending Romeo must stay alive long enough to hold a sixty-seven-line conversation with the waking Juliet, and a poison that acts instantly makes the conversation impossible. Shakespeare's Romeo dies on the line "O true apothecary! Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3), the speed of the poison sealing the play's irony of mistimed knowledge. Garrick's text reverses the statement to "not so quick", slowing the drug to the pace his design requires. The three altered words are the adaptation in miniature: where Shakespeare's verse enacts a world in which speech arrives too late, Garrick's rewrites the sentence so that speech can happen, trading the irony of timing for the pathos of a final exchange.

### Q: Who wrote the words and the music of the Drury Lane funeral dirge?

Garrick wrote the words himself and William Boyce composed the music for the Drury Lane procession introduced in the autumn of 1750. The dirge opens "Rise, rise heart-breaking sighs", was sung by seven of the company's leading singers, and was advertised from the production's third night as part of "the Funeral Procession to the Monument of the Capulets". Boyce's setting survives only in manuscript, held by the Bodleian Library. The rival Covent Garden dirge the same season used different words set by Thomas Arne, opening "Ah hapless maid", so the two houses competed in music as in acting, each procession with its own commissioned score.

### Q: What did John Rich answer when George Anne Bellamy credited her acting for a run's success?

Rich answered, "Yes Mistress! but it is owing to the procession." Bellamy, who played Juliet at Covent Garden from the autumn of 1754, tells the story in her Apology: when she implied in conversation with Rich that the success of the run was due to her acting, the manager took a pinch of snuff, turned on his heel, and delivered the deflation. The anecdote, polished as memoir anecdotes are, still records a manager's honest arithmetic. The funeral procession, not any single performance, was the attraction that filled the house season after season, and Rich, the great showman of the age, knew precisely which part of his production the public was paying to see.

### Q: What share of Drury Lane's receipts did Romeo and Juliet take in its first Garrick season?

The play took a twelve per cent share of Drury Lane's overall receipts in the 1748 season, and it was the most performed of the forty-eight mainpieces the house staged that year, on Fiona Ritchie's calculation in Women and Shakespeare in the Eighteenth Century. The figures convert the restoration story into economics. A tragedy effectively absent from the repertory for decades, displaced by Otway's Roman rewrite, returned under Garrick's management as the theatre's leading earner. That commercial weight explains what followed: Covent Garden buying the rights within a year, the rival productions of 1750, and the procession invented to defend the asset. The Garrick version was a property before it was a text.

### Q: Why did Covent Garden buy the rights in Garrick's version in 1749?

Covent Garden bought the rights because the play had become the most valuable Shakespeare property in London, and John Rich intended his own mounting of it. George Winchester Stone Jr.'s research dates the purchase to before 23 November 1749, within a year of the Drury Lane premiere. Rich's house was the home of spectacle, and the acquisition set up the contest of autumn 1750, when Covent Garden's Barry and Susannah Cibber faced Garrick and George Anne Bellamy in rival productions and both houses armed the play with a funeral procession. The purchase is the clearest documentary proof that Garrick's restoration had succeeded commercially before his own printed text existed: the asset traded on the production's fame.

### Q: What did Tate Wilkinson remember about the rival funeral processions of 1750?

Tate Wilkinson, who as a stage-struck boy had frequent access to Covent Garden, remembered that Rich's procession was very grand, unequalled in spectacle, but that Garrick did not promise any procession or dirge in his bills, though the unadvertised scene gave "a striking effect and an agreeable surprise". His testimony has usually been read as proof that Garrick copied Rich's idea at short notice. The playbill evidence cuts the other way: Drury Lane's first-night advertisement warned gentlemen off the stage on account of the scenery and music used in the play, suggesting a procession prepared but deliberately unannounced. Modern study of the episode concludes that both managers knew the other's plans and both mounted the scene within days.

### Q: How did strolling companies manage the funeral procession when their troupes were tiny?

They reduced the procession to its irreducible minimum: a bier, a bell, and every available body. John Bernard's Retrospections describe a strolling company of the early 1770s so small that Juliet had to sing her own dirge while Romeo, who could not appear in the train because he was needed alive in the following scene, tolled the funeral bell from offstage, with the heroine represented on the bier by a dummy under a pall. Samuel Ryley's memoir of strolling players records a company that substituted an iron pot struck with a rolling pin for the bell. The anecdotes measure the procession's grip: companies that could afford nothing else still could not afford to omit the scene.

### Q: How does Garrick's printed version treat the part of Mercutio?

It reduces the part, as the Library of Congress survey of the Garrick printings summarizes the version. Mercutio is the chief carrier of the play's wordplay and sexual wit, the two elements Garrick's announced policy targeted when he undertook to clear the play of its jingle and quibble and its indecency. Trimming the Queen Mab brilliance and the prose fencing shrank the character who in Shakespeare threatens to outshine the lover, and the reduction serves the adaptation's central design: a smaller Mercutio leaves Romeo less examined by his sharpest friend, so the absolved, single-hearted hero of the Rosaline cut stands at the centre of the play unironized. The gain was focus. The loss was the tragedy's intelligence about its own hero.

### Q: What was the "New Masquerade Dance proper to the Play" advertised in 1748?

It was a dance inserted into the Capulet ball in Garrick's first Drury Lane production, advertised in the General Advertiser in December 1748 as a new masquerade dance fitted to the play. The patent theatres built musical and dance entertainments into their evenings as a matter of course, and Garrick wove the attraction into Shakespeare's feast rather than appending it as an afterpiece, so that the lovers' meeting gained a choreographed frame. The detail survives because Baldwin and Wilson's study contrasts it with the bills of 1750: in 1748 the dance required no warning against gentlemen standing on the stage, while the later production's scenery and music did, a small documentary clue to the scale of what Garrick added two years afterward.

### Q: Which later printings kept Garrick's added deathbed conversation?

Editions of 1794, 1814, 1819 and 1874 adopted Garrick's alterations, including the deathbed conversation, as the Library of Congress records from its holdings, and its survey counts no fewer than seven printings containing the added scene. Prefaces attached to these editions defended the retention, some on grounds of taste and some on the argument that Garrick's ending stood closer than Shakespeare's to the Italian sources of the story, in which the lovers do meet awake. Print surrendered the interpolation later than the stage did. Charlotte Cushman had restored Shakespeare's conception of the ending in performance in 1845, yet Garrick's conversation remained available in reading editions into the 1870s.

### Q: Where does Boyce's setting of Garrick's dirge survive?

Boyce's setting of "Rise, rise heart-breaking sighs" survives only in manuscript, held in the Bodleian Library, while the rival Covent Garden setting by Thomas Arne was published in the mid-1760s. The contrast explains a feature of the procession's provincial spread. The words of the dirges were in print quickly and could be reset by local composers, which is why Edinburgh heard Nicolo Pasquali's setting of the Covent Garden words by 1751 and Newcastle heard Charles Avison's setting of Garrick's words in 1755. London's original music travelled badly; the scene itself, a bier, friars, torches and a tolling bell, travelled everywhere. The procession outlived its scores because its essence was choreography and grief, not any particular notes.

### Q: What did Elaine McGirr argue about Garrick's claim to have restored Shakespeare?

Elaine McGirr argued, in "'What's in a Name?': Romeo and Juliet and the Cibber Brand" in the journal Shakespeare in 2017, that the restoration claim was a competitive weapon in a family and repertory struggle. On her account Garrick chose the play as a vehicle for Susannah Cibber and as part of his campaign of restoring Shakespeare from the alleged taint of Cibberian adaptation, yet the return of the play had begun as a Cibber enterprise in 1744, the 1748 staging used Theophilus Cibber's version, and Susannah's Juliet was the draw. Garrick's pose as cleanser converts a takeover of a rival's going concern into a personal crusade. The argument corrects the origin story while leaving the 1750 printed text, and its century, to be explained.

### Q: How long did Garrick's version hold the British stage, and whose restoration closed the span?

Garrick's version held the British stage for ninety-seven years from its first performance in 1748, and Charlotte Cushman's restoration of Shakespeare's conception in 1845 closed the span. Cushman, playing Romeo herself, restored the silent tomb in which the lovers die without the waking conversation, reclassifying Garrick's most beloved scene from improvement to interpolation. The ninety-seven years measure an era of licensed alteration: the procession, the erased Rosaline, the reduced Mercutio and the expanded farewell were not survivals clinging to the play but the play itself for three generations of playgoers. What Cushman ended was less a text than a theory of editing, the theory that a great actor-manager's taste outranked the author's page.

### Q: When did Rosaline return to the printed play, and what else did that edition restore?

Rosaline returned in an edition of 1882, which also let Romeo die, as Shakespeare wrote the scene, immediately after the poison and the kiss, without the deathbed conversation Garrick had added, as the Library of Congress survey records. The date deserves emphasis because print was the Garrick version's last stronghold. The stage had recovered Shakespeare's ending under Cushman in 1845, but reading editions went on adopting Garrick's alterations into the 1870s, and only in 1882 did a printed text restore both the hero's first love and the hero's unwitnessed death. Rosaline's return to the page is therefore a convenient single marker for the moment the Garrick century ended in both media, the play's first act and its last scene recovered together.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Garrick ledger to argue that Garrick restored the repertory rather than the text?

An essay should sort the ledger's rows by kind. The rows that restore are institutional: Verona recovered from Otway's Rome, the play returned to the repertory as a leading earner, the tragedy fixed on the stage for ninety-seven years. The rows that alter are textual: Rosaline erased, wordplay and bawdy cut, Mercutio reduced, verse smoothed to the point of reversing the apothecary line, sixty-seven lines interpolated, a procession added. Because the first group describes what Garrick did for the play's public existence and the second describes what he did to its words, the ledger supports the precise thesis that his achievement was repertory restoration executed through continuing adaptation. The essay should then weigh the two columns against each other, conceding that no restoration was available to his century except through alteration, and judging the price by the losses the ledger itemizes.
