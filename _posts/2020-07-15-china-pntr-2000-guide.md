Permanent Normal Trade Relations with China changed the legal footing of trade between the two countries without changing the tariff charged on a single shipment. For twenty years before permanent status took effect, goods from China entered the United States at normal column 1 rates because a president renewed a waiver each year. Those rates were real, and trade moved under them, but the authority behind them ran for one cycle at a time. The statute ended that yearly permission. After its central provision took effect, normal treatment rested on ordinary law, and no annual renewal stood between an importer and the rates printed in the schedule.

![Permanent Normal Trade Relations With China Explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-36.webp)

The formal measure was the United States-China Relations Act of 2000, Public Law 106-286, 114 Stat. 880, enacted as H.R. 4444 and signed by President Bill Clinton on October 10, 2000. Its official title described a law to authorize extension of nondiscriminatory treatment, called normal trade relations treatment, to the People's Republic of China and to establish a framework for relations between the two countries. Section 101 terminated the application of chapter 1 of title IV of the Trade Act of 1974 to China. Section 102 tied that termination to a condition outside the statute itself: accession by China to the World Trade Organization. The law therefore decided the domestic question in 2000, while the change in treatment waited for the international step that the text required.

![Customs and congressional records tracing how permanent normal trade relations changed the legal status of imports from China](/assets/images/blog-01.webp)

The negative fact sits at the center of the record. The act lowered no United States tariff. Applied rates on goods from China were identical on the day before permanent status took effect and on the day after, because column 1 rates already applied under the waiver. No schedule in the act cut a duty, phased a duty down, or set a future date for a United States rate to fall. China, under commitments negotiated in a bilateral agreement signed in Beijing on November 15, 1999 and in its multilateral accession terms, cut its own tariffs and opened agriculture, services, distribution and other sectors. Those concessions belonged to the accession package. They were not rate cuts written into Public Law 106-286.

The distinction matters because the risk removed was large even though the price charged was unchanged. Behind the waiver stood column 2 of the tariff schedule, rates set largely in the Tariff Act of 1930 and far above the normal rates. A failed renewal would have moved imports from China to that higher schedule in one step. Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott, in the American Economic Review in July 2016, measured the average gap between column 2 and applied normal rates at about 33 percentage points across industries, with a standard deviation of about 15 points. A firm signing a supply contract, building a plant, or shifting a production stage had to price that possible jump into a decision whose returns would arrive over years. Ending the annual vote removed the jump from the calculation.

Congress paired the status change with machinery for the relationship that followed it. The act created the Congressional-Executive Commission on the People's Republic of China to monitor human rights and the development of the rule of law, required an annual compliance report from the United States Trade Representative on China's World Trade Organization commitments, and added a temporary China specific safeguard against market disruption. China acceded to the World Trade Organization on December 11, 2001. On December 27, 2001, President George W. Bush signed the proclamation that ended the application of title IV to China, and permanent status took effect on January 1, 2002. A reader who holds those pieces together can see the shape of the change at once: rates unchanged, annual renewal ended, revocation risk removed, and monitoring substituted for a yearly vote on treatment itself.

## The Annual Waiver Regime and the Yearly Vote on Trade Treatment

For two decades before permanent normal trade relations took effect, trade treatment for China rested on a permission renewed on a fixed calendar, under a condition written for a different purpose. The governing framework was Title IV of the Trade Act of 1974, and its central provision for this purpose was section 402, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2432 and named for Senator Henry Jackson and Representative Charles Vanik. That provision set no tariff rate for any product and negotiated no market access. Instead, it set a threshold condition for a class of countries. A non-market economy country that denied freedom of emigration was barred from nondiscriminatory tariff treatment, described in the statute and in later debate as normal treatment, and it was also barred from United States government credits. The bar was general in form, and its application to China followed from the classification, not from a China specific finding written into section 402 itself.

Understanding the waiver regime starts with the architecture of the trade statute into which Title IV was placed, because the Trade Act of 1974 combined negotiating authority, tariff treatment, and conditions on eligibility in a single framework that later Congresses inherited as a whole; readers who want that wider structure can consult [the Trade Act of 1974 complete guide](/2020/06/01/trade-act-1974-complete-guide/) for the act as a complete system. Within that system, Title IV dealt with trade relations with countries that did not have nondiscriminatory treatment at the time the act was framed. Chapter 1 of Title IV, the part later terminated as applied to China by the statute at the center of this article, contained the eligibility rules, the emigration condition, and the waiver authority that made conditional access possible. The design therefore had three moving parts that must be kept separate. First came a statutory denial, written as the default position. Second came a presidential power to suspend that denial for a limited period. Third came a congressional power to block the suspension, exercised through legislation rather than through a simple vote of objection. Much of the history between 1980 and the end of the century is the history of those three parts working against one another.

The freedom of emigration condition was the substantive test at the center of section 402. In plain terms, a covered country was expected to allow its citizens to leave, and a denial of that freedom triggered the statutory bar on normal tariff treatment and on government credits. The condition operated as a gate rather than as a tariff schedule. A country that failed the test did not face a somewhat higher duty on selected goods under section 402; it faced loss of the nondiscriminatory category itself, with the tariff consequences that followed from falling outside that category. The condition also operated before any question of product competition or market opening arose. Those subjects entered the China debate in later years through other routes, but the legal mechanism that forced an annual decision was the emigration provision of Title IV. That mismatch between the legal trigger and the wider subjects argued around it became a recurring feature of the renewal process. Members of Congress who wished to vote on the relationship as a whole had an annual vehicle for doing so, yet the vehicle was built around a single named condition and a presidential waiver of that condition.

The presidential waiver was the mechanism that kept trade functioning inside that restrictive design. Section 402 allowed the President to waive the bar, and in practice the waiver for China was extended on an annual cycle, with an extension running from July 3 each year. The date mattered because it turned eligibility into a calendar event. Traders and members of Congress could all see the next decision point approaching, and that approach organized testimony, lobbying, and floor debate into a seasonal pattern. A waiver did not amend the statute. It did not repeal the emigration condition, remove China from the class of countries covered by Title IV, or convert conditional treatment into a permanent entitlement under domestic law. It suspended the effect of the bar for the period covered, allowing normal rates to apply while the underlying statutory structure stayed in place. When the period ran toward its end, the same question returned in the same form, the intended consequence of a time limited waiver in a statute whose default position was denial.

### Why did an emigration provision govern trade with China?

Section 402 attached trade benefits to freedom of emigration for non-market economy countries as a class, without naming any single country. Because China fell within that class and lacked a separate exemption, its access to normal tariff treatment passed through the same statutory gate, renewed by waiver rather than granted permanently.

The congressional check on a waiver extension was a joint resolution of disapproval, and its form explains much of the outcome across twenty annual cycles. A joint resolution is not a sense of Congress statement and not a committee report. To take legal effect it must pass both chambers in identical form and be presented to the President, like other legislation. Congress could therefore overturn an extension only by enacting that resolution, with the full weight of the legislative process behind that word. The President who had just issued or extended the waiver was then asked to sign a measure repudiating that decision. A veto was the expected response. Once a veto occurred, the resolution needed the support of two thirds of both the House and the Senate to become law over the President's objection. The arithmetic favored the waiver at every stage. Supporters of renewal needed to hold only one chamber against a disapproval resolution, or, failing that, to hold one third plus one in a single chamber against an override. Opponents needed successive majorities and then successive supermajorities, in both chambers, within the window the procedure allowed.

That asymmetry did not make debate pointless, but it did separate the power to force an argument from the power to change the legal result. A disapproval resolution could command a majority in one chamber and leave the waiver untouched. It could pass both chambers and fail at the veto stage. It could even survive a veto in the House and fail in the Senate. The mechanism also shaped strategy for both sides. Opponents could use the resolution to build a public record and to press an administration for concessions outside the resolution itself, even when the legal outcome was not in serious doubt. Supporters of renewal could concentrate on the narrower task the arithmetic set for them, defending the waiver where defense was cheapest rather than winning every argument on the merits in both chambers.

For China, the waiver system became operative through a bilateral trade agreement signed in 1979, which took effect on February 1, 1980. From that point, Presidents issued the Jackson-Vanik waiver for China every year from 1980 through 2001. The practical effect of each waiver was access to column 1 rates, the normal rates applied to goods from countries receiving nondiscriminatory treatment. The alternative was not a modest surcharge. Without a waiver, the default was column 2 of the tariff schedule, rates set largely in the Tariff Act of 1930, the statute associated with Smoot and Hawley, and far above the normal rates. The distance between those two columns is what gave the annual decision its economic weight. No one needed to propose a new tariff law each summer for tariffs to become an urgent question. The existing schedule already contained the higher rates, waiting behind the waiver, and the calendar posed only whether the suspension of the bar would be extended again or the default would return.

The distinction between the rates in force and the legal security behind them deserves emphasis, because later arguments about permanent treatment turned on it almost entirely. Under the waiver, Chinese goods entered at normal rates in fact, year after year, and trade could and did proceed on that basis. What the waiver could not supply was durability. A contract or investment that depended on normal rates remaining in place rested on a sequence of future presidential decisions and future congressional non-actions, each one scheduled and open to challenge. The risk was not that rates changed frequently; the waiver issued every year without interruption from 1980 through 2001. The risk was that rates could change through a single break in that sequence, and that the size of the change was fixed in advance by the gap between column 1 and column 2. Annual repetition therefore produced a paradox. The system looked stable in its outcomes, because renewal happened every year, and unstable in its legal foundations, because nothing in a completed renewal bound the next one.

The character of the annual fight changed after June 1989. Following the Tiananmen crackdown of that month, the renewal that had operated as a recurring trade procedure became a contested congressional vote on the whole relationship between the United States and China. The statutory question on the calendar had not changed. Section 402 asked about a waiver of the emigration bar, and the President acted on the July 3 cycle. What changed was the range of subjects that members brought to that vote and the intensity with which outside groups treated the vote as a judgment on Chinese policies and on American policy toward China more broadly. Trade, human rights, political prisoners, and weapons questions all entered arguments formally attached to a tariff eligibility decision. The waiver process could absorb that wider argument because it required an annual, public, recorded decision. It could not resolve that wider argument, because the only legal outcomes available were extension or disapproval of the extension under the Title IV mechanism, neither of which wrote a detailed China policy into law.

The first post-1989 disapproval efforts set the pattern for the decade. The House passed joint resolutions of disapproval in 1991 and again in 1992. The Senate never passed one. That paired result, a House majority for disapproval in two successive years alongside a Senate record that kept every resolution from enactment, showed the mechanism working exactly as its arithmetic predicted. A chamber that wished to register opposition could do so, and its vote entered the political record and signaled the strength of feeling among members and their constituents. Yet a single chamber's passage changed no tariff, ended no waiver, and imposed no new condition, because the joint resolution had to complete the full legislative path to have legal effect. Across the life of the regime, the Senate never passed a disapproval resolution on the China waiver. Supporters of renewal needed to hold the Senate, or the veto, or an override vote, and in those years they held the first of those lines.

A second strategy moved beyond disapproval toward conditional status, and its fate supplied a second demonstration of the same arithmetic. In 1991 and 1992 Congress passed legislation that would have attached conditions to China's trade status rather than simply terminating the waiver outright. President George H. W. Bush vetoed both measures. The House then voted to override the veto on both occasions, and the Senate did not override, so both measures failed and the waiver system continued on its existing terms. The sequence separates three positions that debate often blurred together. One position was unconditional renewal under the existing waiver. A second was outright disapproval, ending normal treatment through the Title IV route. A third was renewal with legislated conditions, which sought to use the annual pressure of the waiver to obtain specified changes while keeping trade in place. That third position could assemble ordinary majorities in both chambers and an override majority in the House. It could not, in either year, assemble the final supermajority in the Senate that enactment over a veto required.

President Clinton entered the same structure and tested a version of the conditional approach from inside the executive branch. In 1993, by executive order, Clinton linked the 1994 renewal to human rights conditions. The linkage was significant precisely because the underlying statute had not changed. The condition in section 402 concerned emigration, while the 1993 order tied the coming renewal decision to a broader set of human rights expectations, using the President's control over the renewal decision itself. For supporters of linkage, the annual cycle offered a scheduled moment, fixed by law, at which an administration had to decide and defend its decision in public, an opportunity that a permanent grant of treatment would not offer. The 1993 order therefore sharpened the underlying question rather than settling it. If renewal was to be conditional in practice, on what conditions, judged by whom, and with what consequence when an administration concluded that the conditions and the renewal could no longer be held together?

The answer came on May 26, 1994. On that date Clinton renewed the trade status and formally delinked renewal from human rights, keeping only a ban on imports of Chinese munitions. The decision left the Title IV waiver mechanism intact; renewals after 1994 proceeded under Jackson-Vanik, ran on the July 3 cycle, and exposed the administration of the day to a disapproval resolution in Congress. What delinkage changed was the stated basis on which the executive branch would make its renewal decision. Human rights concerns were to be pursued, in the administration's stated approach, through channels other than the annual threat to tariff treatment, and the single trade restriction expressly kept was the munitions import ban. Opponents of renewal lost the argument that the administration itself had accepted the previous year, namely that renewal and human rights performance were formally linked, while supporters of renewal gained a presidential statement that separation of the two questions was official policy. The statutory gate, however, admitted no such separation on its face. As long as Title IV applied, an annual waiver was required, and Congress retained its annual occasion.

Debate through the rest of the decade confirmed that retention in practice. Disapproval resolutions were introduced, debated, and defeated after 1994, and two examples illustrate the pattern. H.J. Res. 96 in 1995 and H.J. Res. 79 in 1997 were both adversely reported by the Committee on Ways and Means and were not enacted. An adverse report placed the committee of jurisdiction on record against the resolution before the wider House considered the question. The resolutions in those years functioned much as the earlier resolutions had functioned. They supplied a recorded vote and extended argument over the whole relationship. They did not supply an easy path to enactment. Between the committee position, the need to pass both chambers, and the veto arithmetic waiting at the end of the process, a late decade disapproval resolution faced every obstacle that the resolutions of 1991 and 1992 had faced, with the added weight of a presidential delinkage policy stated in 1994.

A separate development then made continuation of the waiver system legally untenable in its existing form, apart from whether any participant preferred it. The World Trade Organization operates on a requirement that members extend nondiscriminatory treatment immediately and unconditionally. An annual waiver, revocable through a disapproval process and conditioned on a domestic statutory test, is the opposite of unconditional treatment in structure, even in years when the waiver issues routinely and rates never change. As China's accession to the World Trade Organization approached, the consequence followed directly: the annual waiver regime could not survive that accession unchanged. If the United States wished to receive the benefits of the commitments China undertook as part of accession, it could not keep China's eligibility for normal treatment on a year to year waiver under a provision that allowed the treatment to lapse. The alternative paths were unattractive in different ways. The United States could invoke non-application as between itself and China within the organization, or it could forgo the benefits of China's accession commitments while other members received them. Neither path preserved the existing arrangement, and the decision could not be postponed past accession without consequences.

The structural problem also reframed what a vote for permanent treatment would decide. Ending the waiver regime as applied to China did not require any member to conclude that the annual debates had been mistaken. It required a judgment about legal compatibility and about which instruments should carry which subjects. Other subjects could be placed in provisions designed for them, as the 2000 statute in fact did elsewhere in its own structure. Tariff eligibility, once China entered an organization built on unconditional nondiscriminatory treatment among members, could not remain on an annual emigration waiver without breaking the form that membership required. Earlier votes had decided whether a waiver would survive another year. The approaching accession forced a vote on whether the waiver system itself could remain the legal basis of the relationship at all.

The prior regime thus left a record that is best read on two levels at once. On the surface, it was a sequence of renewals unbroken from the February 1, 1980 effective date of the bilateral agreement through the waiver issued for 2001, punctuated by House disapproval votes in 1991 and 1992, by two vetoed conditional status bills and two House override votes that the Senate did not match, by a linkage order in 1993 and a delinkage decision on May 26, 1994, and by further disapproval debates in the years after delinkage that ended without enactment. Beneath that sequence sat a design whose outcome those events repeatedly confirmed: a default statutory bar in section 402 of Title IV, a presidential waiver that suspended the bar from July 3 for a single cycle at a time, and a congressional check that required enactment of a joint resolution over a likely veto to bite. The design gave Congress an annual forum and every President an annual decision, while giving traders the normal column 1 rates in practice and none of the legal permanence that a settled tariff relationship ordinarily supplies. When the unconditional treatment rule of the World Trade Organization met that design, the question stopped being how the next waiver would fare and became what would replace the waiver altogether, and that replacement question is the one the 2000 statute answered.

## What the Statute Changed in Law and What It Left Untouched

The United States-China Relations Act of 2000, Public Law 106-286, is best understood by separating the legal change it made from the economic change many readers assume accompanied it. The act changed the status under which goods from China entered the United States. It did not change the tariff rates applied to those goods on the day its central provision took effect. That separation is not a technical footnote. It is the statute. Permanent Normal Trade Relations, in this instance, meant that a treatment already being applied year by year under a presidential waiver would instead apply as ordinary law, without an annual renewal, without a recurring congressional vote on disapproval, and without the standing threat that a failed renewal would return imports from China to far higher rates. The rates stayed where they were. The risk attached to those rates was removed. A reader who misses that distinction will misread both the narrowness of the legislative act and the scale of the consequence that researchers later traced to the removal of uncertainty.

The structure of the legislation shows the same point in statutory form. The act has two divisions. Division A contains the trade status change itself, in Title I. Division B, the United States-China Relations division, builds a framework around that change, with general provisions, a new commission, and duties for monitoring and enforcement. Congress did not pass a bare tariff bill. Congress passed a status bill accompanied by institutions and reporting obligations meant to govern the relationship after the annual vote disappeared. Division B filled part of the oversight gap left by ending the annual Jackson-Vanik debate, although it did not recreate the leverage of a vote that could end normal treatment. A report and a commission can inform Congress and the public. Neither can, by itself, restore column 2 rates or suspend normal treatment.

Division A, Title I, is compact, and its four sections should be read in order. Section 101 is the operative provision. It terminates the application of chapter 1 of title IV of the Trade Act of 1974, cited at 19 U.S.C. 2431 and following, to China. Section 102 sets the effective date for that termination, and in doing so makes the termination conditional rather than immediate. Section 103 addresses relief from market disruption and adds a China-specific safeguard as section 421 of the Trade Act of 1974, cited at 19 U.S.C. 2451. Section 104 amends section 123 of the Trade Act, concerning compensation authority. Read together, the four sections do three things. They end one legal regime as applied to one country, they tie that ending to an external event specified in the statute, and they leave or create trade remedy and compensation mechanisms that operate apart from the status question. The brevity of Division A is instructive. A measure that rewrote the tariff schedule would have required schedules and product by product amendment. Division A required none of that, because no United States rate was being rewritten.

Section 101 deserves close attention for both its reach and its limits. It terminated the application of the Jackson-Vanik provisions to China. It did not repeal title IV of the Trade Act of 1974 for all countries, and it did not erase the statute under which non-market economy countries that denied freedom of emigration could be barred from nondiscriminatory treatment and from United States government credits. The centerpiece of that earlier regime, section 402 of the Trade Act, cited at 19 U.S.C. 2432, remained part of the Trade Act as a general framework. What ended was its application to China. The distinction between repeal and termination of application is a standard legislative technique, and in this case it carried practical meaning. Congress acted country by country, because the waiver history, the bilateral agreements, and the accession negotiations all concerned China specifically. The act therefore left the older statutory architecture standing while removing China from under it, once the condition in section 102 was satisfied.

Before that termination, the governing arrangement rested on a waiver. Under the Jackson-Vanik framework, the President could waive the bar on nondiscriminatory treatment, and a waiver extension ran from July 3 each year, subject to congressional disapproval through a joint resolution enacted over a possible veto. For China, presidents issued the waiver every year from 1980 through 2001, following a bilateral trade agreement signed in 1979 that took effect on February 1, 1980. In each of those years, the practical result was the same. Goods from China received column 1 rates, the normal rates in the tariff schedule. The legal foundation for that result, however, was temporary by design. It depended on an annual presidential act and on Congress choosing not to overturn that act. The treatment was normal in its rates and abnormal in its footing. Every year the footing had to be laid again, and every year the alternative remained visible in the schedule itself.

That alternative was column 2. Column 2 contained the rates that applied in the absence of normal treatment, and those rates had been set largely in the Tariff Act of 1930, commonly associated with Smoot-Hawley. Column 2 was not a modest penalty rate or a surcharge of a few points. It was a separate and much higher schedule, preserved across decades while negotiated normal rates fell or were held well below it. So long as the waiver operated, column 2 rates for China were a background threat rather than an applied reality. Importers did not pay them. Firms deciding where to build a plant, sign a supply contract, or commit capital over a period longer than a single waiver cycle nevertheless had to account for them, because a failure of renewal would have moved applied rates from column 1 to column 2 in one step, without a new tariff statute and without a product by product negotiation. The act removed that step from the future by removing the annual process on which it depended.

The size of the threatened step can be stated with precision because researchers measured it. Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott, in work published in the American Economic Review in July 2016, defined the gap between column 2 rates and applied normal rates as the NTR gap and calculated an average gap of about 33 percentage points across industries, with a standard deviation of about 15 percentage points. Those figures describe variation as well as scale. Some industries faced gaps well above the average and some faced gaps well below it, which is why Pierce and Schott could later compare outcomes across industries with larger and smaller gaps. What Congress removed was not a small procedural inconvenience. It was exposure to a very large, industry specific tariff increase that existing law made possible every year.

Against that background, the central negative fact about the act should be stated without qualification. The act lowered no United States tariff. Applied United States rates on Chinese goods were identical the day before and the day after permanent status took effect, because normal column 1 rates already applied under the waiver. No importer received a new rate under the act. No product moved from a higher applied rate to a lower applied rate by reason of section 101. No schedule in the act cut a duty, phased a duty down over a transition period, or set a date on which a United States duty would fall. Statutes that liberalize trade in the conventional sense usually do at least one of those things, whether directly or by approving an agreement that does them. This statute did none of them on the United States side. Its liberalizing effect, to the extent researchers and contemporaries described one, ran through certainty. It told firms that the rates they already faced would not be withdrawn through the annual waiver process, and that the column 2 schedule would no longer stand behind the column 1 schedule as the consequence of a failed renewal.

### If no rate changed, what exactly became permanent?

What became permanent was the legal basis for normal treatment itself. The waiver, renewed year by year and exposed to disapproval, gave way to a termination of the Jackson-Vanik application to China, so normal rates applied as ordinary law rather than as a presidential exemption renewed under congressional threat of reversal.

The answer in that paragraph is deliberately narrow, because permanence in the act had a specific legal object. What became permanent was not a tariff rate in the sense of a number frozen against future legislation. What became permanent was the end of a condition. Under the waiver system, normal treatment for China existed only for so long as the President renewed it and Congress allowed the renewal to stand. Under the act, after the effective date, normal treatment existed because the statutory bar no longer applied to China at all. The difference appears in the questions a firm or an official would have to ask after January 1, 2002, the date permanent status took effect. Before the change, the relevant question was whether renewal would occur in the next cycle. After the change, that question no longer arose under title IV. There was no next cycle for China, no extension to disapprove, and no date each summer on which treatment would lapse unless an affirmative presidential act preserved it.

Section 102 explains why that result did not follow immediately from enactment on October 10, 2000, when President Bill Clinton signed the measure. The termination in section 101 was tied to accession by China to the World Trade Organization. That condition linked the domestic statute to an international process that the statute itself did not control. Congress could authorize permanent normal treatment, and did, but the authorization would operate only when China became a WTO member and the reciprocal framework that justified the change was in place. The reason for the condition lies in the logic of WTO membership. WTO law requires members to extend nondiscriminatory treatment immediately and unconditionally, so the annual waiver regime could not survive accession by China unchanged. Without permanent treatment, the United States would have had to invoke non-application or deny itself the benefits of the accession commitments negotiated with China. Section 102 avoided that result prospectively. It placed the domestic change on the same timeline as the international change, so that neither would occur in isolation.

The condition was satisfied in the sequence the statute anticipated. Accession by China to the WTO came on December 11, 2001, after approval at the Doha ministerial in November 2001. On December 27, 2001, President George W. Bush signed the proclamation terminating the application of title IV to China and extending permanent normal trade relations, effective January 1, 2002. Those dates, and the order among them, matter for a correct reading of the act. Enactment in 2000 created the authority and the condition. Accession in December 2001 satisfied the condition. The proclamation later that month executed the change, and the first day of 2002 was the first day on which permanent status governed as a matter of domestic law. Accounts that compress those steps into a single moment in 2000 obscure the conditional design Congress chose. The vote in 2000 was a commitment to a future legal state, contingent on an event outside the control of Congress, rather than an immediate alteration of treatment on the day the bill became law.

The one way direction of the tariff concessions in the wider package reinforces the same reading. The United States changed no rate. China, by contrast, cut its own tariffs and opened sectors under commitments negotiated in two settings that should not be confused with the act itself. The first was bilateral. On November 15, 1999, the United States and China signed a bilateral agreement in Beijing, negotiated for the United States by Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky, covering the terms on which the United States would support accession and the market opening China would undertake. The second was multilateral, in the accession terms through which China entered the WTO and accepted obligations toward all members. Under those commitments, China reduced its own tariffs and opened agriculture, services, distribution, and other sectors to greater foreign participation. None of those reductions was a reduction in a United States duty, and none was enacted by Public Law 106-286 as a change to the United States schedule. The act was the domestic legal step that allowed the United States to receive the benefit of concessions made by China, on a nondiscriminatory basis consistent with WTO membership, without holding its own treatment of China hostage to an annual waiver that WTO rules would not accommodate.

Title II of Division B contains general provisions, including findings, statements of policy, and definitions. Provisions of that kind do not themselves grant or withdraw tariff treatment. They frame the purposes Congress attached to the new framework, supply the defined terms the later titles use, and assign continuing work to institutions created or directed elsewhere in the division.

Title III of Division B, beginning at section 301 of the act, establishes the Congressional-Executive Commission on the People's Republic of China. The commission was designed as a mixed body rather than as a committee of one branch. Its composition under the act was nine senators, nine members of the House of Representatives, and five senior administration officials appointed by the President, drawn from the Departments of State, Commerce, and Labor. Its mandate was to monitor human rights and the development of the rule of law in China, to submit an annual report to the President and to Congress, and to maintain a political prisoner database. Each element of that mandate connects to a strand of the debate that had run through the annual waiver votes of the preceding decade, when renewal debates had become contests over the whole relationship, including human rights. The commission did not continue that contest in the same form. It replaced the possibility of an annual sanction, in the form of lost trade status, with a standing obligation to document and report. Whether that substitution was adequate was contested at the time, but the institutional choice itself is plain on the face of the act. Congress chose monitoring without a recurring status vote over monitoring attached to a recurring status vote.

Title IV of Division B carries monitoring and enforcement concerning compliance by China with its WTO commitments, and Title IV is where a persistent numbering confusion must be cleared away. Under Title IV, section 421 of the act itself imposes an annual compliance report duty on the United States Trade Representative, concerning compliance by China with its WTO obligations. That section 421 belongs to Public Law 106-286. It is a reporting provision in the act that created permanent status. Separately, section 103 of the act added a different section 421, section 421 of the Trade Act of 1974, cited at 19 U.S.C. 2451, which created the China-specific safeguard against market disruption. That second section 421 belongs to the Trade Act. It is a remedy provision, under which an investigation could lead to added duties or quotas where increased imports from China were a significant cause of material injury under the market disruption standard. The two provisions share a number by coincidence of codification in different statutes, and they perform opposite kinds of work. One requires the executive branch to report to Congress on whether China kept its commitments. The other authorizes a process that could restrict particular imports from China. A passage that merges them will attribute a trade remedy to a reporting duty, or a reporting duty to a trade remedy, and will misstate the architecture Congress enacted. Keeping the two apart is therefore not pedantry. It is the only way to describe Title IV and section 103 accurately in the same account.

What the word permanent meant, as a matter of law, needs the same care. Permanent did not mean irreversible. Congress retained its full constitutional authority to legislate on foreign commerce, to amend the Trade Act, to alter tariff treatment by a later statute, and to enact new conditions or restrictions if a later majority chose to do so and a later law survived any required presidential action or veto override. No Congress can bind a later Congress by ordinary legislation, and Public Law 106-286 contains no exception to that principle. A later statute could have changed the treatment of imports from China the day after permanent status took effect, just as a later statute can change any other rule of trade law. Permanence, in the act, described the absence of a built in expiration and the absence of a recurring condition, not the presence of a legal barrier against future legislation. Under the waiver, treatment ended unless it was renewed. Under the act, treatment continued unless Congress affirmatively changed the law. The burden of legislative action had been reversed, and that reversal, rather than any claim of irrevocability, is what the word permanent carried.

Taken together, the act did one central thing and built a framework around it. Section 101 ended the Jackson-Vanik application to China, section 102 made that ending effective on accession to the WTO, accession came on December 11, 2001, and the presidential proclamation of December 27, 2001 gave permanent status effect on January 1, 2002, while Division B added general provisions, the Congressional-Executive Commission, and monitoring and compliance reporting, and section 103 preserved a separate and temporary safeguard in the Trade Act. The act did not lower any United States tariff, because column 1 rates already applied under the waiver, and the change it made was to remove the annual renewal, the risk of revocation, and the threat of reversion to column 2 rates that Pierce and Schott measured at an average gap of about 33 percentage points. Permanent, accordingly, meant that normal treatment rested on ordinary law rather than on a yearly exemption, subject as all ordinary law is subject to change by a later Congress, and that distinction between a rate unchanged and a status transformed is the distinction on which any sound account of the statute depends.

## The passage campaign and the two roll calls

The campaign in Congress began with a negotiation concluded in Beijing half a year before any bill existed. On November 15, 1999, United States Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky signed a bilateral agreement on the terms under which China would enter the World Trade Organization. The agreement was the precondition for everything that followed in Washington, because it settled, between the two governments, the commitments China would carry into its multilateral accession. Under those commitments, China cut its own tariffs and opened agriculture, services, distribution and other sectors. The United States changed no tariff rate in the agreement. American duties on Chinese goods were already the normal column 1 rates, applied year by year under a presidential waiver, and the rates charged the day before permanent status took effect were the same rates charged the day after. What the agreement placed before Congress was a choice about permanence: whether normal treatment, renewed annually for two decades, would become unconditional in domestic law, so that the United States could take the full benefit of the accession commitments negotiated in Beijing and in the multilateral talks.

That choice carried a legal edge both sides acknowledged. World Trade Organization law required members to extend nondiscriminatory treatment to one another immediately and unconditionally. An annual American waiver, subject each year to a possible congressional vote of disapproval, sat uneasily beside that requirement. Without permanent normal trade relations, the United States would have had to invoke non-application toward China or deny itself the benefits of the accession commitments its own negotiators had obtained. Supporters presented that prospect as a reason to act before accession was complete. Opponents presented the annual process as the reason to wait, because the yearly renewal was the instrument through which Congress had debated the whole relationship, including human rights and labor questions, since the crackdown at Tiananmen Square in June 1989. The House had passed joint resolutions of disapproval in 1991 and 1992, and Congress had passed conditional status legislation in both years, only for President George H. W. Bush to veto each measure. The 2000 bill proposed to end that annual forum for China, and the contest over ending it became the central argument of the campaign.

President Bill Clinton opened the formal campaign on March 8, 2000, when he transmitted his proposal for permanent normal trade relations to Congress. The transmittal moved the question from negotiation to legislation, and the President campaigned for the proposal personally. Contemporaneous reporting in The New York Times counted meetings or calls with well over 100 House members as part of that effort. The scale of the lobbying matched the arithmetic the administration faced in the House, where the President's own party was divided and where the measure could not pass on Democratic votes alone. Administration officials argued that the accession agreement served American economic interests because the concessions ran toward market opening in China, in agriculture, in services and in distribution, while American tariff rates stayed unchanged. They argued that bringing China into the World Trade Organization placed bilateral trade inside a rules based system, with commitments the United States could invoke and monitor. They argued that engagement through trade offered a better path on human rights than the annual confrontation over renewal, and that the bill added new monitoring machinery aimed at those questions.

The supporting coalition gave that campaign its organized presence in the districts. Support came from the United States Chamber of Commerce, the Business Roundtable and the United States-China Business Council, together with high technology and agriculture groups. Those organizations argued that the accession commitments opened the Chinese market to American farm products, manufactured goods and services on terms American exporters could not obtain if the United States stood aside from the accession settlement. Farm organizations stressed the agriculture provisions of the November 1999 agreement, under which China accepted tariff cuts and wider access for imported farm goods. Technology and services firms stressed the openings in distribution and services. Business supporters also argued that annual renewal created uncertainty for firms planning sales, sourcing and investment, because a failed renewal would have returned Chinese goods to column 2 rates set largely under the Tariff Act of 1930. In their stated case, permanent status removed that recurring risk while leaving American applied rates exactly where they had been, and the coalition directed that case at undecided House members in both parties through the spring.

Organized labor led the opposition, and its campaign reached into the same undecided offices from the opposite direction. The AFL-CIO, under its president John Sweeney, organized against the bill and argued that permanent status would reward a government whose labor practices the federation condemned, while exposing American workers to a sharper flow of imports produced under conditions American law did not permit at home. Labor's stated case held that the annual renewal vote gave Congress a recurring point of pressure on human rights, on freedom of emigration and on workplace conditions, and that surrendering the vote surrendered that pressure without enforceable labor protections in its place. The federation and its allies also warned that making normal treatment permanent would encourage American firms to shift production toward China and weaken the bargaining position of American workers facing import competition, a line of argument that readers can place in a longer statutory context through [the history of United States labor legislation since 1950](/2015/05/15/us-labor-legislation-since-1950/). The opposition case, like the supporting case, was directed less at the tariff schedule, which neither side disputed would stay unchanged, than at the consequences of permanence.

Inside the House, the opposition found its leadership in the Democratic ranks. Minority Leader Richard Gephardt and Minority Whip David Bonior led the campaign against the bill within the chamber, arguing that the annual process should be retained or replaced with enforceable conditions rather than ended. Their stated objections joined the human rights record of the Chinese government to the labor and trade concerns raised by the unions. They argued that Congress had used the yearly debate after 1989 precisely because the relationship raised questions beyond tariffs, and that a permanent grant, decided before accession and without a recurring vote, removed the occasion for that scrutiny. They pointed to prison labor imports, to restrictions on emigration and expression, and to the absence of independent unions as grounds for withholding permanent treatment. Supporters answered that the annual vote had never in fact revoked China's treatment, that the disapproval resolutions and conditional bills of the early 1990s had all failed or fallen to vetoes, and that a threat never carried out carried little weight in Beijing. A Gallup poll in May 2000 found the public leaning toward the supporters' position, with 56 percent in favor and 37 percent opposed, figures reported by Gallup for that month.

The legislative vehicle reached the House on May 15, 2000, when Representative Bill Archer of Texas, the chairman of the House Committee on Ways and Means, introduced H.R. 4444. Its official title described a measure to authorize extension of nondiscriminatory treatment, called normal trade relations treatment in the title itself, to the People's Republic of China, and to establish a framework for relations between the United States and that country. Section 101 terminated the application of chapter 1 of title IV of the Trade Act of 1974 to China, on the condition that the termination take effect at China's accession to the World Trade Organization, and companion sections set the effective date, added a China specific safeguard against market disruption and adjusted compensation authority. A second division carried framework provisions on relations, including findings, policy statements and definitions, together with monitoring titles that later additions enlarged.

The Committee on Ways and Means reported H.R. 4444 favorably on May 22, 2000, by 34 to 4, recorded in H. Rept. 106-632. A margin of that size signaled that the core trade provision commanded broad support among the members most practiced in tariff legislation, and it gave the floor campaign a bipartisan committee endorsement to cite. The four negative votes showed that opposition reached into the panel itself, though far below the division expected on the floor. With the report filed, only the terms of floor consideration remained open. Members concerned about human rights monitoring, compliance and prison labor sought additions that could be attached without reopening the trade title itself, and the result was a package brought to the floor through the rule governing debate.

That package took the names of its two House sponsors, Representative Doug Bereuter, a Republican of Nebraska, and Representative Sander Levin, a Democrat of Michigan, and it entered the bill through the special rule for consideration, H. Res. 510. The House adopted the rule on May 23 to 24, 2000, by 294 to 136, and adoption of the rule added the Bereuter-Levin provisions to the measure under consideration. The margin on the rule exceeded the margin the bill itself would receive a day later, a pattern that reflected the package's purpose. Its provisions gave members who supported permanent treatment, but who also wanted a continuing institutional response on human rights and compliance, a set of commitments to cite in explanation of a yes vote. The additions did not alter section 101, did not condition termination of the annual waiver process on any certification, and did not change any tariff rate. They built a structure of commissions, reports and statements around the grant. Labor's leadership treated the package as insufficient because none of its elements restored an annual vote or attached enforceable labor conditions to the grant itself. The rule vote thus functioned as a preliminary roll call on the amended form of the bill.

The Bereuter-Levin additions covered five subjects. The first created the Congressional-Executive Commission on the People's Republic of China, composed of nine senators, nine House members and five senior administration officials appointed by the President from departments including State, Commerce and Labor, and charged the commission with monitoring human rights and the development of the rule of law in China, reporting annually to the President and Congress, and maintaining a database on political prisoners. The second added monitoring and enforcement provisions for China's World Trade Organization commitments, including compliance review and an annual compliance report by the United States Trade Representative under section 421 of the act, a provision distinct from the China safeguard that the trade title placed in the Trade Act. The third established a task force directed at prohibiting imports produced with prison labor. The fourth stated the position that Taiwan's accession to the World Trade Organization should follow immediately after China's accession. The fifth authorized $99 million in fiscal 2001 for international broadcasting into China. Taken together, the provisions answered the monitoring side of the opposition argument while leaving the central grant unconditional.

House passage came on May 24, 2000, when the chamber approved H.R. 4444 by 237 to 197, with 1 member not voting, in roll call 228 as recorded by Congress.gov. Republicans voted 164 to 57 in favor, supplying the larger part of the majority, while Democrats voted 73 to 138 against the position their own President had transmitted in March. Independents voted 0 to 2. In proportional terms drawn from the same roll call, roughly three of four Republicans voted yes and roughly two of three Democrats voted no. The result vindicated the administration's decision to lobby the House personally and at length, because the margin depended on holding Republican support while limiting Democratic defections to a number the Republican yes votes could absorb. It also confirmed the standing of Gephardt and Bonior as leaders of a majority within their own caucus on this question, and the 40 vote margin was narrow enough that a shift of 21 votes would have reversed it.

Attention then moved to the Senate, where the committee process had run on a parallel track. The Senate Committee on Finance reported its own bill, S. 2277, on May 17, 2000, by 19 to 1, recorded in S. Rept. 106-305. The Senate bill was a clean measure, carrying the trade grant without the additions the House rule had attached, and its committee margin was even wider than the margin in Ways and Means. Ordinarily, two chambers that pass different texts resolve the differences in a conference committee, whose report must then pass both chambers again. A conference carried risks that Senate floor managers weighed through the summer. The House additions had been assembled to hold a particular coalition together, and reopening the text invited changes from either direction, from members who wanted conditions attached to the grant and from members who wanted the monitoring provisions removed. The Senate leadership chose a simpler path. Rather than pass S. 2277 and negotiate, the Senate would take up H.R. 4444 itself and, if it passed that bill without amendment, send the identical text directly to the President.

### Why did the Senate take up the House bill instead of its own?

The Senate Finance Committee had reported S. 2277 by 19 to 1, according to its committee report. Taking up H.R. 4444 instead meant the Senate could pass identical text the House had already approved. If the Senate amended that text, the differences would require a conference committee and further House action.

The choice of vehicle shaped the endgame. Every amendment offered on the floor became a decision about whether to preserve final passage without a conference, since any successful amendment would send the bill back across the Capitol. The clean Senate bill never became the vehicle for passage, and the Bereuter-Levin provisions, which a clean bill would have left behind, stayed in the text under debate. Opponents therefore had to defeat the whole House package or amend it at the cost of a conference. That frame held through a debate that stretched across the summer and into September.

Senate floor action began in earnest in late July. On July 27, 2000, the Senate invoked cloture on the motion to proceed by 86 to 12, a margin wide enough to show that a filibuster against taking up the bill had no prospect of success. The motion to proceed itself was agreed to on September 7, 2000, by 92 to 5. Debate then ran for 11 days, during which the Senate considered 19 amendments and rejected every one. The rejected amendments included proposals on sanctions for weapons proliferation, on prison labor and on human rights certifications, subjects that reached back to the objections Gephardt, Bonior and the labor campaign had pressed in the House. Each rejection kept the House text intact. Supporters argued that conditions and certifications belonged outside the grant itself, in the monitoring structure the Bereuter-Levin provisions had created and in other laws. Opponents argued that amendments were the last opportunity to attach consequences to the commitments under discussion. The unbroken record of rejections converted the procedural choice of vehicle into the substantive result: the bill that reached a final vote was the bill the House had passed in May, word for word.

The Senate passed H.R. 4444 on September 19, 2000, by 83 to 15, with 2 senators not voting, in record vote 251 as recorded by Congress.gov. Republicans voted 46 to 8 and Democrats voted 37 to 7, and the 2 senators who did not vote, Senators Akaka and Lieberman, were both Democrats. The contrast with the House roll call was sharp. In the Senate the grant drew majorities of both parties, and the Democratic division that had defined the House campaign narrowed to a 37 to 7 split in favor. The overall margin was the widest recorded at any stage of passage, far wider than the 237 to 197 House division. Because the Senate had passed the House bill without amendment, no conference committee met, no conference report required a further vote in either chamber, and the enrolled bill went to the President in the form the House had approved on May 24, with the decisive resistance concentrated in the first chamber and the second chamber supplying an emphatic confirmation.

President Clinton signed the bill on October 10, 2000, and H.R. 4444 became the United States-China Relations Act of 2000, Public Law 106-286. The signing completed a sequence that had begun with the transmittal of March 8, passed through the Archer introduction of May 15 and the two committee reports, and ended with the Senate vote of September 19. The statute's trade provision operated on the condition built into section 101: termination of the application of title IV of the Trade Act of 1974 to China took effect at China's accession to the World Trade Organization, an event the act anticipated but did not itself accomplish. Congress in 2000 decided the domestic law question, whether annual renewal and the threat of a return to column 2 rates would end, while the international step that would trigger the change remained ahead. For supporters who had organized around the November 1999 agreement, the signature secured the American side of the bargain before accession. For opponents who had organized around the annual vote, the signature closed the last stage at which that vote could have been preserved in its existing form.

The passage record shows a campaign decided by sequence and by coalition as much as by argument. A negotiated agreement in Beijing on November 15, 1999 fixed the terms and forced the question, and a presidential transmittal on March 8, 2000, backed by personal lobbying of well over 100 House members, supplied the executive pressure. A business and agriculture coalition and an opposing labor coalition, led in the House by Gephardt and Bonior and measured in public by Gallup at 56 percent in favor and 37 percent opposed in May 2000, supplied the outside and inside campaigns. Committee margins of 34 to 4 and 19 to 1 showed early institutional support, the Bereuter-Levin package adopted through a 294 to 136 rule vote supplied the monitoring commitments that accompanied the grant, and the roll calls of 237 to 197 and 83 to 15 recorded two chambers answering the same text in very different party patterns. Neither campaign persuaded the other, and the record attributes no tariff change to the outcome. What the sequence changed, by the statute's own terms, was the permanence of the treatment and the structure of scrutiny built around it.

## The Removal of Revocation Risk and the Logic of Exposure

The central puzzle in the economic account of permanent normal trade relations is that the statute produced large effects through a channel that left every applied duty unchanged. A conventional trade liberalization lowers a rate, makes an imported good cheaper at the border, and gives buyers and producers a direct reason to reorganize around the new price. The United States-China Relations Act of 2000 did none of that work through prices. Goods from China had entered under normal rates during the annual waiver years, and goods from China entered under the same normal rates after permanent status took effect. The change lay instead in what could happen next. Before permanent status, each year carried a formal possibility that renewal would fail and that a much higher set of rates would return. After permanent status, that formal possibility was gone. Explaining how a removed possibility could move employment, imports, and production decisions is the task that Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott set for themselves, and their answer turned on measuring how large the threatened increase would have been in each industry.

Pierce and Schott published that answer as Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott, "The Surprisingly Swift Decline of US Manufacturing Employment," American Economic Review, July 2016, volume 106, number 7, pages 1632 to 1662. The article had circulated earlier as a working paper in 2012, identified on the record for this series as National Bureau of Economic Research working paper 18655. According to Pierce and Schott, manufacturing employment in the United States fell from 19.6 million in 1979 to 13.7 million in 2007 on that Bureau of Labor Statistics series. The long span matters because the authors did not argue that permanent status explained the whole movement across three decades. Their question was narrower and concerned speed and timing. Within the longer fall, they drew attention to the sharp loss around the start of the 2000s, reporting from the same Bureau of Labor Statistics data that 1.5 million manufacturing jobs were lost in the first year of the 2001 recession. Pierce and Schott noted that the figure exceeded the 900,000 manufacturing jobs lost in the first year of the Great Recession on the comparison presented in the paper. A loss of that scale in a single year, in a sector already contracting over the long term, called for an explanation that could account for the timing after 2001 and for the uneven incidence across industries producing different goods.

The vocabulary of the argument begins with the two columns of the United States tariff schedule. Column 1 is the set of normal rates, the rates applied to goods from countries receiving nondiscriminatory treatment, also called normal trade relations treatment. Column 2 is the set of non-normal rates, the much higher rates reserved for countries without that treatment. Pierce and Schott described those column 2 rates as largely set in the Tariff Act of 1930, the statute associated with the Smoot-Hawley tariff. It was an inherited schedule, preserved for countries outside normal treatment, whose levels stood far above the normal rates that trade actually faced year after year under the waiver system. China received the column 1 rates through the annual presidential waiver of the Jackson-Vanik restrictions, and presidents issued that waiver every year from 1980 through 2001. The legal foundation for that treatment, however, was a yearly act of renewal that Congress could contest, and the alternative embedded in the schedule was the column 2 treatment that no shipment had to bear as long as renewal succeeded.

That yearly structure created a particular kind of risk, distinct from the ordinary commercial risks that importers and producers manage in any period. Revocation risk concerned the policy rule itself. If the annual renewal failed, the tariff treatment of an entire category of goods could move from column 1 to column 2, not by a small adjustment negotiated over years, but by reversion to the standing non-normal schedule. The size of that reversion differed sharply by product, which gave Pierce and Schott a way to measure exposure industry by industry. They called the measure the NTR gap. In plain words, the NTR gap for an industry is the difference between the column 2 rate that would have applied if normal treatment had been withdrawn and the applied normal rate that did apply under the waiver. A large gap meant that a failed renewal would have produced a large increase in the duty on goods in that industry. A small gap meant that even a failed renewal would have changed the duty by little, because the inherited non-normal rate and the normal rate stood close together. Across industries, Pierce and Schott reported, the average gap was about 33 percentage points, with a standard deviation of about 15 percentage points. The spread around that average meant the threat was severe for some industries and minor for others.

The deterrence prediction follows from the timing of business commitments, and Pierce and Schott stated the logic in terms of decisions that have to be made before any tariff increase would have occurred. A United States firm that wanted to source components or finished goods from China had to find suppliers, assess their reliability, arrange specifications and inspection, organize shipping, and in many settings commit money to tooling, contracts, or distribution before the first large shipment arrived. Continuing imports required a similar commitment of managerial time and working capital. Offshoring, meaning the movement of a production stage to a supplier or affiliate in that country, required a still deeper commitment, because equipment, training, and quality systems had to be put in place in a new location. Each is harder to justify when the rule is subject to an annual political test whose failure would raise the duty by an amount large enough to erase the cost advantage that motivated the move. Pierce and Schott argued that firms facing a large possible tariff jump had reason to hold back from those commitments even while the low applied rate was in place, because the relevant calculation was not the duty paid in a good year but the expected cost across good and bad outcomes, weighted by the chance that renewal might fail and by the loss that failure would impose on a partly built supply relationship.

The same logic explains why the deterrent effect would have been uneven, and the unevenness is what allowed the authors to test the argument against industry data. If uncertainty about renewal mattered, it should have mattered most where the NTR gap was largest, because that is where the cost of a bad outcome was greatest. A firm in a low gap industry could treat a failed renewal as an unwelcome expense and still plausibly continue the relationship. A firm in a high gap industry faced a different calculation, since a failed renewal could multiply the duty burden and strand investments made on the assumption that normal rates would continue. Before permanent status, rational caution therefore predicted a pattern of restrained engagement concentrated in the high gap industries: fewer new importing relationships, less willingness to shift production stages, and more reliance on suppliers in places whose tariff treatment was not subject to the same yearly test. It would appear as projects deferred, contracts kept short, dual sourcing maintained at extra cost, or a decision to expand a domestic plant rather than reorganize supply around a lower cost source whose access could be withdrawn. Pierce and Schott treated that quiet restraint as the baseline before permanent status, against which behavior after the change could be compared. Removing the threat should therefore relax that restraint first where the gap had been largest.

### How can removing a threat move factories without moving a single rate?

Permanent status left every applied duty unchanged, so no importer gained a lower price at the border. What changed was the calculation behind long commitments. Once renewal could no longer fail, firms could build supply relationships, sign longer contracts, and shift production stages without pricing in a sudden return to much higher duties.

Pierce and Schott kept that distinction at the center of the paper. In a tariff cut, the sequence begins with a lower duty, passes through a lower landed cost for the imported good, and reaches the buyer as an incentive that exists even for a single transaction with no future commitment. A purchaser with no relationship to the seller and no plan beyond one shipment can respond to a lower rate at once. In the revocation risk mechanism, the sequence begins with an unchanged duty and a changed expectation about duties in future years. The value of a relationship that will generate shipments for years is different, because the purchaser no longer has to discount that value for the chance of a reversion to column 2. Sourcing decisions, which are decisions about relationships and capacity rather than about one shipment, are therefore the natural place for the effect to appear. Investment decisions respond for the same reason, because their returns arrive over years that annual renewal placed in doubt. Pierce and Schott argued that the policy changed behavior by changing the horizon over which private commitments could be planned, not by changing the price written on a customs entry in the first week after enactment.

The date structure of the statute gives the argument its before and after. The termination of the Jackson-Vanik application to China was conditional on accession to the World Trade Organization. That country acceded on December 11, 2001. On December 27, 2001, President George W. Bush signed the proclamation terminating the application of title IV to China and extending permanent normal trade relations effective January 1, 2002. Pierce and Schott organized their comparison around the change that those dates completed, treating the period after 2001 as the period in which the annual threat no longer governed decisions. Firms did not need years of experience under the new regime before revising their plans, because the legal source of the risk had been removed rather than merely left unused for a season. Permanent status removed the yearly test itself. That compression is consistent with the swift character of the employment decline in the title Pierce and Schott gave their paper, since a stock of postponed decisions, once released, can produce a faster change than a flow of entirely new opportunities appearing one by one.

The employment finding was the first and most consequential result Pierce and Schott reported. Industries with larger NTR gaps lost significantly more employment after 2001 than industries with smaller gaps. The comparison is between industries, not between the years before and after in the aggregate, and that design is what ties the finding to the mechanism rather than to a general downturn that pressed on all producers at once. The 2001 recession, weak demand, and other economy wide forces could explain a broad fall in factory employment across the board. Those forces, on their own, do not explain why the fall should have been systematically deeper in the industries where the threatened tariff increase had been largest, unless exposure to the removed threat is part of the story. The industries for which a failed renewal would once have been most costly were the industries that contracted most after the failure became impossible. High gap industries had been held back, in part, by that caution. When that caution was withdrawn along with the threat, the reorganization proceeded and employment fell by more in those industries than in industries where little restraint had been imposed and little was therefore released.

The trade and firm findings reported alongside the employment result fill in the steps between a changed expectation and a changed payroll. Pierce and Schott found that higher gap industries saw larger increases in imports from China after 2001. They also found movement on both sides of the importing relationship in counts of firms. The number of United States firms importing from China rose by more in the more exposed industries, which indicates that the response was not confined to a fixed set of established importers buying larger volumes from partners they already used. On the other side of the trade route, Pierce and Schott found that the number of firms in China exporting to the United States also rose by more in those industries, including foreign owned firms operating in China. A change driven only by established buyers placing bigger orders might have shown volume growth without much change in firm counts. A change driven by removed uncertainty should show entry, because uncertainty deters the formation of relationships in the first place, and its removal allows relationships that had never been attempted to begin. The pattern the authors reported, with volumes and counts moving together and moving most where the gap was largest, matches the entry account more closely than an account built only on larger orders within old ties.

Adjustment inside the United States, in the authors' findings, operated through two margins that are worth separating because they describe different experiences for workers and communities. The first margin was plant exit. Some plants in more exposed industries closed entirely after 2001, taking their whole payrolls out of the industry counts. The second margin was reduced employment at continuing plants. Plants that survived in more exposed industries employed fewer workers, as production volumes shifted, as stages of work moved to suppliers abroad, or as managers reorganized the work that stayed. Pierce and Schott found evidence of both margins in the higher gap industries. Alongside those margins, the authors reported evidence consistent with offshoring by United States firms and with a composition shift in the production that remained: activity moved toward less labor intensive plants and less labor intensive ways of producing. That shift helps explain why employment can fall even where output does not fall by the same proportion. The stages that remained tended to use more machinery and fewer production workers per unit of output.

The European comparison gave Pierce and Schott a control comparison against the most obvious alternative reading of the timing. The alternative reading holds that something about China itself changed around 2001, and that importers everywhere responded in the same industries for reasons unrelated to United States tariff policy. If that reading were sufficient, a similar industry pattern should have appeared in another large importing economy whose firms faced the same exporters in the same years. Pierce and Schott reported that the European Union supplied the comparison and did not show the pattern. The European Union had granted that country the equivalent of normal status in 1980, two decades before the period in question, so European importers had faced no annual renewal threat of the United States kind and had no such threat removed after 2001. In the authors' comparison, the European Union shows no parallel post 2001 pattern linking industry exposure to the employment and import movements found in the United States data. A shock located in the exporting country should travel to both destinations. A shock located in the removal of a United States specific uncertainty should appear in the United States data and not in the European data, and that is the configuration Pierce and Schott reported.

Several cautions belong with the findings. Pierce and Schott did not claim that the applied tariff fell, and any summary that describes permanent status as a round of rate reductions misstates the statute and the paper at once. The applied rates were the column 1 rates before the change and the column 1 rates after it. They did not claim that uncertainty was the only force acting on factory employment in the 2000s, and their own context figures placed the episode inside a decline that began in the late 1970s and included the ordinary losses of recessions. The 19.6 million figure for 1979 and the 13.7 million figure for 2007, drawn from Bureau of Labor Statistics data as used in the paper, mark a long contraction within which the movement after 2001 was one especially swift segment. The gap is an industry level measure built from tariff schedule differences, with a mean of about 33 percentage points and a standard deviation of about 15 percentage points in the authors' reporting, and results stated at that level describe tendencies across many establishments. Individual plants faced individual demand, management, and location conditions that the measure does not capture.

In synthesis, Pierce and Schott located the economic action of permanent normal trade relations in the removal of revocation risk rather than in any reduction of applied duties. Column 1 rates governed trade before and after, column 2 rates stood as the inherited alternative largely set in 1930, and the NTR gap between them, averaging about 33 percentage points across industries in the authors' measure, graded each industry by how much it stood to lose from a failed annual renewal. While that risk stood, it deterred the supply chain commitments, importing relationships, and offshoring decisions that low applied rates alone might have encouraged, most strongly where the gap was widest. When permanent status removed the risk effective in 2002, the deterred behavior was released in the same graded pattern, and the higher gap industries recorded the largest employment losses, the largest import increases, and the largest growth in participating firms on both sides, with adjustment through plant exit and smaller continuing plants and a shift toward less labor intensive production. The European Union, which had granted the equivalent status in 1980, showed no parallel pattern. Prices did not fall because a duty fell. Decisions changed because a risk disappeared.

## Concentrated Losses in Exposed Local Labor Markets

A large part of the research on the consequences of permanent normal trade relations asked what happened in the American places that faced the sharpest rise in imports from China, rather than what happened in the national economy taken as a whole. Import competition did not arrive evenly. Factories and their suppliers were clustered in particular towns and industrial regions, and workers lived and searched for work within labor markets far smaller than the country. A national average could therefore combine very different local experiences into a single figure that described none of them well. The researchers reviewed in this section chose the local labor market as the unit of observation, traced employment, wages, household income, and transfer receipt across places with different levels of exposure, and then extended the local design into a national accounting of job losses. Read together, the studies presented adjustment that was concentrated in space, spread across sectors inside the exposed places, and slow to reverse within the periods the authors examined.

The scale of the trade change behind the research was large and rapid. Census Bureau figures reported by the Congressional Research Service put United States goods imports from China at about $100 billion in 2000 and about $321.5 billion in 2007, a rise of more than three times in seven years. The same figures placed the share of United States imports coming from China at about 8 percent in 2000 and about 16 percent in 2007, so that country doubled its share of the American import market during the same interval. Imports on that scale did not by themselves prove that any particular worker lost a job, and the authors of the local studies did not treat the totals as proof. The totals instead set the question: when imports expand that quickly in product lines that American plants also make, some combination of output, employment, wages, and firm entry or exit has to change somewhere. The commuting zone literature was an attempt to locate those changes, to measure them against places that faced less of the same pressure, and to separate the trade effect from other forces acting on American manufacturing during the same years.

The founding study of the literature was David H. Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon H. Hanson, "The China Syndrome: Local Labor Market Effects of Import Competition in the United States," published in the American Economic Review in 2013, volume 103, number 6, pages 2121 to 2168. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson divided the continental United States into 722 commuting zones, areas built from counties linked by the journeys residents made to work, so that each zone approximated a market in which people looked for jobs and employers looked for workers. The authors then compared changes across those zones over two periods, 1990 to 2000 and 2000 to 2007. The first period gave the design a baseline decade in which imports from China were rising but from a smaller base. The second period covered the years after the statute took effect, when the import totals reported by the Congressional Research Service rose most steeply. Stacking the two periods allowed the authors to ask whether places that were more exposed in each period changed in the same direction in both, a check that a single decade, with its particular business cycle, could not provide on its own.

Exposure in the 2013 study was a constructed measure, and the construction shaped what the findings could and could not say. For each commuting zone, Autor, Dorn, and Hanson combined the national growth of imports from China in each industry with the industries that employed the zone population at the start of a period, and they expressed the result as growth in imports from China per worker. A zone whose workers had been concentrated in furniture, apparel, electronics assembly, or other industries facing fast import growth received a high exposure value. A zone centered on health care, education, local government, or services sold mainly to nearby customers received a low value, even though residents of both zones bought imported goods as consumers. The measure therefore captured competition felt through production and employment, not competition felt through store prices. It also rested on starting industry shares, on the logic that a place inherited an industrial structure from its past, and that the later national import surge struck that structure with a force the place itself did not choose.

A central problem for any such comparison is that imports do not rise at random. American demand for a product can pull in imports at the same time that weak demand is cutting domestic employment, and a simple correlation between import growth and job loss could then record American conditions twice rather than a foreign supply effect once. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson addressed the problem with an instrument built from import growth in other high income countries. Where imports from China grew in the same industries in those other economies, the authors treated the common movement as evidence of a supply side expansion in Chinese production and exporting capacity, rather than a movement specific to demand inside the United States. The local exposure measure was then predicted from that external import growth, combined with the same starting industry shares, and employment and wage changes were related to the predicted component. The authors also stated the limit that followed from the design: the estimates described the relative experience of more exposed places compared with less exposed places. They were not, without further assumptions, a complete count of jobs gained or lost for the country as a whole.

The employment findings were consistent across the two periods. In "The China Syndrome," more exposed commuting zones had significantly larger reductions in manufacturing employment than less exposed zones over 1990 to 2000 and 2000 to 2007. Alongside the fall in manufacturing jobs, more exposed zones recorded higher unemployment and higher labor force nonparticipation, meaning that a larger share of working age residents were either looking for work without holding it or had left the labor force altogether. That pairing mattered for interpretation. If displaced factory workers had moved at once into other jobs at similar rates of pay, manufacturing employment could fall while total employment and labor force attachment held steady. The authors found a different pattern. Total attachment to work weakened in the more exposed places, which indicated that the manufacturing contraction was not matched, worker for worker, by absorption elsewhere in the same local economy during the periods examined. The place based design made that result visible in a way that an industry only study, tracking jobs by sector without regard to where the workers lived, would have missed.

Autor, Dorn, and Hanson gave readers a benchmark that translated the statistical relationship into a scale that could be compared across places. In their estimates, a $1,000 per worker increase in import exposure reduced the manufacturing share of the working age population by about 0.6 percentage points. The figure is best read as the authors framed it, as a relative effect tied to their exposure measure and to the two periods, 1990 to 2000 and 2000 to 2007, rather than as a prediction for any single town in any single year. A commuting zone facing an exposure rise several thousand dollars per worker above another zone would, on the benchmark, be expected to record a manufacturing share lower by a multiple of the 0.6 percentage point figure. The authors used the share because it tied the industry shock to the whole working age population of a place, including residents who had never held a factory job but whose prospects changed when a large local employer group contracted.

The wage and income findings extended the losses beyond the factory gate. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson found lower wages in more exposed commuting zones, and the reduction appeared in nonmanufacturing as well as in manufacturing. That result followed a local demand logic the authors described. When an import competing employer group cut payrolls, spending by its former workers fell, orders to local suppliers fell, and employers that sold to the local market faced weaker demand for their own labor. The shock could therefore reach retail, construction, transport, and personal services even though firms in those sectors did not compete directly with imports. Household income was lower in more exposed zones, and the combination indicated that other earners inside the household did not fully compensate, on average, for the earnings lost. The authors did not claim that every worker in an exposed zone lost ground. Their claims were about average outcomes across places, and the averages moved downward for wages and for household income in the places facing the larger trade shock.

Public transfer programs absorbed part of the loss, and the transfer findings formed a distinct strand of the 2013 results. More exposed commuting zones recorded higher government transfer receipts, and receipt of disability benefits through Social Security Disability Insurance rose in the more exposed places. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson treated transfer receipt as an outcome of the labor market shock rather than as a separate policy event, because applications and awards followed the deterioration in local employment and earnings. A worker who left a factory job and qualified for disability support appeared in the data as a transfer recipient rather than as an unemployed job seeker, which meant that the unemployment rate alone understated the withdrawal from work in exposed zones. Disability receipt stood out in the authors reporting because it tended to mark a lasting exit from employment rather than a short spell between jobs. Earnings losses partly replaced by public payments were not fully borne by the affected households, but the replacement came through budgets and benefit rolls, as income support rather than new employment in the same place.

### Did displaced workers move to new jobs or new places?

The studies found little movement of either kind. Autor, Dorn and Hanson reported that more exposed commuting zones did not gain offsetting nonmanufacturing jobs and did not lose population through outmigration at rates that restored balance, so displaced workers more often left employment or accepted lower wages where they lived.

Two absences in the findings carried as much weight as the measured declines. The first was the absence of offsetting job growth in nonmanufacturing inside the more exposed commuting zones. Accounts of sectoral change often describe workers leaving a shrinking industry and entering expanding ones, with local service employment growing as manufacturing contracts. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson found little of that offset within the periods studied, which left the manufacturing decline visible in the wider employment figures. The second absence was migration. Population did not move away from the more exposed zones at a pace that equalized conditions across places, so the losses stayed attached to the places where the shock had landed. The authors reported little geographic migration in response to the shock. Together, the two absences helped explain why local effects persisted rather than fading as workers changed sector or changed address.

The local design answered a relative question, and Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon H. Hanson, and Brendan Price extended the work to a national count in "Import Competition and the Great US Employment Sag of the 2000s," published in the Journal of Labor Economics in 2016, volume 34, number S1, pages S141 to S198. The period changed with the question. Where the 2013 study had used 1990 to 2000 and 2000 to 2007, Acemoglu and coauthors examined 1999 to 2011, a span that began just before the statute was enacted and ran through the recession at the end of the first decade and the recovery that followed it. The authors built the national estimate in layers. The starting layer used the same logic of industry exposure that the commuting zone work had developed. Added layers then followed the shock through input output linkages, the connections by which one industry buys materials, parts, and services from other industries, so that a contraction in an import competing industry also reduced demand for domestic suppliers behind it. A further layer counted aggregate demand channels, by which job and earnings losses in affected industries reduced spending and employment in parts of the economy with no direct trade exposure.

The central estimates in the 2016 Journal of Labor Economics study were that rising import competition from China accounted for total job losses in the range of 2.0 to 2.4 million across the whole economy over 1999 to 2011, once input output linkages and aggregate demand channels were counted. Manufacturing accounted for roughly one million of the total, in the authors central estimates. The relationship between the two figures was itself a finding. Manufacturing made up less than half of the central range, which meant that more than half of the estimated national loss arose through the supplier connections and demand effects that the layered accounting was built to capture. Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson, and Price presented the range rather than a single point because the total depended on how the indirect channels were modeled. The estimates were accounting exercises grounded in the exposure design, not a registry of individually identified jobs, framed as the employment effect attributable to the import rise over the stated period against a counterfactual in which that rise had not occurred.

The methods of the two studies differed in scope and their figures are not interchangeable. The 0.6 percentage point benchmark belongs to commuting zones and to an exposure rise of $1,000 per worker. The 2.0 to 2.4 million range belongs to the national economy and to a thirteen year accounting over 1999 to 2011 with channels the local design did not sum. Kept apart, they describe the same shock at two levels: a concentrated local deterioration measured place by place, and a national total that required modeling how the shock propagated through the rest of the economy.

Autor, Dorn, and Hanson returned to the evidence in "The China Shock: Learning from Labor-Market Adjustment to Large Changes in Trade," published in the Annual Review of Economics in 2016. The article was a synthesis rather than a new estimate, and its contribution was to state what the accumulated research implied about adjustment itself. The authors concluded that adjustment to the shock had been slow and incomplete, and that losses persisted in exposed places through the periods the underlying studies covered. In the account given in the synthesis, exposed workers moved to lower paying jobs or out of the labor force rather than to new industries on terms that restored their earlier earnings. Employers in affected local markets did not expand other activities fast enough to absorb the displaced labor, and workers did not reallocate across sectors or regions at the speed that models with frictionless movement assumed. The synthesis used the word shock to mark the size and speed of the import rise documented in the Census Bureau figures reported by the Congressional Research Service, and the gap between expected adjustment and the adjustment that the commuting zone evidence recorded.

Persistence was the thread that tied the synthesis to the earlier empirical work. In the 2013 findings, higher disability receipt and higher labor force nonparticipation were not short term markers, because both described states that workers tended not to leave quickly. In the 2016 synthesis, Autor, Dorn, and Hanson stressed that the exposed local economies had not converged back toward the less exposed ones within the observed periods, and that earnings losses for affected workers were not made up by later gains elsewhere in the same markets. Judged worker by worker in national data, some displaced employees did find new employment, and individual histories varied widely. Judged place by place, the deterioration in employment rates, wages, household income, and benefit receipt formed a coherent local pattern that individual success stories did not erase. The authors argued that both levels of observation were real. Their account left the losses geographically concentrated: the shock had an address, and the address could be named in commuting zone data years after the import rise itself.

Comparison with an earlier trade liberalization sharpened the distinctiveness the authors claimed for the episode. Displacement after the North American Free Trade Agreement had been studied place by place and industry by industry, and readers who want that record set beside the later one can follow [the economic impact of NAFTA](/2020/09/01/nafta-economic-impact/). In the framing used by Autor, Dorn, and Hanson, the import rise from China stood out for its speed, for its concentration in labor intensive manufacturing lines where American employment had been large, and for the breadth of local outcomes that moved together, from factory employment to disability rolls. The comparison was one of degree and of adjustment rather than of kind. In both episodes, trade exposure fell unevenly across places because industry had been unevenly distributed before the policy change. In the later episode, on the evidence of the three studies reviewed in this section, the unevenness proved harder to outgrow, because the affected places generated little replacement employment in other sectors and because workers stayed in place even as local prospects narrowed.

Taken together, the concentrated loss literature rested on a chain of evidence presented with stated methods, periods, and limits. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson showed in the American Economic Review in 2013 that 722 continental commuting zones with greater exposure to rising imports from China, measured per worker and instrumented with import growth in other high income countries, recorded larger manufacturing losses, higher unemployment and nonparticipation, lower wages in and beyond manufacturing, lower household income, and higher transfers and disability receipt over 1990 to 2000 and 2000 to 2007, with a benchmark effect of about 0.6 percentage points on the manufacturing share for each $1,000 per worker rise in exposure. Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson, and Price carried the accounting to the national level for 1999 to 2011 in the Journal of Labor Economics in 2016 and placed total losses at 2.0 to 2.4 million jobs, about one million of them in manufacturing, after counting input output and aggregate demand channels. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson then synthesized the record in the Annual Review of Economics in 2016 as a case of slow, incomplete adjustment in which losses persisted in exposed places. Within the terms those authors set, the literature described a trade change whose costs fell heavily on identifiable places and workers, and whose local effects were visible at the end of every period the studies examined.

## Offsets, Price Effects, and the Safeguard Record

A second body of research asked a different question from the studies of concentrated local loss. Instead of starting with a single commuting zone and tracing what happened to workers in the industries most exposed to imports from China, these studies started with the whole economy, with exports as well as imports, with input linkages between industries, or with prices paid by buyers. Their findings did not erase the local losses described in the companion section. They placed those losses inside a wider accounting, in which the same opening that raised import competition also expanded export demand, lowered prices for manufactured goods, and raised aggregate welfare in a calibrated model, while the gains and the losses fell on different workers, different industries, and different places. The offsetting studies used national accounts, industry trade data, and general equilibrium models rather than the commuting zone comparisons used in the loss studies, so their totals answered a national question: whether demand created elsewhere in the economy matched, exceeded, or fell short of demand destroyed by import competition, and what buyers gained when prices fell. The safeguard record then supplies a separate, institutional test of the adjustment story. The statute created a China specific remedy that was meant to be easier to obtain than ordinary safeguard relief, and the record of its use shows how that remedy operated in practice between enactment and its expiry in December 2013.

Robert C. Feenstra and Akira Sasahara, in "The 'China Shock,' Exports and U.S. Employment," published in the Review of International Economics in 2018, approached the employment question through global input-output accounting. Their data came from the World Input-Output Database, and their accounting period ran from 1995 to 2011. The method traced demand through supply chains across countries and industries, so that an export sale counted not only the jobs in the exporting plant but also the demand it created for domestic inputs, and an import counted the domestic demand it displaced, including demand for inputs that domestic producers would otherwise have supplied. On that accounting, growth in United States exports over the period supported demand for about 6.6 million jobs. The composition mattered to their argument. About 2.0 million of those jobs were in manufacturing, about 0.5 million were in resource industries, and about 4.1 million were in services, a distribution that reflected the weight of services in the wider economy and in the supply chains behind merchandise exports. Against that export supported demand, Feenstra and Sasahara estimated that imports from China reduced demand by about 2.0 million jobs, split between about 1.4 million in manufacturing and about 0.6 million in services. When merchandise export growth was set against imports from that country alone, the authors found that United States merchandise export growth relative to imports from China created net demand for about 1.7 million jobs over 1995 to 2011. The result is a demand accounting rather than a count of individual workers hired or dismissed. It records the labor demand consistent with observed trade flows and input-output relationships, and at that level of aggregation the export side of the ledger was larger than the China import side. Feenstra and Sasahara presented the finding as a correction to any accounting that counted imports without counting exports. In their framing, a shock defined only by rising imports from one partner missed the simultaneous expansion of United States sales abroad during the same years, including sales made possible by the same integration of world production that raised imports.

Robert C. Feenstra, Hong Ma, and Yuan Xu, in "US Exports and Employment," published in the Journal of International Economics in 2019, volume 120, pages 46 to 58, brought the export offset back into the industry and commuting zone frameworks that the loss literature had used. Their question was narrower and, for that reason, closer to a direct comparison. Holding the import side of the earlier commuting zone research in view, they added export growth industry by industry and place by place, and asked whether the places and industries that lost demand to imports from China gained demand through exports to the world. At the industry level, they found that export driven job gains largely offset the job losses associated with imports from China. Their preferred estimate put the net result at about 379,000 jobs gained over 1991 to 2011. The sign of that industry level result deserves emphasis, because the authors reported it as a gain rather than a smaller loss. At the commuting zone level, the same exercise produced a different and more qualified answer. Gains and losses at that local level were roughly balanced, and the preferred estimate was a slight net loss of about 68,000 jobs over the same period, with a wide range across specifications. Feenstra, Ma, and Xu also reported a separate calculation for the shorter period from 1991 to 2007, in which the net result was a loss of roughly 0.2 million to 0.3 million jobs. The contrast between the industry result and the commuting zone result carried an analytical point that the authors stated directly. Exports and imports did not fall on identical local labor markets in identical proportions. An industry could gain nationally through export sales while particular towns that had specialized in import competing production recorded losses that export growth elsewhere did not reach. Aggregation therefore changed the answer, not because either calculation was hidden, but because trade created demand in one set of plants and places and destroyed it in another. Their preferred totals, reported at full strength, support two statements at once: at the level of industries across the national economy, export gains largely offset import losses and left a net gain of about 379,000 jobs over 1991 to 2011, and at the level of local labor markets where workers lived, the balance was close to even, with a small net loss of about 68,000 jobs in the preferred commuting zone estimate.

Lorenzo Caliendo, Maximiliano Dvorkin, and Fernando Parro, in "Trade and Labor Market Dynamics," published in Econometrica in 2019, volume 87, number 3, pages 741 to 835, estimated the same shock inside a dynamic general equilibrium model. Their model allowed workers to move between labor markets and industries over time at a cost, allowed trade to affect prices and production through linked sectors, and was calibrated to the period from 2000 to 2007. Within that structure, Caliendo, Dvorkin, and Parro estimated that the China trade shock reduced United States manufacturing employment by about 0.55 million jobs. They expressed that figure as about 16 percent of the observed decline in manufacturing employment during 2000 to 2007, a share that assigned most of the observed manufacturing decline in those years to causes other than the trade shock while assigning a substantial and identified portion to it. The same model produced aggregate welfare gains for the United States. Lower prices and a reallocation of production toward more productive uses raised total welfare in the calibration, even as manufacturing employment fell and even as the movement of workers between markets took time and imposed costs. Effects varied widely across local labor markets. Workers in places tied to the most exposed industries faced longer transitions and larger losses, while consumers and workers elsewhere gained through lower prices and expanding activities. Transition costs in the model were substantial and uneven, which meant that an aggregate welfare gain and a severe local adjustment problem were outputs of the same calculation rather than competing claims. Caliendo, Dvorkin, and Parro thus supplied a general equilibrium statement of the offset argument at its own strength: over 2000 to 2007 the shock accounted for about 0.55 million lost manufacturing jobs, a minority share of about 16 percent of the manufacturing decline observed in that window, and the United States as a whole gained in aggregate welfare, with the gains and the adjustment costs spread very unevenly across places and workers.

Mary Amiti, Mi Dai, Robert C. Feenstra, and John Romalis, in "How Did China's WTO Entry Affect U.S. Prices?," published in the Journal of International Economics in 2020, volume 126, and circulated earlier as Staff Report 817 of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in 2017, measured the opening through prices rather than employment. Their central estimate was that entry by China into the World Trade Organization reduced the United States manufacturing price index by about 7.6 percent between 2000 and 2006. A price reduction of that size represented a direct gain to buyers of manufactured goods, including households buying finished products and firms buying intermediate inputs, and it formed part of the aggregate side of the ledger in the same way that export supported demand did. The channel behind the price reduction was the finding that gave the study its distinctive place in the debate. Amiti, Dai, Feenstra, and Romalis attributed at least two-thirds of the reduction to China cutting its own tariffs on imported inputs. Those input tariff cuts lowered costs for Chinese producers, raised their productivity, and allowed them to charge lower prices, so a large part of the price benefit received in the United States originated in tariff liberalization inside China under its accession commitments rather than in any change to United States tariff rates, which the statute had left unchanged. The authors also separated a second channel tied directly to permanent normal trade relations. The removal of tariff uncertainty associated with that status operated chiefly through the entry of new Chinese exporters into the United States market rather than through lower prices charged by exporters already selling there. In other words, ending the annual renewal risk changed who exported, as new firms entered once the threat of a return to column 2 rates was removed, and the competition and variety associated with that entry formed part of the mechanism behind the price results. Reported at full strength, the study estimated a manufacturing price index about 7.6 percent lower over 2000 to 2006 than it would otherwise have been, with at least two-thirds of that effect traced to Chinese input tariff reductions and the remainder associated with channels that included the uncertainty reduction and new exporter entry.

One dispute between these research groups concerned the housing boom of the same years, and the literature left that dispute open. In work circulated in 2017 and 2018, Feenstra, Ma, and Xu showed that local housing booms between 2000 and 2007 could mask part of the trade effect in commuting zone estimates, because places with rising house prices gained construction and related employment for reasons separate from trade. When local housing price changes were added to the commuting zone design, the estimated employment response to import exposure fell substantially, by about half on the total employment to population measure. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson replied in published comments in 2017 and defended their original design and results. The exchange turned on specification choices, on whether housing price movements belonged in the design as a control or formed part of the local conditions that trade itself affected, and on how much of the measured local employment decline the original trade exposure variable explained once housing was held separate. A fair description is that the size of the local employment response was contested, with one group reporting that a housing control reduced the estimate by about half and the other group defending the original estimate, while the existence of export offsets, price reductions, and aggregate gains was addressed in the separate studies described in this section on their own terms.

The safeguard provisions of the statute addressed a different part of the adjustment problem, the case of a particular product rather than the economy as a whole. Section 103 of the United States-China Relations Act of 2000 added section 421 of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2451, as a China specific safeguard. The provision implemented the transitional product specific safeguard written into the accession protocol under which China joined the World Trade Organization, and it was modeled on section 406 of the Trade Act, the earlier market disruption remedy for imports from Communist countries. The governing standard was market disruption. Relief was available where imports from China increased rapidly and were a significant cause of material injury, or of a threat of material injury, to a domestic industry. Each element of that standard was chosen to make the China specific remedy easier to obtain than the general safeguard in section 201 of the Trade Act. Section 201 required serious injury and a higher causal showing, while section 421 required material injury and a significant cause, a lower combination that Congress adopted because the remedy was transitional, product specific, and limited to imports from a single country during a defined period after accession. The procedure divided responsibility between the International Trade Commission and the President, in a structure that readers can follow in detail through [how United States tariff law is administered](/2020/10/01/us-tariff-law-administration/). A domestic industry, a union, or another interested party could petition the Commission, which investigated the imports, the condition of the domestic industry, and causation, and reported a determination on market disruption together with a remedy recommendation when the determination was affirmative. The President then decided whether relief would be granted and in what form, with authority to impose added duties or quantitative restrictions. An affirmative Commission finding therefore did not itself impose a duty. It opened a presidential decision, in which wider considerations about the national economic interest could support or defeat relief, and that division between an injury finding by the Commission and a discretionary decision by the President shaped every outcome in the section 421 record.

### How often was the China safeguard actually used?

Seven petitions were filed under section 421 in total. Before the tires case, the Commission rejected two petitions and found market disruption in four, and President George W. Bush denied relief in each of those four cases. Only the tires petition resulted in added duties.

The pattern before 2009 showed the presidential stage operating as the decisive filter. Six petitions were filed before the tires case. In two of those six cases the Commission found no market disruption, and the proceeding ended at the investigative stage. In four cases the Commission found market disruption and recommended a remedy, which established that the statutory test could be met by domestic industries on the facts presented. President George W. Bush denied relief in each of those four affirmative cases. The denials did not reverse the Commission findings on injury and causation. They exercised the separate presidential discretion that the statute had preserved, and they meant that, during the first years of the safeguard, a successful petition at the Commission produced no added duty and no quota. Industries considering a petition therefore faced a two stage test in practice: proof of rapidly increasing imports as a significant cause of material injury before the Commission, and then a presidential judgment that relief served the national economic interest. The four denials also set the background against which the single grant in 2009 was understood, as the first and only case in which both stages were satisfied.

The tires case began on April 20, 2009, when the United Steelworkers petitioned under section 421 over imports of passenger vehicle and light truck tires from China. The Commission investigated and, on June 18, 2009, voted 4 to 2 that market disruption existed. In its remedy recommendation the Commission proposed added duties of 55 percent in the first year, 45 percent in the second year, and 35 percent in the third year. The case then moved to the President under the structure described in this section. On September 11, 2009, President Barack Obama decided to grant relief, at rates below the Commission recommendation. The proclaimed duties were 35 percent in the first year, 30 percent in the second year, and 25 percent in the third year, imposed on top of the existing 4 percent normal duty on those tires. The added duties took effect on September 26, 2009. The sequence in the tires case matched the design of the provision at each step. A union petitioned for a defined product from a single country, the Commission applied the market disruption standard and voted by a stated margin, a remedy was recommended in a declining three year schedule, and the President modified the rates downward while accepting the underlying decision to provide relief. China challenged the measure at the World Trade Organization. On September 5, 2011, the Appellate Body upheld the United States measure, leaving the duties in place for the remainder of their term. The added duties ran their three year course and ended in 2012. No further grant of section 421 relief followed before the authority expired.

Expiry was part of the original design rather than a later repeal. Section 421 was transitional from the start, tied to the accession protocol, and its authority expired in December 2013, twelve years after accession by China to the World Trade Organization on December 11, 2001, under the termination provision in section 423 of the Trade Act. The three year tire duties had already ended in 2012, so the December 2013 expiry closed the remedy itself rather than cutting short an operating measure. That record made the provision neither an unused promise nor a routine source of protection. It functioned as a narrow, time limited remedy whose injury test was met in four of the six cases decided before 2009 and whose relief depended, in every affirmative case, on a presidential decision that went against relief four times and in favor of relief once.

The two bodies of evidence in the consequences debate, and the safeguard record beside them, support a joint conclusion that neither side of the accounting cancels the other. Feenstra and Sasahara, Feenstra, Ma, and Xu, Caliendo, Dvorkin, and Parro, and Amiti, Dai, Feenstra, and Romalis documented aggregate gains in export supported demand, in net industry level employment over their stated periods, in modeled welfare, and in lower manufacturing prices, each figure attributed to its authors, its method, and its period. The loss literature documented concentrated and persistent harm in the local labor markets most exposed to imports from China, with adjustment that was slow and incomplete for the workers and places affected. Aggregate gains and concentrated, persistent local losses are both documented in the peer reviewed record available by July 2020, and the honest synthesis is that both findings stand: the gains do not erase the losses borne in particular places, and the losses do not erase the gains recorded at the national level.

## The assessment the record supports

A fair assessment starts by keeping the legal change and the economic change in separate columns. The legal change was narrow and complete: section 101 ended the Jackson-Vanik application to China once that country entered the World Trade Organization, and permanent status took effect on January 1, 2002. The economic change was wider and uneven. Applied tariff rates did not fall, so the effects traced by researchers ran through certainty, supply chain commitments, import growth, export growth and prices.

The concentrated loss record rests on named work with stated periods. David H. Autor, David Dorn and Gordon H. Hanson, in the American Economic Review in 2013, compared 722 commuting zones over 1990 to 2000 and 2000 to 2007 and found larger manufacturing losses, higher unemployment and nonparticipation, lower wages, lower household income and higher transfer and disability receipt in more exposed zones. Daron Acemoglu and coauthors, in the Journal of Labor Economics in 2016, placed total losses from rising import competition from China at 2.0 to 2.4 million jobs over 1999 to 2011, with about one million in manufacturing, after counting input output linkages and aggregate demand. Autor, Dorn and Hanson, in the Annual Review of Economics in 2016, described adjustment as slow and incomplete, with losses persisting in exposed places.

The aggregate record rests on work of equal standing and points the other way in part. Robert C. Feenstra and Akira Sasahara, in the Review of International Economics in 2018, used the World Input-Output Database for 1995 to 2011 and found that export growth supported demand for about 6.6 million jobs, while imports from China reduced demand by about 2.0 million. Robert C. Feenstra, Hong Ma and Yuan Xu, in the Journal of International Economics in 2019, found export driven gains largely offset import losses at the industry level, for a preferred net estimate of about 379,000 jobs gained over 1991 to 2011. Lorenzo Caliendo, Maximiliano Dvorkin and Fernando Parro, in Econometrica in 2019, estimated a manufacturing loss of about 0.55 million over 2000 to 2007 and an aggregate welfare gain in the same model.

Price evidence adds a gain that wage evidence in exposed zones does not capture. Mary Amiti, Mi Dai, Robert C. Feenstra and John Romalis, in the Journal of International Economics in 2020, estimated that entry by China into the World Trade Organization reduced the United States manufacturing price index by about 7.6 percent between 2000 and 2006, with at least two thirds traced to China cutting its own tariffs on imported inputs. Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott, in the American Economic Review in 2016, supplied the mechanism: removal of the threat of column 2 rates, averaging about 33 percentage points above applied rates, released commitments where the threatened jump had been largest. One dispute was open in the literature available by July 2020. Feenstra, Ma and Xu reported that a housing control reduced the local employment estimate by about half, and Autor, Dorn and Hanson defended their result in reply.

The record therefore supports neither a verdict of pure gain nor a verdict of pure loss. Gains arrived as lower prices, export demand and modeled welfare, spread across buyers and industries on a national scale. Losses arrived as lost payrolls, lower wages and higher benefit receipt, concentrated in towns whose industry mix had placed them in the path of the import rise. A household could gain as a buyer and lose as a worker. Readers placing the statute in the longer run of American trade law can follow [the history of United States trade legislation since 1950](/2020/10/15/us-trade-legislation-since-1950/), a sequence that places the China vote inside the longer development of the Trade Act framework.

The safeguard and the monitoring titles show what Congress put in place of the annual vote. Section 421 of the Trade Act offered a product specific remedy under a market disruption standard, produced one grant of added duties in the tires case of 2009, and expired in December 2013. The Congressional-Executive Commission and the annual compliance report supplied documentation without a yearly threat to treatment. Permanent status also left Congress free to legislate again, because permanence meant the absence of a built in expiry rather than a bar on later law. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did permanent normal trade relations with China do?

Permanent normal trade relations ended the annual waiver system for China and placed normal tariff treatment on ordinary law. Section 101 of the United States-China Relations Act of 2000 terminated the application of chapter 1 of title IV of the Trade Act of 1974 to China, conditional on accession by China to the World Trade Organization. The change removed the yearly presidential renewal, the congressional disapproval vote that could follow it, and the threat that a failed renewal would restore far higher column 2 rates. The act also added a China specific safeguard, created the Congressional-Executive Commission on China, and required monitoring of compliance with accession commitments. Permanent status took effect on January 1, 2002.

### Q: Did permanent normal trade status lower tariffs on China?

No. Permanent normal trade status lowered no United States tariff on goods from China. Applied rates were identical on the day before permanent status took effect and on the day after, because normal column 1 rates already applied under the annual presidential waiver. No schedule in the act cut a duty, phased a duty down, or set a date for a United States rate to fall. The tariff cuts in the wider package ran the other way. China, under its bilateral agreement of November 15, 1999 and its multilateral accession commitments, cut its own tariffs and opened agriculture, services, distribution and other sectors. The United States changed the legal security behind its rates, not the rates themselves.

### Q: Why did ending annual review matter for permanent normal trade?

Ending annual review mattered because the review carried a large and dated threat behind it. A presidential waiver extension ran from July 3 each year, and Congress could try to overturn it through a joint resolution of disapproval. If renewal had failed, imports from China would have reverted to column 2 rates set largely in the Tariff Act of 1930. Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott measured the average gap between column 2 and applied normal rates at about 33 percentage points across industries. Firms making supply, plant and sourcing decisions with returns spread over years had to discount those decisions for the chance of that jump. Removing the yearly vote removed the discount tied to the jump.

### Q: How did Congress vote on permanent normal trade relations?

Congress passed the bill by different margins in the two chambers. The House approved H.R. 4444 on May 24, 2000, by 237 to 197, with one member not voting, in roll call 228. Republicans voted 164 to 57 in favor, Democrats voted 73 to 138 against, and independents voted 0 to 2. The Senate passed the same House bill without amendment on September 19, 2000, by 83 to 15, with two senators not voting, in record vote 251. Republicans voted 46 to 8 and Democrats voted 37 to 7 in the Senate. The two senators who did not vote were Daniel Akaka and Joseph Lieberman, both Democrats. President Bill Clinton signed the bill on October 10, 2000.

### Q: What is the China shock after permanent normal trade relations?

The China shock is the name researchers gave to the rapid rise in imports from China and the local labor market effects that followed in exposed American places. Census Bureau figures reported by the Congressional Research Service put United States goods imports from China at about $100 billion in 2000 and about $321.5 billion in 2007. David H. Autor, David Dorn and Gordon H. Hanson studied 722 commuting zones and found that more exposed zones recorded larger manufacturing losses, higher unemployment and nonparticipation, lower wages, lower household income and higher transfer receipt. In their 2016 synthesis in the Annual Review of Economics, the same authors described adjustment to the shock as slow and incomplete, with losses persisting in the places most exposed to the import rise.

### Q: Did permanent normal trade relations end Jackson-Vanik for China?

Yes, for China, by termination of application rather than by repeal of the whole framework. Section 101 terminated the application of chapter 1 of title IV of the Trade Act of 1974, the Jackson-Vanik provisions, to China. Section 402 of the Trade Act, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2432, stayed in the statute as a general rule for the class of countries it covered. The termination was conditional on accession by China to the World Trade Organization. China acceded on December 11, 2001, President George W. Bush signed the terminating proclamation on December 27, 2001, and permanent status took effect on January 1, 2002. From that date, no annual Jackson-Vanik waiver was required to keep normal rates in place for imports from China.

### Q: What safeguard came with permanent normal trade relations?

The safeguard was section 421 of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2451, added by section 103 of the act. It implemented the transitional product specific safeguard in the accession protocol for China and was modeled on section 406 of the Trade Act. Relief was available where imports from China increased rapidly and were a significant cause of material injury, or a threat of material injury, to a domestic industry. That market disruption standard was easier to meet than the serious injury standard of the general safeguard in section 201. The International Trade Commission investigated a petition and reported a determination and a remedy recommendation, and the President decided whether relief would be granted and in what form, as added duties or quotas.

### Q: Did permanent normal trade relations require WTO accession?

Yes. The termination of the Jackson-Vanik application to China was conditional on accession by China to the World Trade Organization, under section 102 of the act. Congress authorized permanent treatment in 2000, but the authorization operated only when the condition was met. China acceded on December 11, 2001, after approval at the Doha ministerial in November 2001, and permanent status took effect on January 1, 2002 following the presidential proclamation of December 27, 2001. The condition reflected the structure of the organization. World Trade Organization law required members to extend nondiscriminatory treatment immediately and unconditionally, so an annual waiver could not survive accession unchanged. Without permanent treatment, the United States would have had to invoke non-application or deny itself the benefits of the accession commitments.

### Q: When did permanent status take effect for imports from China?

Permanent status took effect on January 1, 2002. The date followed a three step sequence that the statute itself required. Congress passed H.R. 4444 in 2000 and President Bill Clinton signed it on October 10, 2000, but section 101 was conditional and produced no change in treatment on the signing date. China acceded to the World Trade Organization on December 11, 2001, which satisfied the condition in section 102. President George W. Bush then signed a proclamation on December 27, 2001 terminating the application of title IV to China and extending permanent normal trade relations. Imports from China entered under permanent status from the first day of 2002, at the same column 1 rates that had applied under the waiver.

### Q: What is the difference between most favored nation wording and normal trade relations wording?

In the record of this statute, the two wordings describe the same kind of treatment rather than two different tariff schedules. The official title of H.R. 4444 paired the terms in one phrase, authorizing extension of nondiscriminatory treatment, called normal trade relations treatment in the title itself, to the People's Republic of China. Nondiscriminatory treatment meant that goods from China received the normal column 1 rates applied to goods from other countries with that status, instead of the column 2 rates reserved for countries outside it. A change in the label attached to the treatment changed no rate. The act changed the legal basis of the treatment, from an annual waiver to ordinary law, and that legal change, rather than any wording change, was the substance of the statute.

### Q: What were column two rates and where did they come from?

Column 2 rates were the non-normal rates in the United States tariff schedule, applied to countries that lacked nondiscriminatory treatment. For China, column 2 was the default that waited behind the annual waiver. Those rates had been set largely in the Tariff Act of 1930, the statute associated with Smoot and Hawley, and they stood far above the normal column 1 rates that trade actually faced under the waiver. Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott measured the average gap between column 2 rates and applied normal rates at about 33 percentage points across industries, with a standard deviation of about 15 points. Some industries faced gaps well above that average and some faced gaps well below it, a spread that later allowed researchers to compare outcomes across industries by exposure.

### Q: What does the Congressional-Executive Commission on China do?

The Congressional-Executive Commission on the People's Republic of China monitors human rights and the development of the rule of law in China. The act, in Title III of Division B beginning at section 301, composed the commission of nine senators, nine members of the House of Representatives and five senior administration officials appointed by the President, drawn from the Departments of State, Commerce and Labor. Its duties under the act were to monitor those subjects, to submit an annual report to the President and to Congress, and to maintain a political prisoner database. The commission replaced the annual waiver debate as the standing congressional instrument on those questions. It could document and report, but it could not by itself suspend normal treatment or restore column 2 rates.

### Q: Who supported and who opposed the bill?

Support for the bill came from the Clinton administration, most Republicans in Congress, and organized business and farm groups. The named supporters in the record included the United States Chamber of Commerce, the Business Roundtable, the United States-China Business Council, and high technology and agriculture groups, which argued that the accession commitments opened the Chinese market on terms exporters could use. Opposition was led by organized labor, through the AFL-CIO under its president John Sweeney, and inside the House by Minority Leader Richard Gephardt and Minority Whip David Bonior. Most House Democrats voted against the bill their own president had transmitted. A Gallup poll in May 2000 found 56 percent of the public in favor and 37 percent opposed, a division that matched the contested character of the campaign in both chambers.

### Q: What did China agree to change under its accession commitments?

China agreed to cut its own tariffs and to open sectors of its economy that had been closed or restricted. The commitments came through two channels. A bilateral agreement signed in Beijing on November 15, 1999, negotiated for the United States by Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky, set the terms on which the United States would support accession. The multilateral accession terms then carried obligations toward all members of the World Trade Organization. Under those commitments, China reduced its tariffs and opened agriculture, services, distribution and other sectors to greater foreign participation. The direction of the tariff concessions matters for reading the American statute. The United States changed no rate in Public Law 106-286. The rate cuts and sector openings in the package were commitments made by China as the price of entry into the organization.

### Q: How did researchers measure the employment effect of the change?

Researchers used two main designs, each built on a comparison across exposure. Justin R. Pierce and Peter K. Schott compared industries by the NTR gap, the difference between the column 2 rate a failed renewal would have restored and the applied normal rate. Industries with larger gaps lost significantly more employment after 2001 and recorded larger import increases and more participating firms on both sides of the trade. David H. Autor, David Dorn and Gordon H. Hanson compared 722 commuting zones by growth in imports from China per worker, using import growth in other high income countries as an instrument to separate a Chinese supply expansion from American demand conditions. The two designs asked related questions at different units, industry and place, and each reported its methods, periods and limits alongside its findings.

### Q: How long did the China safeguard last and was it used?

The China safeguard lasted on a fixed term and was used once to grant relief. Section 421 of the Trade Act was transitional from the start, tied to the accession protocol, and its authority expired in December 2013, twelve years after accession by China on December 11, 2001. Seven petitions were filed in total. In the six cases before 2009, the International Trade Commission found no market disruption in two and found disruption in four, and President George W. Bush denied relief in each of the four affirmative cases. The United Steelworkers petition on tires from China produced the single grant. President Barack Obama proclaimed added duties of 35, 30 and 25 percent over three years in September 2009, and those duties ended in 2012.

### Q: Could Congress change trade treatment again after granting permanent status?

Yes. Permanent status did not place trade treatment for China beyond the reach of later legislation. Permanent, in the act, meant the absence of a built in expiration and the absence of a recurring waiver condition. Treatment continued as ordinary law unless a later Congress changed it. No Congress can bind a later Congress by ordinary legislation, and Public Law 106-286 contained no exception to that principle. A later statute could amend the Trade Act, alter tariff treatment, or enact new conditions through the ordinary legislative process, including any presidential signature or veto override that process required. The contrast with the waiver regime was the burden of action. Under the waiver, treatment ended unless it was renewed each year. Under permanent status, treatment continued unless Congress affirmatively acted to change the law.

### Q: Why did organized labor oppose permanent status?

Organized labor opposed permanent status on labor, rights and bargaining grounds stated during the campaign. The AFL-CIO, under president John Sweeney, led the opposition and argued that a permanent grant would reward a government whose labor practices the federation condemned, while exposing American workers to a sharper flow of imports produced under conditions American law did not permit at home. Labor also argued that the annual renewal vote gave Congress a recurring point of pressure on human rights, freedom of emigration and workplace conditions, and that ending the vote surrendered that pressure without enforceable labor protections in its place. A further stated warning was that making normal treatment permanent would encourage American firms to shift production toward China and weaken the bargaining position of American workers facing import competition at home.

### Q: What happened to import volumes from China after permanent status took effect?

Import volumes from China rose sharply in the years after permanent status took effect, on Census Bureau figures reported by the Congressional Research Service. United States goods imports from China were about $100 billion in 2000 and about $321.5 billion in 2007, a rise of more than three times in seven years. United States goods exports to China also rose, from about $16 billion in 2000 to about $65 billion in 2007, from a far smaller base. The share of United States imports coming from China rose from about 8 percent in 2000 to about 16 percent in 2007. Those totals set the scale for the research debate. They showed the size and speed of the trade change, while the studies of employment, prices and local labor markets supplied the evidence on what that change did to workers, places and buyers.

### Q: Did prices in the United States change after China entered the World Trade Organization?

Yes. A study of prices found a measurable reduction in the United States manufacturing price index after entry by China into the World Trade Organization. Mary Amiti, Mi Dai, Robert C. Feenstra and John Romalis, in the Journal of International Economics in 2020, estimated that entry reduced the index by about 7.6 percent between 2000 and 2006. The authors attributed at least two thirds of that reduction to China cutting its own tariffs on imported inputs, which lowered costs for Chinese producers, raised their productivity and allowed lower prices. The channel tied directly to permanent normal trade relations operated chiefly through the entry of new Chinese exporters into the United States market, rather than through lower prices charged by exporters already selling there. The price gain reached buyers of manufactured goods, including households and firms buying intermediate inputs.
