Trotskyism and Stalinism are most often used as insults or as badges, traded between people who have usually inherited their allegiance before examining the evidence. Stripped of the polemic, the two names denote something more precise and more interesting: rival analyses of one and the same state, the Soviet Union, offered by people who began inside a single revolutionary movement and agreed about almost everything except what that movement had produced. One side held that the state remained socialist in its foundations and required defence and development. The other held that a privileged bureaucracy had usurped political power while leaving the property foundations of the revolution intact, and required removal by a political revolution that would preserve those foundations. Around those two positions, dissidents from the second camp later built two further analyses, arguing that the bureaucracy was not a parasite on the system but the ruling class of a new kind of exploitative society. Any honest comparison has to handle all four, because the argument among them is where the substance lives.

![Trotskyism vs Stalinism, four analyses of the Soviet state and its bureaucracy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The method of this comparison is stated at the outset so that neither tradition can quote it selectively. Each of the four analyses will be graded by the same three tests: what it predicted before the events, what it explained once the events had occurred, and what it could not explain without strain. A second discipline runs alongside the first, and it gives this article its namable claim. Where a tradition argues that it would have done better in power, the first evidence to examine is the recorded conduct of the people who would have held that power. That discipline is the counterfactual constraint applied throughout what follows, and speculation is labelled as speculation wherever it appears, because a counterfactual asserted without a label is a conclusion smuggled past the reader. A third discipline completes the method. A class analysis, which asks what a ruling stratum is and how its power and income arise, is kept separate from a moral verdict, whose business is the deeds themselves and the question of their justification. Confusing those two operations is the most common defect in writing on this subject, and it corrupts history written from every side of the argument.

The comparison also has to be fair to the losing side of the original dispute in one specific way. The doctrine called socialism in one country is usually presented by its opponents as a cynical invention, a pretext manufactured to justify a conservative turn. It was not that, and presenting it as that destroys the comparison before it starts, because the doctrine rested on an observable fact that every participant could verify: the revolution had not spread. Germany had failed, Hungary had failed, the revolutionary wave that followed the First World War had receded, and the Soviet state stood alone in a hostile world with a ruined economy and an exhausted population. The choice its leaders faced was to build what could be built within the territory held, or to treat the revolution as provisional and wait for rescue from abroad. A serious person could take either side of that choice, and serious people did. The argument that follows weighs the doctrine on its merits and its record, not on the motives attributed to its author.

## The state at the centre of the argument

Every current inside the Russian revolutionary movement was eventually forced to answer a question none of them had expected to face for long: what, exactly, was the Soviet state? The question was not academic. Classification decided conduct. If the state was a workers' state, however flawed, then communists abroad owed it defence against hostile powers and owed its leadership a measure of loyalty. If it had become something else, an exploitative order wearing the revolution's vocabulary, then loyalty was complicity and opposition was duty. Between those poles lay questions of reform and revolution: whether the ruling stratum could be corrected by pressure from within the party, whether it had to be removed by an uprising of the working class, and whether removing it meant preserving or destroying the property relations established after October 1917. Each answer reorganised a person's politics completely, which is why the classification dispute consumed so much of the revolutionary left's energy across several decades and why it still organises argument long after the state itself has disappeared.

The object of the dispute was a stratum rather than a person, and it is worth describing that stratum before judging any analysis of it. Government in the Soviet Union was exercised through an apparatus of party committees, state ministries, factory administrations, planning organs and security services, staffed by officials who were appointed through lists controlled from above. The party itself had been transformed from the relatively loose and argumentative organisation of the revolutionary years into a hierarchy in which advancement depended on loyalty to the prevailing leadership. Officials enjoyed access to goods, housing, travel and education that were unavailable to the workers in whose name they ruled, and those advantages attached to the office rather than to personal wealth. Beneath them, the working class that had made the revolution had been scattered by civil war, famine and factory closures, and then reconstituted in new factories under conditions of discipline imposed from above. Trade unions functioned as transmission belts for production policy rather than as independent defenders of their members. Soviets, the councils from which the state took its name, had long since become ceremonial. Whatever the state was, it was not governed by its working class in any direct sense, and every analysis had to begin from that shared observation.

The four analyses diverged over what followed from the observation. The official analysis held that the apparatus was an administrative instrument of a socialist society, distorted by scarcity and by the cultural backwardness inherited from the old regime, but fundamentally sound. Trotsky's analysis held that the apparatus had become a parasitic caste ruling over property forms that remained historically progressive. The state capitalist analysis held that the apparatus was a ruling class in its own right, collectively exploiting labour through the wage relation under conditions of forced accumulation. The bureaucratic collectivist analysis held that the apparatus was the ruling class of a new mode of production, neither capitalist nor socialist, whose members possessed the state jointly and ruled through that joint possession. These are not quarrels over adjectives. Each classification implies a different account of where the system's dynamics come from, a different prediction about its future, and a different instruction to anyone who wishes to change it, and the sections that follow take each in turn, beginning with the doctrinal dispute from which the whole comparison grew.

## The doctrinal root: permanent revolution

Trotskyism took its central doctrine from a theory Trotsky had developed in the aftermath of the 1905 revolution, when he attempted to explain how a backward country, whose bourgeoisie was too weak and too frightened to complete its own revolution, might nevertheless move beyond bourgeois limits. The theory of permanent revolution, set out in his work Results and Prospects, argued that the democratic tasks of such a country, land reform, national independence, political liberty, could be accomplished only by the working class taking power and that a working class in power could not halt at the boundaries of private property. The exposition at [permanent revolution explained](/2014/09/15/permanent-revolution-explained/) works through the doctrine's architecture in full; what matters for the comparison is its structure, which falls into three connected claims. The first concerns the growing over of democratic into socialist tasks. The second concerns the uninterrupted character of the revolution within the country. The third, and the one that collided head on with socialism in one country, concerns the revolution's international dependence.

### What did permanent revolution's third component claim?

The third component held that a revolution in a backward country could begin national tasks but could complete them only as part of an international process. Confined within one country's borders, the revolution would face pressures its own economy could not answer, and would either spread or degenerate.

That third component was not a decorative addition. It followed from the theory's account of backwardness itself. A country that industrialised late did so with borrowed technology, foreign capital and a working class concentrated in a few large enterprises, and its economy remained entangled with richer neighbours whose productivity set the standard against which its own survival would be measured. A workers' government in such a country would face military pressure, economic competition and internal scarcity simultaneously, and its ability to build a society superior to capitalism depended on the revolution reaching countries whose productive forces could relieve the pressure. Trotsky drew the conclusion without flinching: within national limits, the contradictions could be managed but not resolved, and the longer the isolation lasted, the more the government would be driven to substitute coercion and administrative privilege for the consent and abundance it could not supply. Read against the subsequent history of the Soviet bureaucracy, the third component has the character of a prediction, and its defenders have never tired of pointing that out. Its critics reply that a prediction which demands rescue from abroad is also a confession that the policy cannot be carried out with the materials at hand, and that dispute over how to read the prediction is the real substance of the doctrinal quarrel.

The theory also had a prehistory on the other side of the later divide, and intellectual honesty requires noticing it. In April 1917 Lenin returned to Russia and argued, against the hesitations of his own party's established leadership, that the revolution could not stop at its bourgeois stage and that power should pass to the soviets, a practical convergence with much of Trotsky's schema even though Lenin rejected the label and the two men arrived by different routes. The October insurrection was carried out under a political logic both men could sign. This matters because the later polemic, on both sides, rewrote the past into a story of permanent opposition between two doctrines, whereas the historical record shows two revolutionaries whose frameworks converged at the decisive moment and diverged afterwards over new questions that 1917 had not posed: isolation, bureaucratisation and the terms on which socialism could be constructed in one country. Trotsky's doubts about bureaucratic substitution, moreover, long predated the succession struggle. Writing in 1904 against Lenin's organisational proposals, the young Trotsky warned that the logic of substitution could end with a single dictator substituting himself for the Central Committee, a sentence his later critics quoted against him with relish and his defenders cited as proof that his critique of the apparatus began from principle rather than from defeat. Both readings draw on the same text, which is itself a small lesson in how this comparison should be conducted: the record is common property, and the argument is over its weight.

## Socialism in one country argued at full strength

The doctrine that Trotskyism defined itself against was announced in the autumn of 1924, when Stalin argued that socialism could be built and completed within the Soviet Union alone, reversing a position he had himself stated earlier that year. The full exposition of the doctrine and its textual history belongs to the companion treatment of [socialism in one country](/2019/04/01/socialism-in-one-country/), and the theoretical system erected on it is examined under [stalin and marxism leninism](/2019/03/15/stalin-and-marxism-leninism/); the task here is narrower and, for a fair comparison, more demanding, which is to present the doctrine's argument at its strongest before any verdict is reached. That argument began from the failed revolutions. Béla Kun's Hungarian Soviet Republic had collapsed in 1919. The German revolution had been defeated in successive waves, the last in the autumn of 1923, when the Communist Party's planned insurrection was called off and its one armed episode, in Hamburg, was crushed. The Bulgarian rising of 1923 had failed. By the middle of the decade the conclusion pressed itself on anyone willing to look: the European working class was not about to take power, and a Soviet government that organised its policy around imminent rescue was organising around a fiction.

From that fact the doctrine drew a practical programme rather than a metaphysics. The Soviet Union possessed a sixth of the world's land surface, enormous natural resources and a population large enough to constitute a complete economy. Its industry, though backward, could be developed from domestic accumulation if the state held the commanding heights, banking, foreign trade, large industry, and used them to direct investment. The peasantry, on this view, could be drawn into socialist construction gradually through cooperatives, credit and the supply of manufactured goods, without a second civil war in the countryside. Bukharin, the doctrine's most sophisticated defender, added the corresponding account of tempo: the new society would be built at what he called a snail's pace if necessary, because a construction that outran the alliance with the peasantry would collapse into coercion and destroy the worker and peasant alliance on which the whole state rested. Gradualism of that kind is easy to caricature as timidity. It is better understood as a theory of survival, holding that the revolution's conquests were real, fragile and worth consolidating, and that consolidation was a creative task rather than a betrayal of the original mission.

The strongest version of the argument also contains the strongest objection to the opposition, and it deserves to be stated in the form its proponents would recognise. The opposition's programme, on this reading, required an international revolution that had already failed to arrive on schedule, and it therefore amounted to demanding that the only existing revolutionary state stake its survival on events outside its own control. A government cannot eat a prediction. It must collect grain, run railways, defend borders and answer for the consequences to a population that did not choose to live inside a theory. If the choice was between building socialism slowly in the territory held and holding the territory as a besieged outpost awaiting deliverance, the second option was not more revolutionary; it was an abdication disguised as intransigence, and its end point, when deliverance failed to come, would not be a more democratic socialism but no socialism at all. That objection has real force and any Trotskyist answer that ignores it is answering a weaker opponent than the one that actually existed. The answer, when it comes, has to concede the international component's character honestly: extending the revolution was a demand on circumstances, not a policy a Soviet government could enact, and the opposition's strength lay elsewhere, in what it proposed within the country itself.

### Why did the succession struggle turn on the peasantry?

Because the peasantry formed the large majority of the population and its grain decided whether the towns ate, industry received raw materials, and exports paid for machinery. A policy toward the village was therefore simultaneously a food policy, an industrial policy, and a judgement about how fast socialism could move.

Within the country, the opposition's proposals were neither vague nor gentle, and the economic argument between the camps is the part of the comparison most distorted by later mythology. The procurement crises of the middle nineteen twenties arose from a price structure that rewarded peasants for withholding grain: industrial goods were scarce and dear, grain prices were low, and the state found itself unable to feed the towns or to export enough to pay for imported machinery. Preobrazhensky, the opposition's economist, had already supplied a theory for this bind in his account of primitive socialist accumulation, which held that a workers' state surrounded by a sea of private peasant production would have to transfer resources from the private sector to state industry through prices, taxation and the monopoly of foreign trade, much as early capitalism had transferred resources from colonies and peasants. That was not a gentle programme. In several respects it pressed the peasantry harder, earlier, than the policy the majority was then pursuing, and the opposition also demanded faster industrialisation, a plan with teeth, and a turn against the richer peasants and private traders who were prospering under the New Economic Policy. What the opposition combined with that economic hardness was political softness of a specific kind: democracy inside the party, elected officials, published platforms, the right of minorities to argue their case before congresses rather than be administratively destroyed. The combination matters for the counterfactual examined later, because it shows the alternative on offer in the nineteen twenties was not leniency in general but a particular mixture, tougher on the village economy and looser in the party's internal regime.

## Trotskyism's answer: the degenerated workers' state

Against the official doctrine Trotsky eventually set the analysis that defines his tradition, worked out across the exile writings and given canonical form in The Revolution Betrayed, completed in 1936. Its starting point was a concession his critics rarely quote: the revolution's property conquests were real and enormous. Land, factories, banks and foreign trade had passed out of private hands; production was organised by plan rather than by market anarchy; and these achievements, in his judgement, constituted a historic gain for the working class that had to be defended against both foreign attack and internal restoration. The same book then turned to the political regime standing guard over those conquests and described it in terms no apologist could use. Political power had been monopolised by a bureaucratic stratum that distributed privilege to itself, silenced the soviets and the party, and ruled through the security apparatus. The formula that joined the two judgements is the hinge of the whole comparison, and it is stated in the snippet answer below because generations of examination candidates have been required to reproduce it exactly.

### What is a degenerated workers' state?

Trotsky's term from 1936 for a state in which capitalist property relations have been abolished, which he counted as a gain, but where a privileged bureaucratic caste has taken political power from the working class. His conclusion was that a political revolution, not a social one, was required.

The distinction between a political and a social revolution carried the entire practical weight of the analysis. A social revolution transforms property relations; a political revolution transforms only the regime governing them. If the bureaucracy was a parasitic growth on a healthy property foundation, then the working class needed to remove the regime while preserving and defending the nationalised economy, much as workers might defend a trade union against a corrupt leadership without dissolving the union itself. Trotsky reached for the French analogy of Thermidor, the reaction that overthrew Robespierre while leaving the French Revolution's property settlement intact, and argued that the Soviet Union had undergone its Thermidor: a political counter-revolution on the soil of the revolution's own social conquests. The analogy did heavy work. It explained how the regime could be counter-revolutionary in politics while the state remained, in his classification, a workers' state, and it located the turning point in the middle nineteen twenties rather than in October itself, preserving the legitimacy of the revolution he had helped to lead.

### What separates a caste from a class in this argument?

A class, in the Marxist usage Trotsky inherited, is defined by a stable relation to the means of production, held and transmitted across generations. A caste consumes privilege without owning the productive apparatus, cannot bequeath its position as property, and therefore lives parasitically on a property form it did not create.

The caste designation followed rigorously from that definition and was not a euphemism chosen to soften the indictment. An official's income, dacha, car and access to closed shops flowed from his position in the hierarchy and evaporated when he fell from favour; he could not sell his ministry, mortgage his committee seat or pass his post to his children as an asset, however effectively officials in practice arranged advantages for their families. Trotsky concluded that a stratum so constituted lacked the independence of a class and lived as a parasite on property relations created by the revolution and still, in their planned and nationalised form, superior to capitalism. The designation also explained the bureaucracy's political behaviour as he observed it: cowardly toward imperialism because it feared war would expose its usurpation, brutal toward the working class because any revival of soviet democracy threatened its monopoly, and conservative in ideology because it needed the prestige of October to legitimise a rule October never authorised. From the analysis flowed the programme of the movement he founded: defence of the Soviet Union's property forms against imperialist attack, combined with irreconcilable opposition to its regime and preparation for the political revolution that would restore soviet democracy. Both halves were obligatory, and later splits inside his own tradition usually began when one half was quietly dropped.

## The four analyses grid

Comparison among four positions becomes manageable when each is forced to answer identical questions in adjacent cells, and the four analyses grid is built for exactly that discipline. Its columns ask what the bureaucracy is, what property relations obtain, what political conclusion follows, and what evidence would count against the analysis. The last column is the unusual one and the most valuable, since a classification that cannot state the observation that would refute it is not an analysis but a loyalty. Students meeting the dispute for the first time are usually surprised by how much of the argument the grid absorbs: rows that sound like rival slogans turn out to differ over specific, checkable claims about inheritance, income and the motor of accumulation, and the political conclusions in the third column follow from those claims with genuine logic rather than by association.

| Analysis | What the bureaucracy is | Property relations | Political conclusion | What would count against it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Socialist state | An administrative stratum of a socialist society, distorted by scarcity and inherited backwardness | Social property: nationalised and collective ownership embodying socialist relations | Defend the state, correct defects through the party, build toward communism | Privilege and repression persisting or deepening generations after scarcity recedes |
| Degenerated workers' state | A parasitic caste without property of its own, usurping political power | Post-capitalist: nationalised property and planning survive as conquests of the revolution | Political revolution against the caste; defend the property forms against restoration | The caste surviving a world war intact and consolidating itself as a hereditary ruling group |
| State capitalist | A collective capitalist class exploiting labour through the wage relation | State property functioning as capital under the compulsion of accumulation | No defence of the state; social revolution by the working class against its exploiters | Accumulation proceeding without competitive compulsion and without capitalist crisis patterns |
| Bureaucratic collectivist | The ruling class of a new exploitative mode of production | Class property held collectively through total control of the state | No defence of the state; a third social system must be superseded like capitalism | The system collapsing rapidly into ordinary capitalism rather than consolidating as a durable new order |

Reading the grid vertically is as instructive as reading it across. The first column shows that the quarrel was never about whether the bureaucracy was privileged or repressive, since three of the four rows concede or insist on both, and the fourth explains the same facts as distortion. The real disagreements sit in the second column, over property: whether nationalised forms are socialist relations, revolutionary conquests under occupation, capital in state dress, or the foundation of a new class order. That is why the argument proved so durable and so resistant to settlement by moral appeal. Each side could agree completely about the queues, the prisons and the privileges while drawing opposite practical conclusions, because their conclusions were driven by a theory of what the economy underneath the regime actually was. The grid also exposes the burden each position carries into the grading exercise later in this article. The official row must explain why a socialist society reproduced coercion at vast scale. The Trotskyist row must explain the longevity and stability of a supposedly transient caste. The state capitalist row must explain accumulation without a market of competing capitals. The collectivist row must explain why its new mode of production failed to behave like a durable historical formation. No row escapes the exercise unmarked, which is precisely what a grid is for.

## The rival left analyses: state capitalism and bureaucratic collectivism

The third and fourth rows of the grid were built by deserters from the second, and their biographies are part of their meaning. Bureaucratic collectivism appeared first in Bruno Rizzi's work of 1939, which argued that the Soviet bureaucracy had become a possessing class ruling through the state, and it was developed in the United States by Max Shachtman, who had been among Trotsky's closest collaborators before breaking with him in 1940. James Burnham, briefly a leading Trotskyist intellectual, carried a neighbouring idea into his 1941 book on the managerial revolution, generalising the Soviet case into a worldwide claim that managers rather than owners or workers were becoming the ruling stratum of the age. State capitalism had a longer and more scattered ancestry, with anticipations inside several traditions, but its systematic form arrived with Tony Cliff's analysis of 1948, written as an internal document of the British Trotskyist movement, and the American theorist Raya Dunayevskaya reached a state capitalist conclusion by an independent route during the same period. The provenance matters because it corrects a lazy assumption: these were not analyses imposed by hostile outsiders but conclusions drawn by people who knew the degenerated workers' state formula intimately and decided, under the pressure of specific events, that it could no longer be sustained.

The pressure came chiefly from the war and its aftermath, the very test Trotsky had nominated in advance. The bureaucracy survived the greatest war in history not weakened but enlarged, and it then extended its system across Eastern Europe, creating regimes in Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary and elsewhere in which the property forms of the Soviet model were installed by occupation armies and security police, without any workers' revolution from below. Trotsky's followers answered with a patch, classifying those states as deformed workers' states, born degenerate rather than degenerated, and the ingenuity of the patch is itself diagnostic: a transitional form theorised as the unstable product of a revolution thwarted from within was now being applied to stable orders imposed from outside, some of which would outlast their founders by decades. A caste that reproduces itself across generations, survives total war, expands its territory and manufactures replicas of itself abroad begins, its critics argued, to exhaust the explanatory value of the word caste. Shachtman's collectivism answered that the bureaucracy had to be recognised as a class of a new type, ruling through collective state property and entitled to the same unsparing opposition as any exploiting class. Cliff's state capitalism answered differently, locating the class character of the bureaucracy in the economy's driving compulsion: cut off from world markets yet locked in military competition with the capitalist West, the Soviet economy was forced to accumulate for accumulation's sake, extracting surplus from wage labour exactly as capital does, with the arms race supplying the competitive whip that internal markets did not. On that reading the bureaucracy was the collective personification of capital, and the workers' relation to the means of production, hired labour producing a surplus disposed of by others, was capitalist in substance whatever the juridical forms proclaimed.

## Party life, the opposition, and the road to the trials

The doctrinal dispute was fought inside institutions, and its institutional history explains why argument turned into annihilation. The Left Opposition formed in 1923 around Trotsky's critique of the party's economic course and internal regime, set out in his letters on the New Course, which warned that the apparatus was substituting itself for the party's membership and that economic planning was drifting without democratic control. The grievances were concrete: appointed secretaries overruling elected committees, discussion curtailed before congresses and stage managed during them, and a widening gap between the party's official egalitarianism and the visible privileges of its functionaries. Lenin's own final writings had circled the same danger, and his last dictated notes proposed removing Stalin from the post of General Secretary on grounds of rudeness and disloyalty, a judgement the leadership suppressed. After Lenin's death in January 1924 the succession struggle proceeded through shifting blocs, each using the machinery of appointment and the ban on factions, adopted in the emergency of 1921, to outlaw the next minority's right to organise. Zinoviev and Kamenev first allied with Stalin against Trotsky, then broke with him and joined Trotsky in the Joint Opposition of 1926, whose Platform demanded a turn in economic policy and the restoration of party democracy, and whose supporters were expelled wholesale in November 1927 after attempting to demonstrate publicly on the revolution's tenth anniversary.

### What did the Left Opposition demand inside the party?

The opposition demanded restoration of inner-party democracy: elections to party posts instead of appointment from above, freedom to form factions and circulate platforms, open debate before decisions hardened into commands, and workers' control revived in the factories. It framed these as recoveries of the party's own earlier practice, not imports from outside.

Expulsion was followed by exile, first to Alma Ata in Kazakhstan in January 1928 and then, in February 1929, by deportation from the Soviet Union altogether, an act without precedent for a figure of Trotsky's standing and one that converted an internal dispute into an international schism. From exile in Turkey, France, Norway and finally Mexico, Trotsky built the International Left Opposition and then the movement for a new International, while inside the Soviet Union the regime moved through the defeat of the Right Opposition around Bukharin, the forced collectivisation of the peasantry, and the famine years, toward the catastrophe of the nineteen thirties. The full biographical arc, including the exile writings and the assassination that ended them, is traced in the [leon trotsky intellectual biography](/2014/09/01/leon-trotsky-intellectual-biography/); the comparison needs only the sequence that leads to the trials, because the trials are where the dispute between the traditions stops being doctrinal and becomes a matter of fabricated crime and physical extermination of the losing side.

The Moscow Trials of 1936 to 1938 presented the old opposition, in three public proceedings, as agents of foreign powers and organisers of sabotage, assassination and espionage. Zinoviev and Kamenev were tried in August 1936, Radek and Pyatakov in January 1937, and Bukharin and Rykov in March 1938, each proceeding resting almost entirely on confessions extracted in custody and each ending, for the principal defendants, in death sentences. The charges contained demonstrable impossibilities, meetings alleged at hotels that had been demolished, flights to airports whose records showed no arrival, and the scholarly consensus across otherwise divided historiography treats the proceedings as frame ups constructed by the security apparatus. The terror of the succession period, of which the trials were the theatrical surface, is reported here as the scholarship presents it: a machinery of arrest, interrogation and execution directed first at the old opposition and then outward through the party, the army command, the state administration and the population at large, with historians continuing to debate the balance between central direction and local escalation. Figures for its toll belong to the Soviet cluster of this series, which handles them under its own protocol, and none are asserted in this article's voice. What belongs here is the fate of the family at the centre of the comparison, reported factually and without rhetorical use. Trotsky's son Sergei, who had remained in the Soviet Union and kept out of politics entirely, was arrested and shot in 1937. His elder son Lev Sedov, his father's closest collaborator in exile, died in a Paris clinic in 1938 after an operation, in circumstances that have been disputed ever since. His daughter Zinaida had died by her own hand in Berlin in 1933 under the pressure of illness and persecution. Trotsky himself was murdered in Mexico City in August 1940 by an agent of the Soviet security services. An independent commission of inquiry, appointed in the late nineteen thirties and chaired by the philosopher John Dewey, examined the charges against him and reported them unproven.

### What did the commission of inquiry decide?

The commission chaired by John Dewey heard Trotsky's testimony in Mexico in 1937 and examined the trial transcripts and evidence, and it reported that the Moscow confessions bore the marks of fabrication and that Trotsky and his son stood not guilty of the charges framed against them.

The commission's verdict did not end the argument, because the argument was never only about the charges. Defenders of the Soviet government of the period, and a fringe of later writers, continued to treat the trials as genuine, while the Soviet state's own later leadership, in the closed proceedings of 1956 and the rehabilitations that followed, withdrew many of the accusations against the defendants of 1938 without restoring the reputation of the opposition of 1927. For the purposes of the comparison, what matters is the effect of the trials on the four analyses. The official analysis was required to defend proceedings that its own successors partially disowned, an explanatory burden it has never comfortably carried. The Trotskyist analysis gained the moral authority of a persecuted founder while acquiring, in the trials, its most powerful confirmation that the bureaucracy ruled by methods incompatible with any workers' democracy. The rival left analyses took the terror as evidence about the system rather than about personalities: a ruling class or caste defending a monopoly of power and privilege, on either of their readings, would predictably annihilate an alternative leadership that threatened the monopoly. Each tradition thus found in the same events a confirmation of itself, which is a warning the grading section takes seriously, since an analysis confirmed by everything is confirmed by nothing.

Between the expulsion of the opposition and the opening of the trials lies one further scene that the traditions read in opposite ways and that a comparison should not skip: the Seventeenth Party Congress of 1934, remembered in the literature as the Congress of the Victors. The defeated oppositionists appeared on its platform in turn to perform the ritual of recantation, praising the leadership they had opposed and abasing themselves in the prescribed formulas, and the congress received them with the applause due to a victory parade. Within four years a majority of that congress's delegates, and most of the Central Committee it elected, had been arrested and shot in the terror the trials inaugurated. Kirov, the Leningrad leader whose popularity at the congress reportedly alarmed the victors, was assassinated in December of the same year, and his murder supplied the pretext for the emergency decrees that began the machinery's acceleration. The official tradition subsequently treated the congress as a milestone of unity restored; the Trotskyist tradition treated it as the bureaucracy's dress rehearsal for cannibalism, a gathering at which the caste first compelled its defeated rivals to applaud and then consumed applauders and defeated together. The archival record supports the sequence without adjudicating the metaphor, and the sequence itself carries the analytical weight: a system whose congress of victory became, by an unbroken administrative path, a congress of the condemned had demonstrated something about the location of power within it that no classification can afford to ignore, namely that survival inside the apparatus depended on no loyalty, seniority or service that the apparatus itself was bound to respect.

## Strategy against fascism: the united front against social fascism

No arena tested the two traditions against reality as brutally, or as quickly, as Germany between 1930 and 1933. The Communist International, directed from Moscow, had adopted at its Sixth Congress in 1928 the line of the so called Third Period, under which capitalism was entering its final crisis and the chief obstacle to revolution was declared to be social democracy, branded with the term social fascism and treated as a twin of the fascist movement itself rather than as a rival workers' organisation with which alliances could be made. The practical consequences in Germany were severe. The Communist Party directed its main fire against the Social Democrats who still commanded the loyalty of millions of workers, cooperation against the Nazis was refused at the level of principle, and on one notorious occasion Communist and Nazi organisations found themselves on the same side of a referendum aimed at a Social Democratic state government. Trotsky, writing from exile in a stream of pamphlets addressed to German workers, argued the opposite course with mounting urgency: a united front of all workers' organisations, unions, parties and factory committees, for the physical defence of the movement against the Nazi assault squads, on the principle of marching separately while striking together. He warned that a Nazi victory would mean the annihilation of the German labour movement and a war of extermination against the Soviet Union, and he identified the refusal of the two great workers' parties to combine their forces as the mechanism by which a minority movement of gangsters would be allowed to conquer the strongest organised working class in Europe.

### How did the two camps read the German catastrophe?

Trotsky's camp read the Nazi victory as the fruit of a criminally divided labour movement, pointing to the Communist line that treated Social Democrats as the main enemy. The official camp read the same events as proof that independent opposition weakened the working class and that discipline within one camp was survival.

Hitler was appointed Chancellor in January 1933 without the general strike or armed resistance that a united movement might have mounted, and the German labour movement, its unions, parties, newspapers and funds accumulated over two generations, was destroyed within months almost without a battle. Trotsky drew the conclusion he had resisted drawing through the preceding decade: the Communist International, in his judgement, had died as a revolutionary instrument, its German section had collapsed without attempting the defence he had prescribed, and a new International would have to be built from the fragments. The movement that took shape around that conclusion founded the Fourth International in 1938, at a conference held in secrecy in France, and the fate of the organisation then created, its splits, its persistence and its claims, is assessed in [fourth international legacy](/2014/12/01/fourth-international-legacy/). For the grading exercise, Germany is the strongest exhibit in the Trotskyist portfolio, because the prediction was made in advance, in writing, with the mechanism specified: divide the workers' movement, anathematise its largest organisations, and fascism will walk through the gap. The counter argument deserves its hearing too. The official tradition replied that the Social Democratic leadership bore the primary guilt, having suppressed the German revolution in 1919, tolerated Brüning's austerity, and refused unity on terms that would have strengthened the revolutionary wing; and it noted that the Comintern reversed course by 1935 toward the broad Popular Front alliances that Trotsky then criticised from the other side as class collaboration. Both rebuttals contain history. Neither dissolves the central fact that the line actually pursued in Germany between 1928 and 1933 ended in the total destruction of the movement it claimed to lead, and that the man in exile diagnosed the disaster in advance while those holding power certified the policy that produced it.

## The economics beneath the slogans

Beneath the doctrinal formulae lay a material problem that no slogan could dissolve, and the comparison is incomplete until the problem is stated in its own terms. The Soviet economy of the middle nineteen twenties was a dual structure: a nationalised industrial sector, small, war damaged and technologically dependent on imports, set within an ocean of small peasant farms that fed the country and supplied the export earnings with which machinery could be bought abroad. The New Economic Policy had restored production roughly to pre war levels by permitting peasants to sell their surplus and small traders to operate, but restoration was not growth, and every current agreed that industrialisation had to happen. They disagreed over who would pay for it and at what speed. Industry needed investment on a scale the state budget could not supply from its own resources. The countryside was the only large reservoir from which a surplus could be drawn, and the terms on which grain moved from village to town, the famous scissors between agricultural and industrial prices, became the technical form of the century's largest political question.

Bukharin's answer proceeded from the alliance. Peasants who were taxed and squeezed too hard would stop sowing, hide grain or slaughter livestock, and the state would face hunger in the towns and collapse in the export plan; therefore industry had to grow at the pace the market for its goods allowed, peasants had to be enriched through cooperatives that would draw them voluntarily toward socialism, and the surplus for investment had to be accumulated slowly from the growth of the whole economy. Preobrazhensky's answer, the law of primitive socialist accumulation, proceeded from the historical analogy: every developing system had begun by pumping resources out of a pre existing sector, capitalism from colonies and enclosures, and a socialist state without colonies would have to pump from its own peasantry through unequal exchange, pricing industrial goods high and agricultural goods low under the state's monopoly of trade. He presented the transfer as a necessity to be conducted openly, limited by the danger of killing the source, and administered by a plan rather than by the blind movement of prices. The contrast between the two economists is the economic skeleton of the whole comparison, because each embodied his camp's deepest instinct: Bukharin's camp feared the peasantry's veto and organised policy to avoid provoking it, while Trotsky's camp feared stagnation and the growth of a private sector inside the state economy, and organised policy to outrun both dangers.

The sequence of events after 1927 gave each camp material for its indictment of the other. The procurement crisis of the winter of 1927 and 1928, when grain collections fell catastrophically short, convinced the victorious leadership that the peasantry had declared a delivery strike and that extraordinary measures were unavoidable; the emergency requisitioning that followed in the Urals and Siberia crossed, in practice, into the coercion the majority had denounced when the opposition proposed milder versions of compulsion through prices. Within two years the leadership had adopted a tempo of industrialisation more frenzied than anything the opposition had demanded and a collectivisation of the peasantry more violent than anything Preobrazhensky's formulae contemplated, carried through by administrative force in the countryside rather than by the economic levers he had specified. Trotskyists have drawn the obvious moral ever since: the bureaucracy first denounced the opposition's programme as adventurist, defeated it politically, and then enacted a caricature of it under panic conditions, at a cost in famine and dislocation that a planned transfer conducted earlier and lawfully would have avoided. The official tradition answered that the crisis left no choices worth the name, that delay would have left the country defenceless before the invasion that duly arrived in 1941, and that the industrial base built in the nineteen thirties, transplanted east of the Urals in the war's first catastrophe, was the material foundation of survival. Both arguments are live in the grading that follows, and both are arguments about a counterfactual, which is why they must pass through the constraint constructed in the next sections rather than around it.

## The record in office: Trotsky during the civil war

The counterfactual at the centre of the Trotskyist claim, that a different leadership would have governed differently, runs into evidence that is neither obscure nor drawn from hostile sources, because Trotsky held power himself during the harshest years of the revolution and left a full record of what he did with it. He was the organiser of the Red Army, built from near nothing in 1918 into the force that won the civil war, and the methods of that construction were his own: the conscription of former tsarist officers supervised by political commissars, draconian discipline including the decimation of deserting units in the war's worst moments, the use of blocking detachments and hostage taking against the families of officers whose loyalty was doubted. His defenders describe these as the unavoidable instruments of a besieged revolution fighting for survival against armies supplied by foreign intervention. His critics describe the same record as evidence that the authoritarian toolkit of the later regime was assembled, and legitimised, while the future leaders of both camps still served together. Both descriptions refer to identical facts, and any weighting of them belongs to the reader; what cannot be done honestly is to treat the toolkit as an invention of the faction that won the succession.

The civil war emergency also produced, with Trotsky's active support, two decisions whose consequences ran far beyond the emergency. The first was the militarisation of labour, proposed and defended by him in 1920, under which trade unions were to be shaken up from above, converted into organs of production discipline, and labour itself organised on quasi military lines, with workers assigned to tasks and punished for unauthorised departure as soldiers were. The proposal was debated fiercely inside the party, Lenin's own position was more cautious, and the trade union controversy of 1920 and 1921 showed Trotsky arguing for the statification of the unions against critics who warned, in terms the future opposition would reuse, against bureaucracy strangling the workers' own organisations. The second decision was the ban on factions adopted by the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, in the same days that the Kronstadt naval garrison rose in revolt demanding free soviets. Trotsky voted for the ban and defended it as a temporary emergency measure. The congress closed ranks while the guns were audible, the Kronstadt rising was suppressed by Red Army units under the authority of the government in which Trotsky held the military portfolio, and the ban intended for a season remained in force permanently, becoming the juridical instrument by which every later opposition, including Trotsky's own, was defined as an illegality before its arguments were heard.

Trotsky's later treatment of this record is itself part of the evidence. He never repudiated the civil war measures, defending them to the end of his life as necessities of a revolution fighting for existence in a ruined and peasant country, and he argued explicitly that the ends attained justified methods that would be crimes in other circumstances. On the faction ban he was more evasive, maintaining that the measure had been conceived as temporary and that its permanent conversion into a system of rule was the bureaucracy's doing rather than the congress's intention. Historians friendly to him, notably Isaac Deutscher in his three volume biography, treated the early record as the record of a revolutionary government rather than a personal indictment, while noting the irony that its author became the ban's most famous victim. Historians hostile to him treat the continuity as the whole story. The assessment that this article's discipline requires is narrower: the record establishes what Trotsky did when he held coercive power under pressure, and it therefore disciplines any claim about what he would have done with the same power under the pressures of the nineteen thirties. A writer who asserts that the terror would have been avoided under his leadership is making a claim about a man whose documented response to emergency was more coercion, organised more efficiently, and the assertion must carry that weight openly instead of travelling under the flag of his later opposition writings.

## The counterfactual constraint applied

The governing claim of this article can now be put to work, and it is worth restating in operational form before it is applied. The counterfactual constraint applied to a tradition's favourite hypotheticals means the following. Take the claim that events would have run better under the defeated alternative. Identify the people who would have staffed that alternative and the decisions they would have faced in their actual sequence. Test the claim against those people's recorded conduct in comparable situations, and against the structural pressures that confronted every government of the period regardless of its composition. Whatever survives both tests may be asserted as reasoned speculation. Whatever depends on the alternative's leaders behaving contrary to their records, or on the pressures politely suspending themselves, must be labelled as faith rather than analysis. The constraint is not a device for acquitting the victors. It applies to their counterfactuals with the same force, as the second application will show, and it leaves standing a large territory of legitimate criticism of the regime that actually ruled, since most of that criticism rests on the record rather than on the hypothetical.

### What is the counterfactual constraint?

The counterfactual constraint is the rule that a claim about what a defeated alternative would have done must be tested first against the recorded conduct of the people who would have enacted it. Speculation remains permitted, but it is marked as speculation and it carries no more weight than the record supports.

The first favourite counterfactual is the Trotsky succession: Lenin lives a few years less, or the party acts on his last dictated notes, and the General Secretary is removed in 1923 or 1924, leaving Trotsky the revolution's natural heir. Apply the constraint in sequence. The decisions awaiting that leadership are known: the procurement crises, the scissors, the tempo of industrialisation, the defence burden of an isolated state, the management of a party already habituated to the faction ban its new leader had supported. The leader's recorded conduct in comparable situations is also known: confronted with scarcity and disorder in 1920, he proposed militarising labour; confronted with dissent inside a besieged party in 1921, he voted to ban organised dissent; confronted with the peasantry's economic weight, his camp's economists proposed extracting the accumulation fund from the village through rigorously unequal exchange. None of that makes the succession scenario worthless, since other elements of his programme pointed the other way: the demand for party democracy, for elected and recallable officials, for published disagreement, was genuine, was argued consistently from 1923 onward, and addressed the precise mechanism by which the apparatus was hardening into a caste. A constrained version of the counterfactual therefore survives, and it runs as follows. It is reasoned speculation that a Trotsky led government would have industrialised earlier and with more planning and less improvisation than the one that ruled, and that it would have kept more space for argument inside the party, at least while its founder's own authority rested on persuasion. It is not established, and cannot be asserted as history, that such a government would have spared the peasantry coercion, preserved factional rights once its own policies were resisted, or avoided terror when its survival appeared threatened, because the founder's record supplies contrary indications on all three points. The distance between those two sentences is exactly the distance the constraint exists to measure.

The second favourite counterfactual is the unexpelled opposition: the Joint Opposition survives 1927, wins the argument by stages, and steers the state without the rupture of exile and prison. The constraint cuts this one differently, because the scenario contains an internal difficulty its advocates rarely face. The opposition demanded democracy inside a party operating a one party state in a country where the majority class, the peasantry, had its own demands and no institutional channel for them; an internal party democracy that left the surrounding structure untouched was a reform balanced on a point. Its survival would have required either extending political pluralism outward, toward the soviets and eventually toward other parties, which no Bolshevik current of any camp proposed in the nineteen twenties, or containing democracy within an ever narrower circle while the apparatus outside that circle continued to accumulate power. The opposition's own programme, read carefully, gestures at the first path through its calls for soviet revival without ever specifying how far the revival was meant to run. None of this proves the experiment would have failed. It establishes that the experiment's success depended on innovations its protagonists had not theorised, which places the counterfactual in the category of open possibility rather than suppressed certainty, and open possibility is a weaker thing than the moral verdicts usually erected on it.

Symmetry requires the same instrument on the victors' counterfactual, which is rarely stated as one: the claim that without the leadership that won, the Soviet state would have collapsed into restoration, invasion or disintegration, so that the terror and the famine were the price of the state's existence. The structural pressures invoked are real, as this article has conceded: isolation, backwardness, a hostile encirclement and an invasion that in fact arrived in 1941. But the claim that the specific methods used were dictated by those pressures, and that no alternative tempo or policy could have preserved the state, is precisely the kind of assertion the constraint exists to test, and it fares no better than its mirror image. The decision to collectivise at breakneck speed, the refusal to moderate procurement when famine was documented, the extension of the terror from the old opposition to the party's own cadres and the officer corps on the eve of the very war used to justify it, were choices among options that contemporaries inside the system itself identified and proposed. A defence of the regime that rests on necessity must show that each escalatory choice was the only available one, and the record of internal debate shows alternatives being articulated and suppressed. Necessity, like innocence, cannot simply be presumed for the side that won; both are counterfactuals, and the constraint grades them by one standard.

## Grading the official and Trotskyist analyses

The grading discipline announced at the outset can now be applied row by row, beginning with the analysis that held power. The official classification predicted, from the middle nineteen thirties onward, consolidation: the constitution of 1936 proclaimed socialist relations achieved, exploiting classes abolished and the state entering a phase in which coercion would progressively wither as abundance and education advanced. Its explanatory strength was real and should be recorded without grudging. It explained the system's undeniable industrial achievements, the defeat of the invasion of 1941 by an economy that had been deliberately built for war, the mass literacy and technical education of a formerly illiterate population, and the genuine pride many Soviet citizens took in those attainments. What it could not explain was its own central embarrassment. A socialist society in which classes had been abolished proceeded, in the years immediately following the proclamation, to subject its own population, including the cadres of the ruling party and the commanders of its army, to a terror of an extent that the official analysis could process only as the work of hidden enemies, a category that expanded until it consumed the explanation itself. The withering of coercion did not occur; the apparatus of coercion grew across the decades in which scarcity was receding. Privilege did not dissolve with the proclaimed abolition of classes; it stabilised into a system of graded access attached to rank. The analysis survived these failures by treating each as an aberration attributable to a personality, the formula eventually adopted at the Twentieth Party Congress in 1956, and personality explanations are precisely what a class analysis exists to avoid. Judged by the three tests, the official row therefore stands as follows: substantial achievements registered and explained, the regime's defining violence unexplained in its own terms, and its central prediction, the withering state, falsified by the state's own trajectory.

A postscript to the official grading concerns the analysis's performance at the very end, because a doctrine's behaviour in the presence of its object's death is a final examination no tradition can decline. When the Soviet state entered its terminal crisis after 1989, the official analysis, by then administered by the state's own ideological departments, proved unable to describe the process in its own vocabulary at all. Its categories offered a choice between socialism consolidating and enemies conspiring, and the spectacle of the state's own apparatus dismantling the state's own property forms, under slogans borrowed from the doctrine's critics, fitted neither. The ideologists improvised; some rediscovered the market as a component of socialism, some rediscovered the nation as a value above class, and the catechism that had been guarded for six decades as the condition of the system's survival was discarded by its custodians in a single season without a synod, a schism or a defence. Trotsky's tradition read the scene as the caste completing its conversion. A colder reading registers a fact about doctrines maintained as state property: they are believed, in the end, by nobody with the power to act on the belief, and their falsification, when it arrives, is administered not by argument but by the quiet defection of their own priesthood. The comparison's other three analyses, whatever their failures, were carried by people who held them at personal cost and could state what would change their minds. The official analysis could state neither, and its grading closes on that deficiency, which is epistemological before it is political.

The Trotskyist row faces the opposite configuration of strengths. As a prediction machine it performed, in its founder's hands, with unusual precision at several points. The bureaucratisation of the party and the substitution of the apparatus for the class were diagnosed in 1923, before the process had a name. The German catastrophe was forecast with its mechanism named. The bureaucracy's political character, cowardly abroad and savage at home, acquisitive in privilege and conservative in doctrine, was described in The Revolution Betrayed with a specificity that later archival scholarship has largely corroborated in outline. Most remarkably, the founder stipulated in advance the observation that would refute him. In his polemics of 1939 and 1940, addressed to erstwhile followers who were drifting toward the collectivist conclusion, he laid down a condition of an unusual kind: should the Soviet bureaucracy pass through the approaching war unshaken, its dominion not merely preserved but enlarged, the caste concept would stand refuted, and honest adherents would be obliged to speak instead of a new exploiting class. The stipulation was printed while the result remained undecided, and it stands as the single most intellectually honourable act in the entire dispute, the reason this analysis, alone among the four, carries the instrument of its own correction inside itself.

### What test did Trotsky set for his own theory?

Trotsky stipulated that if the bureaucracy came through the approaching war with its power intact and still expanding, his description of it as a caste would fail and it would have to be reclassified as a new exploiting class. He published the condition himself, before the outcome was known.

The war's outcome is not in dispute. The bureaucracy emerged from 1945 enlarged, victorious and territorially expanded, and it reproduced itself for a further four and a half decades, transmitting status across generations through education and patronage even though it could not transmit office as property. By the letter of the founder's stipulation, the caste designation failed its test, and the traditions that took the stipulation seriously, drawing the collectivist or state capitalist conclusion, have the cleaner logical title to his method. The orthodox Trotskyist response rested on the other prong of his prognosis: he had also predicted that the bureaucracy, if not removed by political revolution, would eventually seek to convert its privileges into property and restore capitalism, and the events of 1989 to 1991, in which sections of the apparatus itself supervised the transition to private ownership and emerged among its owners, were claimed as the delayed arrival of that very outcome. The claim has genuine force as far as it goes. It does not dissolve the difficulty that a transitional caste theorised as unstable persisted for the better part of a century and fell, when it fell, in the direction of a restoration its theorist expected to be resisted by the property forms' own logic. A fair grade records both entries: the finest predictive episodes in the whole comparison, and a central classification that survived its founder's own deadline only by the ingenuity of his followers.

## Grading the dissident analyses

State capitalism, the third row of the grid, begins its grading with a considerable explanatory asset: it dissolves the anomaly that tormented the other positions, the spectacle of an economy without private capitalists that nonetheless extracted a surplus from wage labour, drove accumulation at the expense of consumption, and treated its workers as a cost to be disciplined rather than as the ruling class in whose name it governed. On Cliff's account none of that requires explanation by paradox, because the relations were capitalist in substance: the worker sold labour power, the surplus was disposed of by others, and the compulsion to accumulate arrived through military competition with the capitalist world, which punished any economy that allowed consumption to outrun investment in heavy industry and arms. The analysis also handles the transition of 1989 to 1991 with notable economy. Where the Trotskyist reading had to describe a social counter-revolution, the overturning of post-capitalist property forms, the state capitalist reading described a ruling class changing the juridical clothing of its rule, moving from collective to individual forms of appropriation with remarkably little resistance from below, which is what one would expect if the class relation underneath had not changed. What the analysis explains less comfortably is the internal texture of the economy it classifies. Competition among capitals in classical capitalism disciplines firms through bankruptcy, unemployment of capital and the equalisation of profit rates, and none of those mechanisms operated among Soviet ministries in recognisable form; prices were administered, enterprises could not fail, and investment followed political priorities that produced chronic shortage alongside surplus capacity in favoured branches. Cliff's answer, that arms competition supplied the missing whip from outside, is ingenious and may even be sufficient for the accumulation dynamic, but it relocates the law of value to the world market and leaves the domestic economy governed by mechanisms the theory names only by analogy. Its prediction record is mixed in an instructive way: it expected the system to display capitalist crisis patterns, and the stagnation of the nineteen seventies and eighties, with falling growth and an accumulation burden the economy could not carry, sits comfortably inside that expectation, while the system's longevity in its high growth decades sat less comfortably.

Bureaucratic collectivism, the fourth row, grades as the boldest and the most exposed of the four. Its explanatory claim was that the Soviet order represented something historically new, a class society founded on the collective possession of the state by its bureaucracy, and that this novelty explained features the older frameworks strained against: the total character of the regime's control, the fusion of economic and political power in a single hierarchy, and the system's capacity to reproduce itself without either markets or soviet democracy. Shachtman drew the political conclusion without flinching, refusing defence of such a state in any war and treating it as a rival imperialism in embryo. The analysis found its most persuasive evidence in the late nineteen forties, when the system expanded into Eastern Europe by administrative installation and showed no sign of the instability Trotsky's caste concept predicted. Its weakness appeared at the other end of the story. A new mode of production, on any classical understanding, should display the durability of the modes it succeeds, outliving its founders and defending its relations against restoration; the Soviet order instead collapsed within months once its directing centre lost confidence, and its ruling stratum converted to private capitalism with a speed that suggested their attachment was to their own position rather than to a distinctive set of property relations they existed to defend. Defenders of the collectivist reading reply that modes of production have died young before and that the bureaucracy's survival through the conversion proves the class analysis rather than refuting it. The reply is available, but it purchases consistency at the price of the theory's original claim, which was that a new civilisation, barbaric but stable, was displacing both capitalism and socialism. A civilisation that folds in a single season was, at minimum, misdescribed in its foundations.

### Why did no Trotskyist current ever hold state power?

No Trotskyist organisation has governed a state, partly because the currents formed in opposition under conditions of illegality and repression, and partly because their programmes presupposed a level of international support that never assembled. The absence is itself evidence in the comparison: the counterfactual alternative remains everywhere untested in office.

That absence deserves a paragraph of its own, because it cuts against every tradition in the dispute simultaneously and is therefore the least exploited fact in the entire literature. The official tradition governed a great power for seven decades and must answer for a record written in institutions, budgets and graves. The Trotskyist tradition governed nothing after 1927, and its founder's tenure in office belongs to the period when both camps were one. Every subsequent judgement of Trotskyism therefore concerns a doctrine and a critique, never an administration, and the asymmetry should discipline the rhetoric of the comparison in both directions. Critics of Trotskyism cannot point to a Trotskyist terror, a Trotskyist famine or a Trotskyist police state, because none occurred; defenders of Trotskyism cannot point to a Trotskyist construction, a Trotskyist industrialisation or a Trotskyist democracy in functioning form, for exactly the same reason. What exists is a critique of extraordinary quality, a programme never tested, and a founder whose only administrative record predates the split and is shared, in its methods and its emergency logic, with the regime he came to oppose. The counterfactual constraint is simply the refusal to let either side forget that distribution of evidence.

## The world movement as the argument's second front

The dispute between the traditions was never confined to the interpretation of Russia, because the Communist International transmitted the succession struggle into every national movement, and several of the century's decisive defeats were argued over as exhibits in the case. China in 1927 supplied the first and bloodiest. The International's leadership, committed to an alliance with the nationalist Kuomintang as the vehicle of the anti imperialist revolution, instructed the young Chinese Communist Party to operate inside the nationalist movement and to restrain the workers' and peasants' organisations whose militancy threatened the alliance. Trotsky protested against the subordination and demanded the Communists recover their independence and build soviets; the documentary record of that protest, and of how late it was permitted to circulate, became a permanent quarrel between the traditions. In April 1927 Chiang Kai-shek's forces massacred the Shanghai Communists and unionists who had delivered the city to him, and by the end of the year the Chinese party had been driven underground with losses from which it recovered only by abandoning the cities for a rural strategy its Moscow advisers had not designed. For the Trotskyist reading, China demonstrated the practical meaning of subordinating an independent workers' movement to a supposedly progressive national ally, and the pattern was generalised into a theory of the International's degeneration: foreign parties had become instruments of Soviet diplomacy, their strategies set by the needs of the one state rather than by the conditions of their own revolutions. The official reading replied that the Chinese catastrophe belonged to the objective weakness of a small party in a peasant country, that Trotsky's alternative of soviets in 1927 was a fantasy unsupported by any social force capable of sustaining it, and that the eventual victory of Chinese communism under Mao vindicated the rural road against both. Each reading protects its tradition's central asset, retrospective in one case and doctrinal in the other, and the honest arbiter notes that the massacre itself is not in dispute, only the responsibility.

Spain a decade later replayed the argument in a European key with the polarities of alliance reversed. After the military rising of July 1936, the Soviet Union became the Republic's principal external supplier, and with the aid came the strategy of the Popular Front: the revolution unfolding in the Republican zone, workers' militias, collectivised factories and farms, was to be subordinated to the winning of the war through alliance with the democratic bourgeoisie and the reassurance of Britain and France. The POUM, a dissident communist party whose leaders had cooperated with Trotsky's followers without accepting his authority, was suppressed in 1937 after street fighting in Barcelona between rival Republican camps, its leader Andreu Nin arrested by Soviet security operatives and murdered in custody. Trotsky, from Mexico, denounced the Popular Front as the strangling of a revolution in the name of a war that would then be lost anyway, which duly occurred in 1939; he also quarrelled bitterly with the POUM's own leadership for signing governmental compromises he regarded as capitulation, a reminder that his tradition's founder was no gentler with near allies than with enemies. The official tradition defended the Spanish policy as the only strategy compatible with the Republic's diplomatic survival and attributed the defeat to fascist superiority in arms and to Anglo French non intervention. Germany, China and Spain together constitute the external evidence in the grading of the two traditions, and a pattern is worth naming: on each occasion the Trotskyist critique identified, in advance or in real time, a mechanism by which the official strategy would disarm the movement it claimed to lead, and on each occasion the catastrophe arrived by roughly the route described. Prediction of other people's disasters is not the same as possession of a viable alternative, a distinction the constraint enforces, but neither is it nothing. A tradition that repeatedly reads events correctly from exile has established something about its analytical instruments, even if it has established nothing about its capacity to govern.

## The social settlement of the nineteen thirties

Classifications of the state are tested not only against wars and trials but against the texture of ordinary life the state organised, and the nineteen thirties supplied a body of evidence that each tradition read according to its lights. The first Soviet decade had been, alongside its coercion, a period of radical social experiment: marriage reduced to a civil registration, divorce obtainable at the request of either party, abortion legalised in 1920 as a public health measure, homosexuality decriminalised under the early codes, and an avant garde in art, architecture, pedagogy and design encouraged to imagine the forms of a liberated society. The decade that followed reversed much of this settlement in the name of stability. Abortion was recriminalised in 1936 except on narrow grounds, divorce was made progressively harder and dearer, the family was re glorified as the cell of socialist order, homosexuality was recriminalised, school discipline and uniforms returned, and in the arts the experimental movements were dissolved into a single official style charged with depicting reality in its revolutionary development. Wage policy moved in the same direction, from the egalitarian compression of the revolutionary years toward steep differentiation, piece rates and celebrated shock workers whose earnings set them visibly apart from their workmates.

For the Trotskyist analysis, this conservative turn in social policy was among the most legible symptoms of the bureaucracy's character, and The Revolution Betrayed devoted some of its sharpest chapters to it. A caste consolidating its own comfort required a stable family to transmit advantage informally, a disciplined and hierarchised workforce, a culture of deference, and an ideology that sanctified the existing order rather than agitating for its transformation; the restoration of familiar hierarchies in school, family and factory was exactly what the caste theory predicted of a stratum settling into possession. The official analysis answered that the early experiments had been the luxuries of a revolutionary intelligentsia in a country that needed births, order and output, that the population itself welcomed stability after two decades of upheaval, and that the social policy of the nineteen thirties represented the maturation of the revolution rather than its betrayal. The dissident analyses read the same settlement as a ruling class equipping itself with the ideological furniture of rule. What an impartial grading can register is the direction of travel, which none of the traditions disputes: the society became more hierarchical, more patriarchal in its official morality, and more hostile to experiment across precisely the years in which the bureaucracy's political monopoly became absolute. Any analysis of the state that cannot accommodate that correlation is incomplete, and the official analysis, which attributed the turn to the demands of construction, accommodated it least comfortably of all, since construction on its own premises was supposed to be dissolving hierarchy rather than refurbishing it.

## The national question between the traditions

One doctrinal fault line has been left implicit so far, and drawing it out sharpens the comparison, because on the national question the two traditions inherited the same founder and developed his legacy in opposite directions. Lenin had insisted, against much of his own movement's instinct, that the right of nations to self determination up to secession was a democratic demand socialists were bound to defend, and the early Soviet federation was constructed, at least in its forms, as a voluntary union of republics whose languages and cultures were to be cultivated by the policy of indigenisation. The bureaucratisation of the state altered the substance behind the forms: indigenisation was wound down in the nineteen thirties, the Russian language and Russian cadres advanced through the apparatus of the union republics, whole peoples were deported during and after the war on accusations of collective disloyalty, and the federation's formal right of secession survived as a clause nobody was permitted to invoke. Trotsky's writings on the national question are among the least convenient in his canon for his own legend, because he combined an iron insistence on the centralised planned economy with a defence of national rights that led him, in 1939, to raise the slogan of an independent workers' and peasants' Ukraine, arguing that the bureaucracy's Great Russian chauvinism, as he called it, had made the federation a prison for its non Russian peoples and that the Ukrainian question would become an explosive force in the coming war. The official tradition treated the national settlement as solved in principle by the federation itself, and read national grievance as the residue of bourgeois influence or the work of foreign intrigue, a classification that relieved it of explaining why the grievances regenerated under its own administration. When the union finally dissolved in 1991, it dissolved along the borders of its constituent republics, and the national question that both traditions had pronounced in their different ways subordinate to the class question turned out to be the solvent of the state itself. That outcome grades poorly for every row of the grid, since none of the four analyses had assigned the national movements the independent weight they proved to possess, and it stands in this article as a deliberate reminder that the grading instrument is not rigged for any contestant: all four classifications were theories of class and property, and history dismissed the state along a seam their common framework had taught them to underrate.

## Defencism, the pact, and the war

No doctrine of the Trotskyist tradition caused more anguish inside it, or more derision outside it, than unconditional defencism: the position that in a war between the Soviet Union and a capitalist power, supporters of the degenerated workers' state analysis must defend the Soviet side, not from any loyalty to its regime, which they sought to overthrow, but because the property forms at stake were a conquest of the working class whose destruction would be a historic defeat. The position was tested almost immediately by diplomacy. In August 1939 the Soviet government signed a non aggression pact with Nazi Germany, complete with secret protocols dividing Eastern Europe, and the official communist parties of the world reversed their anti fascist alignment overnight, discovering that the war begun days later was an imperialist contest in which neither side deserved support. Trotsky refused both easy exits. He held that the pact was the bureaucracy's diplomacy and deserved no defence, that the annexations following it were acts of the caste, and that the classification of the state was untouched by either, so that defencism survived the disgrace of the government exercising it. The contortion was not lost on his followers. The American organisation split in 1940, with the minority around Shachtman and Burnham departing toward the collectivist conclusion, in significant part over this very question: a state whose foreign policy was indistinguishable from an imperialist power's, they argued, could not be a workers' state in any sense that obliged socialists to defend it, and the war became the furnace in which the fourth row of the grid was finished. When Germany invaded in June 1941, the Soviet Union's survival struggle mobilised sacrifices on a scale the article will not attempt to enumerate, and the victory of 1945 delivered the bureaucracy the prestige of having saved the country it ruled, a prestige that insulated it from internal challenge for a generation. Trotsky did not live to grade his doctrine against that outcome, having been assassinated eleven months before the invasion, but his stipulated test had been framed in exactly these terms, and the grading section has already recorded what the outcome implies. The episode matters beyond its doctrinal intricacies because it displays the human cost of consistency within a framework: ordinary supporters in several countries were asked to defend, simultaneously, their opposition to a regime and that regime's state in a war, a position of undeniable logical structure and nearly impossible political psychology, and the movements built on it bore the strain in every subsequent split.

## The argument after the founder and after Stalin

Stalin's death in March 1953, and still more the Twentieth Party Congress of February 1956, subjected all four analyses to a second great test, this time administered by the regime itself. Khrushchev's closed session speech catalogued crimes against party members, the fabrication of cases and the destruction of the officer corps, attributing them to the cult of one man's personality and to violations of the norms the speaker promised to restore. Arrests slowed and then stopped at the terror's former scale; the camps disgorged millions of survivors into a society instructed to forget the mechanism that had filled them; and the bureaucracy undertook, from within and without any political revolution, a partial dismantling of the system of rule by fear. Each tradition mined the speech for its own vindication. The official analysis presented it as proof of self correction, the system's capacity to purge its distortions through its own institutions. Trotskyists read the catalogue as a confession extracted from the bureaucracy itself, confirming the founder's description of the terror as the caste's method of rule, while noting that the restorer of legality was himself a lifelong functionary of the apparatus the speech declined to analyse. The dissident traditions observed that a ruling class which could abolish mass terror by administrative decision, without resistance from the class in whose name terror had supposedly been a structural necessity, behaved more like a government changing policy than like a social order defending its foundations, and each folded the observation into its own frame.

The decades that followed offered each analysis a slower and less theatrical examination. The Hungarian rising of 1956 and the Prague Spring of 1968 showed the system's satellite order generating, from within its own working class and its own party reformers, movements that the centre suppressed by invasion rather than argument; Trotskyists coined the vocabulary of political revolution for Budapest's workers' councils, which had demanded, in the midst of an anti Soviet insurrection, the preservation of nationalised property under democratic control, as though a paragraph of the founder's programme had been enacted by strangers. Reform from above, attempted most seriously under Gorbachev after 1985, released the forces that dissolved the state within six years: glasnost permitted the public grading of the regime's history by its own citizens, the suppressed record poured into print, and the bureaucracy's factions resolved their competition by converting position into property where they could. The wider assessment of whether the state's seven decades justify the word socialist is reserved to [was the ussr socialist](/2019/05/15/was-the-ussr-socialist/), which treats it as a question in its own right rather than as a premise of this comparison. What belongs here is the observation that the terminal crisis was read by each tradition, in real time, through the grid's four rows, and that the readings survive as the last predictive exercise the Soviet material will ever offer. The official analysis, by then the property of the state's own ideologists, proved least able to describe what was happening to its own object. The others divided the honours of the autopsy according to taste, and the argument over the corpse's classification continues among historians who have never belonged to any of the traditions, which is the final form the dispute has taken.

## How the history has been written

The comparison has a historiography as well as a history, and the reader is entitled to know the main shelves of it, because the four analyses survive in scholarship long after the movements carrying them dwindled. Isaac Deutscher's three volume biography of Trotsky, published between 1954 and 1963, established the sympathetic scholarly portrait: the prophet armed, unarmed and outcast, a tragic figure whose critique of the bureaucracy the biographer shared while dissenting from his subject's organisational hopes. Robert Conquest's account of the terror, first published in 1968, fixed in Western understanding the image of a machinery directed from the very top, and the partial opening of the archives after 1991 complicated that image without acquitting its subject: a generation of archival historians, among them J. Arch Getty and Oleg Khlevniuk in different registers, documented a centre that demanded quotas of arrests and simultaneously lost control of the machinery it had licensed, so that initiative and denunciation from below fed the process the centre had ignited. Stephen Kotkin's biography of Stalin, built on those archives, reconstructed the dictator as an ideologically driven builder of a civilisation he intended as socialism, a portrayal that treats the official analysis's self understanding with a seriousness its scholarly critics had rarely granted, while documenting the violence of the construction in full. Trotskyist scholarship produced its own defences and revisions, from Ernest Mandel's systematic restatements of the degenerated workers' state theory to the state capitalist school maintained by Cliff's followers, each armed with citations the academic literature sometimes ignores at its cost. The methodological point for the reader is that the archival turn did not close the comparison, because the archives settle facts, arrests, orders, memoranda, while the four analyses are classifications of a totality, and classifications are argued from facts rather than read off them. Two historians can accept the same archival file and file it in different rows of the grid. Knowing which row a writer occupies, including the writers of this article's sources, is part of reading responsibly in this field, and the grading discipline used throughout is designed to make that act of location explicit rather than to pretend it away.

## The succession struggle as it unfolded

Abstract doctrines acquired their force from a sequence of concrete struggles, and the sequence deserves telling in its own right, because the victors at each stage were determined less by the arguments than by control of the apparatus that counted the votes. Lenin's final illness removed the one authority both camps acknowledged. His dictated letter of December 1922, supplemented in January 1923, warned the coming congress about the danger of a split between Trotsky and Stalin and recommended the latter's removal as General Secretary for rudeness and intolerance; the document was read to delegations at the Thirteenth Congress in 1924 and then withheld from publication, a suppression in which the whole leadership colluded, including men who would later reproach one another with it. Trotsky, ill and absent at decisive moments, and reluctant, as even his most critical biographers grant, to deploy the enormous prestige of his civil war role in a factional contest, was outmanoeuvred by the troika of Zinoviev, Kamenev and Stalin, who organised the campaign against Trotskyism as a deviation from Leninism, a category essentially invented for the purpose in the literary discussion of late 1924 after Trotsky's Lessons of October had embarrassed Zinoviev and Kamenev by recalling their opposition to the insurrection of 1917. The episode established the method of the whole decade: the past would be re edited as a weapon, orthodoxy would be defined by the majority of the moment, and the definition would be enforced by the secretariat's power to appoint the delegates who elected the committees who appointed the secretariat.

The blocs then rotated with a speed that later made the period hard to narrate honestly. Zinoviev and Kamenev, having supplied the ideological artillery against Trotsky, discovered by 1925 that the General Secretary's machine was being turned on their own Leningrad base, broke with him, and were defeated at the Fourteenth Congress. In 1926 they joined the man they had spent two years anathematising, forming the Joint Opposition with Trotsky on a platform substantially written by him, an alliance whose lateness Trotsky himself acknowledged as a reproach. Defeated in the cells and at the Fifteenth Conference, the opposition took its case to the streets on the tenth anniversary of October and was dispersed; Trotsky and Zinoviev were expelled from the party in November 1927, and the Fifteenth Congress that December expelled the opposition's ranks wholesale and declared adherence to its views incompatible with membership. Zinoviev and Kamenev recanted and were readmitted to a humiliating probation; Trotsky refused the price and was exiled, then deported. Stalin then executed the turn the narrative of the doctrines cannot omit: having defeated the Left in alliance with Bukharin's Right, he adopted from 1928 a violent acceleration that outran the Left's own industrial demands, broke Bukharin by 1929 as a right deviationist, and stood alone over a party whose internal life had been abolished in the course of defeating, in turn, every organised expression of opinion within it. The point of rehearsing the sequence inside a comparison of doctrines is not melodrama. It is that the doctrinal outcome, which doctrines would be available to the next generation as live options, was decided by this machinery, and any grading of the doctrines that forgets the machinery will mistake a selection effect for an intellectual verdict. Opposition ideas were not refuted in 1927; they were made unprintable, their holders unemployable and eventually unalive, and the traditions external to the Soviet Union are in large part the record of what survived that filtration.

## Privilege examined closely

Since three of the four analyses turn on what the bureaucracy's privilege actually consisted of, the privilege itself repays close description, because loose talk of luxury has obscured a structure that was distinctive and, for the caste argument, decisive. The official salary scales of the nineteen thirties were, by the standards of capitalist executives, modest; the system's real currency was access. Senior officials drew goods from closed distribution networks whose existence was not acknowledged in print: special shops stocking food and consumer goods unavailable in the open stores, apartments allocated by institution rather than by queue, dachas in restricted settlements, cars with drivers, places in sanatoria and in the better schools for children, and the telephone, the era's true status instrument, connecting the holder to other holders. Income differentiation in wages widened sharply after the abolition, in the early nineteen thirties, of the party maximum that had once capped Communists' earnings at a skilled worker's level, and the Stakhanovite movement of 1935 onward celebrated individual earnings many multiples of the average as a socialist virtue. Trotsky, cataloguing this structure in exile, treated the distribution of privilege as the bureaucracy's material root and its confession: a stratum that must conceal the scale of its own consumption from the class it rules acknowledges, in the concealment, that the property it administers is not its own. Critics of the caste analysis turned the same catalogue around. Access that ran through education and appointment had begun, by the second generation, to run through family strategy, officials placing children through the institutes that fed the apparatus, so that the stratum reproduced itself socially with an efficiency that ownership alone does not guarantee and that the formal absence of inheritance concealed rather than prevented. Soviet sociologists were forbidden to study their own ruling stratum as a stratum, and the regime's dissolution, when it came, revealed the outcome of the debate in an unexpected register: the conversion of access into ownership proceeded in the nineteen nineties not by the bureaucracy storming the property forms from outside, as the restoration scenario had imagined, but by office holders privatising the assets their offices controlled, transforming networks of access into networks of wealth with a continuity of personnel that surprised only those who had believed the juridical forms were the substance. That denouement is claimed, with some justice on every side, by the state capitalists as continuity of a ruling class, by the orthodox Trotskyists as the bureaucracy's long predicted drive to convert privilege into property, and by historians outside the traditions as evidence that privilege, access and power had always been the real triad and the property debate, in part, a dispute over which single key should open a three lock door.

## Codification: the official doctrine as a finished system

What the official tradition contributed to Marxist theory in its own right is often dismissed by its opponents as mere censorship with footnotes, and the dismissal misses a phenomenon of real intellectual interest: the conversion of a method of inquiry into a closed catechism, administered as the ideology of a state. The Short History of the Communist Party published in 1938, from which a generation of cadres across several continents learned their Marxism, contained a chapter on dialectical and historical materialism attributed to Stalin personally, which reduced the dialectic to a list of features and laws, arranged for memorisation, and the whole book organised the past as a procession of correct lines and unmasked deviations culminating in the present leadership. Whatever else this codification was, it was supremely teachable: portable, examinable, identical in every classroom, and aligned with the conclusions the state required. Trotskyist writing, by contrast, remained argumentative to the point of self harm, its classics dense with polemic against positions the reader must reconstruct, its movement splitting periodically over the correct interpretation of its own founder's conditional sentences. The contrast explains a distribution of influence that pure argument quality would not predict. Codified doctrine staffed the ideological apparatus of states and parties numbering in the millions; the critical tradition survived in small organisations, exile publishing houses and the footnotes of scholars. Judging the codification as theory, the grading of this article has already recorded its falsified predictions and unexplained terror; judging it as an instrument, its success is undeniable and its costs are written in the same record, since a theory administered as a catechism loses the capacity to register its own errors while the errors are still correctable. The Twentieth Congress speech of 1956 had to be delivered in secret, to a stunned hall, by the system's own heir, precisely because the system contained no legitimate mechanism by which the catalogue it recited could have been assembled in public by ordinary inquiry. A tradition's epistemology, its rules for how error is found and owned, turns out to be as consequential as its doctrines, and on that axis the comparison between a canon guarded by police and a critique maintained by argument is not close, even when particular arguments of the critique fail their own tests.

## The doctrine tested abroad: revolutions nobody scripted

The second half of the twentieth century presented Trotskyism with a problem its founder had not lived to formulate, and the tradition's handling of it is among the most instructive episodes in the grading. Revolutions succeeded after 1945 in places where the official communist parties, or movements of peasant guerrilla war, took power and abolished capitalist property: Yugoslavia in defiance of Moscow, China in 1949 after a civil war fought from rural bases, Cuba in 1959 under a leadership that began outside communism altogether. For the degenerated workers' state framework, each new case required classification, and the classification chosen was that these were deformed workers' states, post capitalist in property from birth, deformed rather than degenerated because no healthy soviet democracy had ever existed within them to be lost. The ingenuity was real and so was the strain. Trotsky's original theory had held that nationalised property forms were the conquest of a working class in revolution, requiring defence for precisely that reason; the new cases detached the property forms from the class agency entirely, finding them installed by armies and parties the theory regarded as bureaucratic from the first day. Defenders argued that the world economy's pressures, and the example and weight of the Soviet model, had created routes to post capitalist property that the founder could not have foreseen, and that the property criterion had always been the theory's foundation. Critics inside and outside the tradition replied that a criterion which certifies as workers' states regimes built by movements that shot workers' leaders was a criterion that had eaten the theory, and the state capitalist and collectivist schools recruited steadily from those who reached that conclusion. China's trajectory after 1978, in which the party that had abolished private property supervised its restoration in sector after sector while retaining power throughout, administered a further examination that no row of the grid passes without commentary: the official tradition celebrated the outcome as socialism with national characteristics, the Trotskyist schools divided over the date and even the fact of restoration, the state capitalists recognised their oldest claim that the class relation had never changed, and the independent observer was left with the spectacle of a ruling party traversing, under one continuous leadership, classifications that the four analyses had treated as separated by revolutions. The episode's lesson generalises beyond this dispute. Property forms are more plastic, and ruling strata more adaptable, than any of the classical frameworks assumed, and a comparison written to last should record that verdict against all four rows impartially.

## The Thermidor analogy interrogated

One piece of Trotsky's conceptual machinery deserves separate examination because it carried more weight than any other and attracted the most damaging criticism from his own side: the analogy of Thermidor. In the French Revolution, the Ninth of Thermidor overthrew Robespierre and the Jacobin dictatorship while leaving intact the property settlement the Revolution had created, above all the land transfers to the peasantry; reaction governed through the revolution's own forms until a soldier closed the sequence at Brumaire. Transferred to Russia, the analogy yielded an elegant thesis: the bureaucracy's victory was a political counter-revolution accomplished without a social one, the new rulers governing through soviets and a nominally communist party as the Thermidorians had governed through the Convention, and the analogy carried a hopeful corollary, since Thermidor in France had proved a phase rather than a final state and the gains of the revolution beneath it had survived every subsequent regime. The analogy's critics, including historians sympathetic to its author, have pressed three objections that the grading should register. The first is chronological elasticity: the date of the Soviet Thermidor migrated in Trotsky's own usage, located variously at 1924, at the defeat of the opposition, or deferred into a process still completing itself, and an analogy whose event cannot be dated within a decade is functioning as rhetoric rather than as chronology. The second objection is structural: the French Thermidorians were themselves revolutionaries moderating their own revolution from inside a representative assembly, whereas the Soviet bureaucracy was an administrative stratum of a type the eighteenth century could not supply, so the analogy imports a drama of personalities into a process whose essence was institutional. The third objection is the corollary's comfort: the French settlement beneath Thermidor endured because a numerous class of peasant proprietors existed to defend it with their own interest, while the Soviet property forms rested on a state apparatus whose personnel drew their income from administering them, so the analogy's ground for optimism about the foundations was weaker than its author allowed. Trotsky himself, late in exile, began supplementing the analogy with darker alternatives, speculating in his private notes about Bonapartism and about the bureaucracy as a formation without precedent, and the unfinished quality of that revision is part of why his tradition inherited a research programme rather than a closed doctrine. The analogy remains worth teaching, with its objections attached, because it dramatises the comparison's central distinction better than any abstraction: regimes can be overthrown and property orders survive, or property orders can be dissolved while regimes persist, and a politics that cannot tell the two operations apart will misread nearly everything that happened between 1924 and 1991.

## The official theorist on his own terms

Fairness to the comparison requires a section its Trotskyist sources rarely supply: an account of the official tradition's founder as a theorist in his own right, since the grading of doctrines cannot rest on the convenient fiction that one side had a thinker and the other a policeman. Stalin's theoretical interventions were derivative in the strict sense, built from Lenin's texts and from the party's collective work, but they were neither random nor stupid, and two of them shaped the century. The codification of dialectical materialism, whatever its scholastic aridity, answered a genuine administrative problem: a multinational party state required a single teachable doctrine, and the catechism supplied it, remaining the philosophical curriculum of a third of the world for two generations. The second intervention came at the very end. In Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR, published in 1952, the aging dictator corrected his own orthodoxy on points of substance: commodity production, he conceded, persisted under socialism because two forms of property, state and collective farm, exchanged their products as commodities; the law of value operated within limits; and the replacement of commodity circulation by direct products exchange belonged to a future that could not be decreed. He also rebuked economists who proposed abolishing the collective farms' property by fiat, insisting that the forms had to be outgrown rather than confiscated. The pamphlet is a strange and revealing document, simultaneously the system's supreme authority revising doctrine by ukase and a record of the system's real problems breaking, however muffled, into its official theory. Trotskyists dismissed it, predictably, as the bureaucracy's bookkeeping; but the grading discipline of this article requires noting that its author, in his last theoretical act, moved toward the recognition that socialist construction had unresolved economic contradictions of exactly the kind his doctrine had declared settled in 1936. Neither tradition made much of the admission at the time. Historians of economic thought have been kinder to it than the polemics of either camp, and it stands as evidence that the official doctrine was a living instrument of rule, capable of registering reality in the only register its institutions allowed, which was the register of one man's corrections issued to a silent profession.

## The exile's information problem

A word is owed to the conditions under which the Trotskyist analysis was produced, because grading a theory fairly includes asking what its author could have known. Trotsky wrote his anatomy of the bureaucracy from Turkey, France, Norway and Mexico, cut off from the archives, dependent on the official Soviet press, on emigré testimony, on the reports of travellers and on a network of correspondents that the Soviet security services penetrated with increasing success. His secretariat was infiltrated; one of his collaborators in Paris was a Soviet agent who rose to head the organisation of Russian emigré military veterans before vanishing; his son's entourage was watched and, on the account that most historians of the episode accept, steered toward the clinic where he died. Defectors such as Walter Krivitsky, the Soviet intelligence officer who broke in 1937 and published his account before being found dead in a Washington hotel room in 1941, supplied information that was simultaneously invaluable and unverifiable, seasoned for Western consumption. Under those conditions the errors of The Revolution Betrayed are as instructive as its accuracies. Its statistics were the regime's own, massaged at the source; its picture of internal party life leaned on correspondents whose fates were mostly fatal; yet its structural judgements, on privilege, on the political monopoly, on the regime's fear of its own working class, were corroborated when the archives opened two generations later to a degree that surprised scholars who had dismissed the book as exile polemic. The official tradition composed its self account under opposite conditions, with total access to information and total prohibition on publishing most of it, and produced a literature whose factual base its own successors disowned. The contrast belongs in the grading, because it illustrates the epistemological point made earlier from the other direction: the quality of an analysis depends less on the volume of information available to its author than on whether the author's framework permits the information to contradict him. Trotsky's framework contained, as the war test showed, its own refutation clause. The catechism contained none, and its custodians experienced each contradiction as an attack to be suppressed rather than a datum to be absorbed, which is why the critique written in exile in poverty reads, on the central questions, as the more reliable document.

## Two theories of the party

Beneath every other disagreement lay a dispute about the instrument itself, the revolutionary party, and it is the layer of the comparison most likely to be skipped by readers who prefer doctrines to organisations. Both traditions claimed Lenin's practice as their inheritance, and both could quote him, because Lenin's practice contained multitudes: the clandestine disciplined party of What Is To Be Done, the mass open party of 1917 recruiting by the hundred thousand, the insurgent party of October, and the governing party of the civil war suppressing rival socialist organisations and then, in 1921, its own factions. The official tradition canonised the last of these phases as the essence, building the monolithic party in which a decision, once taken, bound every member in action and increasingly in speech, and in which the apparatus's personnel policy was the hidden constitution. Democratic centralism, in this reading, emphasised the centralism and retired the democracy to ceremonial elections whose outcomes the secretariat arranged. The Trotskyist tradition located the essence in 1917: a party of ferocious internal argument whose congresses were genuinely contested, whose minorities published and campaigned and sometimes won, and whose unity was reproduced by persuasion rather than by administrative filtration. Historians outside both camps have noted that each tradition idealised its chosen phase and that the party of 1917 already contained the practices, the treatment of rival socialists above all, that the governing phase generalised. The substitutionist warning of 1904 belongs here: the young Trotsky had predicted that a party organised on the principle of substitution would end with the apparatus ruling in the class's name, and the older Trotsky, confronting the fulfilment of his own prophecy, conspicuously declined to draw the conclusion some of his followers urged, that the organisational model itself was the poison. He blamed the social conditions, isolation, backwardness, exhaustion, for the degeneration of a form he continued to regard as necessary. Critics from Rosa Luxemburg's tradition, and the council communists after her, held that this refusal was the founder's deepest blind spot, the place where his critique stopped at the threshold of his own biography, since to indict the model was to indict 1917 as he had helped to make it. The question remains genuinely open in the scholarship, and the comparison is enriched rather than settled by admitting it, because it identifies the one variable all four analyses held constant and none examined: each row of the grid presupposes some answer to how a revolutionary organisation should be built, and the grid's silence on that row of the problem is the surest sign that the argument the traditions began is not finished.

## The army decapitated: the military purge as evidence

Among the episodes each tradition must process, the destruction of the Red Army's high command in 1937 and 1938 has a special evidentiary status, because it resists every comfortable reading simultaneously and therefore functions as a test of intellectual honesty rather than of allegiance. In June 1937 Marshal Tukhachevsky, the most innovative Soviet military thinker and Trotsky's former subordinate and critic, was tried in camera with seven senior commanders on charges of espionage for Germany and shot the same day; the purge then consumed a large part of the officer corps the state had spent two decades training, on the eve of the war every analysis agreed was coming. The archival scholarship has established the fabrication of the charges and the mechanics of the operation, including the role of forged materials circulated through foreign intelligence channels, while continuing to argue over the proportions of central direction and institutional momentum. For the official analysis, the episode was processed at the time as the unmasking of treason and has since been processed as an aberration of the cult, a formula that cannot explain why a socialist state, governed by the class whose state it was, would decapitate its own defence on fabricated evidence and then rehabilitate the victims posthumously while retaining the system that produced the fabrications. For the Trotskyist analysis, the purge confirmed the caste's psychology with horrible precision: a usurping stratum, fearing any alternative centre of authority and any institution with an independent esprit, destroyed the commanders whose prestige might have rivalled its own, even at the price of the state's survival prospects, because its first interest was never the state but its monopoly. Trotsky, who had built the officer corps and feuded with Tukhachevsky within it, wrote of the affair as the bureaucracy's self exposure. The dissident analyses folded the episode into their accounts with equal ease, too much ease, and the disciplined reader should be suspicious of all three confirmations arriving so punctually. What the episode settles, minimally and really, is a fact about the regime's decision structure: it could take an action catastrophic to its own stated purposes, on false information it had manufactured, without any institutional mechanism capable of arresting the process once begun. Every row of the grid must carry that fact, and the rows differ mainly in whether they classify it as an accident of personnel, a symptom of caste rule, or the normal functioning of a ruling class securing itself, a difference the reader now has the equipment to grade.

## Collectivisation: the hardest case in the comparison

If one episode must be chosen at which every tradition's account is strained to its limit, the collectivisation of the peasantry between 1929 and 1933 is that episode, and it is handled here under the contested record discipline this cluster requires: described as the scholarship establishes it, in qualitative terms, with the demographic accounting reserved to the Soviet cluster's own protocol. The sequence is not in serious dispute. Faced with procurement failures and committed, from 1928, to breakneck industrialisation, the leadership resolved to abolish individual peasant farming as the state's economic base, driving the peasantry into collective farms by a campaign that combined propaganda, taxation, arrest and the deportation of those classified as kulaks, a category elastic enough to include any household that resisted. The peasantry answered with the weapons of the weak and some that were not weak at all: slaughtering livestock rather than surrendering it, concealing grain, fleeing to the construction sites, and in places rising in revolts that the security forces suppressed. Livestock herds collapsed; sowing contracted; and in 1932 and 1933 famine struck the grain regions, Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the North Caucasus and the Volga among them, while the state continued to extract and export grain and sealed the borders of the afflicted regions against the starving. The human cost belongs, by the rule this article has adopted, to the cluster that counts it; the scale, in the historians' qualitative register, was catastrophic, a demographic disaster of the first rank inflicted by policy on the population in whose name the state ruled.

Now watch each tradition process the episode, because the processing is the comparison. The official tradition, after 1956, classified the catastrophe as an excess of implementation: the line had been correct and necessary, the methods had violated the voluntary principle Lenin had laid down, and individual leaders bore responsibility for costs that a correct application would have avoided. The formula protects the classification of the state by converting a structural outcome into a personnel failure, and it has to explain away the awkward fact that the violations were not local improvisations but directives, quotas and deadlines issued by the centre and enforced by its plenipotentiaries. Trotsky's analysis, written as the famine unfolded, held that the bureaucracy had lurched from years of concession to the peasantry into an adventurist assault upon it, that collectivisation decreed by administrative terror, without the technology, the agronomy or the consent that alone could make collective farming productive, had produced a caricature of socialisation whose costs the caste then concealed. His criticism had an edge of priority, since the opposition had warned against both the earlier drift and the later panic; it also had a limit its critics have always underlined, because the opposition's own programme had proposed extracting the accumulation fund from the same peasantry through the price mechanism, and the distance between extraction by unequal exchange and extraction by requisition, while real and morally significant, is a distance within a shared premise that the village would pay for the town's industry. The dissident traditions read the famine years as the bureaucracy's founding act of class rule, the violent subordination of the whole rural population to the accumulation plan, and state capitalism in particular rested much of its case on this reading: an economy whose surplus pump was operated by terror against its own producers was, on that account, exhibiting the primitive accumulation of a new exploiting order rather than the growing pains of a workers' state. The grading cannot award the episode to any contestant outright. It can record that the premise shared by the two principal traditions, that the peasantry's surplus would finance industrialisation, was enacted by the victors in a form so violent that it destroyed much of the surplus's source, and that the tradition which warned against the method had no tested method of its own to set against the warning. That is the counterfactual constraint operating at the level of a whole society's fate, and it is why this episode, more than any other, forbids the morality play format in which the comparison is usually sold.

## Rehabilitations and the memory of the defeated

The Soviet state's own handling of the comparison's memory offers a coda in which the grading continued by administrative means. The rehabilitations that followed 1953 proceeded in stages and by category: first the surviving prisoners and the posthumously cleared of the lesser cases, then, after the Twentieth Congress, the party cadres destroyed in the late nineteen thirties, whose names were restored to the record by the very institution that had erased them. The defendants of the great public trials waited longest. Bukharin and Rykov were judicially rehabilitated only in 1988, in the glasnost years, when the state was attempting to recover a usable past and found it needed the economist of gradualism it had shot fifty years earlier; Zinoviev and Kamenev were cleared in the same period. Trotsky was never rehabilitated by the Soviet Union at all. His name remained, to the end of the state he had co founded, in the category of the unpersons, his books unprinted, his role in the revolution and the civil war assigned in the textbooks to others or to no one, the famous photographs retouched to remove him from podiums he had shared with Lenin. The asymmetry is itself an analysis in negative form. Bukharin could be rehabilitated because his tradition, gradualist construction within the state, could be annexed to the state's own later needs; Trotsky could not, because his analysis named the state's ruling stratum as the usurper, and a bureaucracy does not posthumously acquit the man whose charge against it is its own existence. After 1991 the memory war migrated into the open market of Russian public life, where the founder of the Red Army has been variously demonised as a bloody adventurer, mourned as the revolution's lost alternative, and strip mined for quotation by camps he would have opposed; the Soviet state's successor has never found a settled use for him. For the purposes of this comparison the episode contributes one disciplined observation: institutions reveal their classifications in what they can afford to remember, and the official tradition's lifelong inability to print the name of the revolution's second figure, except as a curse, is evidence about that tradition's epistemology as weighty as any of its falsified predictions, since a doctrine confident of its classification of the past does not need to airbrush the photographs.

## The transitional method and the founding of a tradition

A comparison of doctrines should not close its narrative sections without the document in which Trotskyism attempted to convert its analysis into a strategy for the epoch it believed was opening, because later generations of the tradition treated that document as the founder's testament and graded one another by fidelity to it. The Transitional Programme, adopted at the founding conference of the Fourth International in 1938 under the title The Death Agony of Capitalism and the Tasks of the Fourth International, proposed a method rather than a list of ultimate aims: a system of transitional demands, sliding scales of wages and hours, workers' control of production, the expropriation of monopolies under workers' management, soviets wherever struggle threw them up, designed to begin from the immediate needs of workers and to lead, by the logic of the struggle itself, toward the question of power. The method embodied the founder's diagnosis of the epoch's central problem, which he stated as a crisis of leadership: the productive forces, on his account, had outgrown the old society, the objective conditions for revolution were ripe to the point of rotting, and what was missing was a party capable of acting on the analysis he had spent exile constructing. Read against the comparison, the programme is the point at which Trotskyism completed its self definition as a tradition: it possessed a doctrine of the revolution's dynamics, an analysis of the state that had issued from the revolution, a strategy of demands, and a martyrology. What it did not possess, and what the founder's death in 1940 froze beyond revision, was any experience of administering its programme or any mechanism for updating the epochal diagnosis when the postwar world declined to behave as a death agony, delivering instead the longest boom in capitalism's history and the bureaucracy's greatest territorial expansion in the same decade. The tradition's subsequent history, a long argument between those who treated the 1938 texts as a completed science and those who treated them as a method to be reapplied, recapitulates in miniature the comparison's deepest theme, the difference between a canon and a critique. It is a difference the official tradition never had to face, because its canon was guarded by a state; the founder's followers faced it without one, and their uneven record, preserving the critique at the cost of fragmenting over its custody, is the tradition's final exhibit in its own case.

## Class analysis and moral verdict kept separate

The third discipline announced in the introduction can now be stated with the whole argument behind it, because the material has supplied examples of every way the two operations can be confused and of the damage each confusion does. A class analysis identifies what a social stratum is: the source of its income, its relation to the means of production, the mechanisms by which it reproduces itself, the interests those facts generate. Its claims are empirical and can be checked, argued with, revised. A moral verdict evaluates what was done: the fabricated trials, the deportations, the famine policy, the assassination of an exile with an ice axe in his study, and renders judgement on the persons and institutions responsible. Its claims are ethical and answer to evidence of a different kind, the evidence of acts and intentions. Serious history requires both operations and is corrupted by substituting either for the other. When a moral verdict is made to do the work of analysis, the writer concludes that because the regime was criminal, its economy must have been capitalist, or its property forms worthless, or its achievements illusory, none of which follows; criminality is a property of conduct, not a classification of property relations, and history is full of exploitative orders whose crimes do not settle what they were. When analysis is made to do the work of verdict, the corruption runs the other way: the writer concludes that because the property forms were progressive, or the bureaucracy merely a caste, or the terror explicable by encirclement, judgement should be suspended on acts that would be condemned without hesitation in any other setting, and explanation curdles into the machinery of excuse. Both corruptions flourish in the literature of this comparison because both traditions have institutional reasons to commit them, the one to convert its founder's murder into a classification, the other to convert its classification into an acquittal.

### How should class analysis and moral judgement be separated?

A class analysis asks what a stratum is, how its income and power arise, and what interests follow, and its claims can be checked against evidence. A moral verdict asks what was done and whether it can be justified. Confusing the two produces history in which condemnation substitutes for explanation.

Applied consistently, the separation yields judgements that partisans of both camps find uncomfortable, which is the operational sign that it is working. It is possible to hold that Trotsky's classification of the bureaucracy as a caste was, on the evidence of inheritance and property, the shrewdest single judgement in the dispute, and simultaneously to hold that its author bears his share of responsibility for the coercive order whose germs were present in the government he served, since responsibility attaches to acts and his acts are documented. It is possible to hold that the trials were fabrications and the terror a crime of historic scale, and simultaneously to hold that the industrialisation achieved under the bureaucracy's rule was real, that the literacy campaigns reached millions, and that the state's war effort saved its population from a conqueror whose plans for them were exterminatory, since achievements are also facts and do not launder the crimes committed alongside them. It is possible to grade the official analysis as falsified in its central predictions and still to refuse the conclusion that everything built under its banner was a fraud, just as it is possible to grade the Trotskyist analysis as the finest critique of the bureaucracy ever written and still to refuse the counterfactual that its author in power would have inaugurated an age of soviet democracy. Each of those compound judgements is available only to a reader who keeps the two ledgers separate, and the habit of keeping them is, in the end, the transferable skill this whole comparison exists to teach, because every subsequent argument about a revolutionary state, a liberation movement in power, or a ruling stratum governing in the name of the ruled, will present the same temptation to settle the classification with a verdict or to dissolve the verdict in a classification.

## What the comparison leaves standing

The grading is complete and its results can be gathered without pretending they amount to a coronation. Of the four analyses, the official classification carries the heaviest failures: falsified in its prediction that coercion would wither, unable to explain the terror in its own terms, driven to personality cults of explanation that its own method forbade, and dependent for its survival on the suppression of the very inquiry it claimed to embody. The Trotskyist classification carries the finest predictive record of any contestant, the only self refutation clause in the field, and a central concept that outlived its stipulated test only through its followers' reinterpretations; it remains the indispensable starting point for studying the bureaucracy and an insufficient resting point. The state capitalist classification explains the economy's extractive dynamics and the ease of the final conversion better than its rivals, at the price of an account of competition that works by analogy. The collectivist classification named the system's novelty and its ruling stratum's class character most unflinchingly, and was answered by the system's refusal to consolidate as the durable civilisation the theory announced. Around the four rows sits the counterfactual, graded by the constraint rather than by sympathy: the defeated alternative was neither the democracy of its later legend nor the simple duplicate of the victors, and its founder's record in office, the opposition's own economic severity, and the untested character of every Trotskyist programme must be entered on the same page as the victors' crimes, which require no counterfactual to be judged and lose nothing by the company. What a reader takes away, finally, is a set of instruments rather than a team: a grid that forces any classification to state its refutation conditions, a constraint that forces any hypothetical to pay for its assumptions out of the record, and a habit of keeping the two ledgers, class and conscience, in separate columns. The argument between the traditions will continue wherever the Soviet experience is studied, because the questions it posed, what a revolution becomes in isolation, what a ruling stratum is, how a movement should judge its own defeats, have outlived the state that posed them and show no sign of retiring.

## The nearest approaches to power: Bolivia and Ceylon

The statement that no Trotskyist current has held state power is accurate at the level of governments and incomplete at the level of events, and the two occasions on which the tradition came within reach of power repay examination precisely because they test the counterfactual from an unfamiliar angle. In Bolivia in 1952, a revolution destroyed the army and placed the tin miners' militias in effective control of the streets; the miners' federation had adopted in 1946, at Pulacayo, a programme drafted with the participation of the country's small Trotskyist party, a document in the transitional method demanding nationalisation of the mines under workers' control and a government of the labour movement. The revolution's leadership passed instead to a nationalist party, which nationalised the mines, conceded universal suffrage and land reform, and gradually reintegrated the miners into a state the militias had made possible; the Trotskyist party, influential in the unions and marginal in the state, split over whether to enter the government its programme had helped to inspire. The episode is the closest the tradition came to the levers, and its lesson is double edged: the transitional method demonstrably organised a real class's demands into a programme that shaped a revolution, and the party holding the method proved unable to convert programmatic influence into power against a nationalist rival with deeper roots. In Ceylon, the Lanka Sama Samaja Party, founded in 1935 and Trotskyist in its mature allegiance, became for a generation the principal opposition party of a parliamentary state, the largest Trotskyist organisation in the world relative to its country, with genuine mass support among plantation and urban workers. Its crisis arrived in 1964, when its leadership entered a coalition government with the bourgeois nationalist party it had spent decades opposing, accepting ministries under a programme of capitalist management; the Fourth International expelled it, and the decision has been argued over in the tradition ever since as the moment a Trotskyist party, offered the substance of office at the price of its programme, chose office. Neither episode refutes the observation that the tradition never governed; both refine it. Where Trotskyists approached power, they did so as the junior partner of forces their theory classified as alien, and the discipline their theory imposed, no confidence in nationalist governments, no subordination of the workers' movement, was the first casualty of the approach. The pattern confirms, from the opposite side of the world and the opposite end of the century's middle decades, the structural dilemma the counterfactual constraint identifies at the centre: programmes written for a revolutionary situation meet situations that are always partly something else, and the distance between the programme and the situation is where traditions are decided.

## One constitution read four ways

Worked examples discipline general claims, and the Soviet constitution of 1936 offers a compact one, because all four analyses were obliged to read the same document and produced four incompatible readings of a single text. The constitution proclaimed universal, equal and direct suffrage by secret ballot, replacing the class weighted voting of the earlier Soviet charters; it guaranteed in writing freedoms of speech, press, assembly and street demonstration; it entrenched the two forms of socialist property, state and collective, as the economic foundation; and it was adopted, after a staged national discussion, in the same season that the first Moscow trial was being prepared. The official reading was straightforward: the charter registered achieved socialism, its democratic forms now safe to universalise because exploiting classes no longer existed to abuse them, and its guarantees embodied the constitution of a society that had solved the problem of power. Trotsky's reading, delivered in The Revolution Betrayed with the ink barely dry, treated the document as the bureaucracy's juridical self portrait and its insurance policy. The extension of the ballot, he argued, abolished even the formal privilege of the proletariat as a class, dissolving the workers as a political body into an undifferentiated population of individual citizens among whom the caste could rule without a class constitution to remind anyone of the original distribution of power; the guarantees were decorations on a monopoly, since the party's leading role, written into the text, reserved every organised means of exercising the freedoms to the apparatus itself. The state capitalist reading declined to be interested in the superstructure's drapery at all, locating the constitution's significance in its property chapters, which codified the state sector's dominance and the collective farms' subordinate form exactly as an exploiting order codifies its relations. The collectivist reading found in the charter the new ruling class's frank self constitution, a basic law in which the bureaucracy, through its party, assigned itself permanent direction of the state as a constitutional principle rather than a temporary usurpation. Four readings, one document, and the disagreement is not about what the text says, which any reader can verify, but about what kind of object a constitution is: a register of social relations achieved, a mask adopted by a caste, a codification of exploitation, a charter of class rule. The worked example generalises. Every major artefact of the period, a plan fulfilled, a trial transcript, a victory parade, a famine denied, admits the same four readings generated by the same four premises, and a student who can generate all four from the premises, and then say which premise the evidence in the artefact actually strains, has learned the comparison rather than memorised it.

## The historian in exile: Trotsky's second arsenal

Any grading of Trotsky as an analyst that confines itself to his polemics understates the instrument he brought to the comparison, because his most influential analytical work was historical, and the method of his History of the Russian Revolution, published in exile between 1930 and 1932, is the unspoken foundation of everything his tradition later claimed about the Soviet state. The History argued, through the narrative of 1917, that revolutions are made by masses whose consciousness changes at a speed no leadership can schedule, and that the party which succeeded did so by a rare attunement to that changing consciousness rather than by the mechanical virtues of its apparatus. Its author, who had chaired the Petrograd Soviet and organised the insurrection, wrote himself into the account with a candour about his own role that embarrassed his followers and armed his enemies, and produced, almost incidentally to the polemic, the theory of uneven and combined development as a general account of how backward countries enter modern history: not by repeating the stages of the advanced, but by combining the most archaic with the most modern forms under the whip of external necessity. That theory mattered to the later comparison more than any slogan, because it supplied the framework inside which the Soviet bureaucracy could be analysed as a product of combination, a modern planning apparatus fused with the administrative habits of the old regime, rather than as a conspiracy or a fall from grace. It also mattered to the founder's own standing as a writer, which was a material fact in the dispute's history: the exile's books were read far beyond his movement, his history entered the general literature of revolution, and the official tradition, possessing every printing press in a sixth of the world, never produced a narrative of 1917 that could be read outside the catechism. Stalin's own historical writing, the Short Course, achieved circulation on a scale the exile could not dream of, and achieved it as an instrument of instruction rather than of inquiry; the two books, set side by side, embody the comparison's epistemological axis in physical form. One tradition's founder wrote a history that specialists who reject his politics still assign; the other tradition's founder supervised a history that its own successors withdrew. Grading analyses includes grading their instruments, and on the instrument of history the exile's advantage is not contested even by the scholars who grade his classifications most severely.

## Late Stalinism: the bureaucracy at war with itself

The final years of the founder of the official tradition, from the victory of 1945 to his death in 1953, supply a closing body of evidence about the system he built, and the comparison would be incomplete without it, because those years tested the system's capacity for self correction at the moment of its greatest triumph. Victory brought no relaxation. The ideological campaigns of the Zhdanov period tightened control over literature, science and scholarship, extending to the celebrated session at which genetics was placed under the authority of Lysenko's doctrines and a discipline was ruined by decree. The Leningrad Affair of 1949 and 1950 destroyed the leadership of the party's second city on fabricated charges of separatism, a purge directed not at any opposition, since none existed, but at a network of the regime's own most loyal administrators whose offence was a degree of institutional coherence the centre chose to read as conspiracy. In January 1953 the announcement of a Doctors' Plot, in which Kremlin physicians were accused of murdering Soviet leaders on behalf of foreign intelligence and Jewish organisations, opened what appeared to be the overture to a new terror of the old scale, accompanied by a press campaign of undisguised antisemitic undertone; the dictator's death in March arrested the process, and within weeks the new leadership released the doctors and acknowledged the case as a fabrication of the security organs. Each tradition filed the sequence in its row without difficulty, which is again a reason for the disciplined reader to discount the filings and attend to the undisputed structure underneath. A system in its fourth decade, victorious in war, unchallenged by any organised opposition, nonetheless reproduced fabricated conspiracies against its own administrators and its own doctors as a routine instrument of management, and possessed no mechanism, short of the death of the man at its apex, by which the fabrication could be interrupted. Trotsky's caste theory had predicted the bureaucracy's fearfulness and its reliance on the police as the arbiter of its internal life; it had not predicted, because it could not, the specific pathology of the last years, in which the arbiter began manufacturing the threats it existed to defeat. The official tradition's own account of the period, a cult's excesses corrected by the party, names the phenomenon and declines to explain why the party's institutions were the cult's instrument rather than its restraint. On the evidence of the final years, the comparison's verdict on the two traditions' master concepts can be sharpened to a sentence: the bureaucracy behaved, at the end as at the beginning, as a power whose preservation outranked every purpose it proclaimed, and the analyses differ, in the last analysis, only in what they call such a power and in what they instruct the powerless to do about it.

## The argument in the colonial world

The comparison's geography has so far been European, and correcting that imbalance changes its complexion, because in one colonial territory the Trotskyist tradition was not a sect of exiles but a mass movement that outpolled its official rival in open elections. In French Indochina during the nineteen thirties, the Trotskyists around Ta Thu Thau built, under conditions of colonial illegality, the strongest opposition current in the Vietnamese workers' and intellectual movement, winning municipal elections in Saigon in alliance with, and then against, the official party, and publishing newspapers whose circulation their rivals could not match. Their analysis applied the dispute's categories to a colonial revolution: against the International's line of alliance with a supposedly progressive national bourgeoisie, the Indochinese Trotskyists argued for the independent organisation of workers and peasants and for soviet forms, pointing to Shanghai in 1927 as the standing warning of what subordination cost. The end of the story belongs in any honest grading. In the uprising of August 1945 and the settling of accounts that followed, the Viet Minh, directed by the official tradition, hunted the Trotskyist cadres; Ta Thu Thau was captured and executed in September 1945, and his movement was physically annihilated as an organised force within months, in one of the few episodes where the dispute between the traditions was resolved, on the ground, by the method the Moscow trials had pioneered. The wider colonial world offered the two traditions different inheritances. The official parties commanded the prestige and the resources of the one state that called itself socialist, and their strategy of national fronts subordinated social revolution to anti imperialist alliance in country after country, a strategy whose costs and benefits the Chinese and Spanish cases had already exhibited. The Trotskyist critique of that strategy, formulated for Europe, travelled to the colonies in the baggage of students and sailors and proved, in Indochina at least, to have a mass audience waiting for it. What the colonial evidence adds to the grading is asymmetry of a new kind: the official tradition's analysis was everywhere backed by a state and its instruments, including, in Vietnam, the instrument of execution against its rival, while the Trotskyist analysis repeatedly demonstrated an ability to win audiences it had no power to protect. A theory's truth is not decided by its audiences, any more than by its martyrs, but a comparison that omits the colonial front mistakes the dispute's centre of gravity, which was never only Moscow and never only doctrine.

## Lenin as the disputed inheritance

Both traditions marched under a portrait, and the struggle over the meaning of Lenin's legacy was not a decorative quarrel about a mausoleum but the constitutional argument of the whole dispute, since in a movement that treated theory as the guide to action, custody of the founder's texts was custody of legitimacy itself. The very word Leninism entered circulation as a factional instrument in 1924, popularised by the troika's spokesmen to denote a finished orthodoxy against which Trotskyism could be measured and found deviant; Trotsky, who had spent the years before 1917 in sharp conflict with Lenin over organisation and had been reconciled with him in the revolution, was thereby assigned the permanent status of latecomer to a doctrine defined by his opponents. His counter claim rested on the convergence of 1917 and on texts: Lenin's April Theses had adopted the strategic perspective of permanent revolution in substance, Lenin's last writings had diagnosed the bureaucracy in terms the opposition could quote, and Lenin's testament had recommended the removal of the man the troika's machinery was enthroning. The official counter archive replied with Lenin's polemics against Trotsky across two decades, his criticisms of the militarisation proposals and the trade union statification of 1920, and his insistence, at the end, on the alliance with the peasantry as the regime's lifeline, which Bukharin's camp read as a charter for gradualism. Archival scholarship has complicated every one of these deployments without rescuing any of them entirely, because the documentary Lenin is genuinely multiple: the same man authored the April Theses and the defence of the faction ban, the proposal to remove Stalin and the insistence that the party's unity was the supreme law of its survival. The traditions' fight over the inheritance therefore replays, inside the exegesis of one corpus, the comparison's entire structure. Each side selected the Lenin its analysis required, which is why the exegetical argument could never be settled and why the serious student is advised to treat the duelling quotations as data about the traditions rather than as verdicts about the founder. What can be said without partisanship is narrower and more valuable: Lenin's final dictated notes show the founder of the state identifying its bureaucratic deformation as the central danger before either tradition had a name, and the suppression of those notes by the men who claimed his mantle is the first document in the archive of the comparison, preceding every doctrine it contains.

## The names themselves: an asymmetry in the vocabulary

The comparison's very title conceals an asymmetry that the reader should register before relying on either term. Trotskyism is a name its bearers accepted and eventually embraced, though it began as an accusation coined by opponents to suggest a personal deviation from a collective doctrine. Stalinism is a name its subject never accepted and its bearers in power repudiated: the official tradition called its doctrine Marxism Leninism, understood Stalin as that doctrine's continuator rather than its founder of a separate system, and treated the word Stalinist as a hostile label imposed by enemies to personalise a collective achievement. Inside the Soviet Union the approved vocabulary ran the other way, with Trotskyite functioning as a term of criminal abuse, expandable to cover any deviation and eventually any victim, while no corresponding criminalisation attached to the name of the man in power. The asymmetry matters for method. A comparison that uses one side's self description and the other side's indictment as its two neutral terms has rigged its scales at the level of vocabulary, and the honest procedure, adopted throughout this article, is to use both contested labels while remembering that each is a claim. Trotskyism claims that the founder's analysis defines a coherent tradition distinguishable from the doctrine that ruled in Moscow. Stalinism claims that what ruled in Moscow was a distinct formation, personal and systematic enough to bear one man's name, rather than Marxism itself or an anonymous bureaucracy's improvisation. The four analyses of the grid can each be restated as a ruling on that second claim: the official row denies the category, the Trotskyist row replaces it with the caste, the state capitalist and collectivist rows replace it with their respective classes. Textbook usage, which prints the two names as symmetrical isms facing one another across a chapter, therefore smuggles in the one conclusion the comparison exists to test, that the dispute was between two doctrines of comparable type. It was, on the evidence assembled here, a dispute of a stranger shape: between an analysis produced in exile, which named its subject a bureaucracy, and a state doctrine which denied that any subject existed to be named beyond the building of socialism itself, and much of the dispute's ferocity, and its longevity, comes from that mismatch of genres as much as from any disagreement over facts.

## What the opposition would have needed

The counterfactual section graded the defeated alternative against its protagonists' records; a complementary exercise, less often attempted, asks what institutional innovations the alternative would have required in order to become what its later legend describes, and the answer clarifies why the legend is a legend. A party democracy restored in 1927 would have operated inside a state that had already criminalised every other party, suppressed the independent soviets as functioning bodies, and subordinated the unions; the opposition's platform spoke of reviving all three, but revival is not a policy, it is a direction, and between the direction and the destination lay questions its authors never answered in print. Would legalised factions inside the party have been permitted their own press, their own funds, their own candidates for soviet election against the majority's? Would soviet elections have admitted candidates of the suppressed socialist parties, the Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries whose organisations the government both camps served had dissolved? Would the security police have been placed under soviet control, its budget published, its powers of administrative exile abolished, and if so, by what majority, in what congress, guarded by which armed force? Trotsky's writings gesture toward the logic of these questions, his demand for soviet democracy implies affirmative answers to several, and his record supplies negative evidence on others, since the man who would have presided over the liberalisation had organised the suppression of the Kronstadt soviet's demands when they were raised against his own government. The point of the exercise is not to convict the opposition of hypocrisy. It is to mark the distance between a platform and a constitutional order, and to show that the alternative tradition, for all its analytical superiority on the bureaucracy, never produced the institutional design that would have made its alternative self sustaining. Traditions are graded in this article by prediction, explanation and failure, and the missing design belongs in the failure column of the tradition that diagnosed the disease most accurately, because a diagnosis whose therapy is unspecified is a prognosis, and the prognosis, degeneration without end, is what the subsequent century in fact delivered. The official tradition, for symmetry, never produced an institutional design either, for the opposite reason: its institutions existed, functioned exactly as designed, and required no specification because specification would have described them.

## Reading The Revolution Betrayed closely

Because the whole comparison leans on one book more than on any other, the book itself deserves a reading rather than a citation, and its architecture explains both its power and its limits. Trotsky organised the work as a descending examination, beginning with the achievements he insisted on registering before any indictment: the industrial growth rates, the abolition of unemployment, the spread of literacy and technical training, the transformation of a peasant empire into an industrial power within a generation. Only after the ledger of gains had been entered, at a length apologists rarely acknowledge, did the analysis turn to the distribution of the product, and there the statistics became an indictment, because the official figures themselves, corrected for their manipulations, showed a society stratifying by income and privilege at a speed the author compared, to the discomfort of his friends, with the stratification of capitalist societies at a similar stage. The middle chapters follow the bureaucracy into its habitats: the party, where membership had become a career; the family, where the conservative turn was anatomised as the caste's domestic constitution; the youth, being trained in obedience and informing; the army, officered increasingly by a privileged command stratum with restored ranks and salutes. The famous formulation of the state's dual nature arrives only after the evidence has been walked through institution by institution, and the book closes not with a prophecy of doom but with the prognosis its grid row records: the regime of the bureaucracy is a transitional formation whose contradictions would be resolved either by the working class overthrowing the caste or by the caste converting itself into a possessing class, and the book declares the second outcome a possibility written into the first's delay. Two features of the text explain its survival when so much exile polemic has dated. The first is the discipline of the opening ledger: by entering the achievements at full value before the indictment, the author made the indictment unanswerable by the charge of blindness, and the official responses, which were mostly silence inside the Soviet Union and denunciation outside it, never engaged the ledger at all. The second is the book's candour about its own uncertainty, its repeated insistence that the formation it described was unprecedented and its categories provisional, the quality that flowered two years later into the stipulated war test. A close reading also exposes the limits fairly. The book's Russia is seen through the press and through informants, its village is thin compared with its ministries, and its author's political hopes organise the prognosis in ways he would have been the first to detect in an opponent. None of that diminishes the central fact for the grading: of all the texts produced by any party to the dispute, this is the one that later evidence, from the archives and from the state's own terminal crisis, has treated most kindly.

## Two funerals and an oath

The dispute's opening scene was a funeral, and its staging contained the whole subsequent argument in miniature. Lenin died in January 1924 at Gorki, and the party he had founded converted its mourning into a foundation ritual: the body was embalmed against the wishes expressed by his widow, a mausoleum raised in Red Square, the city of Petrograd renamed for him, and a mass recruitment drive, the Lenin Levy, enrolled hundreds of thousands of new members whose induction the apparatus controlled and whose votes in the cells diluted the old guard that remembered the party's argumentative past. Trotsky was absent from the funeral, convalescing in the Caucasus from the illness that dogged him through the succession years, and the message that misinformed him of the funeral's date became an item of permanent controversy: his defenders called it the apparatus's first forgery, his critics a self serving legend constructed afterwards, and the archives have not wholly closed the question of who sent the telegram and what it said. What is documented is the use made of the absence. At the memorial session of the Congress of Soviets, Stalin delivered the funeral oration in the form of a litany of vows, we swear to thee, Comrade Lenin, repeated clause by clause like a liturgy, a performance that announced the new style of the regime's politics with perfect clarity: doctrine as oath, loyalty as liturgy, the founder's texts as scripture to be guarded by a priesthood of which the speaker was presenting himself as chief celebrant. Trotsky's partisans have always read the scene symbolically, the orator of the insurrection absent and silenced while the organisation man swore the creed over the coffin, and the symbolism is legitimate so long as its status is recognised: it is interpretation, not evidence, and the counterfactual built on the missed train, the founder's heir cheated of his succession by a telegram, is the smallest and most sentimental of all the hypotheticals the constraint exists to grade. The funeral's documented consequences need no symbolism. Within the year, the party's composition had been transformed by the Levy, its internal newspapers had been brought under discipline, the testament recommending the General Secretary's removal had been read only to delegations under instruction of secrecy, and the doctrine of Leninism had been defined in a series of lectures by the man the testament warned against, as the standard against which the absent mourner was measured and condemned. Institutions are rarely present at their own creation, but the archive of January 1924 comes close: a movement founded on the expropriation of the expropriators began its bureaucratic career by expropriating a funeral.

## The International as a machine

The official tradition's instrument in the world movement deserves the same institutional attention as its doctrine, because the conversion of the Communist International from a federation of parties into an arm of one state's policy was a process with mechanisms, dates and personnel, not a metaphor of decline. The conditions of admission had from the beginning required discipline; what changed across the nineteen twenties was the direction of accountability. National leaderships came to be confirmed, removed and sometimes annihilated by decision in Moscow, financed substantially from Moscow, and trained in Moscow's schools, where the curriculum of the catechism was instilled into cadres who returned home as its local custodians. The Bolshevisation campaign of the middle decade standardised the internal regime of the member parties on the model of the Russian party after the faction ban: leaderships imposed from above, minorities defined as deviations, congresses arranged as ratifications. Generations of able revolutionaries accepted the discipline as the price of belonging to the one victorious revolution's general staff, and some of the century's most courageous political lives were spent inside that bargain, which should forbid any easy contempt in the grading. The Trotskyist critique of the machine had the advantage of precision about its operations and the disadvantage of proposing, as its alternative, small groups held together by doctrine alone, chronically unable to decide whether the sickness of the official movement was a reason to build independently or a sentence to wait outside history until it healed. The Spanish and Chinese catastrophes were the machine's products, but so, in a longer perspective, was its eventual dissolution by decree in 1943, performed as a diplomatic gesture to wartime allies without consulting the federated parties whose existence it terminated: an International that could be switched off by one state's convenience had confessed, in the act, what it had become. The comparison's doctrinal sections have graded the machine's strategy; its institutional epitaph can be written in one sentence that both traditions' historians can sign, from opposite premises: the organisation founded to generalise the revolution became the instrument by which one state's ruling stratum disciplined the revolution's other heirs, and the Trotskyist tradition exists because a defeated faction of the Russian party refused, at the price of exile and death, to accept the demotion of a world movement into a ministry.

## The Commune yardstick both traditions claimed

One standard for judging the Soviet state was supplied by the traditions' common inheritance and accepted, in words, by every party to the dispute, which makes it the fairest single instrument the comparison possesses. In The State and Revolution, written in hiding in 1917, Lenin revived Marx's account of the Paris Commune as the discovered form of working class rule and distilled its institutional rules: officials elected and recallable at any time, officials paid at workmen's wages so that administration could not become a career of enrichment, the standing army replaced by the armed people, and the fusion of executive and legislative functions in working bodies rather than a parliamentary talking shop set above society. Measure the mature Soviet state against the yardstick its founder had cut. Officials were appointed from above and irrecallable from below. The party maximum that had capped official salaries was abolished, and the access system described in the privilege section replaced the workman's wage with a graded hierarchy of consumption. The standing army and a political police of unprecedented size became the state's most characteristic institutions. Soviets survived as decorative ratifying bodies meeting to applaud unanimous decisions. On the yardstick's four measures, the state failed four. Trotsky's tradition made this measurement the core of its case, arguing that the Commune rules were not utopian ornament but the specific antibodies the revolution had evolved against bureaucratisation, and that their abandonment one by one between 1918 and the faction ban had opened the road the caste later travelled. The official tradition answered that the Commune rules presupposed conditions, a politically experienced working class, a short transition, a revolution spreading across advanced countries, that isolation had abolished, and that judging an embattled state by the constitution of a seventy two day commune repeated the error of the opposition in general: measuring survival against scripture. The answer has the now familiar structure, circumstance against doctrine, and the comparison has already supplied the reader with the means to grade it. Circumstance explains departures; it does not explain why the departures were never reversed when the emergencies receded, why the official wage cap was abolished in peacetime, or why the yardstick itself disappeared from the catechism's account of the state, replaced by a theory in which the Commune's anti bureaucratic devices have no function. A standard that both sides accepted in 1917, and that one side's state inverted on every count within twenty years, is not a partisan weapon. It is the comparison's control experiment, performed by history itself.

## The argument handed down

A comparison of doctrines should end its narrative by tracing how the argument reached the generations who inherited it as history rather than as allegiance, because the channels of transmission explain the form in which the dispute survives. The shock of 1956, when the Twentieth Congress speech leaked into the world's press, broke the official tradition's monopoly on its own past and sent a generation of its adherents searching for an account of how the revolution they served had produced the catalogue the speech recited. Many found the Trotskyist analysis waiting for them, a ready made explanation composed before most of the crimes it explained had been fully documented, and the founder's books, reprinted by independent publishers in the nineteen sixties, acquired a second life as the suppressed archive of the movement's conscience. The student movements of 1968 read the dispute through the lens of their own revolt against bureaucracies everywhere, and found in the Left Opposition's fate a parable of revolutions devoured by their apparatuses, a reading that sometimes flattened the record's complications, above all the founder's own years in power, into the morality play format this article has resisted. In the nineteen seventies the large Western communist parties attempted their own exit from the inheritance in the episode called Eurocommunism, distancing themselves from the Soviet model, affirming democratic liberties as permanent values rather than bourgeois forms, and reaching, without ever acknowledging the source, toward ground the opposition of the nineteen twenties had occupied from the other side of the barricade; the experiment dissolved with the parties themselves, leaving its central question, whether a communist movement could be democratic in means as well as in proclaimed ends, as the dispute's unanswered bequest. What each inheriting generation did to the comparison is instructive in a final way. Each selected from the four analyses the row that explained its own experience of power and disappointment, and each discovered, in the row it had not selected, the explanation of the selection's limits. The grid survives its believers, which is the most that can be asked of any analytical instrument, and the classroom exercises in the persona sections that follow are built on exactly that property: the analyses can be taught as instruments to students who owe allegiance to none of them.

## The opposition Platform read as a programme

One document in the dispute repays the closest reading of all, because it is the defeated alternative in its own words rather than in its founder's later retrospection: the Platform of the Joint Opposition, drafted in 1927 and signed by Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev and their associates as their submission to the Fifteenth Party Congress, which refused to print it. Its economic chapters demanded a genuine five year plan drawn up before rather than after the fact, a systematic transfer of the tax burden toward the kulak, the Nepman and the higher paid official, the use of the foreign trade monopoly and of long term foreign credits to accelerate industrialisation, and measures against the unemployment and the housing famine that the market recovery had left untouched. Its political chapters demanded the restoration of the party's inner life: congresses that decided rather than ratified, officials elected and removable, the discussion of platforms legalised, the apparatus cut back and its salaries restrained, and the soviets revived as organs through which workers actually governed. Its international chapters demanded the independence of foreign communist parties from diplomatic subordination and drew the lessons of the Chinese catastrophe while the catastrophe was still warm. Reading the Platform with the whole subsequent record in view produces a double impression that the grading must hold simultaneously. The document is more concrete, more administrative and less lyrical than the tradition's legend suggests, the work of men who expected to govern and were costing their government; and it is silent, as the earlier section on the opposition's missing design observed, on the constitutional questions its political chapters imply, saying nothing about rival parties, an uncensored press beyond the party's own, or the legal position of the security police whose administrative exile it protested in particular cases. The congress it addressed suppressed it unheard, expelled its signatories, and thereby converted a programme into an indictment: whatever the Platform's insufficiencies, the system that could not permit its printing inside the ruling party's own congress had announced something essential about itself, and the Trotskyist tradition has rested its moral case on that announcement ever since. The official tradition's answer, that the Platform was a factional manoeuvre by defeated leaders seeking a road back to power, is probably accurate as biography and wholly inadequate as refutation, since the truth of a programme's charges does not depend on the motives of the men who sign it. Between the document's concreteness and its silences, the fair grading locates the opposition precisely: a real alternative government with a real programme, standing inside a one party order whose logic it had helped to build and whose outermost walls it never proposed to breach, which is why its defeat could be at once a catastrophe for the revolution's possibilities and something less than the murder of a democracy that had yet to be designed.

The Platform's physical fate deserves a sentence of its own, because it condenses the institutional history of the whole dispute into a single episode. Denied access to the party press, the opposition attempted to print its own document in a privately arranged print shop in Moscow, and the security police raided the premises, seized the type and arrested those involved, presenting the possession of a printing press by members of the ruling party as evidence of an underground conspiracy allied with former White officers, a charge constructed partly around an agent provocateur's contacts. The episode was small and its consequences were large. It established that the argument inside the party would henceforth be policed as a species of crime, that a platform addressed to a party congress could be treated as contraband, and that the distinction between factional disagreement and treason, on which any internal democracy depends, had been administratively abolished. When the congress met that December, delegates voted the opposition's views incompatible with membership in a hall from which the document itself had been excluded as evidence of the crime it was accused of committing by existing. Historians of every school treat the print shop raid as a curiosity of the succession struggle. Read institutionally, it is the moment the comparison's central question stopped being which analysis of the state was correct and became what kind of state makes the asking punishable, and the traditions divided thereafter over a prior question they rarely state: whether the punishing was the system's essence or its emergency reflex, prolonged past all emergency by the men it served.

## For students and exam candidates

Examination questions on this comparison reward a specific architecture, and candidates who build it in advance convert a sprawling topic into a manageable answer. Open by defining the object of the dispute, one state and four analyses of it, rather than by narrating two biographies; markers credit classification over chronology. Deploy the four analyses grid as the answer's skeleton, one row per paragraph, and state for each row the evidence that would count against it, because that single habit lifts a script out of summary and into evaluation. Where the question invites judgement on the counterfactual, Trotsky in power or the opposition unexpelled, apply the counterfactual constraint explicitly: name the founder's record between 1918 and 1921, the opposition's economic severity toward the peasantry, and the absence of any tested Trotskyist administration, then mark the residue as reasoned speculation. Keep the class analysis and the moral verdict in separate paragraphs so that neither contaminates the other's evidence. The companion treatment at [luxemburg trotsky exam answers](/2014/12/15/luxemburg-trotsky-exam-answers/) models this architecture under timed conditions, and the four analyses lend themselves to being kept as a revision card in the study notes tool at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), where each row can carry its own refutation condition as a prompt. Candidates most often lose marks in three places: presenting socialism in one country as a pretext instead of an argument from circumstance, treating the degenerated workers' state as the only alternative classification, and asserting the counterfactual as fact. All three errors are avoidable by the disciplines above, and avoiding them is the difference between a script that retells the dispute and one that adjudicates it.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The four analyses grid was designed in this article as a findable artefact, and its most productive classroom use is as a sorting exercise with the evidence column blanked. Give students the completed first three columns and a dossier of exhibits drawn from the periods covered here: the 1936 constitution's property chapters, the abolition of the party maximum, the text of the war test Trotsky published in 1939, the rehabilitations of 1988, the conversion of office into ownership in the nineteen nineties, the destruction of the officer corps, the Prague Spring's workers' councils. Each group files every exhibit under the analysis it confirms and the analysis it strains, then defends the filing against a group that filed differently; the argument that follows teaches the comparison more durably than any lecture, because students discover that the exhibits are rarely in dispute and the classifications always are. Seminar leaders should enforce the two disciplines as discussion rules: any counterfactual offered in debate must be paid for out of the protagonist's record before it is entertained, and any moral verdict must be labelled as such and prevented from silently classifying property relations. The topic attracts inherited allegiances like few others in the syllabus, and the leader's neutrality is best protected by the method rather than by the chair's authority: steelman each position from its own best text, The Revolution Betrayed for one tradition and the strongest statement of the argument from circumstance for the other, and let the grading tests, prediction, explanation and failure, distribute the honours. A closing exercise that reliably exposes shallow learning is to require each student to write the strongest paragraph against the analysis they personally find most convincing.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The primary literature of the dispute is unusually accessible and unusually treacherous, and working in it requires source habits this article has tried to model. Begin with the programmatic texts in their chronology: the Platform of the Joint Opposition of 1927 for the defeated programme in its own words; Preobrazhensky on primitive socialist accumulation for the economics underneath it; The Revolution Betrayed for the caste analysis at full stretch, read with its author's information conditions in view; the report of the Dewey Commission, published as Not Guilty, for the trials tested by an independent inquiry; and the Short Course, together with the 1952 Economic Problems pamphlet, for the official doctrine's self understanding at its most and least confident. The archival turn has re based every one of these texts without retiring them: use the post 1991 scholarship to verify events, quotas, orders, memoranda, and use the classics to recover the classifications the events were filed under by their contemporaries, since the classifications are themselves evidence about the system that produced them. Journalists borrowing the vocabulary should note the asymmetry documented in this article, that one of the title's two terms is a self description refused by its subject, and should attribute both labels rather than naturalising either. Researchers will find the open problems where the four analyses share a silence: the national question's independent weight, the institutional design the opposition never specified, and the party form all four rows presupposed and none examined. Those silences, rather than the endlessly regraded predictions, are where new work on this comparison remains to be done, and the grid's empty spaces are a more honest map of the field than any tradition's claim to have closed it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Trotskyism and Stalinism?

The difference begins as a disagreement about one state. Trotskyism, named for Leon Trotsky, holds that the Soviet Union was a degenerated workers' state: its nationalised property was a conquest of the revolution, while political power had been usurped by a parasitic bureaucratic caste that required removal through political revolution. The doctrine its opponents call Stalinism held that the same state was socialist, that socialism could be built in one country without waiting for world revolution, and that the party's monolithic unity was the guarantee of construction. Behind those positions stand further divides over permanent revolution, the pace of industrialisation, policy toward the peasantry, internal party democracy, and strategy against fascism, each examined in the body of this article.

### Q: What is socialism in one country?

Socialism in one country was the doctrine, announced from 1924, that the Soviet Union possessed the resources, territory and population to build a complete socialist society within its own borders, without making its survival conditional on revolution abroad. Its strongest form was an argument from circumstance rather than a slogan: the European revolutions had failed, the revolutionary wave had receded, and a government had to build with the materials in its hands or treat its own state as a temporary outpost awaiting rescue. Bukharin gave the doctrine its gradualist economics, proposing that the peasantry be drawn into socialism slowly through cooperatives while industry grew at a sustainable pace. Trotsky's tradition answered that construction in isolation would subordinate the movement everywhere to one state's interests and empower the bureaucracy at home.

### Q: What is a degenerated workers state?

The phrase is Trotsky's classification of the Soviet Union as he judged it from exile. Private ownership of land, factories and banks had been swept away by the revolution, and that transformation he counted a historic gain worth defending against any attempt to restore the old owners. Political rule, however, had passed to a privileged layer of officials who governed without soviet democracy, distributed advantages to themselves, and held power through the party apparatus and the police. Because the defect, on his account, lay in the regime rather than in the property foundations, the remedy followed logically: the working class should carry out a political revolution to remove the bureaucracy while defending the nationalised economy the revolution had created.

### Q: Why did Trotsky call the bureaucracy a caste and not a class?

The distinction turned on property and inheritance. A class, in the Marxist vocabulary he inherited, owns a defined share of the means of production and transmits that position to its children as an asset. A Soviet official's privileges flowed from his office and died with his fall from favour: he could not sell his post, bequeath his ministry, or found a dynasty on his committee seat, however skilfully officials arranged their children's advancement in practice. Trotsky concluded that the stratum fed on property relations it had not created and could not own, which made it a parasitic caste squatting on the revolution's conquests rather than a ruling class in its own right. His critics answered that hereditary advantage through education and placement made the distinction thinner than the theory required.

### Q: What were the rival left analyses of the Soviet Union?

Two further analyses emerged from within the Trotskyist movement itself when dissidents concluded that the degenerated workers' state formula could not survive the evidence. State capitalism, developed systematically by Tony Cliff from the late nineteen forties and reached independently by Raya Dunayevskaya, held that the bureaucracy functioned as a collective capitalist: workers still sold their labour power, the surplus was accumulated by others, and military competition with the West supplied the compulsion that markets supply elsewhere. Bureaucratic collectivism, formulated by Bruno Rizzi in 1939 and developed by Max Shachtman, held that the bureaucracy was the ruling class of a new and exploitative mode of production, neither capitalist nor socialist, resting on its collective control of the state. Both currents refused any defence of the Soviet state.

### Q: Did Trotsky admit his analysis might be wrong?

He did something rarer than an admission: he published, in advance, the condition under which his central classification would have to be abandoned. In polemics written in 1939 and 1940, against erstwhile followers drifting toward bureaucratic collectivism, he laid down the condition plainly: let the bureaucracy survive the coming world war unshaken, its dominion enlarged rather than diminished, and the description of it as a parasitic caste collapses, leaving a new exploiting class as the classification honesty would require. Because the stipulation appeared in print while the result remained undecided, it functions as a genuine test rather than a retrospective excuse. The bureaucracy did survive the war enlarged, and the dispute among his followers over whether his other prediction, eventual capitalist restoration, rescued the classification has continued ever since.

### Q: What were the Moscow Trials?

The Moscow Trials were three public proceedings between 1936 and 1938 in which the surviving leaders of the old Bolshevik opposition were presented as traitors, saboteurs and agents of foreign powers. The first proceeding, in August 1936, consumed Zinoviev and Kamenev; the second, five months later, took Pyatakov and Radek; the third, in March 1938, took Bukharin and Rykov; the principal defendants confessed in open court to fantastic conspiracies and were shot. The cases rested almost entirely on those confessions and contained demonstrable impossibilities, including meetings at demolished hotels and flights that never occurred. An independent commission of inquiry chaired by John Dewey examined the evidence in 1937 and reported the charges against Trotsky and his son unproven. The scholarly consensus treats the trials as fabrications constructed by the security apparatus.

### Q: Was the opposition's economic policy gentler?

No, and the belief that it was is one of the comparison's durable myths. The opposition's economist, Preobrazhensky, had theorised primitive socialist accumulation: the deliberate transfer of resources out of the peasant economy into state industry through unequal exchange, the tax system and the state's grip on foreign trade, conducted openly and by plan. The opposition demanded faster industrialisation than the majority of the middle nineteen twenties was pursuing, sharper taxation of the richer peasants, and a harder line against the private traders flourishing under the New Economic Policy. Its comparative gentleness lay in method and in politics, not in the size of the claim on the village: extraction through prices rather than requisition, and a party regime in which the policy could be argued about. Collectivisation as eventually carried out was both harsher and less planned than anything the opposition had proposed.

### Q: Would Trotskyism have avoided the terror?

The honest answer applies the counterfactual constraint and marks the remainder as speculation. Against a confident yes stands the founder's own record: between 1918 and 1921 Trotsky organised the Red Army with draconian discipline, proposed the militarisation of labour, and supported the ban on party factions under whose authority his own movement was later outlawed. A leadership formed in that school, facing isolation, scarcity and a hostile peasantry, would have commanded formidable instruments of coercion and had shown the will to use them. In the other scale stand the opposition's demands from 1923 onward for elected officials, published platforms and factional rights, which addressed the mechanism by which repression became self perpetuating. A reasoned speculation may credit those demands; it cannot promote them into a guarantee, and no Trotskyist administration exists anywhere to settle the question by example.

### Q: Why does this argument still matter?

The argument matters because its categories outlived the state they were built to classify. Every later debate about a revolution that produced a ruling stratum, a liberation movement hardened into a government, or a planned economy governed without democracy, has been conducted with tools forged in this dispute: the distinction between property forms and political regimes, the question of whether a privileged layer is a class, a caste or a management, and the demand that any classification state the evidence that would refute it. The dispute also preserves a methodological inheritance independent of its subject: steelman the doctrine you oppose, test counterfactuals against the records of their protagonists, and keep the classification of a system separate from the verdict on its crimes. Those habits are transferable to any contested history.

### Q: How does the counterfactual constraint apply to the four analyses of the Soviet state?

Each row of the grid smuggles in a counterfactual, and the constraint audits them one by one. The official analysis implies that without its line the state would have perished, which must be tested against the alternatives contemporaries proposed inside the system and were forbidden to test. The degenerated workers' state implies that political revolution was available and would have restored soviet democracy, which must be tested against the founder's own record in office and the absence of any specified institutional design. The state capitalist and collectivist analyses imply that different property classifications would have yielded better strategy, a claim untestable in office since neither current governed. The constraint does not forbid such claims; it requires them to be labelled speculation and priced according to the record.

### Q: What should a student separate between a class analysis and a moral verdict?

A student should keep two ledgers. The class analysis ledger records what a stratum is: where its income comes from, whether it owns or merely administers the means of production, how it reproduces itself, and what interests follow, all of it checkable against evidence. The moral verdict ledger records what was done: the fabricated trials, the deportations, the famine policy, the murder of exiles, and the responsibility attaching to those who ordered and performed them. Marks are lost when an entry migrates between ledgers, when a student argues that because the regime's crimes were enormous its economy must have been capitalist, or that because its property forms were progressive its crimes should be weighed more gently. Examiners reward the candidate who can enter a fact in both ledgers without letting either falsify the other.

### Q: Why did the bureaucracy's nature become the tradition's central question?

Because every practical question in the movement was downstream of the classification. If the bureaucracy was a parasitic caste on a workers' state, the duties were defence of the state's property against imperialism, combined with preparation for political revolution against its regime. If it was a ruling class, whether capitalist or collectivist, no defence was owed and the state joined the list of exploitative orders to be overthrown socially. If it was merely an administrative stratum of a socialist society, loyalty and correction from within were the duties. Alliances, war policy, party building and even the reading of new revolutions all waited on the answer, which is why a dispute about the correct noun for a stratum of officials consumed the revolutionary left for two generations and why the argument was always, beneath the vocabulary, about what was to be done.

### Q: How do the four analyses differ on whether the state could be reformed?

The grid's rows give four distinct answers. The official analysis held that defects were distortions correctable by the party itself, through criticism, education and the removal of faulty individuals, since the system's foundations were sound. The Trotskyist analysis denied that the caste could reform itself out of existence: a stratum ruling through a monopoly of appointment would not vote away its monopoly, so change required political revolution, though one that preserved the property forms. The state capitalist analysis treated the state as the property of an exploiting class, against which reform of personnel changed nothing essential and social revolution was the only remedy. The collectivist analysis agreed on the remedy for its own reasons, regarding the new class society as no more reformable from within than capitalism, and expecting it to be superseded rather than amended.

### Q: What evidence did each analysis predict would settle the argument?

Each analysis placed a different wager. The official analysis predicted that coercion would wither and privilege dissolve as socialist construction matured and abundance approached. The Trotskyist analysis predicted the bureaucracy's instability, and its founder added the explicit test that survival through a world war, intact and expanding, would refute the caste designation, alongside the alternative prognosis that the caste would seek to convert privilege into property. The state capitalist analysis predicted behaviour mirroring capitalist accumulation, including crisis tendencies generated by the arms burden. The collectivist analysis predicted that the new class order would consolidate and prove durable like the modes of production before it. The events of 1945, 1956 and 1989 to 1991 are the graded results of those wagers, and each tradition's reading of the results is examined in the body of this article.
