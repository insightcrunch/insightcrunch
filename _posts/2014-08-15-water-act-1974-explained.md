The Water Act 1974 is the statute that built the institutional skeleton on which almost all Indian environmental regulation hangs. Before it, no Indian law created a specialised regulator for pollution of any kind. After it, every state had a board, every significant discharge needed that board's written consent, and a factory that poured untreated effluent into a river was committing an offence against a named prohibition rather than merely behaving badly. The Act did something else that turned out to matter even more than its water provisions: it invented the pollution control board as an institution, and that institution was later handed the Air Act to administer, made the consent authority under the umbrella environment statute's rules, and given the day-to-day running of hazardous waste, biomedical waste, plastic waste and electronic waste regulation. A statute about rivers became the operating system of Indian environmental law, and it remains the place where the system's characteristic strengths and characteristic failures are most clearly visible.

It also carries a distinction no other major Indian statute shares. The Act was enacted by Parliament under Article 252 of the Constitution, on the strength of resolutions passed by state legislatures, because water is a state subject and Parliament had no independent power to legislate on it for the states. That origin has a consequence which is not historical at all. When Parliament amended the Act in 2024 to decriminalise most of its offences and replace imprisonment with monetary penalties, the amendment could not simply extend across India. It took effect in the first instance in Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan, the two states whose assemblies had passed resolutions asking for it, and in the Union territories, and it reaches every other state only when that state's legislature passes a resolution adopting it. The result is that the same discharge of polluting matter is a crime punishable with a minimum of eighteen months in prison in most of India and a monetary penalty in two states and the Union territories, and the map changes each time another assembly resolves to adopt the amendment. No account of the Water Act that misses this mechanism understands the statute, because the mechanism is the statute's deepest feature: it is a central law whose very text is a record of state consent, given state by state, and alterable only in the same way.

## What stood in place of the Act before 1974

The legal cupboard was not bare before 1974, but what it held was not designed for industrial water pollution and could not be made to fit it. The Indian Penal Code punished public nuisance and the fouling of a public spring or reservoir, provisions aimed at individual acts of contamination rather than at continuous industrial discharge, carrying small fines and requiring proof shaped for a different kind of wrong. The law of tort offered the injured riparian owner a suit in nuisance or under the rule in Rylands v Fletcher, which compensated the plaintiff who could afford to sue and proved his damage, but deterred nobody in advance and did nothing for a river whose victims were a whole downstream population rather than one landowner with a lawyer. Municipal and public health legislation empowered local bodies over drains and sanitation within their limits, and municipal sewage was itself among the largest sources of river pollution, so the supposed regulator was frequently the largest unregulated discharger. A handful of states had enacted their own measures of narrower scope, among them the Orissa River Pollution Prevention Act of 1953 and the Maharashtra Prevention of Water Pollution Act of 1969, but these were exceptions, geographically confined and institutionally thin, and most of the country had no water pollution statute at all.

Three structural defects ran through this patchwork. The first was the absence of standards. Nothing in the older law said how much contamination a discharge might lawfully contain, so a court asked to stop a discharge had to construct the wrong out of nuisance doctrine case by case, with no effluent limits to measure against. The second was the absence of a regulator. Enforcement depended on a magistrate, a municipal inspector or an aggrieved plaintiff, none of whom possessed laboratories, sampling powers, technical staff or a continuing mandate to watch a river system rather than react to a complaint. The third was the absence of prevention. The older law was almost entirely ex post: it punished or compensated after pollution had occurred. Nothing required an industry to obtain permission before it began discharging, to build treatment capacity as a condition of operating, or even to tell anyone what it proposed to pour into a stream. A legal regime for industrial water pollution needed all three missing elements, a standard, a standing technical regulator, and a prior permission system, and it needed them in a form that could be applied to thousands of dischargers by an administration of modest capacity.

The policy weather of the early 1970s moved the question from the technical to the political. After the Stockholm conference, the Government of India constituted a National Committee on Environmental Planning and Coordination in 1972, and the case for comprehensive pollution legislation, made in official reports through the preceding decade, acquired a forum and a deadline. The Water Act was the first fruit of that turn, and it is best understood as a deliberately institutional answer rather than a penal one: Parliament's chosen instrument against water pollution in 1974 was not a fiercer criminal code but a new kind of public body, technically staffed, armed with standards, sampling and permission, and the criminal provisions that closed the Act were understood by its designers as the guarantee behind the institution rather than as the institution's daily work.

The official mind had reached this conclusion well before Parliament acted. A committee appointed in 1962 to examine the problem produced a draft enactment for the prevention of water pollution, and that draft was circulated to the state governments and considered by the Central Council of Local Self-Government, from whose deliberations a model bill emerged. The delay of a decade between the committee's work and the statute was substantially a constitutional problem rather than a failure of diagnosis. Water, in the Constitution's distribution of powers, belongs to the states: Entry 17 of the State List covers water supplies, irrigation and canals, drainage and embankments, water storage and water power, subject to the Union's power over inter-state rivers under Entry 56 of the Union List, and public health and sanitation sit in Entry 6 of the same state list. Parliament could legislate on water pollution for the Union territories, but for the states it needed a constitutional door, and the available doors were Article 249, which requires a Rajya Sabha resolution declaring a state subject to be of national interest and lasts only a year at a time, Article 250, which operates only during an emergency, and Article 252, which lets Parliament legislate on a state subject for those states whose legislatures resolve that it should. The United Nations Conference on the Human Environment at Stockholm in 1972, at which India participated and after which the national policy conversation on environmental legislation sharpened considerably, supplied the political impetus. Article 252 supplied the legal machinery. The states were asked, and twelve of them resolved, that Parliament should legislate.

The Statement of Objects and Reasons that accompanied the Bill states the legislative theory in terms worth recovering, because later argument about the Act often proceeds as if the theory were different. The problem identified is the growth of industrialisation and urbanisation discharging increasing quantities of sewage and trade effluent into rivers and streams, rendering the water unfit for the uses to which it is put and injurious to public health. The inadequacy identified is legal: the existing provisions, in the few places they existed, were not designed for prevention, were confined to particular areas or bodies, and supplied no machinery for fixing standards or for acting before pollution occurred. The remedy proposed follows the committee work of the previous decade: central and state boards with technical functions, a consent system for discharges, and penalties in support. The theory, in short, is preventive and institutional; nothing in the founding documents suggests that Parliament expected the criminal courts to be the system's engine, and the subsequent disappointment with prosecution rates is a disappointment with a function the Act's designers assigned to the backstop.

## Article 252 and the making of the Water Act 1974

Article 252(1) provides that if the legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions to the effect that a matter in the State List should be regulated by Parliament by law, Parliament may legislate on that matter for those states, and the resulting Act applies to the resolving states and to any other state that afterwards adopts it by resolution. Article 252(2) completes the arrangement and gives it its rigidity: an Act so passed may be amended or repealed only by Parliament in like manner, and the legislature of a state to which it applies cannot amend or repeal it itself. The consenting state therefore makes a one-way transfer. It asks Parliament in, and thereafter the text belongs to Parliament, although Parliament in turn can alter it for the consenting states only on the footing of further resolutions of the kind that produced the original Act. The preamble of the Water Act recites this foundation expressly, recording that Parliament has no power to make laws for the states on these matters except as provided in Articles 249 and 250, and that resolutions had been passed by all the Houses of the legislatures of Assam, Bihar, Gujarat, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Karnataka, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Tripura and West Bengal. The Act, Act 6 of 1974, received the President's assent on 23 March 1974, and Section 1(3) brought it into force at once in the twelve resolving states and the Union territories, and in any other state on the date of that state's adoption.

The geographic consequence deserves emphasis because it is unusual in the Indian statute book. Most central Acts commence everywhere on a date the Central Government notifies. The Water Act commenced at different times in different places by design, beginning with twelve states and the Union territories and spreading as further state legislatures adopted it over the following years, until its coverage extended to all but a handful of states and, through the Central Board's role in the territories, to every Union territory. The mechanism has a less noticed implication for the Act's interpretation. Because the statute applies to a state by virtue of that state's own resolution, a state that dislikes the Act as amended cannot legislate its way out: it cannot repeal the Act for its territory, cannot soften its penalties by state law, and cannot stiffen them either. Its remedies are political, to press Parliament for amendment, or constitutional. This is the same structural position that the land legislation in an earlier cluster illustrates, where an Article 252 statute on urban land ceilings could likewise be undone only through the [route that the repeal of the urban land ceiling legislation eventually took](/2013/12/01/urban-land-ceiling-act-repeal/), and it explains why, fifty years after enactment, the argument about the Water Act's contents is still an argument conducted in Parliament on the strength of state resolutions rather than in twenty-eight state capitals.

### Does the Water Act 1974 apply to the whole of India?

In substance it applies across nearly the whole country, but not by the route most central Acts take. It began in twelve states whose legislatures had resolved under Article 252 that Parliament should legislate, together with the Union territories, and spread state by state as further legislatures adopted it, accumulating its reach by consent.

The Water Act is not Article 252's only famous product, and the company it keeps is instructive. The Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 had been enacted through the same door two years earlier, on resolutions of state legislatures inviting Parliament to legislate on wild animals and birds, a State List subject; the urban land ceiling legislation followed in 1976 on the same footing. The pattern marks a period in which the Union and the states used the consent mechanism as a working legislative technique for subjects on which national uniformity was wanted but central competence was lacking, and the environmental statutes were its leading case: both the great conservation Act of 1972 and the great pollution Act of 1974 stand on state resolutions. That history matters to how the Water Act should be read. Its uniformity across India was never the uniformity of a command; it is the uniformity of an invitation accepted at different times by different legislatures, and the states' continuing inability to vary it, locked in by Article 252(2), is the deliberate price of that original bargain rather than an oversight in its drafting.

The 2024 amendment has reactivated the mechanism in a way that makes the Act's federal architecture freshly important. The amendment's own preamble recites that resolutions were passed by the Legislative Assemblies of Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan to the effect that the Act should be amended by Parliament, invoking Article 252(1) read with Article 252(2), the clause that governs amendment of a consent-based statute. Its applicability clause mirrors Section 1 of the parent Act almost word for word: it applies in the first instance to Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan and the Union territories, and to any other state that adopts it by resolution, coming into force in an adopting state on the date of adoption. Parliament could not have proceeded otherwise. A decriminalisation of offences under this Act, enacted for non-consenting states by Parliament acting alone, would have sat on no legislative competence, because the original resolutions of 1973 and 1974 consented to the Act they produced and not to every future revision of it. Each state must therefore make a fresh decision about the criminal character of water pollution within its borders, and the states are making those decisions at different speeds, which is why descriptions of the amendment as a national reform are simply wrong. It is a reform available nationally and effective only where adopted, and any compliance advice that fails to ask which side of that line the reader's state stands on is defective at its first step.

## The shape of the statute

The Act is arranged in eight chapters running to Section 64, and the arrangement is itself a map of the regulatory idea. Chapter I gives the short title, extent and commencement and the definitions on which everything else depends. Chapter II constitutes the Central Board and the State Boards. Chapter III provides for Joint Boards for states that agree to share one. Chapter IV sets out the powers and functions of the boards. Chapter V, on prevention and control of water pollution, contains the Act's operative core: the power to declare control areas, the information and sampling powers, the prohibition in Section 24, the consent system in Sections 25 to 28, and the emergency and court provisions in Sections 31 to 33A. Chapter VI deals with funds, accounts and audit. Chapter VII contains penalties and procedure, including the bar on courts taking cognizance except through the routes Section 49 allows. Chapter VIII is miscellaneous: boards' power to delegate, the overriding effect of the Act, the protection of action taken in good faith, and the rule-making powers of the Central and State Governments. Within the [complete guide to environmental law in India](/2014/08/01/environmental-law-india-complete-guide/), this statute occupies the position of the framework piece: the later Air Act copied its institutions, the umbrella Act of 1986 built its authorities alongside them and borrowed its vocabulary, and the National Green Tribunal Act later built an appellate forum over the decisions the boards take under it.

The definitions do real work and repay attention, because they fix the reach of the prohibition and the consent system. A stream includes a river, a water course whether flowing or dry for a part of the year, inland water whether natural or artificial, subterranean water, and sea or tidal waters to the extent the State Government notifies. Groundwater is therefore within the statute, a point of practical importance that is often missed by readers who take the Act to be about rivers alone. Pollution means such contamination of water, or such alteration of its physical, chemical or biological properties, or such discharge of sewage or trade effluent or other liquid, gaseous or solid substance into water, directly or indirectly, as may create a nuisance or render the water harmful or injurious to public health or safety, to domestic, commercial, industrial, agricultural or other legitimate uses, or to the life and health of animals, plants or aquatic organisms. The definition is effect-based rather than list-based: the question is what the discharge does to the water, which is why a substance harmless in one receiving body may be pollution in another, and why the boards' standards, laid down stream by stream and industry by industry, become the operative measure of the wrong.

Two further definitions mark the regulatory boundary. Trade effluent includes any liquid, gaseous or solid substance discharged from premises used for carrying on any industry, operation or process, or treatment and disposal system, other than domestic sewage; sewage effluent means effluent from a sewerage system or sewage disposal works and includes sullage from open drains. An outlet includes any conduit, pipe or channel, open or closed, carrying sewage or trade effluent. The occupier, in relation to a factory or premises, is the person who has control over its affairs, and in relation to a vehicle, the person in possession of it; the 1988 amendment tightened this definition by substituting a wider and more precise text, because earlier prosecutions had spent their energy on identifying the proper accused. Read together, the definitions tell a compliance officer exactly where the statute bites: any substance leaving any premises through any channel into any water, including water underground, is inside the regime, and the person in control of the premises is the person the regime addresses.

The Act's vocabulary outgrew the Act. Occupier, trade effluent, sewage effluent, outlet, the board itself and the consent that authorises a discharge became the standard terms of Indian environmental law, taken up by the Air Act, which defines its regulated activity against the boards this Act creates, and by the rules under the umbrella statute, which assign the boards their authorisation work in the same language. A reader who masters the definitions in Section 2 of this Act holds the key to the drafting of the whole cluster, which is one reason this statute, rather than any of its more famous successors, is the right first text in the field. The institutional priority matches the verbal one: the Central Board and the State Boards predate the environment ministry's own regulatory machinery in their field, and the umbrella Act of 1986, when it came, built its authorities in parallel rather than displacing them, leaving the boards in possession of the consent system and of the field staff through whom national policy is actually delivered.

## The boards: composition, terms and design

Chapter II creates the two tiers. Section 3 empowers the Central Government to constitute the Central Pollution Control Board, as the body came to be renamed by the 1988 amendment, which had earlier styled it the Central Board for the Prevention and Control of Water Pollution. The Central Board consists of a chairman who is a person having special knowledge or practical experience in dealing with matters relating to environmental protection or knowledge and experience in administering institutions dealing with such matters, nominated by the Central Government; up to five officials of the Central Government; up to five members nominated from among the members of the State Boards; up to three non-officials nominated to represent the interests of agriculture, fishery or industry, trade or any other interest; two persons to represent companies or corporations owned, controlled or managed by the Central Government; and a full-time member-secretary, appointed by the Central Government, possessing qualifications, knowledge and experience of scientific, engineering or management aspects of pollution control. Section 4 applies the same template one tier down. A State Board consists of a chairman with the same qualifications, nominated by the State Government; up to five officials of the State Government; up to five members nominated from among the members of local authorities functioning within the state; up to three non-officials representing agriculture, fishery, industry, trade or other interests; two persons representing companies or corporations owned, controlled or managed by the State Government; and a full-time member-secretary with the prescribed technical qualifications. Section 4(4) adds the territorial rule: the Central Board performs the functions of a State Board for the Union territories, although it may delegate those functions to a specified body or person, which is the statutory root of the Pollution Control Committees that administer the territories.

The design choices embedded in this composition explain a good deal of the boards' later behaviour. The statute creates an expert body in form but a government-nominated body in fact: every member sits by nomination of the Central or State Government, the chairman's qualifying test is expressed at a level of generality that accommodates career administrators as readily as scientists, and the member-secretary, the office that runs the institution day to day, is an appointee of the same government whose industries and municipalities the board must regulate. Representation is corporatist rather than democratic: agriculture, industry and local authorities are given seats as interests, which brings practical knowledge into the room and also brings the regulated into the regulator's council chamber, a bargain the 1974 Parliament struck deliberately in the belief that consent and cooperation would achieve more than confrontation. Section 5 governs terms and conditions of service, resignation and removal, and requires a member who is directly or indirectly concerned or interested in a matter under discussion to disclose the interest; Section 6 lists disqualifications, among them insolvency, unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court, conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude or for an offence under the Act, and holding an interest in a firm or company manufacturing pollution control equipment or holding a patent for such a device. Section 7 deals with vacancies and meetings, Section 8 with committees the boards may constitute, and Section 11 with the officers and employees the boards may appoint, subject in the State Board's case to the prior approval of the State Government for the strength of its establishment, a financial dependence that has mattered greatly in practice.

One structural fact radiates through everything the boards do. The same State Board that administers the Water Act administers the Air Act as well, because the Air Act was written to be run by the boards this Act creates: when a state consented to the Air Act, its existing board under the Water Act became its board under the Air Act too, a point developed in the [companion account of the Air Act 1981](/2014/09/01/air-act-1981-explained/). The economy is obvious. One laboratory network, one inspectorate, one consent file per factory covering water and air, one chairman and member-secretary speaking for pollution control generally. The cost is less obvious but real: a single institution's weaknesses, an unfilled vacancy, a captured chairman, a starved budget, degrade two statutes at once, and the water regime that this article describes shares its institutional fate with the air regime throughout the country.

### What is a Joint Board under the Water Act?

A Joint Board is a single board constituted for two or more states, or for a state and a Union territory, by agreement between the governments concerned under Chapter III of the Act. Sections 13 to 15 govern its constitution, the apportionment of its expenses between the participating governments, and the references each participating government may make to it.

The boards' internal working completes the design picture. A board may constitute committees of its members for the efficient performance of its functions, and the consent system in the larger states runs substantially through such committees and through delegated authority to the chairman and member-secretary, because a board meeting periodically cannot itself decide tens of thousands of applications; the Act also allows a board to associate with itself, for particular purposes, persons whose assistance or advice it needs, without making them members, a device for importing expertise the nominated composition lacks. Acts done by a board are not invalidated by vacancies in its membership or defects in a member's appointment, a standard protection that has the practical effect of insulating consent decisions from collateral attack on the board's constitution. Members and officers of the boards are deemed public servants, which places the Penal Code's protections and offences around the inspectorate in both directions: obstruction of their work aggravates, and their own misconduct in office is punishable. The board's seal, its power to contract, and its capacity to hold property are provided for in the usual statutory terms. What the Act does not provide is any security of tenure for the chairman or member-secretary beyond the prescribed terms, or any selection process independent of the nominating government, and the absence is structural rather than accidental: the drafters of 1974 designed a government agency with expert colouring, not an independent regulator in the later sense, and the boards have lived inside that choice ever since.

Chapter III is little used, because states have generally preferred boards of their own, but its presence in the statute records an expectation worth noticing: that river systems ignore state boundaries and that the sensible unit of regulation is the basin rather than the state. The Act, constrained by its Article 252 origin, could not impose basin authorities on unwilling states, so it offered joint boards by agreement and left the offer largely unaccepted. The Central Board, in which the states sit by nomination and which can coordinate their boards and resolve disputes between them, became the substitute for the basin-level institution the drafters could imagine but could not create.

## What the boards do: Sections 16, 17 and 18

Section 16 assigns the Central Board the function of promoting the cleanliness of streams and wells in different areas of the states, and then lists the content of that charge. The Central Board advises the Central Government on matters concerning prevention and control of water pollution, coordinates the activities of the State Boards and resolves disputes among them, provides technical assistance and guidance, carries out and sponsors investigation and research, plans and organises the training of persons engaged in pollution control, organises a comprehensive programme through mass media, collects, compiles and publishes technical and statistical data, lays down, modifies or annuls, in consultation with the government of the state concerned, the standards for a stream or well, and plans and executes a nationwide programme for prevention and control. Section 17 gives the State Board the parallel and more operational list: to plan a comprehensive programme for prevention, control and abatement of pollution of streams and wells in the state and secure its execution; to advise the State Government; to collect and disseminate information; to encourage, conduct and participate in investigations and research; to collaborate with the Central Board in training; to inspect sewage and trade effluent works and review plans and specifications for treatment and disposal systems in connection with the grant of consent; to lay down, modify or annul effluent standards for the discharge of sewage and trade effluents, and the classification of waters; to evolve economical and reliable methods of treatment, utilisation of sewage and trade effluents in agriculture, and disposal on land; to ensure that effluents are properly treated and that modified or new methods evolved by the board are adopted; to advise the State Government on the location of industries whose location is likely to pollute a stream or well; and to perform such other functions as may be prescribed or entrusted by the Central Board or the government.

The relationship between the two tiers is settled by Section 18 and the second sub-section of Section 17, and it is a relationship of supremacy dressed as coordination. Section 17(2) makes a State Board bound by such directions in writing as the Central Board may give to it and by directions of the State Government, with a proviso resolving conflicts: where a direction given by the State Government is inconsistent with a direction given by the Central Board, the matter is referred to the Central Government for its decision. Section 18 contains the Central Board's corresponding power to give directions to a State Board, which the State Board is bound to comply with. The State Board is therefore subject to two masters whose instructions the statute ranks, placing the Central Board above the State Government on any matter where the two conflict, subject to the Centre's decision on a reference. In a statute that exists by state consent, this is a striking clause, and it was accepted by the resolving states in 1974 as part of the price of a national regime. It has been used sparingly but consequentially, most visibly in the Central Board's directions requiring online continuous effluent monitoring in the most polluting categories of industry and common treatment facilities, directions which bind every State Board to enforce a monitoring architecture designed in Delhi.

The data function deserves a sentence of its own, because so much of the system leans on it. The Central Board operates a national water quality monitoring programme through a network of monitoring stations on rivers, lakes, ponds, tanks and groundwater sources across the country, whose periodic measurements against the classification criteria produce the long data series by which stretches are designated as polluted and priorities for action are set. When a river stretch is publicly identified as polluted, the identification ordinarily traces to this monitoring architecture and to the Central Board's published assessments of water quality, and the State Boards' own monitoring feeds the same series. The boards thus sit at both ends of the evidentiary system: their stations establish the condition of the water, and their laboratories establish the contribution of the individual discharger, and it is the junction of the two that a prosecution or a closure direction has to prove.

The functions lists also locate the boards' real centres of gravity. Neither board is primarily a prosecutor. The Central Board is a standard-setter, coordinator and knowledge institution; the State Board is a planning, advisory and consenting institution whose coercive powers sit inside a much larger body of technical and administrative functions. Parliament in 1974 imagined regulation as engineering first and law second: the centre of the scheme is the board that knows the river, classifies its waters by their best designated use, fixes the effluent standards a discharge into it must meet, and works through consent conditions and technical guidance. The penal chapters, on this design, were the backstop for a system that was expected to run on expertise and permission, which is one reason the decriminalisation debate of 2024 divided opinion so sharply: the two sides disagree about how much of the system's working order ever rested on the backstop.

## Standards: the numbers a discharge must meet

The prohibition and the consent system both run on standards, and the Act distributes the making of them with some care. The Central Board classifies waters by their designated best use, the scheme under which a stretch of river or a water body is assigned the most demanding use it is meant to serve, drinking water source, bathing, irrigation, industrial cooling, or controlled waste disposal, with water quality criteria fixed for each class, and this classification frames what protection a particular stream receives. The State Board, under Section 17(1)(g), lays down the effluent standards for the discharge of sewage and trade effluents into a particular stream, taking into account the minimum fair treatment for that stream and the tolerance limits of pollution permissible in its waters after the discharge of effluents, so the standard for a discharge is in principle a function of the receiving water and not a uniform national figure. In parallel, standards notified under the Environment (Protection) Rules, including the general standards for the discharge of environmental pollutants in Schedule VI of those Rules and the industry-specific standards notified for particular sectors, supply national floors that consent conditions must respect. The consent order for a given plant is where these layers meet: it converts the board's stream standards and the notified national standards into the biochemical oxygen demand, chemical oxygen demand, suspended solids, pH and sector-specific parameter limits that bind that discharge, and against which the board's samples are judged.

The layering has a logic and a cost. The logic is that water quality is local: a discharge that a large, fast river can assimilate may destroy a small stream, and a standard fixed in Delhi for every water body in India would be at once too lax for the vulnerable and too strict for the resilient, so the statute gives the standard to the body that knows the stream. The cost is variability and contest. Effluent standards differ between states and between streams within a state; an industry operating in several states answers to several regimes for the same process; and every standard is a judgment about assimilative capacity resting on data of uneven quality, which is why the Central Board's classification and data functions under Section 16 matter to the system's rationality. It is also why sampling evidence, gathered under Section 21 against the standards in a plant's consent, carries the prosecutions: the standard is not an abstraction in the courtroom but a number in a document the occupier accepted, and the analysis either exceeds it or does not.

Two distinctions organise the standards system and repay the reader's attention. The first is between the quality to be maintained in the receiving water itself, the in-stream or ambient standard that the classification expresses, and the quality required at the end of the pipe, the effluent standard that the consent enforces. The two are connected by the receiving water's capacity to assimilate the discharge, and the State Board's statutory task under Section 17(1)(g) is precisely to work from the first to the second for the stream in question. The second distinction is between stream-specific standards and the minimal national standards the Central Board evolved for particular industries, floors below which no consent in any state was to let a listed industry fall, whatever the local assimilative argument. The floors answer the competitive objection to the local system: without them, a state could bid for industry by tolerating dirtier water, and the consent competition between states would set the real national standard at the level of the most permissive board.

One further standard-setting practice belongs in this account because it has reshaped what consent conditions contain. For the sectors with the heaviest pollution loads, boards acting under directions of the Central Board and the tribunal's supervision have required common effluent treatment plants for clusters of small units that cannot treat individually, and zero liquid discharge conditions for particular sectors in particular catchments, under which the unit must recycle its treated effluent and discharge nothing to the stream at all. Zero discharge converts the Section 24 question from how much pollution is permitted to whether any outlet may exist, and it is imposed through the consent power rather than through any amendment of the prohibition, which illustrates the breadth the consent architecture gives the boards when they choose to use it.

## The prohibition: Section 24

Section 24 is the Act's single substantive prohibition, and everything else in the statute is machinery for giving it effect. No person shall knowingly cause or permit any poisonous, noxious or polluting matter determined in accordance with such standards as may be laid down by the State Board to enter, whether directly or indirectly, into any stream or well or sewer or on land. No person shall knowingly cause or permit any other matter to enter a stream if it tends to impede the proper flow of the water in a manner leading or likely to lead to a substantial aggravation of pollution due to other matters discharged into it. Three features of this text deserve emphasis. The verb structure, cause or permit, reaches both the discharger and the person who allows a discharge from premises under his control, so the occupier cannot shelter behind a contractor or a tenant. The first limb depends on standards: the matter must be poisonous, noxious or polluting as determined in accordance with standards laid down by the State Board, which ties the criminal wrong back to the board's technical work and makes the published standard part of the offence. The mental element is knowledge: the prosecution must show that the accused knowingly caused or permitted the entry, which, as the enforcement record shows, is where prosecutions under the section have most often been contested.

One operational consequence of the consent linkage is frequently litigated and should be understood. A consent is not a licence to pollute up to a general level; it is permission to discharge subject to the conditions in the consent order, and a discharge that exceeds its consented limits in concentration, volume or point of outlet is not sheltered by the existence of the consent. The excess stands, in the eyes of the enforcing board, as a discharge otherwise than in accordance with the Act's permission structure, exposing the occupier to directions under Section 33A, to the consequences the Act attaches to the contravention in question, and, in the unamended regime, to prosecution. Occupiers who treat the consent as a general charter and the conditions as aspirations misunderstand the document they hold: the conditions are the consent, and everything outside them is unconsented.

Section 19 gives the State Government, after consultation with or on the recommendation of the State Board, the power to declare any area a water pollution prevention and control area by notification in the Official Gazette, to alter its boundaries, and to merge one such area with another. Within a declared area, the consent provisions apply with adaptations the statute spells out, and the declaration power was designed to let states bring the regime down on particular catchments and industrial estates with full rigour while the general system matured. In the event, the boards' practice applied the consent system generally, and Section 19 has occupied a background position. Its significance for the reader is architectural: the Act's designers expected graduated application, strict where pollution pressure demanded it, and built the instrument for it into the text.

## The consent regime: Sections 25 to 28

The consent system is the Act's working heart, and Section 25 is drafted as a near-total prohibition subject to permission. Subject to the section's provisions, no person shall, without the previous consent of the State Board, establish or take any steps to establish any industry, operation or process, or any treatment and disposal system or extension or addition to it, which is likely to discharge sewage or trade effluent into a stream, well, sewer or on land. No person shall, without previous consent, bring into use any new or altered outlet for the discharge of sewage or trade effluent, or begin to make any new discharge of sewage or trade effluent into a stream, well, sewer or on land. The two limbs correspond to the two permissions that board practice calls consent to establish and consent to operate: the first governs the creation of the discharging capacity, the second governs the commencement and continuation of the actual discharge. An application is made in the prescribed form and with the prescribed fees, an inquiry is held, and the board may grant consent subject to conditions or refuse it for reasons recorded in writing. The conditions the board may impose go to the heart of plant design and operation: the point of discharge, the nature and composition, temperature, volume and rate of discharge, the construction and operation of treatment works, and the use of a specified outlet.

Section 25(5) answers the fait accompli problem. Where an industry or disposal system is set up without consent, the State Board may serve a notice imposing conditions of the same kind it could have imposed on a consent application, so the unauthorised establishment does not gain an advantage over the compliant one, although the establishment remains exposed to the penal provisions. Section 25(6) requires the board to maintain a register containing the particulars of the conditions imposed, open to inspection by any person interested in or affected by the conditions, an early statutory transparency device that gives downstream users and residents a documentary route to knowing what a discharge is permitted to contain. The fees that accompany an application are fixed by the state rules, and state schedules commonly grade them by the category of the industry and the scale of the investment or discharge, so the consent system carries its own tariff. The inquiry that follows the application is conducted by the board's officers, and the statute obliges the board to communicate its decision; a consent granted is entered with its conditions in the register Section 25(6) maintains. Renewal is a fresh exercise of the same discretion on the record the previous consent period produced, which is why a unit's compliance history in inspection and sampling is the real substance of a renewal file.

Consent administration has also been the site of repeated procedural reform from within the states. Several state governments and boards have introduced online application and processing systems that move the paper file into a portal, fix the stage each application has reached, and compute the four-month period of Section 25(7) automatically; some have adopted policies of automatic or deemed renewal for the lower pollution categories on the strength of the occupier's self-certification and compliance record, reserving full inquiry for the red category and for units with adverse inspection history. These reforms operate inside the statute's text, which requires previous consent, an inquiry and recorded reasons, and they show the consent regime's capacity to be tightened or loosened administratively without amendment; they also frame the setting into which the 2024 amendment's Section 27A guidelines will speak, since a central power to prescribe time-bound disposal and consent validity will discipline state practices that have diverged widely under an identical section.

Section 26 extends the net to discharges existing when the Act came into force in an area: the person discharging sewage or trade effluent immediately before commencement must apply for consent, and the board processes the application as if under Section 25, with the protection that the conditions imposed must not, for a limited transitional period, operate so as to require what the provision shields. Section 27 gives the board the continuing power that makes consent a living control rather than a one-time licence: the board may review a consent it has granted, impose new or modified conditions, or refuse an application outright, and may withdraw or vary consent, but not without giving the person concerned a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and a refusal of consent or of renewal must likewise follow a hearing. The hearing requirement is written into the section itself, which matters when the boards' orders are challenged, because a consent withdrawn without the opportunity the section promises is vulnerable on the statute's own terms.

The inquiry that precedes a grant is the board's opportunity to shape the plant rather than merely license it. Because consent under Section 25(1)(a) is required not only for a new industry but for steps to establish one and for any extension or addition to a treatment and disposal system, expansion and process change re-enter the consent net, and the board may condition its grant on additional treatment capacity, a relocated discharge point, a metering installation or a monitoring regime. Conditions imposed must be recorded with the grant, refusal must carry reasons in writing, and Section 25(4) requires that the consent be subject to the conditions the board specifies on the matters the section enumerates, which include the point of discharge and the nature, composition, temperature, volume and rate of the effluent. A consent is specific to the premises, the process and the occupier to whom it is granted; board practice requires intimation and fresh processing where ownership or the legal identity of the unit changes, so a purchaser of a running plant takes the consent file as it stands and must put it into its own name rather than treating the permission as a transferable asset of the sale.

Consent generates paper in industrial quantities: applications with site plans and process descriptions, water balance and effluent calculations, inspection reports, sample results, returns, and the consent orders themselves, renewed and reissued across a plant's life. A compliance office that keeps these records in order, and can retrieve the operative conditions of a consent granted years earlier, holds the practical difference between a defensible file and an exposure to closure, and teams that work from scanned office documents use tools such as [the ReportMedic office file viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) to open and read those records in a browser when the originals arrive in mixed formats. The statute's designers understood the documentary character of the regime they were building; the register under Section 25(6), the recorded reasons for refusal, and the written conditions are all devices to make the board's exercise of discretion inspectable, and therefore challengeable, and therefore disciplined.

### How long does a Water Act consent remain valid?

A consent is granted for the period specified in the consent order itself, and Section 25 directs the State Board to indicate that period when it grants consent subject to conditions. In practice the boards fix the period by the category of the industry, longer for less polluting categories, and the consent must be renewed before the specified period expires.

### What happens if the State Board does not decide a consent application in time?

Section 25(7) answers with a deeming provision: if consent is neither given nor refused within four months of a complete application, the consent sought is deemed given unconditionally when that period expires. It disciplines silence by converting it into permission, and places the burden of proving the application complete, and its date, on the applicant.

From a refusal, variation or condition the Act provides an appeal. Section 28 gives any person aggrieved by an order made by the State Board under Section 25, Section 26 or Section 27 thirty days to appeal to an appellate authority constituted by the State Government, consisting of one person or three persons as the government thinks fit, appointed from among persons who are or have been judges or who in the government's opinion possess adequate knowledge and experience in the matters the authority will handle. The authority may condone delay for sufficient cause, must give the appellant and the board an opportunity of being heard, and must dispose of the appeal as expeditiously as possible, with power to annul or substitute conditions it finds unreasonable. Section 57 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts over matters the appellate authority is empowered to determine. The design is characteristic of 1974: a specialist executive appeal, speedily disposed of, insulated from the civil courts. The appellate map was redrawn a generation later, when the National Green Tribunal Act created a tribunal with appellate jurisdiction over orders and directions under this Act, a development traced in the [account of the National Green Tribunal Act 2010](/2014/11/15/national-green-tribunal-act-2010/), and the coexistence of the Section 28 authority and the tribunal is one of the untidier corners of the system, in which the forum for challenging a consent order depends on the character of the order and the route the challenger takes.

The appellate untidiness is worth a closer look because litigants must navigate it. The Section 28 authority is an executive appellate body of the State Government's creation, near at hand, quick in principle and confined to orders under Sections 25, 26 and 27. The Tribunal's appellate jurisdiction under its own Act reaches orders and directions under the Water Act more broadly, including the Section 33A directions that matter most in practice, and carries its own limitation period and its own power to award relief and compensation. A direction of closure is therefore challenged before the Tribunal; a refused consent may be taken either to the Section 28 authority or, on jurisdictional questions, into writ proceedings; and the 2024 amendment adds the Section 45C appeal for penalties, also to the Tribunal but on a sixty-day clock and against a deposit. The boards themselves must defend on all these fronts simultaneously, which is one of the unglamorous reasons direction practice is cautious: every coercive order is drafted for the appellate audience that will read it within weeks.

## Consent in practice: categorisation, monitoring and the inspection cycle

The consent system the sections describe acquired, over five decades of board practice, an administrative superstructure that a practitioner must know alongside the statute. The Central Board's categorisation of industries, substantially revised in 2016 on a pollution index built from the effluents, emissions, hazardous waste and resource consumption of each sector, sorts industries into red, orange, green and white categories in descending order of pollution potential. The category drives the consent process in the states: red category industries face the closest scrutiny, the shortest consent periods and the most demanding conditions; white category industries, assessed as practically non-polluting, are in the generality of state practice not required to obtain consent at all, and need only intimate the board, which is the administrative forerunner of the exemption power the 2024 amendment wrote into the proviso to Section 25(1). State governments and boards fix the validity periods for each category in their consent policies, running from a year or two for the most polluting categories in stricter states to long periods for green units, and renewal applications must precede expiry, with the deemed consent provision of Section 25(7) standing behind an applicant whose complete application the board leaves undecided for four months.

Between grant and renewal, the regime runs on inspection and self-reporting in proportions that vary with a board's staffing. Consent conditions typically require the occupier to maintain treatment works in good order, to keep records of water consumption and effluent quality, to file returns and environmental statements, and to admit the board's officers for inspection and sampling under Sections 21 and 23. For the seventeen categories of highly polluting industry identified in the Central Board's programme, together with common treatment facilities and large sewage plants, the Central Board's directions under its statutory direction powers required the installation of online continuous effluent monitoring systems transmitting quality data to the board's servers without the mediation of an inspector's visit, a change that altered the evidentiary basis of enforcement for the largest dischargers: exceedances appear in the regulator's data as they occur, and the argument shifts from whether the board sampled correctly on a given day to why the plant's own instruments recorded a breach. The monitoring direction was issued under the direction powers in this Act and the umbrella statute together, and State Boards enforce it through consent conditions and Section 33A directions against units that fail to install, calibrate or transmit.

Consent under this Act does not stand alone in a plant's permission file, and its interaction with the neighbouring regimes is part of working knowledge. A new industrial project commonly needs an environmental clearance under the impact assessment notification issued under the Environment (Protection) Act before construction, and the State Board's consent to establish is sought in the same preparatory phase, the two processes examining overlapping material through different authorities. Authorisations for hazardous waste and other waste streams under the rules framed under the umbrella statute are administered in most states by the same State Board that grants the Water Act consent, so a single regulator holds the plant's water consent, air consent and waste authorisations, and boards have used that conjunction in consent conditions, making membership of a common effluent treatment plant, or connection to a common hazardous waste facility, a condition of operating in an industrial estate. Several states have wrapped these applications into single-window clearance systems by executive arrangement, but the statutory decisions remain distinct, and a clearance under one instrument does not cure the absence of another, a distinction that enforcement practice insists on whenever a unit argues that its project clearance entitles it to discharge.

The inspection cycle exposes the system's dependence on craft. A consent file is only as reliable as the inspection that verifies it, and boards with thin engineering staffs ration inspections by category and complaint, so the red category and the complained-against unit receive attention while the compliant middle is governed largely by its own returns. Occupiers experience the system at this level, in the gap between the conditions on paper and the verification on the ground, and every evaluation of the Act returns to it: the consent regime is among the most completely specified in Indian regulatory law, and its failures are seldom failures of specification.

## Information, sampling and entry: Sections 20 to 23

A regulator that cannot find out what is being discharged cannot regulate, and Chapter V arms the boards accordingly. Section 20 empowers a board, or any officer empowered by it, to obtain information: to require the person in charge of premises where an industry, operation or process is carried on to furnish information about the abstraction of water, the composition of effluents, and the means of disposal, and to call for returns. Sub-sections (2) and (3) go further and let the State Board give directions of the same informational and restraining kind to persons abstracting water from a stream or well in substantial quantities and to persons constructing or operating works for the disposal of sewage or trade effluent or for digging wells, and the failure to comply with a direction under Section 20(2) or 20(3) is separately penalised by Section 41(1). Section 23 gives the board's empowered officers the right of entry and inspection: to enter any place at all reasonable times, with such assistance as the officer considers necessary, to examine and test equipment, industrial plants, records, registers and documents, to inspect premises where he has reason to believe an offence is being committed, and to seize material evidence. Wilfully obstructing such an officer, failing to produce documents or information, or giving false information is penalised by Section 42, which also penalises the person who, for the purpose of obtaining consent, knowingly or wilfully gives false information or withholds a material particular.

The most carefully engineered of these provisions is Section 21, the sampling power, because its product is evidence. A board or an officer empowered by it may take, for the purpose of analysis, samples of water from any stream or well, or samples of any sewage or trade effluent passing from any plant or vessel or from or over any place into any stream, well, sewer or land. The result of an analysis is not admissible in legal proceedings unless the section's procedure is complied with. The officer must serve on the occupier or his agent a notice of his intention to have the sample analysed; divide the sample into two parts, in the presence of the occupier or agent; cause each part to be placed in a container marked, sealed and signed by both the officer and the occupier; and send one container without delay to the laboratory established or specified under the Act, the occupier being entitled to the analysis of the other part. Where the occupier wilfully absents himself, the officer takes the sample, divides and seals it alone and informs the analyst of the absence. Section 22 then provides for the report of the Government Analyst or Board Analyst, whose certified report may be used as evidence of the facts stated in it in any proceeding under the Act. The design is a chain of custody written into a statute: the prosecution's science is only as good as the board's compliance with its own procedure, and a generation of contested prosecutions under the Act turned on seal, signature, notice and dispatch. That is not pedantry. A sampling regime that the accused cannot test invites fabrication and convicts on assertion; Section 21 is drafted on the assumption that the board's evidence will be challenged and builds the answer into the taking of the sample itself.

The analysis end of the chain is institutionalised with the same care. The Act provides for a Central water laboratory and State water laboratories, whose functions include the analysis of samples sent to them under the Act, and for two classes of analyst: Board analysts, appointed by a board with the approval of the State Government to analyse samples sent to the board's own laboratory, and Government analysts, appointed by the government to analyse samples sent to a government laboratory. The report of either, signed and certified in the manner Section 22 provides, may be used as evidence of the facts stated in it, which relieves the prosecution of proving the chemistry afresh through oral evidence unless the report is displaced. The occupier's sealed second container is the counterweight: the defence may have its part analysed and set the results against the board's, and where the two diverge materially the benefit travels to the accused. Boards' litigation losses under the Act cluster at exactly these joints, samples taken without notice where notice was possible, containers sealed without the occupier's signature where the occupier was present, reports procured from laboratories not established or specified under the Act, and dispatch delayed beyond what the section tolerates, which is why experienced boards treat Section 21 as a drill to be rehearsed rather than a formality to be recited.

## Emergencies and restraint: Sections 31, 32 and 33

Section 31 imposes the duty of candour in a crisis: where an accident or other unforeseen act or event discharging, or likely to discharge, poisonous, noxious or polluting matter into a stream or well or onto land occurs at any place, the person in charge of that place must forthwith intimate the occurrence to the board and to such other authorities or agencies as may be prescribed. Section 42(1) makes failure to intimate an offence, which closes the obvious incentive to say nothing and hope the contamination disperses. Section 32 then gives the board its emergency powers. Where it appears to the board that poisonous, noxious or polluting matter is present in a stream or well, or on land, by reason of such an accident or event, the board may remove or cause the matter to be removed and properly disposed of, remedy or mitigate the pollution caused, and issue orders prohibiting or restraining the person responsible and any other person from discharging or continuing to discharge such matter. The emergency power is remedial first and punitive not at all: the board acts as a fire brigade for water, removing and mitigating, and its costs and authority run ahead of any question of fault.

Section 33 addresses apprehended rather than completed pollution. Where it is apprehended by a board that the water in any stream or well is likely to be polluted by reason of the disposal, or likely disposal, of matter, or by the use, or likely use, of a stream for the disposal of sewage or trade effluent without compliance with the Act, the board may apply to a court, not inferior to that of a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class, for restraining the person from causing such pollution or doing the apprehended act, and the court may make such order as it deems fit. The provision matters less for its frequency of use than for what it reveals about the original design: in 1974, the route to coercive restraint of a polluter ran through a magistrate's court on the board's application, a judicial gate interposed between the regulator and the factory. The board could sample, report, prosecute and apply, but it could not itself order a unit closed. That limitation lasted fourteen years, and its removal is the single largest change the 1988 amendment made to the Act's working character.

## Section 33A: the direction power

The Amendment Act of 1988, Act 53 of 1988, effective from 29 September 1988 in its substituted provisions, recast Section 25 in its present form, tightened the definition of occupier, wrote the citizen complaint route into Section 49, and inserted Section 33A. Section 33A provides that, notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, but subject to the provisions of the Act and to any directions the Central Government may give, a board may, in the exercise of its powers and performance of its functions under the Act, issue any directions in writing to any person, officer or authority, and such person, officer or authority shall be bound to comply with those directions. The Explanation states, for the removal of doubts, that the power to issue directions includes the power to direct the closure, prohibition or regulation of any industry, operation or process, and the stoppage or regulation of the supply of electricity, water or any other service. It is difficult to overstate what this clause did. The board ceased to be a suitor before magistrates and became a commander within its field: it could order a polluting unit closed, order an electricity board to disconnect its supply, order a municipal body to act, and direct any person whose conduct contributed to a discharge, and disobedience of a Section 33A direction was made one of the Act's gravest offences by Section 41(2).

The power's breadth made its procedural discipline the central legal question of the amended regime. A closure direction takes away a livelihood and strands capital, workers and creditors, and the courts, in a consistent line of rulings on directions under this Act and its air counterpart, have required that drastic directions rest on material disclosing the pollution complained of and that the person directed be given an opportunity to show cause, except where a genuine emergency of continuing discharge requires immediate action, in which case a prompt post-decisional hearing has been accepted. Boards in practice proceed by show-cause notice, inspection report, personal hearing and reasoned direction, and the direction, not the prosecution, became the system's main instrument: most significant enforcement episodes under the Act, the sealing of dyeing units in a river town, the disconnection of power to a cluster of electroplating shops, the direction to a municipal corporation to stop an untreated outfall, proceed under Section 33A. Prosecutions, by contrast, remained slow, technically vulnerable and rare. Any account of the Act's deterrent structure must hold both facts together: the criminal provisions were the formal summit of the enforcement pyramid, but the pyramid's actual working surface was administrative direction backed by the threat that disobedience of the direction, rather than the discharge itself, carried the heaviest sentence in the Act.

Two further instruments grew up on the direction power in practice and belong to any complete account of how the boards coerce. The first is the bank guarantee. A board issuing a direction that allows a unit time to achieve compliance, install treatment or complete a common facility connection commonly requires the occupier to furnish a bank guarantee, forfeitable on default, so that the direction carries an immediate financial hostage rather than depending on a later prosecution for its breach. The guarantee is not named in Section 33A; it is an administrative device developed within the direction practice and sustained as incidental to the power to regulate the industry concerned. The second is environmental compensation. Applying the polluter pays principle that the Supreme Court declared part of the law of the land, the Central Board developed a methodology for computing compensation for environmental damage from defaulting discharges, and the National Green Tribunal and the boards have applied compensation demands to units and local bodies in continuing default, quantified by the scale and duration of the violation. Compensation converts a discharge into a running monetary liability without a criminal trial, and in the years before the 2024 amendment it was already performing, administratively and imperfectly, much of the function that the amendment's penalties are designed to perform by statute: pricing the violation. The defenders of the amendment can point to this practice as proof that fiscal enforcement is the system's real language; its critics answer that compensation, being discretionary and formula-driven rather than adjudicated, shows exactly why penalty power needs the procedural discipline of an adjudicating officer and a tribunal appeal, which the new Sections 45B and 45C supply.

The direction power also runs against the state itself, and this is among its most consequential uses. A Section 33A direction may be issued to any person, officer or authority, and boards have directed municipal corporations and local authorities to stop untreated outfalls, to complete sewage treatment capacity and to divert drains, and have directed electricity utilities to disconnect the supply of units ordered closed, making the utility the instrument of the board's closure. In the Union territories, where the Central Board performs the State Board's functions, it wields the direction power directly. The direction to a government body places the regulator in the posture of commanding the administration that appoints it, and the record of compliance by municipalities is among the weaker parts of the system's history, not because the power is doubted but because the directed body pleads the same incapacity, of funds and time, that the courts have refused to accept from it when its duties are enforced against it. Directions against industry can be executed by disconnection; directions against a municipality can be executed only by persistence, litigation and the slow pressure of the tribunal's supervision, and the difference shows in the rivers below India's towns.

## The citizen route: Section 49

Section 49 controls access to the criminal courts. No court shall take cognizance of any offence under the Act except on a complaint made by a board or an officer authorised in that behalf by it, or by any person who has given notice of not less than sixty days, in the prescribed manner, of the alleged offence and of his intention to make a complaint, to the board or its authorised officer. No court inferior to a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class may try an offence under the Act. Where a complaint is made by a person other than the board, the board must, on demand by that person, make available the relevant reports in its possession, although it may refuse to do so where in its opinion the public interest requires refusal. The sixty-day notice is not a courtesy: it is a jurisdictional condition of the citizen's complaint, designed to give the board the first chance to act on the material and to filter grievances through the regulator before they become prosecutions. The 1988 amendment, which placed this citizen route in the section, converted the Act from a closed enforcement system into one with a public backstop, and it is the provision through which residents' associations and environmental groups have most often forced a reluctant board's hand, since the notice compels the board either to proceed itself or to hand over its reports to a complainant who will.

In practice the notice does strategic work beyond its formal function. A residents' group that serves a Section 49 notice creates a dated record that fixes the board with knowledge of the discharge, starts a clock the board ignores at the price of surrendering its own reports, and arms the group for the alternative route of a writ petition under Article 226 complaining of the board's failure to perform its statutory functions, in which the notice and the board's response, or silence, become the central exhibits. Boards have responded to notices with inspection and direction often enough that the notice is valued by practitioners as a lever rather than as a mere preliminary to prosecution, and where the board does prosecute, the citizen's material, gathered over the notice period, frequently forms part of the evidentiary background even though the complaint is the board's own.

The position set out here is the general scheme of the Act as enacted and amended, offered as legal information and not as advice on any individual dispute; a resident confronting a live discharge, or an industry facing a direction, should take advice on the facts, the state rules in force and the notice and limitation requirements that apply to the particular route before acting on them. Within those limits, the section's practical message is plain. The citizen cannot prosecute on the spot, the board cannot be bypassed without sixty days' notice, and the board's own sampling reports, the strongest evidence in the system, must be surrendered to the complainant on demand unless the public interest is genuinely engaged. Enforcement under the Water Act was thus designed as a relay: information flows to the board, the board acts or discloses, and the criminal court enters last, at the end of a process the statute deliberately slows down.

## The penalty architecture as enacted and amended to 1988

Chapter VII, as it stood after the 1988 amendments and before the amendments of 2024, built a graded pyramid whose grading principle was contumacy rather than harm. At its base, Section 41(1) punished failure to comply with a direction under Section 20(2) or 20(3) with imprisonment up to three months, or fine up to ten thousand rupees, or both, with an additional fine of up to five thousand rupees for every day the failure continued after conviction. Section 42 covered the obstruction and integrity offences: wilfully delaying or obstructing a person authorised by the board in the exercise of its powers, failing to produce documents or return information required under Section 20, failing to intimate the Section 31 accident, knowingly or wilfully giving false information to obtain consent or for any purpose under the Act, and destroying or defacing notices and other board property, all punishable with imprisonment up to three months or fine up to ten thousand rupees, while tampering with a meter, gauge or monitoring device installed by the board carried up to three months or a fine of one thousand rupees under Section 42(2). The mid-tier of the pyramid was Section 41(2): failure to comply with an order under Section 32(1)(c), a direction of a court under Section 33(2), or a direction under Section 33A carried imprisonment for a term not less than one year and six months and extending to six years, together with a fine, and five thousand rupees for every further day of failure. Section 41(3) raised the band to between two and seven years where the failure under sub-section (2) continued beyond a year after the date of conviction.

At the pyramid's summit stood the substantive pollution offences. Section 43 punished whoever contravenes Section 24, the prohibition itself, with imprisonment for a term not less than one year and six months but extending to six years, and with fine. Section 44 applied the same band to contravention of Section 25 or Section 26, discharging without consent or in breach of the consent regime's threshold. Section 45 provided an enhanced penalty for a second or subsequent conviction under Sections 43 or 44, imprisonment of not less than two years extending to seven years, with fine. Section 45A caught everything else: a person who contravenes any provision, or fails to comply with any order or direction, for which no penalty is separately provided was liable to imprisonment up to three months, or fine up to ten thousand rupees, or both, with five thousand rupees a day for continuing contravention. Section 47 dealt with offences by companies, deeming the company and every person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of its business guilty unless that person proved the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it, and extending liability to directors, managers and officers whose consent, connivance or neglect was shown. Section 48 applied the same logic to government departments, deeming the head of the department guilty subject to the same defences. Three features of this architecture explain much of its record. The minimum sentences were mandatory and high, which made magistrates cautious and appellate courts attentive to every procedural flaw in sampling and complaint. The fines, fixed in nominal rupees in 1974 and 1988, decayed with inflation into irrelevance, so the Act threatened the extraordinary and delivered, on conviction, often very little. And the grading punished defiance of the board more reliably than pollution of the river, so the system's criminal energy concentrated on disobedience, while the underlying discharge, the act the statute exists to prevent, was the hardest offence in the book to prove to a criminal standard.

The working life of these provisions in the magistrates' courts explains their reputation. A prosecution began with a board complaint, depended on a Section 21 sample whose taking had to be proved step by step, commonly faced an accused company entitled to test whether the complaint named the right legal person and the responsible officers, and proceeded at the pace of an overburdened criminal docket towards a sentence in which the fine element had been fixed in 1974 and 1988 rupees and the imprisonment element was hedged by mandatory minima that made courts careful. Boards, rationally, preferred the direction route, where Section 33A gave them the outcome they wanted, closure or treatment, in weeks rather than years, and reserved prosecution for defiance and for the record. The criminal chapters thus functioned in practice as the enforcement pyramid's apex in the textbook sense: rarely reached, significant chiefly for the shadow they cast over the administrative measures beneath, a shadow whose depth the 2024 amendment's critics and defenders assess so differently.

## The courts and the boards

The Supreme Court's encounters with the Act have mostly arisen not from its prosecutions but from litigation under Articles 21 and 32 and 226 in which the boards' statutory duties were the ground of the argument. In the Kanpur tanneries litigation in M.C. Mehta v Union of India in 1987 and 1988, the Court ordered tanneries discharging effluents into the Ganga to install at least primary treatment plants or close, treating the board's consent framework as the floor of legality and the right to wholesome water as part of the right to life, and the continuing mandamus in that line of cases made industrial discharge onto the Ganga a subject of judicial supervision for years. In Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v Union of India in 1996, arising from tanneries polluting the Palar river in Tamil Nadu, the Court held that the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle are part of the law of the land, principles which then informed how the boards' standards and directions under statutes including this one were to be read. In A.P. Pollution Control Board v Prof. M.V. Nayudu in 1999, the Court held that scientific uncertainty is not a reason to postpone measures to protect a drinking water source and that the burden lies on the person proposing the potentially harmful activity to show its environmental benignity, a ruling that strengthened the hand of a board refusing consent on precautionary grounds near protected reservoirs. In Paryavaran Suraksha Samiti v Union of India in 2017, the Court directed that industries shall not be permitted to run without functional primary effluent treatment plants, that State Boards must issue directions under Section 33A to defaulting units, and that common effluent treatment plants be completed on a stated timetable, a judgment that effectively instructed the boards to use the direction power this Act gives them at a scale litigation had long demanded.

Two earlier decisions supply the premises on which that line of authority stands. In Municipal Council, Ratlam v Vardhichand in 1980, the Supreme Court upheld proceedings against a municipality whose failure to control the flow of untreated waste into public streets and water had created an intolerable nuisance, holding that a local body could not plead financial inability to escape a statutory duty owed to public health, and the case remains the standing answer to the municipal sewage problem the Act inherited: the duty binds, and poverty is not a defence to it. In Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar in 1991, the Court recognised the right to pollution-free water as part of the right to life under Article 21 and entertained a public interest petition directed at the enforcement of the water and air statutes against industrial discharge into a river, establishing the channel through which citizens, when the boards would not move, could put the boards' own statutory duties before the constitutional courts. The Tribunal created in 2010 works downstream of these authorities: its proceedings on the Ganga and the Yamuna repeatedly measured state and board performance against the consent and direction framework of this Act, treating unconsented discharge and unused Section 33A powers as the index of administrative default.

The other face of the judicial record is procedural, and it cuts against the boards. In U.P. Pollution Control Board v Modi Distillery in 1987, a prosecution foundered because the complaint named a unit rather than the incorporated company responsible, and the Supreme Court's treatment of Section 47 confirmed that offences by companies require the company and the responsible persons to be proceeded against with precision. Across the High Courts, convictions and directions have been lost on Section 21 sampling defects, on complaints filed without authority, on closure directions issued without the hearing their gravity required, and on consent conditions framed too vaguely to enforce. The pattern is instructive rather than accidental. Parliament gave the boards scientific powers and procedural law together, and the courts have held them to the procedure as strictly as they have endorsed the science. A board that samples exactly as Section 21 prescribes, hears before it closes, and records reasons when it refuses is formidable in court; a board that does none of these things reliably loses cases it should win, and the enforcement statistics of the Act, such as they are, reflect institutional craft more than statutory force.

## The cess and the boards' money

The boards were never funded by the Act alone. The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act of 1977 levied a cess on water consumed by specified industries and by local authorities, calculated on the quantity of water consumed for the processes and purposes in its schedule, with a rebate of twenty-five per cent of the cess payable for an assessee that installed a plant for the treatment of sewage or trade effluent and complied with the consent conditions and effluent standards. The cess was credited to the Consolidated Fund and its proceeds were routed to the Central and State Boards, which drew on it for a substantial part of their working finance for four decades; it was, in design, the polluter-pays principle expressed as a water bill, and the rebate made it also an incentive instrument, charging for consumption and rewarding treatment. The cess ended with the goods and services tax settlement. The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2017, Act 18 of 2017, repealed the Cess Act by its Section 18 with effect from 1 July 2017, subsuming the levy in the new indirect tax architecture, and the boards' finances thereafter rested on consent fees, grants from their governments and other receipts. The repeal is generally reported as a tax measure, but for this statute it was an institutional event: a dedicated, industry-linked revenue stream that had underwritten the boards' laboratories and staff since 1977 disappeared, and consent fees, which the boards themselves fix for the applications they decide, acquired a fiscal significance alongside their regulatory one, a conjunction that critics of consent fee dependence have not been slow to notice.

Chapter VI of the Act, which deals with the boards' funds, accounts and audit, shows the modest scale on which the institution was expected to run. The Central Government and the State Governments make the contributions the chapter provides for; each board maintains its own fund into which its receipts, including in the cess years its share of the cess, are credited; budgets are prepared in the prescribed form; accounts are audited in consultation with the Comptroller and Auditor General; and an annual report of the board's proceedings is furnished to the government and laid before the legislature. The design assumed a technical agency of a few hundred staff per state at most, financed substantially by the levy on those it regulated. The consent economy that grew instead, in which a large industrial state's board processes a volume of consent applications and renewals far beyond anything the chapter's drafters envisaged, rests on the same chapter's framework, and the strain the volume places on it is part of the capacity story told later in this account.

## The Act in the states: rule-making and the federal texture

Below the statute sits a large body of subordinate legislation that varies by state, and it is in that layer, not in the Act, that much of the practitioner's law lives. Section 63 empowers the Central Government to make rules on the matters the Act assigns to the centre, including the Central Board's composition procedure, its budget and accounts, and the manner of the Central Water Laboratory's working. Section 64 empowers each State Government to make rules for its state on the matters assigned to it, including the State Board's procedures, the fees for consent applications and appeals, the manner of the Section 49 citizen notice, the authorities to whom a Section 31 accident must be intimated, and the forms and fees of the consent system, with the discipline that state rules are made after consultation with the State Board and must not be inconsistent with the Act or the central rules. Two states' consent files can therefore differ in fee, form, validity period and procedural detail under an identical parent Act, and any statement about how the Water Act works in practice that does not specify the state is incomplete in the way a statement about a purely central regime would not be.

Administration in the Union territories shows the same delegation logic at work. Section 4(4) makes the Central Board the State Board for the territories, and the Central Board in practice delegates its functions there to Pollution Control Committees constituted for the individual territories, which conduct the consent and enforcement work on the ground while remaining creatures of the Central Board's delegation rather than independent statutory boards. Delhi's committee, which administers the capital's consents and directions, is the most visible product of this arrangement. The territories were within the Act from its commencement, requiring no resolution, because Parliament's competence there is direct; and it is by the same competence that the 2024 amendment applies in every Union territory without adoption, so the amended regime's footprint includes two states by consent and the territories by direct power.

The same texture explains the Act's relationship with groundwater, which puzzles many readers. Subterranean waters are within the definition of a stream, so the discharge of polluting matter into groundwater, and its abstraction in the circumstances Section 20 addresses, are inside this Act. The licensing of groundwater extraction itself, the no-objection regime administered by the Central Ground Water Authority, does not rest on the Water Act: it was constructed under the Environment (Protection) Act on the directions of the Supreme Court in the M.C. Mehta groundwater litigation of the 1990s, and it operates through the umbrella statute's authority machinery rather than through the boards' consent system. The division is a fair specimen of how Indian environmental law actually accumulates: a subject inside the 1974 Act's definitions is regulated, a generation later, under a different statute's powers, because the later instrument offered a route that did not depend on state resolutions.

## The amendment of 2024

The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Amendment Act, 2024, Act 5 of 2024, received the President's assent on 15 February 2024. Its route to the statute book explains its shape. The decriminalisation programme that the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023 carried through forty-two central Acts, amending the Air Act and the Environment (Protection) Act among them, did not amend the Water Act at all, although the consultation process that preceded the Jan Vishwas legislation had proposed Water Act amendments: a statute resting on Article 252 could not be recast by omnibus amendment in the same way, and the Water Act was left to be dealt with on its own terms. It was so dealt with. The assemblies of Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan passed resolutions under Article 252(1) read with Article 252(2) asking Parliament to amend the Act, Parliament enacted the amendment for those states and the Union territories, and its applicability clause holds it open for adoption by any other state by resolution, so that the amendment extends its territory by exactly the mechanism that built the parent Act. The [decriminalisation of the Water Act therefore arrived a year behind the matching changes to the Environment (Protection) Act 1986](/2014/09/15/environment-protection-act-1986-guide/), through a separate Act, and with a territorial reach that the other decriminalised statutes do not share.

What the amendment does, in the states where it applies, can be grouped under three heads. The first is the penalty recast. Sections 41, 41A, 42, 43, 44 and 45A are substituted or amended so that the defaults they cover attract a penalty of not less than ten thousand rupees and extending to fifteen lakh rupees, with a further penalty of ten thousand rupees for every day the contravention continues: non-compliance with Section 20 directions under the new Section 41; non-compliance with Section 32(1)(c) orders, Section 33(2) court directions and Section 33A directions under the new Section 41A; the obstruction and false information offences in an amended Section 42; contravention of the Section 24 prohibition under the new Section 43; and residuary contraventions under the new Section 45A. The substituted Section 44 deals with the one interference offence the amendment separately retains in penal form, knowingly or wilfully altering or interfering with a meter, gauge or monitoring device installed or required under the consent provisions. Section 45, the enhanced penalty provision, is omitted. The imprisonment that disappears is the entire mid-structure of the old pyramid: a Section 24 discharge, the gravest substantive wrong in the Act, ceases to be an offence at all in an adopting state and becomes a penalty matter.

The second head is what survives, and it is narrower than most summaries suggest. The new Section 45E keeps failure to comply with Sections 25 and 26 a criminal offence: whoever fails to comply with the consent provisions is punishable with imprisonment for a term not less than one year and six months and extending to six years, and with fine, with an additional fine of up to fifty thousand rupees for every day the failure continues after conviction, and with imprisonment of two to seven years where the failure continues beyond one year after conviction. The amendment thus draws a line that the old Act did not draw at the same place: polluting a river is penalised, but operating without the board's consent remains a crime. Section 45E also creates the enforcement offence for the new system, punishing failure to pay a penalty within ninety days with imprisonment up to three years, or fine up to twice the penalty, or both, and it carries the offences-by-companies liability into its own text, which is why Section 47 could be omitted. Section 49 is amended to add the adjudicating officer to those who may file the complaints through which the courts take cognizance.

The third head is machinery, and it reaches beyond penalties into the boards' government. A new Section 45B empowers the Central Government to appoint adjudicating officers, not below the rank of Joint Secretary to the Government of India or Secretary to a State Government, to hold inquiries in the prescribed manner, with powers to summon and enforce attendance and to require documents, and to impose the new penalties after giving the person concerned an opportunity of being heard. A new Section 45C routes appeals from the adjudicating officer to the National Green Tribunal, within sixty days, and the appeal is entertained only if the appellant deposits ten per cent of the penalty imposed. A new Section 45D credits all penalties realised to the Environmental Protection Fund established under Section 16 of the Environment (Protection) Act, so the money leaves the polluting operation's accounts for an environmental fund rather than for general revenue. A new Section 48 fixes government departments with a penalty equal to one month's basic salary of the head of the department for contraventions by the department, subject to the defences of absence of knowledge and due diligence. Two provisions alter the boards' autonomy more quietly. Section 4(2)(a) is amended so that the chairman of a State Board is nominated by the State Government in such manner as may be prescribed by the Central Government, and Section 5(9) so that the terms and conditions of service of members are prescribed by the Central Government: the manner of choosing the head of a state institution created by a state-consent statute is moved, in adopting states, into central rule-making. A new Section 27A empowers the Central Government, in consultation with the Central Board, to issue guidelines on the grant, refusal or cancellation of consent, covering among other things time-bound disposal of applications and the validity of consent, and obliges every State Board to act in accordance with them. And the proviso to Section 25(1) is substituted so that the Central Government may, in consultation with the Central Board, exempt categories of industrial plants from the requirement of prior consent, a power aimed at categories regarded as non-polluting, which state rules and the Central Board's categorisation of industries into red, orange, green and white had been approximating administratively for years.

### Who decides penalties under the 2024 amendment, and how?

In a state where the amendment applies, an adjudicating officer appointed by the Central Government, ranked not below Joint Secretary to the Government of India or Secretary to a State Government, holds an inquiry, hears the person concerned and imposes the penalty, which is credited to the Environmental Protection Fund and is appealable to the National Green Tribunal.

The governing rules followed in 2024, framing the inquiry procedure, the officers authorised to file complaints for the penalty offences and a six-month period for completing inquiries, and they complete the picture of a system designed to be faster than the criminal process it replaces: notice, inquiry, order, appeal on a deposit, fund. Whether it is also more deterrent is the question the next section takes up.

Equally important is what the amendment leaves standing. The consent system of Sections 25 to 28 is untouched in its structure: prior permission, conditions, register, deemed consent and appeal all continue, and the criminal offence that survives, Section 45E, is anchored to it. Section 33A directions remain the boards' principal coercive instrument, and their disobedience moves from the criminal column to the penalty column without losing the direction's own force, since a board that cannot get compliance by penalty can still close the unit by direction and hold the closure. The citizen's Section 49 route survives for the offences that remain, and the sixty-day notice retains its function of compelling the board to act or disclose. The amendment is therefore best characterised as a change of sanctions laid over an unchanged administrative machine, not as a deregulation of discharge: every permission, standard, sample and direction in the Act operates in an adopting state exactly as it did before, with a different consequence at the end of the process.

The parliamentary passage of the amendment also has something to teach about the statute's condition after fifty years. The Bill was debated in both Houses in February 2024 in the shadow of the Jan Vishwas settlement, and the argument followed the lines the next section sets out, with the government emphasising ease of compliance and the disproportion of imprisonment for defaults under a technical statute, and opposition members pressing the deterrence arithmetic and the federal structure of the changes to appointments and consent guidelines. That the debate happened at all, for a statute whose offences much of the public argument treats as settled history, is itself evidence: the Water Act remains the live instrument through which India argues about what environmental enforcement is for, and the 2024 amendment, applying at first in two states, guarantees that the argument will be conducted state by state for years, in assemblies deciding whether to adopt a penalty regime their predecessors' assemblies made possible by asking Parliament to create the Act.

## The findable artifact: two regimes for the same discharge

The table below sets the pre-amendment text against the 2024 amended text for the same acts of discharge, direction and default. Which column governs a given factory depends on a fact external to the factory: whether the legislature of the state in which the discharge occurs has adopted the 2024 amendment by resolution, a question that must be asked state by state and that no summary of the Act can answer once for the whole country.

| Question | Unamended Act (most states) | 2024 amendment (Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan, Union territories, and adopting states) |
|---|---|---|
| Discharge of polluting matter under Section 24 | An offence: imprisonment of one year and six months to six years, and fine, under Section 43 | A penalty: ten thousand rupees to fifteen lakh rupees, plus ten thousand rupees for each day the contravention continues, under Section 43 as substituted |
| Defiance of a Section 33A direction | An offence: imprisonment of one year and six months to six years, and fine, under Section 41(2) | A penalty of ten thousand rupees to fifteen lakh rupees, plus ten thousand rupees a day, under the new Section 41A |

The table's last two rows are the ones that change the institution rather than the sanction, and they are the rows a reader should carry into the debate that follows. The penalty recast changes what happens to a polluter. The exemption power, the guideline power and the appointment rule change who decides, shifting discretion from the State Boards the 1974 Act created towards the Central Government and the Central Board, inside a statute whose entire legal foundation is that the states asked Parliament to legislate. That tension, between the amendment's content and the Act's constitutional origin, is where the argument about the 2024 changes is most serious.

## How to read the amended Act

A practical consequence of the two-regime position is that the Water Act can no longer be read from a single text. A lawyer advising on a discharge in India must assemble three things: the parent Act of 1974 as amended in 1978 and 1988; the Amendment Act of 2024, with its substitutions and insertions; and the adoption position of the state in which the discharge occurs, which determines which of the two penalty architectures, and which appointments and guideline provisions, are in force for that discharge. Commercial bare acts and board websites in adopting states reproduce the amended text, and in the rest of the country the unamended text, and the two circulate side by side with identical section numbers carrying different content, so that a citation of Section 43 or Section 44 is incomplete unless the reader knows which text is being cited. Conduct that straddles a state's adoption raises the further question of which regime governs acts done before adoption, answered by the ordinary savings principles that protect accrued liability under the earlier law: the amendment changes the consequence of future defaults, and prosecutions for past offences proceed on the text under which they were committed. None of this is exotic to Indian lawyers, who work with state amendments to central codes as a matter of routine, but it is new to this statute, and it changes the research discipline the Act demands of everyone who uses it.

The adoption position of any state is a matter of public record rather than inference: an amendment of this kind takes effect in a state on the resolution of its legislature and the consequential notifications, and the Central Board and the state boards publish the applicability position for their territories. A reader who needs the position for a particular state should take it from the Gazette record and the board that administers the Act there, and not from summaries, including this one, which describe the mechanism rather than track its movement; the mechanism is permanent and the map is not. That caution is the final form of the statute's oldest lesson. The Water Act was built to spread by recorded acts of consent, and it continues to change by recorded acts of consent, and at every stage the legally operative fact is the resolution and the notification, never the general impression that a reform has happened.

## Decriminalisation debated

The government's case, put by the Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav in the Lok Sabha debate on the Bill, rests on proportionality and administrability. Imprisonment of one and a half to six years for contraventions that include technical and procedural defaults, under a statute whose offences were framed without gradation of harm, is disproportionate; criminal process in magistrates' courts is measured in years, consumes the boards' scarce legal capacity, and ends, in the government's account, in acquittals and negligible fines that deter nobody. A penalty of up to fifteen lakh rupees, imposed by a senior adjudicating officer after an inquiry measured in months, appealable to a specialist tribunal, and credited to an environmental fund, is said to be a sharper and more certain instrument than a theoretical prison term that practice rarely delivered, and to fit the compliance philosophy that the consent system embodies: the boards were built to engineer discharges into compliance, and a fiscal lever serves that purpose better than a criminal one. On the federalism objection, the minister's answer was that the Bill followed the Constitution's own route: it applies where state assemblies have resolved for it, the states remain free to adopt or not, and the Central Government's new powers over appointments and guidelines are exercised in consultation with the Central Board and concern uniformity of standards rather than displacement of the states.

The opposing case, made by opposition members in both Houses and by environmental lawyers in the public argument that accompanied the Bill, accepts none of the three steps. A penalty capped at fifteen lakh rupees, they argue, is a licence fee to a mid-sized or large industrial unit, smaller than the cost of running a treatment plant properly for a season, and a flat monetary cap cannot scale with either the harm done to a river or the profit made by discharging untreated: deterrence that does not grow with gain invites the rational polluter to pay and continue, and the daily penalty of ten thousand rupees, modest beside industrial cash flows, does not repair the arithmetic. Imprisonment, on this account, was never mainly about the sentences actually served; it was about the personal exposure of directors and occupiers, the one consequence that concentrates a boardroom's attention, and its removal converts environmental compliance into a priced input. The objectors also press the institutional point: a regime in which the Centre prescribes how state chairmen are nominated, issues binding guidelines on the grant and refusal of consent, and appoints the adjudicating officers, all introduced through a mechanism that required state consent only from the two states that happened to resolve first, hollows out the federal bargain on which the parent Act stands, and does so in the field, water, that the Constitution assigns to the states.

A third voice in the argument belongs to industry, and it is not unanimous either. Industry associations, the Confederation of Indian Industry and FICCI among them, publicly supported the trust-based decriminalisation programme of which the Jan Vishwas Act was the vehicle, arguing that criminal exposure for procedural lapses under technical statutes distorts investment decisions and invites rent-seeking at the inspection level, and compliance officers in regulated sectors make a version of the same point from experience: the threat of a Section 44 prosecution for an unconsented technicality hung over defaults that everyone concerned knew would be regularised, and the regularisation economy that grew around that threat served neither the river nor the honest operator. Against this, industry voices in the water-intensive sectors have been quieter about the surviving Section 45E offence, for a reason that reflects well on nobody: so long as consent itself remains criminal to operate without, the compliance priority inside firms stays exactly where the boards would wish it, on the licence rather than on the discharge.

For national industry there is a further, practical consequence of the amendment's partial reach that the parliamentary debate touched only lightly. An operator with plants in several states will, for a period of years that no one can presently fix, run criminal exposure for discharge defaults in most of its locations and penalty exposure in a few, under consent guidelines issued centrally for the adopting states and state practice elsewhere. Compliance systems are built to the strictest applicable standard as a matter of prudence, so the practical effect of partial adoption may be smaller than the legal map suggests; but the anomaly of a central environmental statute whose sanction for the same act changes at a state border remains, and it is an anomaly the Act's own history created the machinery to produce.

An honest verdict holds parts of both. The proportionality argument is sound as far as it goes: the old pyramid convicted rarely, punished erratically, and reserved its severest mandatory minima for offences that magistrates were visibly reluctant to deploy against marginal offenders, so the claim that decriminalisation abandons a working deterrent overstates what the deterrent was achieving. But the arithmetic objection is also sound, and it is the stronger of the two on the central case, because the comparison that matters is not between the new penalty and the old sentence but between the new penalty and the cost of compliance: where treatment costs exceed fifteen lakh rupees a year, as they do for most genuinely polluting processes, the capped penalty prices non-compliance below compliance, and the system's deterrent then rests entirely on the instruments that were always its working surface, consent refusal, Section 33A closure, and the surviving Section 45E offence for operating without consent. The amendment's own architecture concedes as much: Parliament kept imprisonment exactly where administrative pressure culminates, in unconsented operation, and made the penalty itself enforceable by imprisonment for non-payment. The reform is therefore best read as a reallocation of coercion rather than its removal, from the discharge to the licence, and its success will be measured by whether adjudicating officers impose penalties at the top of the band against repeat and profitable offenders rather than at the bottom against the confused and the small.

## The Act and the river: what fifty years of the regime show

A statute that classifies waters by their designated best use invites an accounting against the classification, and the monitoring record supplies a sobering one. The Central Board's published assessments of polluted river stretches, built from the monitoring programme described earlier, identify stretches where the measured quality falls below the criteria for the designated use, and the recurring entries are the urban stretches of the major rivers, where municipal sewage volumes overwhelm the treatment capacity installed. The pattern inside the finding matters more than the finding itself. Industrial point sources, the dischargers the consent system was built around, are individually regulable and have been progressively brought under consent, treatment and, for the largest, continuous monitoring; the diffuse and municipal load, which no single consent governs and which depends on public investment in sewerage and treatment, has grown with urbanisation and outruns the boards' direction powers. The Act's architecture anticipated both halves of this record: it made the municipality a consent-holder and a direction-taker like any factory, which was legally bold, and it gave the boards no power to build the treatment plants whose absence makes municipal compliance impossible, which was the limit of what a regulatory statute could do. Judged by its own instruments, the regime succeeded in constructing the complete legal apparatus of water pollution control and in disciplining the industrial sector into a permission system; judged by the rivers, the apparatus is necessary and insufficient, and the insufficiency sits in investment and municipal governance rather than in the text of 1974.

The history also disposes of a simplification common in the amendment debate, that the Act's penalties were the system's foundation and their removal its demolition. The foundation, as the record shows, is the consent system and the direction power, with prosecution at the apex and seldom used; the municipal failure, the regime's largest, was never a failure of criminal deterrence at all, since no plausible penalty structure prosecutes a city's sewage into a treatment plant. Where criminal exposure plausibly did work, in concentrating the minds of private occupiers on their consent status, the amendment has preserved it, in Section 45E, at exactly that point. The fifty-year record therefore supports a discriminating judgment about 2024 rather than either slogan: the reform removes a sanction that was largely symbolic for the offence to which it was attached, retains it where it was functional, and leaves untouched both the instruments that work and the problems that no sanction in the Act can reach.

## Why enforcement lags: capacity and incentives

Every audit of the system reaches the same structural findings, and they are findings about institutions rather than law. The Comptroller and Auditor General's performance audits and the reports of the parliamentary committee concerned with the environment ministry have repeatedly recorded sanctioned posts lying vacant in State Boards, laboratories short of accredited capacity, and inspection frequencies too low for the consent conditions to be verified by observation rather than by the occupier's own returns. A board whose engineers cannot visit a unit more than rarely is a board administering self-reporting, and its consent conditions, however carefully drafted, become a paper regime policed principally by the complaints of neighbours and the occasional sampling run. The chairman's office compounds the problem where it is treated as a posting within the administrative service rather than the expert office Section 4 designs: the Act's qualifications clause is satisfied by a wide range of appointees, and the political sensitivity of closure directions against local industry gives every chairman reasons for caution that the statute does not counterweight. The boards' dependence on their state governments for establishment approvals under Section 11, and after the cess repeal on consent fees and grants, completes a picture of formal independence resting on practical dependence.

The human structure of the boards carries the same tension as their finances. The chairman and member-secretary hold office on terms the governments prescribe, and the absence of any statutory security of tenure or independent selection process means the two offices that determine a board's enforcement temper turn over with administrative convenience. Practitioners who work the system learn to read a board by its incumbents: the same statute, the same staff and the same consent files produce visibly different enforcement in the same state under different leadership, and directions practice accelerates or stalls with the persons in the two chairs. The 2024 amendment's transfer of the chairman's manner of nomination to central prescription was defended as a professionalisation measure, an attempt to stabilise the quality of appointments across states; its critics read it as an admission that the appointment problem is real, married to a remedy that relocates rather than removes the discretion.

Set against this, the Act gives a determined board formidable tools, and the record shows boards using them in bursts when litigation, media attention or a determined chairman concentrates the mind: mass closure directions against tannery and dyeing clusters, disconnection of power to defaulting units, prosecution drives in particular districts, and, at the Central Board's level, the monitoring directions that built the online effluent monitoring network for the most polluting industry categories. The variance itself is the finding. The statute is roughly constant across states; outcomes differ by state and by period because the institutions differ, which is the strongest single piece of evidence that the Water Act's problems are problems of the boards' capacity and independence rather than of its legal text, and that amendments aimed at the text, penal or procedural, will move the system only as far as the institutions carrying them allow.

## Verdict

The Water Act 1974 deserves its place at the head of India's environmental statute book on three grounds that survive criticism. It created the regulatory institution, the pollution control board, from which the rest of the structure grew, and it created it in a form that could be generalised to air and to the umbrella statute's work. It built a preventive system around prior consent, standards and sampling evidence rather than relying on punishment after harm, and that architecture remains the right one. And it did both through a constitutional mechanism that respected the states' ownership of the subject, legislating by invitation and spreading by adoption. Its weaknesses are the weaknesses of that achievement over fifty years: criminal provisions drafted too bluntly to be used and fines left to rot in nominal rupees, institutions underfunded and under-independent, and a consent regime whose paper thoroughness has persistently outrun the inspection capacity behind it. The amendment of 2024 answers the first weakness with penalties that may prove too small for the polluters who matter, deepens the second by moving appointment and guideline powers upward, and leaves the third, the decisive one, untouched.

The tests of the amended regime in its adopting states are already identifiable, and they are institutional rather than rhetorical. They are the rank and independence in practice of the adjudicating officers appointed, the position in the penalty band at which their orders settle against large and repeat offenders, the willingness of the Tribunal on appeal to sustain penalties at the top of the band, the contents of the consent guidelines issued under Section 27A and the use made of the exemption power, and, above all, the pace at which further state assemblies resolve to adopt, which will determine whether the two-regime map is a transition or a settlement. On each of these the record will accumulate state by state, exactly as the Act's own record accumulated after 1974, and the judgment of the amendment will be formed from that record and not from the arguments of 2024.

Whether a discharger in a given state faces a court or an adjudicating officer, six years or fifteen lakh, depends on a resolution of that state's assembly, and until the state-by-state map settles, the most important sentence in Indian water law is the one this statute began with: Parliament has no power here except as the states have given it, and what the states have given, state by state, is what the law is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Water Act 1974 prohibit?

Section 24 contains the Act's central prohibition. No person shall knowingly cause or permit any poisonous, noxious or polluting matter, determined in accordance with the standards laid down by the State Board, to enter directly or indirectly into any stream or well or sewer or on land, and no person shall knowingly cause or permit other matter to enter a stream if it tends to impede the proper flow of the water so as to aggravate pollution substantially. The prohibition is effect-based and standard-linked: the wrong lies in introducing matter that the Board's standards classify as polluting into water, including subterranean water, and the second limb catches even matter that is not itself polluting if it obstructs flow and worsens the pollution already present.

### Q: Why was the Water Act enacted under Article 252?

Water is a subject in the State List of the Constitution, principally Entry 17, with public health and sanitation in Entry 6, so Parliament had no general power to legislate on water pollution for the states. Article 252 allows Parliament to legislate on a state subject for those states whose legislatures pass resolutions asking it to do so, and to be adopted subsequently by other states in the same way. Twelve states passed such resolutions, Parliament enacted the Act for them and the Union territories, and the Act extended to other states as their legislatures adopted it. The route also locked the Act's amendment in the same channel: only Parliament can amend it, and amendments rest on state resolutions of the same kind.

### Q: How do consent to establish and consent to operate differ?

Consent to establish is the State Board's prior permission, under Section 25(1), to set up or take steps to set up an industry, operation, process or treatment and disposal system likely to discharge sewage or trade effluent. It is assessed on the proposal: the site, the process, the quantities and the treatment arrangements the applicant undertakes to build. Consent to operate is the permission to bring an outlet into use and to begin or continue the actual discharge, granted once the establishment exists and the treatment system is in place, and it carries the operative conditions on the quality, quantity, point and rate of discharge. In board practice the first authorises construction of the discharging capacity, the second authorises the discharge itself, and operation without the second is the default the penal provisions pursue.

### Q: What powers do Pollution Control Boards have to close a unit?

The boards' closure power comes from Section 33A, inserted by the 1988 amendment. A board may issue directions in writing to any person, officer or authority, who is bound to comply, and the section expressly includes the power to direct the closure, prohibition or regulation of any industry, operation or process, and the stoppage or regulation of the supply of electricity, water or any other service. A board can therefore order a polluting unit shut and order the electricity distribution company to disconnect its supply, without first obtaining a court's permission. The power is exercised on the basis of inspection and sampling material and, because a closure takes away a livelihood, the boards proceed by show-cause notice and hearing except in cases of continuing harmful discharge requiring immediate action.

### Q: Can a citizen prosecute a polluter under the Water Act?

Yes, through the route Section 49 prescribes. A court may take cognizance of an offence under the Act on a complaint by the Board or its authorised officer, or by any person who has first given the Board notice of not less than sixty days, in the prescribed manner, of the alleged offence and of the intention to complain. The notice is a condition of the complaint, not a formality: it gives the Board the opportunity to act on the material itself. If the citizen proceeds, the Board must on demand make available the relevant reports in its possession, such as sampling and analysis reports, unless it considers that the public interest requires refusal. The case is tried by a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class or a higher court.

### Q: What did the 2024 Water Act amendment decriminalise?

In the states where it applies, the amendment converted most of the Act's offences into monetary penalties. Non-compliance with Section 20 directions, defiance of Section 32 orders, Section 33 court directions and Section 33A directions, the obstruction and false information defaults, contravention of the Section 24 prohibition itself, and residuary contraventions all attract a penalty of ten thousand rupees to fifteen lakh rupees, with ten thousand rupees for each day of continuing default, imposed by an adjudicating officer instead of imprisonment imposed by a magistrate. What it did not decriminalise is operating without consent: failure to comply with Sections 25 and 26 remains an offence punishable with imprisonment of one year and six months to six years under the new Section 45E, and failure to pay a penalty within ninety days is itself an offence.

### Q: Why does the 2024 Water Act amendment apply only in some states?

Because the parent Act was passed under Article 252 on the resolutions of state legislatures, and Article 252(2) reserves its amendment to Parliament acting in like manner. The amendment's preamble recites resolutions passed by the Legislative Assemblies of Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan asking Parliament to amend the Act, and its applicability clause therefore extends it in the first instance to those two states and to the Union territories. Any other state joins the amended regime only when its own legislature passes a resolution adopting the amendment, and the amendment comes into force there on the date of adoption. Until a state resolves, the unamended Act, with its imprisonment-based penalties, continues to govern discharges in that state.

### Q: What happened to the water cess?

The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act, 1977 levied a cess on water consumed by specified industries and local authorities, with a rebate for assessees that installed treatment plants and met the prescribed conditions, and its proceeds supported the Central and State Boards' finances for four decades. The cess was swept into the goods and services tax settlement: the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2017 repealed the Cess Act by its Section 18 with effect from 1 July 2017. Since the repeal, the boards have relied on consent fees, government grants and other receipts rather than a dedicated levy on water consumption, a change that altered the boards' funding structure along with the tax system.

### Q: Who sits on the Central Pollution Control Board and the State Pollution Control Boards?

Both boards are wholly nominated bodies built to the same pattern. Each has a chairman with special knowledge or practical experience of environmental protection, or experience in administering institutions dealing with it, nominated by the Central or State Government respectively. Around the chairman sit up to five officials of that government, up to five members drawn from the tier below, local authority members in the case of State Boards and State Board members in the case of the Central Board, up to three non-official members representing agriculture, fishery, industry, trade or other interests, two representatives of government-owned companies or corporations, and a full-time member-secretary with qualifications in the scientific, engineering or management aspects of pollution control. It is the member-secretary who runs the institution day to day.

### Q: What was the law on water pollution in India before the Water Act 1974?

There was no general water pollution statute. The Indian Penal Code punished public nuisance and the fouling of a public spring or reservoir, provisions designed for individual acts of contamination and carrying small fines. Tort law let an injured riparian owner sue in nuisance or under the rule in Rylands v Fletcher, which compensated a plaintiff who could sue and prove damage but prevented nothing. Municipal and public health laws empowered local bodies over drains and sanitation, while municipal sewage was itself a leading source of pollution. A few states had narrow statutes of their own, including the Orissa River Pollution Prevention Act, 1953 and the Maharashtra Prevention of Water Pollution Act, 1969. Nothing fixed effluent standards, created a technical regulator, or required permission before a discharge began.

### Q: How does an industry apply for consent under the Water Act?

The occupier applies to the State Board in the prescribed form with the prescribed fees, disclosing the proposed site, the industry, operation or process, the sources and quantities of water used, the nature and expected composition of the sewage or trade effluent, and the treatment and disposal system proposed. The Board inquires into the application, commonly through inspection of the site and scrutiny of the treatment design, and may grant consent subject to conditions on the point, composition, temperature, volume and rate of discharge, or refuse it for reasons recorded in writing. First the consent to establish is sought, before construction of the discharging capacity; after the plant and treatment works are built, consent to operate is sought for the actual discharge, and renewals follow before each consent period expires.

### Q: Why does the sampling procedure in Section 21 matter in a prosecution?

Because Section 21 makes the analysis result admissible in legal proceedings only if the procedure is followed, and the procedure is demanding by design. The officer taking the sample must serve notice of the intended analysis on the occupier, divide the sample into two parts in the occupier's presence, seal, mark and sign both containers with the occupier's participation, and send one container without delay to the laboratory established or specified under the Act, the occupier being entitled to have the other part analysed. The analyst's report under Section 22 is then evidence of the facts it states. A break in this chain, a missing notice, an unsigned seal, an unexplained delay in dispatch, gives the defence a statutory answer to the prosecution's science, and prosecutions under the Act have repeatedly failed on it.

### Q: What happens when a company commits an offence under the Water Act?

Section 47 governs offences by companies in the unamended Act. Where an offence is committed by a company, the company itself and every person who, at the time of the offence, was in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business are deemed guilty and liable to be proceeded against, unless the person proves the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it. Directors, managers, secretaries and other officers are also liable where the offence is shown to have been committed with their consent or connivance or to be attributable to their neglect. In states where the 2024 amendment applies, this liability is carried into the new Section 45E for the offences that remain criminal, principally operating without consent.

### Q: Can the Central Board issue directions to a State Board?

Yes. Section 18 empowers the Central Board to give directions to a State Board, and the State Board is bound to comply with them. Section 17(2) places the State Board under two direction-givers, the Central Board and its own State Government, and resolves clashes: where a direction of the State Government is inconsistent with a direction of the Central Board, the matter is referred to the Central Government for its decision. In practice this power underlies the Central Board's nationwide enforcement directions, including the requirement of online continuous effluent monitoring for the most polluting categories of industry, which State Boards must then enforce within their territories. The arrangement is unusual in a statute resting on state consent, and the resolving states accepted it as part of the original settlement in 1974.

### Q: Can a State Board refuse, vary or withdraw a consent it has granted?

Yes, within the discipline of Section 27. The Board may refuse an application for consent, and it may review any consent it has granted, impose new or modified conditions, or withdraw the consent, but the section requires that the person concerned be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard before refusal, withdrawal or variation takes effect against him. The conditions in a consent are therefore not immutable promises; a Board that finds a discharge behaving worse than its consent assumed, or standards tightened for the receiving stream, may rewrite the conditions prospectively after hearing the occupier. A refusal or withdrawal without the hearing the section promises is open to challenge on the statute's own terms, apart from the appeal the Act provides against Board orders.

### Q: Does the Water Act regulate sewage, or only industrial effluent?

It regulates both, and the distinction runs through its definitions. Trade effluent covers liquid, gaseous or solid substances discharged from premises used for any industry, operation or process, or from a treatment and disposal system, other than domestic sewage. Sewage effluent means effluent from any sewerage system or sewage disposal works, and expressly includes sullage from open drains. Municipal sewage is therefore inside the Act: local authorities require consent for their discharges, the State Board's functions include inspecting sewage works and evolving methods for the utilisation and disposal of sewage, and the emergency and direction powers apply to an untreated municipal outfall as they do to a factory drain. The volume of untreated municipal sewage in Indian rivers is one of the regime's persistent failures, but it is a failure of enforcement and investment, not of statutory coverage.

### Q: What must an occupier do when an accident sends polluting matter into a stream?

Section 31 imposes the immediate duty. Where an accident or other unforeseen act or event occurs at any place, discharging or likely to discharge poisonous, noxious or polluting matter into a stream or well or onto land, the person in charge of that place must forthwith intimate the occurrence to the State Board and to the other authorities or agencies prescribed. Failure to intimate is itself an offence under Section 42(1). On receiving word, or on otherwise learning of the event, the Board may act under Section 32: remove and dispose of the polluting matter, remedy or mitigate the pollution caused, and issue orders restraining the person responsible, and any other person, from discharging or continuing to discharge it. The duty is to report first and at once; concealment converts an accident into an offence.

### Q: Which states does the Water Act apply to, and how did that list grow?

The Act began with the twelve states whose legislatures had resolved under Article 252 that Parliament should legislate: Assam, Bihar, Gujarat, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Karnataka, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Tripura and West Bengal, together with the Union territories, where the Central Board performs the State Board's functions. Section 1(2) and 1(3) then provided the growth mechanism: any other state could adopt the Act by a resolution of its legislature under Article 252(1), and the Act came into force there on the date of adoption. State by state, through the years that followed, further legislatures resolved, until the Act extended across all but a handful of states. The same mechanism governs the spread of the 2024 amendment, which restarts the state-by-state pattern from two states.

### Q: Where does the money from penalties under the 2024 amendment go?

Section 45D directs that all amounts realised as penalties under the amended provisions be credited to the Environmental Protection Fund established under Section 16 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986. The design is deliberate: penalty money does not disappear into general revenues but accumulates in a fund created for environmental protection purposes, linking the proceeds of enforcement to the purposes the enforcing statute serves. The crediting rule is one of the points on which the amendment aligns the Water Act with the architecture the Jan Vishwas amendments gave the umbrella statute, even though the Water Act itself was amended separately and later, through the Article 252 route its constitutional position required.

### Q: Who lays down the standards that a discharge must meet under the Water Act?

Standard-setting is shared between the two boards, with the State Board holding the operative power for discharges. Section 16 authorises the Central Board to lay down, modify or annul, in consultation with the government of the state concerned, the standards for a stream or well, and the Central Board's nationwide work fixes the classification of waters by their designated best use. Section 17 authorises the State Board to lay down, modify or annul effluent standards for the discharge of sewage and trade effluents and the classification of waters in the state, taking the receiving stream's tolerance into account. Section 24 then keys its prohibition to the standards laid down by the State Board, and individual consent orders translate the standards into the limits binding a particular discharge.
