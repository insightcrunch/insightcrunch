## Cold Open

![Nightjar: The Requisition Chain and the Warm Chair - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-17.webp)

The canal damp comes through the shutters before Mave Ottoline gets her coat off. It sits low in the requisition hall at Carrowmere, green and cold, mixing with lamp light that has burned all night over empty desks, with the smell of stacked paper warming near the vents, with dust from files that no one has opened since the last session ended. She fishes for her keys with numb fingers. The brass is cold enough to ache. One key for the outer door, one for the floor gate, one small and bright from use for the drawer where she keeps her seal. She likes this hour because nothing in it belongs to anyone else yet.

It is not yet six. The corridor lamps buzz and settle. Her shoes leave damp prints on the stone for three steps and then stop leaving them. She hangs her coat on the peg by her desk, sets her bag down without looking, and does what she always does when the backlog has kept her awake half the night: she counts the trays.

Intake, thirty one folders. Queries, nineteen. Returns, eight, each tied with string that has cut shallow grooves into the card. She had left them squared at five yesterday, edges aligned, her pencil laid across the top folder at an angle she would know if anyone had touched it. The pencil is still there. The angle is still hers. Relief moves through her so quickly she is annoyed at herself for feeling it.

Then she sees the light on her terminal.

Not the amber waiting light. The pale working light, steady, the one that means a session is open and a file is being held for reading. The screen has not dimmed. A requisition record fills it, header first, number field bright, the small mark in the corner that shows the session name.

Her name.

Mave stands with one hand still on the back of her chair. She does not sit. She reads the number without meaning to read it, the way a person reads a word written on a wall in a room that should be empty. She does not know the number. She knows the form type at once, knows the section codes down its left side, knows which office raised it and which floor should have processed it and which stamp ought to be sitting in the lower box waiting for a second hand. The lower box is empty.

She looks at the door she has just locked behind her out of habit. Locked. She looks at the floor gate. Locked. The night board by the entrance shows no entry after the cleaners, and the cleaners sign in blue ink, large letters, a different hand each week. This week the letters lean hard to the right. There is no second signature under hers from yesterday, no mark at all in the early column for today. Her own arrival is not yet written up because she has not written it.

If she writes it now, the time will be true. If anyone asks later, the board will say she came in before six and found nothing worth mentioning.

The thought arrives plainly and she hates how ready it is.

She steps closer. The chair at her desk is pushed in, but not as she leaves it. She leaves it turned a fraction toward the window, because the left wheel catches and because she likes to stand and turn it straight as part of beginning. It is straight already. Someone has sat here and, on rising, set the chair back with care. Or someone has not sat at all and has only moved it to reach the terminal and then remembered to correct it.

The keyboard is warm. No, she tells herself. Machines stay warm. She puts two fingers flat to the casing beside the screen and feels a faint heat that could be the lamp, could be the machine working through the night on a held session, could be nothing she can prove to anyone who matters.

The file on the screen is not one of her backlog. It is not from intake, not from queries, not from returns. She checks each tray again to be certain, lifting folders she already knows, because certainty has become something she needs to perform with her hands. None of the thirty one, none of the nineteen, none of the eight. The number on the screen belongs to a chain that passed this floor, or should have passed it, or was made to look as if it had.

She moves the pointer for the first time. The record scrolls a line. A raised requisition, approved for movement between stores, carrying a second reference she half recognises from a form she processed in a hurry during the last heavy week, when the hall was full and the supervisors were asking for speed and everyone stopped reading the second reference because the first one had already been cleared by a floor above theirs. She remembers the tiredness of that day more than the form. She remembers a cup gone cold, a supervisor standing too close, a joke about hands that only move paper never making decisions that count.

Her hand stops.

If she scrolls further she will learn more and knowing more will make the next choice worse. If she stops here she can still say, if she is ever asked, that she saw a file open and assumed a fault in the system left it held overnight. Systems do that. Everyone knows systems do that. Sessions hang. Records stick. Terminals wake with yesterday still on them.

Except yesterday this record was not on her screen at close of day. She would swear to that in a room where swearing still mattered. She closed her session herself. She watched the light go amber. She wrote the time on the board, locked the drawer, turned the chair to its crooked angle, and left with the rain starting over the canal.

Someone has come in after her, or before her this morning, opened a session in her name, called up a record that has no business on this floor at this hour, and left it displayed like a lamp left burning in a window.

Or no one has come in at all, and a session can be opened from another room, another floor, another building entirely, by a hand that knows her login the way she knows it herself. That possibility is larger and colder. It means the lock on the door means nothing. It means the board means nothing. It means her name can be worn by someone else while she sleeps.

Mave looks down the long hall. Desks in rows, lamps hooded, each tray a small skyline of paper. At the far end the high windows show a grey that is not yet morning. Somewhere below, water moves against stone. A pipe ticks as it warms. Nothing else moves.

She thinks of the senior clerk two floors up who told her, in her first month, that the requisition service runs because most people, most days, choose the smaller trouble over the larger one. Report a fault and the day fills with statements. Report a person and the year fills with them. Close a thing quietly, he said, and the work goes on, and the work going on is the only proof anyone needs that the system is sound.

She thinks of her sister waiting for a transfer approval that sits somewhere in a chain like this one. She thinks of the rent note folded in her bag, of the supervisor who praised her in front of the floor last session for never making a backlog into a drama, of the word reliable spoken as if it were a kind of armour she could keep wearing so long as she never tested what it cost.

Her throat is dry. She reaches for the water glass she leaves by the terminal and finds it on the wrong side of the screen. She always leaves it left. It is right now, half full, with a faint ring beneath it on the wood. She does not drink.

The record waits. The cursor blinks in an empty field near the bottom, the field for the processing mark, the field her seal would fill if she completed the record now. For a sick second she understands the shape of what is being asked of her without anyone asking. Complete it, and the chain moves under her name. Leave it open, and whoever opened it can return and find it as they left it. Close it, and she becomes the only person who knows it was ever here, and her silence becomes part of the chain too.

She puts her hand on the control. Her fingers are not steady. She tells herself she is checking the session time, only that, a clerk's ordinary check, and she calls up the small session panel before she can lose the excuse.

Opened at four seventeen. Held since. Session name hers. Origin field blank.

Blank. Not another floor. Not another building. Blank, as if the system itself has decided not to say.

Mave stares at that empty field longer than she has stared at anything in months. A blank can be an error. A blank can also be a privilege, the kind held by people senior enough that the system does not record where their hands have been. She knows of two such privileges on this floor's list. She does not hold either of them.

Footsteps sound on the stair beyond the gate, the first of the day shift, still twenty minutes early and already complaining in a low voice to someone on the step below. She knows the voice. In a moment the gate will rattle, keys will turn, the hall will fill with coats and damp wool and ordinary grievance, and the screen will still be lit, and she will have to have a face ready.

She closes the file.

She closes the session after it, carefully, in the right order, the way she was trained, so that no error flag rises. The working light fades to amber. The screen goes dark and shows her, for an instant, her own face reflected thin and early and frightened, before that too is gone.

She writes her arrival on the board, time true, letters even. She moves her water glass back to the left side. She turns her chair a fraction toward the window, correcting a correction, and sits down in it as the gate opens and the day begins to enter without her.

She tells no one. She pulls the first backlog folder toward her and opens it and starts to read a requisition she can understand, for goods she can picture, raised by a hand she can name. All morning the dark screen stays at the edge of her sight. She knows the silence is wrong in the way a held breath is wrong, necessary for a moment and then a harm, and she holds it anyway, and hates that holding it is so easy to do well.

## Tasking

Mira Kovac had slept through the night without waking, rare enough that she noticed it while dressing. She ate standing by the window, bread and hard white cheese, tea strong enough to clear the last of sleep, and felt the reserve in herself level and unspent. For once she arrived at the Ninth Office with her hands warm and her attention whole.

That wholeness lasted until she saw both names on the summons.

Commissioner Ilse Harrand sent for people. Controller Arvo Leyden sent for people. They did not, in Mira's experience, send for the same person together, on the same slip, with both marks side by side above the hour. The clerk who brought it waited until she had read it, which was its own message.

The commissioner's room had been arranged for three and for no witnesses. No clerk at the side desk, no tray for a record keeper. Leyden closed the door himself and checked the latch with the back of his hand before taking the chair to Harrand's left. Harrand sat behind the wide desk with a single folder before her, unopened, and a narrow slip of card face down beside it.

"You are rested," Harrand said. It was not a greeting. "Tell me if that is true. I would rather know now than guess later."

"It is true," Mira said. "Full reserve. Nothing drawn since yesterday morning, and that was a stores check, light work."

Leyden nodded once, as if a figure had matched one he already held. He looked older in the morning light, one cuff frayed where he worried it when he read. "Then hear this with what you have," he said. "Not with what you can borrow from yourself later. There will be time enough for spending."

Harrand opened the folder. Inside lay a single sheet, a number written large, two smaller references beneath it and nothing else. She turned it so Mira could read it. Mira felt the room alter, not with weight, for paper only just written carried almost none, but with the memory of another form, held by a man whose badge had promised an authority the form had then outrun.

"[The number on the badge man's form](/2026/11/20/nightjar-badge-and-burn-020/)," Harrand said. "You recovered it. You reported it. It was logged and set aside because the case closed around the man and not around the paper that carried him. That was my decision. I am reopening it."

Mira kept her eyes on the number until she had it fixed. "Where does it go?" she asked.

"Into our own house first," Leyden said. "Then out of it. It traces into the Accord requisition chain, through stores and floors and processing desks, in more than one city. How far it runs is not yet established. That is the work."

He said it levelly, as a controller should, but neither of them had brought her a finished picture to carry out of the room. They had brought her a beginning with teeth in it.

Harrand slid the card across and turned it over. On it, in a clerk's careful hand, stood a short column of offices, not names, each with a mark beside it. Raised. Processed. Approved for movement. Held. Released. "Any trained clerk can process a requisition," she said. "Far fewer hands may raise one, and fewer still one of this type, with this movement approval attached. Before anything else, I need those two kinds of hands separated, name by name, without error. A floor of people who only moved paper someone else created is not a chain. It is a crowd standing near one."

Mira thought of trays and seals and early rooms, of how easy it was, inside a building that ran on forms, to confuse nearness to a form with authorship of it. "And if some of the raising hands are ours?" she asked.

Neither of them answered too quickly, which was an answer.

Leyden spoke first. "If the Ninth Office is in its own chain, Kovac, then the Office is in it. You will report what you find in the order you find it, to both of us, and you will not trim a name because the corridor it sits in is one you walk every day."

"And if it stops short of us?" she said.

"Then it stops short," Harrand said. "And you will show the exact step where it stops, and who would have had to take the next one, and why you are certain they did not. Short of home is a finding, not a comfort I am asking you to manufacture."

The card stayed between them. Mira did not touch it yet. A login had put that number in motion or kept it moving. A login was a name worn by a hand, and somewhere in the chain a name might have been worn by the wrong hand, or by no recorded hand at all. She would have to ask whose, in buildings where the asking itself would be remembered.

"What would taking it look like?" she asked.

Leyden exhaled through his nose, not quite a laugh. "Simultaneous," he said. "It cannot be taken desk by desk over a week. Word would run ahead of you down the chain you were climbing. It would mean officers entering Accord buildings in four cities on one morning, at one hour, warrants drawn from the same authority, taking the named desks before any could warn the next. Our own buildings, Kovac. Our own locks opened from inside, our own clerks told to stand away from their trays."

"People I may know," she said.

"People you do know," Harrand said. "Some of them for years. I will not soften that either. If the names hold, you will be the officer who brought the morning to their desks."

Silence settled over the desk. From the corridor came the ordinary sounds of the Office beginning its day, none of it entering the room properly. Mira found she was measuring her breath, a habit from heavy places, though this room carried nothing heavier than paper and decision.

She carried Sarte still: six weeks of slow courage from the extraction work, a man teaching himself not to flinch at the pace his fear set, until the flinching stopped being the loudest thing in any room he entered. She had read it where it settled, in objects touched and thresholds crossed, and kept it as a pressure behind her ribs when her own pace wanted to quicken. And she carried Reni Vosk: a stitched forearm, thread pulled clean through healing skin, and beneath it, brighter and harder to hold, Reni's pleasure at the cut that had made the stitches necessary, a fierce private gladness at having been marked while standing where she had chosen to stand. Mira had read it on the binding and the cup Reni gripped afterward, and had said nothing, because reading was not permission.

As if the thought had a sound, a knock came, two taps, and Reni herself entered with a message sleeve she set before Leyden without looking at the open folder. Her left forearm was bound in fresh linen, the stitches hidden, the hand below it steady. Colour stood high on her cheek. She had plainly been running messages up the stairs to be useful while kept from heavier duty, and was plainly furious at the keeping.

"Sir. Commissioner. The stores liaison is waiting on the lower floor whenever you are ready for him." Her eyes went to Mira and stayed a fraction too long, bright with a question she knew better than to ask in this room. "Officer."

"Reni," Mira said.

The door closed behind her. Leyden set the sleeve aside unread. "She heals," he said.

"She does," Mira said. "At her own speed, which is faster than the surgeon's."

"Good," Harrand said. "You will need steady juniors before this is done. You will not spend that one early to prove a point about readiness. Is that understood?"

"It is."

Petra Dunmore appeared in the doorway, summoned by some signal Mira had not seen, thin prints held against her chest. She did not enter fully. "The first trace is back, Commissioner. The number holds across three stores lists. After that the references divide. One line ends where it should. The other does not end at all within the lists I am permitted to see. I can show you where it divides. I cannot yet show you who was permitted to divide it."

"Then that is where she begins," Leyden said. "Thank you, Petra."

When the door had closed again, Harrand folded her hands on the folder. "Now the cost," she said. "You will hear it from both of us, because it belongs to both of us, and because I will not have you discover later that one of us promised what the other never said."

Leyden leaned forward, his empty hands in sight on the desk edge. "Whatever this finds," he said, "I will not be able to protect you from it. Not from the floors it crosses, not from the cities it touches, not from this Office if this Office is touched by it. I can keep your orders lawful and your reports read in full. I cannot keep you liked, or trusted, or easy to work beside, once you have taken colleagues at their desks on my signature. There is no shelter in it."

"And I will not be able to protect you either," Harrand said. "If the chain runs where I believe it may, people senior to this Office will call the inquiry an attack on the Accord by the Accord, and they will begin with the officer whose name is on the trace. That officer will be you. I can answer for the order. I cannot answer away its consequences. Decide with that understood, not suspected."

Mira looked from one to the other. Joint tasking, a closed room, a cost named twice in plain speech. No one had ever taken this much care to leave her without an illusion. It frightened her more cleanly than the number had, and steadied her too.

The last unspoken question stood between them, hers to voice or swallow. The Office had never taught her that refusal was a door that opened. But Harrand had said decide, and Leyden had not corrected the word.

"And if I say no?" she asked quietly.

"Then you say it here," Harrand said. "Now. Not down the chain, not by slowness, not by a report too careful to act on. You say it to our faces and another officer is found by noon. But understand me, Kovac. Whoever takes it takes all of it. There is no smaller version in which the chain is traced politely and no morning ever comes."

Mira put her hand on the card and drew it toward her. The offices meant floors, and floors meant people at desks before six and after dark, trays squared against the fear that someone, someday, would come counting. Somewhere among them a session had been opened under a name that might not belong to the hands that used it. She would begin with the dividing reference Petra had found, before the floors filled.

"I take it," she said.

Leyden stood first. Harrand rose after him, and Mira with her. At the door, Harrand stopped her with two fingers laid briefly on the folder she now carried, not on her arm. "Full reserve will not last," she said. "Spend it as if you know that."

"I do," Mira said, and went out into the corridor, where Reni was pretending not to wait, and the day, which had begun so unusually whole, began at once to draw on her.

## Ground Truth

Mira began with paper because paper could be checked by someone who did not share her gift and did not need to trust it. That was Petra Dunmore's condition, stated on the first morning in the small records room off the requisition floor, before either of them had looked at a register and before Mira had said what she expected the registers to show. Dunmore set three bound volumes, a bundle of authority warrants tied with faded cord, and a flat tin of processing stamps on the table between them, and she laid her hand flat on the warrants as if to keep them from being reached for too soon. The method would come first, she said. The conclusions would wait until the method had done its work in the open, where both of them could see where a conclusion had come from and where it had not. Mira, who had worked with analysts who wanted the answer before the question had been properly framed, recognized the discipline for what it was and was grateful for it, even as the gratitude sat uneasily beside her impatience.

Dunmore's method, spoken aloud and then followed without deviation for the better part of two days, was plain enough to sound dull and exact enough to catch what intuition would have missed. First, the requisition registers, taken in order, page by page, for every entry that carried the traced number or any number in its immediate family, because a chain that had been built by hand would show its joins in the numbering before it showed them anywhere else. Second, the authority warrants, which were the paper that said who had been granted what power and when that grant had begun and ended, checked name by name against the register entries, so that a signature or a stamp would never be taken as proof of authority when the warrant behind it said otherwise. Third, the processing stamps, which recorded movement rather than origin, the passage of a form from desk to desk and office to office, useful for establishing who had touched a thing and useless, Dunmore insisted, for establishing who had brought it into being. A stamp proved handling. A warrant proved power. A register proved that a number had been entered into the system at a particular point in its traffic. Confuse any one of those for another, Dunmore said, and an investigation would build a handsome case against the wrong people and call the building thorough. Mira wrote the three distinctions down in her own notebook, in her own hand, and underlined the sentence about handling and power twice.

The traced number itself sat at the center of the table like a stone dropped into still water, and everything Dunmore did radiated outward from it in careful rings. It had come to Mira out of an earlier case, carried on a form whose authorising signature four separate departments had examined and honestly denied as theirs, each denial given without evasion and each, on examination, believed, because the signature belonged to nobody. That fact did not soften as the days passed or as the paper accumulated. It hardened. The number was real. The form was real. The movement of the form through the Accord's own requisition chain, here in Carrowmere during the Twenty Ninth Session of the Meridian Accord, was real and stamped and registered at every stage. Only the hand that had set it moving had no owner that any department would claim and no name that any warrant would support. Mira had carried that contradiction from the previous case into this one the way a person carries a splinter that has worked too deep for simple extraction, aware of it in quiet moments, aware of it most of all when she pressed on the place where it lay.

What Dunmore proposed, and what Mira accepted before the first register was opened, was to stop asking who had signed and start asking who could have raised. The distinction mattered more than any other in the building, and it was a distinction the requisition system itself blurred in ordinary speech, where everyone who worked the floor was said to handle requisitions as if handling and originating were the same act wearing two names. They were not. To process a requisition was to receive a form already raised, to check its fields against its supporting papers, to stamp it, to route it, to file its copy, to query an omission and return it for completion. It was skilled work, and in careless hands or corrupt ones it could delay, divert, or quietly bury a form, but it could not bring a form into existence inside the chain. To raise a requisition was a different power altogether. It was the power to open a number, to commit the Accord to a course of procurement or movement or expenditure, to set paper in motion with institutional force behind it. That power was granted sparingly, by warrant, to named individuals, for defined classes of business, and it could be held, suspended, transferred, or allowed to lapse. A processor without raising authority who wished to originate a form would have to borrow, steal, or impersonate the authority of someone who held it, and each of those acts would leave a different kind of trace. Dunmore intended to find out, for every person in the visible chain around the traced number, which side of that line they stood on. Until she had done so, she said, they did not have suspects. They had names in proximity to a number, and proximity was the cheapest evidence in any large institution and the most commonly mistaken for guilt.

The visible chain, when Dunmore had assembled it from registers and stamp records and the duty rosters for the relevant periods, contained eleven names. Mira read the list first as a whole and then again name by name, resisting the pull of familiarity where it appeared and resisting, just as firmly, the pull of strangeness where a name meant nothing to her. Eleven people had touched, routed, checked, filed, supervised, or stood adjacent to the traced number at some point in its passage. Eleven people could be placed, on paper, within reach of it. That was all the list said, and Dunmore, to her credit, refused to let it say more in those first hours, even when Mira caught herself arranging the names into a pattern and had to make herself stop.

Henrik Dall stood first on the list because his stamp appeared earliest in the surviving paper and because Mira had met him before, [in her first case, over a dead supervisor and a room that held no weight at all](/2026/11/01/nightjar-clean-room-001/), when he had worked as a bonded chain assessor, one of the small cadre trusted to examine a chain of custody and certify whether it had held or broken. He worked a requisitions desk now, a quieter posting that he had taken without complaint after the assessor rota had been thinned, and the warrant record showed that he held authority to raise requisitions within a narrow class, a legacy of his assessor bonding that had never been formally withdrawn when he changed desks. His access was broad in one direction and shallow in another. He could open the floor's registers, consult the warrant file, and enter the assessor's store where seals and chain certificates were kept, but his raising authority covered only forms connected to the movement and verification of bonded goods. Mave Ottoline stood second, a requisition officer of nine years on the floor, known for an orderly desk and a memory for incomplete fields that younger clerks relied on the way walkers rely on a marked path through marsh ground. Ottoline held no raising authority at all. She processed. Her access ran to the intake trays, the morning queue, the login terminal bank along the north wall, and the filing cabinets where completed copies slept in their thousands, and within that territory she was formidable, but the warrant file was unambiguous that origination had never been granted to her.

Corin Vell, the third name, was a senior processing clerk with a narrow, careful hand and a reputation for returning forms for the smallest omission, a habit that made him unpopular with departments in a hurry and valuable to anyone who needed a record to survive scrutiny. He processed only. His access covered the checking benches and the returns ledger. Tomasin Hale, fourth, supervised the stamp room on a rotating basis and processed forms when the queue demanded it, which was often, and her access extended to the stamp tin, the seal press, and the locked drawer where superseded stamps were kept until destruction was certified. She held no warrant to raise. Selin Arvad, fifth, worked the night intake two weeks in every five and processed forms that arrived after the main floor had emptied, her access limited to the intake office, the night register, and a terminal that accepted entries only within her shift hours. Bram Kessler, sixth, was a filing clerk who also processed returns, a large, quiet man who knew the cabinet runs the way a librarian knows a beloved and disorderly library, able to lay a hand on a copy years old within minutes. His access was to paper after the fact, to copies and cross references, and his warrant file contained no raising grant of any kind. Tessa Vane, seventh, processed expenditure forms and held the petty authority to query sums below a fixed threshold, which was an authority to question and not an authority to originate, a distinction her own warrant spelled out in language so plain that Dunmore read it aloud twice. Her access ran to the expenditure bench and the query tray. Ferris Nal, eighth, was a runner and relief clerk who processed when benches were short handed and carried sealed bundles between the floor and the registry, his access defined by movement, corridors and cage lifts and the registry counter, rather than by any power over content. Odette Sarr, ninth, processed personnel linked requisitions, secondments and temporary postings and the forms that trailed after them like ducklings, and her access covered the personnel annex and its separate register. She could not raise. Cassian Dray, tenth, was the floor's deputy supervisor, a man whose processing stamp appeared on more forms than any other in the sample Dunmore had drawn, because it was his task to clear blockages and countersign returns, and his access was the widest of any processor on the list, extending to every bench, every tray, and the supervisor's cabinet. Yet width was not depth. His warrant granted supervision and processing in generous measure and raising authority not at all.

The eleventh name was Gregor Halvane, and his entry in the warrant file differed from the other ten in kind rather than degree. Halvane had been a senior requisitions officer, one of the few on the floor ever granted broad raising authority across several classes of business, including the class to which the traced form belonged. He no longer worked the floor. He had transferred to a liaison posting in another seat of the Accord and then, within the year, had retired from that posting on grounds of health that the personnel record stated without elaboration. His access, at the time the traced number had moved, had been complete in the ways that mattered. He could raise, he could process his own raisings if he chose to disregard the convention that separated the two acts, and he could enter every room on the floor without challenge, because seniority had made his presence unremarkable in all of them. Dunmore placed his warrant on top of the pile when she had finished reading it and said nothing for a moment, and Mira understood the silence. Of eleven names, the paper had so far produced exactly two who held or had held the power to bring a form of this class into being. One was a retired senior officer no longer in the building. The other was Henrik Dall, whose raising authority was narrow, bonded, and tied to a class adjacent to but not identical with the class of the traced form. The other nine processed. That was the shape of the list, and Dunmore, having established it, wrote it into a table so that neither of them would be able to misremember it later under pressure or reshape it in recollection to fit a theory.

Mira studied the table until the columns settled in her mind, and she made herself attend to what it did not contain as carefully as to what it did. It contained no motive. It contained no act. It contained the distribution of a power across eleven people who had all, at one point or another, stood near a number that should not have existed in the form it took, and it contained, in the Status column, an honesty that she wanted preserved as long as possible. Not yet questioned. Not yet accused. Not yet anything except named by paper that had been assembled by a method stated before its conclusions. She copied the table into her notebook beneath Dunmore's three distinctions, and the act of copying, slow and word for word, cooled something in her that had wanted to move faster. Speed, she had learned across enough cases to trust the lesson, was usually the wish to stop feeling uncertain. The paper offered no such relief, and she settled, not without effort, into the uncertainty it required.


The floor itself was the next witness, and Mira approached it in the only way her gift allowed, which was early, quietly, and with no expectation that it would give her a name. Weight did not work that way, and she had stopped wishing that it did years before. Weight was residue. It was the emotional weather that people left in the places they inhabited and the objects they handled, fear soaked into a doorway where fear had been felt often and hard, grief settled into a chair, violence clinging to a tool or a floorboard long after the act had ended, intent warming a handle that had been gripped by someone who meant to use what lay beyond it. It needed an anchor, a physical thing or place to hold it. It never identified a person. It never declared whether a remembered feeling had belonged to a truth or a lie, and it never replayed a scene for her like a performance staged for her benefit. In crowds it frayed into uselessness, too many lives pressing their weather into the same air. Among the dead it found nothing at all, because the dead left no fresh residue for her to read and what they had left in life faded without renewal. She had explained these limits so often, to so many officers who wanted a cleaner instrument, that the explanation had become a kind of professional courtesy she performed before she worked, and she performed it for Dunmore in the corridor before the first morning read, not because Dunmore had asked for miracles but because Mira needed the limits spoken aloud in the presence of the work, as a contract between them.

She came to the requisition floor at six in the morning on the first day, an hour before the early clerks were due and two hours before the floor reached its ordinary roar, and she came again at six on the second morning, having slept badly in the interval and dreamed of nothing she could remember. The building at that hour belonged to cleaners and to the night intake staff going off shift with the particular tiredness of people who have worked while the city slept. Carrowmere outside the tall windows was still dark in the way of a seat city that woke by bells rather than by light, the streets below holding the last of the night watch and the first of the market carts in equal, unhurried measure. Mira walked the floor slowly, bench by bench, with her hands at her sides and her attention lowered into the places themselves, the worn wood of the intake counter, the brass rail where generations of clerks had steadied themselves while they waited for a terminal to free itself, the handles of the filing cabinets, the stamp room door. Most of what the floor held was exactly what a working floor should hold. There was the low, ground in residue of routine, thousands of ordinary mornings layered one upon another until they formed a kind of emotional topsoil, patience and boredom and mild resentment and the small satisfactions of a queue cleared before the bell. There was anxiety around the expenditure bench, sharper than elsewhere, the familiar anxiety of people who handled sums that could ruin them if mishandled. There was grief, old and private, soaked into one checking bench in particular, and Mira passed it by without inquiry, because a person's sorrow at their work was not evidence and she had no right to make it into any.

What did not belong was at the terminal bank along the north wall, and she found it on the first morning without being able to say, at first, what she had found. She stood before the third terminal from the window, the one whose chair was kept pushed in with a neatness the other chairs did not share, and she felt, distinct from the general weather of the floor, a residue of patience and routine that was too regular, too settled, too deeply worn for the hours the floor was officially occupied. Someone had sat here, often, at times when the chair should have been empty, and had sat here not in haste or in fear or in the furtive tension that illicit work usually left behind like a scent, but in calm, repeated, unhurried occupation, the patience of a person performing a familiar task in a familiar place at an hour they had made their own. The feeling had the quality of habit worn smooth by repetition. It had no face. It had no name. It gave her no sense of a body, of age, of intention beyond the bare fact of regular presence, and when she moved to the terminals on either side the feeling thinned and vanished, returning only when she came back to the third chair and laid her fingertips, lightly, on its wooden back. She stood there for a long time while the light changed in the windows, and the cold that reading always brought began its slow climb from her hands into her wrists and forearms, the price arriving as it always arrived, unnegotiable and unhurried, a chill that no coat quite reached and that would keep her company for hours.

On the second morning she came earlier still and settled herself in the shadow of the filing cabinets before six, not hidden exactly, because hiding would have made her presence into something it was not if she were discovered, but placed where a person entering the floor would not see her until they had committed themselves to entering. She wanted, if she could manage it without turning the read into a trap she had no authority to set, to learn whether the presence her gift had reported was a living pattern or a finished one, residue laid down in some earlier season and merely preserved by the chair and the terminal like a scent in cloth. The floor answered her at twenty minutes past six, not with a sight but with a sound, the soft, practiced sound of the north door opening on its well kept hinges and closing again with care, and then footsteps that did not hesitate, that crossed the floor by a route that avoided the loose board near the intake counter, and that stopped at the third terminal. Mira did not move. She did not see a face, because the cabinet run blocked her line of sight to everything except a portion of the window light and the edge of the terminal bank, and she chose, in the moment, not to shift for a better view and risk converting an observation into a confrontation she could not yet support. She heard a chair drawn out. She heard the small, definite sounds of a terminal being woken and used, keys pressed at a working pace, neither rushed nor tentative, for perhaps eleven minutes. She felt, through the floor and the anchored furniture of the room, the living confirmation of the residue she had read the morning before, patience and routine renewed in real time, a person present early who by every roster and register Dunmore had examined should not have been present at all. Then the terminal was closed down with the same care with which it had been opened, the chair was pushed in, and the footsteps receded by the route they had come, avoiding the loose board again, and the north door opened and closed, and the floor was empty and officially unoccupied for another forty minutes. Mira remained where she was until her hands had stopped shaking from the cold of the read, and she wrote down times and sounds and nothing else, because nothing else was what she honestly had. Her gift had told her that a presence existed. It had not told her, and would never tell her, whose presence it was.

Henrik Dall gave her the second read of those days, and he gave it freely, which changed its character entirely. She went to him after the second morning, when Dunmore had confirmed from the duty rosters that no one on the list of eleven had been rostered to the floor at that hour on either day, and she found Dall at his desk in the assessor's old corner, where the light was poor and the registers lived within arm's reach. She had remembered him as a careful man with a dry, self deprecating humor and hands that never touched evidence without first stating, aloud, what he was about to touch and why. He was older in the way of a year that had not been kind in small, cumulative ways, thinner in the face, slower to rise from his chair, but the care was intact, and when she explained that the traced number had led her back to the floor and that his name stood on the paper around it, he did not perform innocence or take offense or ask her to leave. He listened to the whole of it with his hands folded on the desk before him, and when she had finished he asked one question, whether she intended to read his desk by weight, and when she said that she would like to, with his permission, and that she would tell him honestly what it gave her and what it could not, he nodded once and stood and stepped away from the desk with a deliberateness that she understood as respect for the work. He even turned his chair to the wall, so that nothing of his own posture would crowd her reading.

His desk held a year of waiting. She knew it within moments of laying her hands on its worn surface, not as a fact she could have sworn to in any formal proceeding, but as the settled, unmistakable residue of a man who had been braced for an event he expected and dreaded and, in some part of himself that the desk had absorbed over long months, desired. There was anxiety in the wood, old anxiety, refreshed daily and never discharged. There was the particular tension of a person who rehearses a conversation he believes is coming, who composes his account in the small hours and revises it at his desk and finds the revision no better than the draft. And beneath the anxiety, unmistakable once she had settled into the read and let its layers separate, there was relief, not yet fully felt but present in anticipation, the relief of a man who has carried something alone for a long time and has begun to believe that the carrying might soon end. The desk did not tell her what he had done. It did not tell her whether the thing he waited to account for was a crime, an error, or an act of obedience that had soured in his conscience over a year of silence. Weight had never once, in all her years of reading, distinguished guilt from sorrow or shame from fear of being misunderstood, and it did not do so now. It told her that Henrik Dall had been waiting for this investigation for a year, and that he was relieved it had come, and she reported exactly that to him when she lifted her hands and the cold settled into her bones like water into cloth. He received the report without surprise. He sat down again, slowly, in the chair he had turned back to face her, and he unlocked the lower drawer of his desk and took out a folded sheet of paper, much handled, softened at its creases, and he laid it on the desk between them without immediately releasing it.

A year earlier, he told her, an instruction had come to him through the floor's ordinary traffic, attached to a form he was processing in his assessor's capacity, directing him to certify a chain as unbroken without performing the middle examination that certification required. The instruction had been verbal in its origin and paperless in its passage, delivered by a colleague who had himself received it from elsewhere, and it had carried the unmistakable implication that it descended from authority too senior to be questioned through ordinary channels. Dall had performed the certification. He had told himself, at the time, the things that people in large institutions tell themselves when an irregular order arrives dressed in the clothes of routine, that the examination had surely been performed elsewhere, that the chain was in any case sound, that a refusal would damage colleagues and change nothing. Within a week he had understood that he could not verify any of those comforts, and within a month he had begun, privately and entirely alone, to keep a note of the instruction, the date as near as he could fix it, the form's class, the colleague who had conveyed it, and the exact words as he remembered them. He had kept the note in his drawer for a year, adding nothing to it after the first writing except, on three occasions, the date on which he had considered destroying it and had not. He slid it across the desk to her now with the air of a man handing over something heavier than paper. Mira read it twice. The colleague named in the note was not among the eleven. The form's class was adjacent to the traced form's class, the same adjacency she had already noted in Dall's warrant. And the instruction, as Dall had recorded it, contained a phrase she had not encountered anywhere in the registers or the warrants, a small, procedural phrase about steps and the taking of them, that she copied into her notebook without yet knowing what it meant and with a feeling, cold along her arms for reasons that had nothing to do with her gift, that she would encounter it again.


Mave Ottoline's account reached Mira through Dunmore rather than directly, because Ottoline had told it first to the one person on the floor she trusted to hear it as a procedural fact rather than as a confession, and Dunmore, having heard it, brought Mira to the intake office at the end of the working day and closed the door and let Ottoline tell it again in her own words. Some weeks earlier, Ottoline said, she had come in early to clear a backlog that had been pressing on her conscience, the ordinary backlog of a busy session, nothing sensitive, nothing unusual except the hour. She had unlocked the intake office, hung her coat on its peg, and crossed to the terminal bank to begin, and her system was already open. Not merely switched on, not merely warmed by a cleaner or wakened by a fault in the building's supply, but open under her own login, at her own queue, with the screen resting at the point in her work where a person who knew her habits might leave it for her to find. She had stood and looked at it for a full minute, she said, while the floor remained empty around her and the morning remained, to every other sense, entirely normal. Then she had closed it down, properly and completely, and she had told no one, and she had come in at her ordinary hour the next day and the day after that and had watched her terminal each morning with an attention she described, with some shame, as the attention of a person who half hopes to have imagined the thing that frightened her. It had not happened again on any morning when she arrived early enough to catch it. She had begun, instead, to arrive at her ordinary hour with a punctuality that her colleagues had noticed and teased her for, because arriving late enough felt, in a logic she had never spoken aloud until now, like a way of not knowing.

Mira asked her, gently, why she had closed it without reporting it, and Ottoline looked at Dunmore before she answered, as if to confirm that the answer would be received in the spirit in which Dunmore had promised it would be. Because a login was a person's word in the system, she said. Because to report that her login had been used in her absence was to invite an inquiry that would begin, whatever its intentions, by suspecting her of having shared it, and she had never shared it, and she could not prove that she had never shared it, and the floor was full of people who had watched colleagues ruined by accusations they could not disprove because the accusation concerned an absence, a thing not done, a care not taken. She had chosen silence the way a person steps around a hole in a familiar road, not from wickedness and not even, she insisted, from fear for herself alone, but from a knowledge of how the building metabolized suspicion. Dunmore, when Ottoline had finished, did not comment on the choice. She opened the login register she had brought with her, a plain, bound record kept by the terminal system and countersigned weekly by the floor supervisor, and she turned it to the relevant pages and laid it open where all three of them could see it. The pattern was there. On eleven mornings across the period in question, Ottoline's login had opened her system before her recorded arrival, in each case by an interval of between twenty and forty minutes, in each case closing again or settling into her queue before she reached her desk. Eleven mornings. Dunmore had checked the register against the duty rosters and against the building's entry book, and the register and the rosters agreed with Ottoline's account in every particular that could be checked. Someone had used her login, in her place, at her terminal, with her habits, on eleven mornings, and the third terminal from the window, whose chair was kept pushed in with a neatness the other chairs did not share, stood at the center of the pattern like a knot at the center of a net. Mira felt the cold of the morning reads still living in her forearms as she looked at the register entries, and she was careful, in that moment, to keep the two kinds of knowledge separate in her mind, the residue her gift had given her, which named no one, and the record Dunmore's method had given her, which named a login but not the hands that had used it. Between those two knowledges lay the whole of the case, and the discipline of the investigation, as Dunmore had framed it on the first morning, was to refuse to pretend that the gap between them had already been crossed.

Vera Kestrel had managed requisitions in Carrowmere for longer than most of the floor had worked in the building, and she had agreed to receive the inquiry's questions in her office at the hour when the floor below was at its loudest, so that the sound of ordinary work came up through the boards beneath her visitor's chair like the sound of a river under ice. She did not dislike Mira, whom she had never met before that afternoon, and she did not fear Dunmore, whose method she privately considered the only honest thing to have entered her floor in a decade. What she opposed, and she had prepared her opposition with the care of a woman who knew that unprepared opposition was merely noise, was the shape the inquiry was beginning to take in the minds of the people it touched. She had watched it happen across two days. Clerks who had processed ten thousand forms in good faith were going home and lying awake over individual forms they could not remember. A young woman in the stamp room had wept at her bench, not because she had been accused of anything, but because a supervisor had asked her, kindly and without any intention of cruelty, whether she had ever passed a form she had not fully checked, and the question had landed in a mind trained since its first week in the building to understand that a form unchecked was a form betrayed. Kestrel had built that training herself, over years, with her own hands and her own voice, in induction sessions and bench side corrections and the thousand small reinforcements by which an institution teaches its people what to notice and what to let pass. Never hold up the traffic for a question the traffic cannot answer. Never send a form back for a doubt you cannot name in a field. Never imagine that origination is your concern when origination has been placed, by warrant and by design, in other hands. She had taught processing clerks, with pride and with complete sincerity, never to ask the questions this inquiry now required them to have asked, and she sat in her office with the noise of her floor rising through the floorboards and understood, with a clarity that gave her no comfort at all, that she was watching her own teaching being entered as evidence against the people she had taught. On that point, and she knew it as she prepared to say it to the investigator's face, she was not defending herself. She was right. The system had trained these people never to ask questions, and it was frightened clerks, not architects, who were paying for the training. She intended to say so, plainly, and to go on saying so in every room where the inquiry's method was discussed, however the inquiry itself concluded.

Mira heard Kestrel out in that office without interrupting, and the hearing changed nothing about the paper and a great deal about the weight Mira assigned to the human cost of following it. She repeated Kestrel's point back to her, not as a concession extracted under pressure but as a fact she intended to carry into every interview that followed, that processing clerks were being frightened for a system that had trained them never to ask questions, and that any account of the chain which ended by blaming the trained for their training would be an account she would not sign. Kestrel, who had expected either dismissal or the soft, managerial agreement that costs the agreer nothing, looked at her for a long moment and then nodded, once, in the manner of a person who has been heard accurately and does not intend to pretend otherwise. The opposition did not end the inquiry. It disciplined it. Mira descended to the floor with Kestrel's sentence traveling with her like a second set of instructions laid over Dunmore's method, and when she and Dunmore arranged the order of the interviews that evening, they placed the question of authority, and not the question of conduct, at the head of every conversation they intended to hold. Who could raise. Who could only process. The paper would answer those questions before any person was asked to answer for themselves, and if the paper acquitted the frightened, then the paper, and not anyone's mercy, would be what acquitted them. It was, Mira thought, the least that an investigation owed to people who had been taught obedience as a virtue and were now to be examined on the consequences of having learned the lesson well.

## The Collapse

The clerical ring was a beautiful theory, and Mira had watched it assemble itself in her own mind with the ease that should have warned her against it. Eleven people. One floor. One traced number passing from hand to hand among them like a coin worked smooth by circulation. A login used by someone other than its owner on eleven mornings, a number that rhymed with the count of the names until the rhyme itself began to feel like evidence. A senior officer retired and conveniently absent. A bonded assessor with a guilty drawer and a year of rehearsed confession. A supervisor whose stamp appeared everywhere, a stamp room keeper with access to superseded seals, a night clerk who worked while the building slept, a filing clerk who could make a copy vanish into the cabinets as easily as another person might lose a coin in deep water. Arranged in one order, the eleven formed a conspiracy of the plausible, each member supplying the capability the others lacked, processing and filing and stamping and supervising woven together into a net fine enough to catch and carry a form that nobody had raised. Arranged in another order, they formed a market, favors traded across benches over months. Arranged in a third, they formed a cell, disciplined and patient, of the kind that the residue at the third terminal seemed, to a mind already furnished with the theory, almost to describe. Mira had not believed the theory outright. She was too experienced to believe any theory outright in the first days of an inquiry. But she had felt its pull, the deep institutional comfort of a ring, because a ring could be arrested, and a ring had edges, and a ring explained a distributed act without requiring anyone to confront the possibility that the distribution itself was the disguise. Dunmore, who had felt no such comfort, killed the theory with paper, in the records room, on the third day, without raising her voice.

She did it with the authority warrants, which she had saved for last in her stated method precisely because they were the documents most likely to be misread if they were read in haste. A warrant was not a job description. It was not a summary of what a person did all day, or what their supervisor believed they could do, or what they had once been permitted to do in an emergency and had gone on doing by custom after the emergency had passed. A warrant was a grant, bounded in time and class and scope, and where custom and warrant disagreed, the warrant was the fact and the custom was merely a habit that had never been tested. Dunmore laid the eleven warrants out in a row across the records room table, in the order of the chain table, and beneath each warrant she laid the register entries and stamp records that had placed its holder near the traced number, and she went through them one by one with a pencil in her hand, marking nothing, because the documents needed no marks to say what they said. Mave Ottoline, process only, her grant explicit and her entire career conducted within it. Corin Vell, process only, his checking authority broad and his originating authority nonexistent. Tomasin Hale, process only, her custody of stamps and seals a custody of instruments, not a power to set them in lawful motion on a form of her own creation. Selin Arvad, process only, her night terminal configured by the warrant's own terms to refuse an origination entry. Bram Kessler, process only, his domain the afterlife of forms, copies and filings, a domain in which a form could be hidden but in which it could not be born. Tessa Vane, process only, her threshold authority to query sums stated, in the warrant's own plain language, to confer no power of origination. Ferris Nal, process only, a carrier and relief clerk whose grant concerned movement between offices and nothing else. Odette Sarr, process only, her personnel annex a separate stream whose register could receive a raised form and could not generate one. Cassian Dray, process only, and here Dunmore slowed, because Dray was the name the ring theory had leaned on hardest, the supervisor whose access was widest and whose stamp was commonest, and his warrant, read whole and read carefully, granted supervision in terms so generous that a hasty reader might mistake them for origination until the final clause, which withheld raising authority in words that admitted no ambiguity at all. Nine warrants. Nine people. Nine careers spent on one side of the line that Dunmore had drawn on the first morning between handling a thing and bringing it into being.

A ring of processors could not have originated anything. Mira said it aloud when Dunmore had finished, because a conclusion of that weight needed to be spoken in the room where it had been earned, and Dunmore nodded and began, methodically, to tie the warrants back into their bundle. A ring of processors could delay a form, could misroute it, could stamp it without checking it, could lose it, copy it, bury it, or pass it hand to hand in the dark. A ring of processors could protect a form, once it existed, with a loyalty or a corruption that an investigation might spend months unraveling. What a ring of processors could not do, by any combination of their granted powers, was raise one. Origination was not a sum that processing could reach by addition. Nine zeros of authority did not add up to an authority. The theory had required the eleven to be a single organism, pooling their access into a capability none of them held, and the warrants showed that access did not pool in that way, that the system, whatever its failures, had kept the one distinction that mattered written down in documents that any clerk could have consulted and that nobody, until Dunmore, had thought to consult against the names. The collapse, when it came, was total and oddly quiet. No one had been exonerated of everything. Ottoline's silence about her login remained a fact with consequences. Dall's certification without examination remained a fact with consequences. Dray's supervision, Hale's custody, Arvad's night hours all remained facts, available to any future account that needed them. But the ring, as an explanation of where the traced form had come from, was dead, and its death cleared the ground with the abruptness of a building demolished overnight, leaving Mira and Dunmore standing in an open space with a much smaller and much harder problem in the middle of it.

Two people on the list could raise. That was what remained. Henrik Dall, whose authority was real but narrow, bonded goods and their movement, a class adjacent to the traced form's class and not identical with it, so that a raising by Dall would itself have been an act outside his warrant, visible as such to anyone who checked the warrant against the form, and Dall, whatever else his year of waiting proved, had shown them a private note whose whole character was that of a man documenting an irregularity he feared rather than engineering one he controlled. And Gregor Halvane, senior, broad in his grants, complete in his access, retired and absent and therefore, Mira noted with a distaste for her own readiness, conveniently shaped to receive a suspicion that could not easily be put to him in person. Attention narrowed, as Dunmore put it while she retied the cord, to the gap between processing and raising, and the gap, examined closely, was stranger than the ring had ever been. Forms had plainly been raised. The traced form existed, registered and stamped and moved through the chain with every appearance of legitimacy, and it was not, so far as the paper showed, an isolated birth. The early logins pointed to a practice. Dall's irregular instruction pointed to a practice. The patience at the third terminal pointed to a practice sustained over time by someone at home in the building's rhythms. Yet the authority to raise such forms existed on paper for two people, one of them retired and absent, the other holding the wrong class of grant, and the signature on the traced form, examined by four departments and denied honestly by all of them, belonged to nobody. Mira wrote that sentence in her notebook exactly as it had first been given to her, because the collapse had not dissolved it and she did not want the new shape of the inquiry to blur the old, hard fact at its center. [The signature belongs to nobody](/2026/11/25/nightjar-unowned-signature-025/). A form had been raised under authority that existed on paper for two people, and signed in a hand that was neither of theirs and no one else's that any record would support, and the system that should have caught the impossibility at its first stamp had instead carried the form from bench to bench with the docile efficiency that Vera Kestrel had taught it, asking nothing, because asking had never been its business.


The interviews began the following morning, and they began, by design, with the three people whose paper was clearest, because Mira and Dunmore had agreed in the wake of Kestrel's opposition that the first conversations should test the method rather than the people. If the warrants were right, then the processors had nothing to answer for on the question of origination, and an interview that opened with authority rather than conduct would tell the floor, faster than any reassurance, what kind of inquiry this was going to be. They saw Mave Ottoline first, in the intake office with the door open, at her own request, and they asked her about the eleven mornings and about nothing else until she had given her account of them in full, the early arrival, the open system, the closed door of her own silence afterward. She answered steadily. She cried once, briefly and without ceremony, when Dunmore showed her the register entries and she understood that the pattern had been larger than the single morning she had witnessed, eleven mornings rather than one, a use of her name in the system so regular that it had acquired the shape of a habit belonging to someone else. She asked whether she was believed, and Mira told her that the register believed her, which was a stronger thing than either of them could offer on their own authority, and Ottoline received that answer with the gravity of a person who had waited weeks to hear the record speak in her defense. They saw Corin Vell second, at his checking bench before the floor filled, and Vell, who returned other people's forms for missing fields as other people breathed, answered their questions about his stamps and his returns with a precision that bordered on relief, as if the inquiry's interest in the exact boundaries of his authority were the first recognition his exactness had ever received from the institution that employed it. He had never raised a form. He had never been asked to raise a form. He had once, two years earlier, refused to check a form whose raising entry he could not read, and he had been overruled by a supervisor whose name he gave them without hesitation, and the form had gone through, and he had entered his refusal in the returns ledger in his narrow hand, where Dunmore found it that same afternoon, exactly where he had said it would be. They saw Tomasin Hale third, in the stamp room among the tins and presses of her custody, and Hale walked them through the life of a stamp as a shepherd might walk a visitor through the life of a flock, which stamps were live, which were superseded, which were awaiting certified destruction, and she opened the locked drawer without being asked and counted the superseded set aloud, complete, every instrument accounted for, her custody as clean as her warrant was clear. None of the three had originated anything. All three, in their different ways, had left the record in better order than they had found it. The method held. The paper held. Mira came out of the stamp room into the noise of the full floor with a confidence in the ground beneath the inquiry that she had not possessed since the traced number first came into her hands.

Within an hour of the third interview, the floor changed its weather, and it changed in a way that no register could have predicted and that Mira's gift, arriving at the benches in the early afternoon, registered before her reason had caught up with it. The residue of the place had sharpened. The ordinary topsoil of routine was still there, patience and boredom and the small satisfactions of cleared queues, but over it lay a fresh, uniform layer of held breath, of conversations stopped at her approach and resumed in a lower register behind her, of a wariness so evenly distributed across people who had no obvious reason to share it that its evenness was itself the information. The interviews had been noticed. Of course they had been noticed. An investigator on a requisition floor was as inconspicuous as a bell in a library, and Mira had never imagined otherwise. What she had not anticipated, and what Dunmore confirmed for her from three separate conversations in the space of that same hour, was the form the floor's response had taken. One after another, the people the inquiry approached for the next round of conversations declined to answer further. The phrasing was identical. Not similar, not variations on a theme shaped by a common fear, but identical, word for word, a formula delivered by a filing clerk and a deputy supervisor and a night intake officer with the same cadence and the same careful neutrality, as if each of them were reading from a card held just below Mira's line of sight. On advice, they would not be answering further questions at this stage. They wished to cooperate. Their adviser had recommended that cooperation take written form. They had nothing further to add at this stage. At this stage, repeated by mouths that had never, to Mira's knowledge, spoken to one another outside the traffic of the floor, in the accents of four different upbringings, with the same pause before the same phrase.

Mira counted them in the records room with the door closed, while Dunmore stood at the window with her arms folded and watched the floor below settle into its new and careful quiet. Seven people had used the formula by the end of that hour. Not the three who had been interviewed, whose accounts stood complete and unretracted in Dunmore's notes. Seven others, drawn from the eleven and from the small penumbra of names the stamp records had placed adjacent to the eleven, supervisors and relief clerks and one registry officer whose name had appeared only twice in the paper and whom Mira had not yet decided whether to question at all. Seven people, declining in identical language, on identical advice, within an hour of the moment when the inquiry's direction must have become clear to anyone watching it. A shared silence of that precision was not a coincidence and it was not a culture. Cultures produced variations. Fear produced variations, eloquent ones, clumsy ones, silences shaped like the people who kept them. This silence had been drafted. Mira said so, and Dunmore, who distrusted any conclusion that arrived ahead of its documents, surprised her by agreeing without qualification, because Dunmore had spent the hour doing with the formula what she did with every other artifact of the case, comparing it instance by instance, and the comparison admitted only one reading. The seven core figures shared one lawyer. One adviser had spoken to seven people, or to people who spoke for seven people, in the space between the third interview and the first refusal, and had given them a single form of words and a single instruction, and the speed of it told its own story about the channels that already existed among them, channels that did not run through benches or trays or any traffic the registers recorded. The ring was dead. The warrants had killed it, and Mira did not mourn it and would not resurrect it. But something organized lived in the space the ring had vacated, something that could move advice to seven mouths in an hour, something that had been watching the inquiry closely enough to know the hour in which to move. Coordination was not origination. Mira held the distinction up in her mind the way Dunmore had taught her to hold all distinctions in this case, firmly and in the open. The shared lawyer did not prove that the seven had raised the form, and it did not restore the nine processors to a capability their warrants denied them. It proved reach. It proved readiness. It proved that whoever had taught seven people a single sentence in an hour had expected, long before that hour arrived, that such teaching might one day be necessary.

That evening, in the records room, with the warrants tied and the registers closed and the chain table lying between them annotated now in Dunmore's hand with the single word that the day had earned, Mira began to speak about time. Every instrument the inquiry had used so far had been sequential. Registers read in order. Interviews held in order. Questions asked in one room, answered, or refused, in the next. Sequential work had virtues, patience chief among them, and the case owed its solid ground to sequence. But sequence had a price that the afternoon had just itemized for them. Each step the inquiry took announced the next. Each interview taught the watched how the watching worked, and gave the organized among them an hour, and then a day, and then a week, in which to arrange their affairs, align their accounts, move their paper, and rehearse their silences. Against an opponent who could put one sentence into seven mouths in an hour, a sequential inquiry was not an investigation. It was a notification service, informing the chain, link by link, of the order in which it would be visited. Dunmore listened to the whole of this without interrupting, and when Mira had finished, she untied the warrant bundle again, not to check the warrants, which neither of them doubted any longer, but to lay a map of the Accord's seats beneath them, the paper geography of the institution, Carrowmere at its center and the other seats marked in the cartographer's careful hand. The stamp records had never confined the traced number's family to one city. Forms in its class moved between seats as a matter of routine, and the processing stamps in Dunmore's sample bore the marks of three other floors besides their own, each with its own registers, its own terminal bank, its own third chair from its own window, for all either of them knew. If the practice the early logins described was a practice, it would not respect the boundary of a single floor, and an inquiry that finished its work in Carrowmere and then traveled, at the speed of ordinary correspondence, to the next seat, would arrive everywhere second, behind the news of its own coming.

The plan formed between them over that map, slowly at first and then with the gathering speed of a shape that had been waiting for its components to be named. Four cities. One morning. The same hour in each, fixed not by the clock on any one wall but by the opening of the requisition floors, so that no seat would have the advantage of a warning carried by the difference in their mornings. In Carrowmere, the floor they knew, the terminal bank and the stamp room and the supervisor's cabinet. In Velstrand, where the registry stamps in the sample were heaviest and where a sister floor handled the class of forms that traveled farthest. In Kelmarsh, the smallest of the four, whose night intake records had twice intersected with Selin Arvad's in ways that might mean nothing and could no longer be assumed to mean nothing. In Tarnwick, where Gregor Halvane had taken his liaison posting before his retirement, and where his broad warrant had last been exercised in the ordinary course of his duties. Four teams, briefed from the same paper, asking the same first question in the same words, the question of authority before the question of conduct, warrants before accounts, method before conclusions. No letters ahead. No courtesy notifications traveling the ordinary channels, because the ordinary channels were precisely what the afternoon had shown to be compromised by speed. The approaches would be simultaneous or they would be useless, and their simultaneity would have to be built out of sealed instructions and trusted hands and a coordination so quiet that the building itself, which had ears in every corridor, would learn of it only when the four doors opened at once. Mira looked at the map for a long time after the shape of the thing had settled, at the four seats and the distances between them, at the chain that the plan proposed to take not link by link but all at once, in a single morning's grip. Somewhere in the gap between processing and raising, in the space where a signature could belong to nobody and a login could open itself in an empty room, the chain had its living center, and it had just demonstrated, with seven identical sentences, how quickly it could close its ranks when it felt a hand upon it. The answer to a chain that closed in an hour was not a faster hand. It was four hands, in four cities, closing at the same hour, on the same morning, before the news of any one of them could reach the others. She reached for her notebook and began, with Dunmore beside her and the cold of the day's reads still deep in her forearms, to write the plan down in the order in which it would have to be built.

## Pressure

She made the decision before the sweep orders were sealed, and she made it where Leyden could watch her make it.

The list lay on his desk in two columns. Eleven names. Nine of them she had already set aside in her own mind, not cleared, but set aside, because the authority audit had shown what it had shown and it would not bend. Nine people who could process a requisition once it existed and could not, by any route the Accord allowed, bring one into existence. Their hands could move a form from tray to tray, from desk to desk, from floor to floor. Their hands could not raise it. That distinction had carried the whole investigation, and she trusted it the way she trusted a floor to hold her weight, because she had tested it at every step.

Two names on the list belonged to people who could raise, after a fashion. Henrik Dall was one of them, and it did not clear him. His grant covered bonded goods and their movement, a class that sat beside the traced form's class and not inside it, so a raising in the traced class would have stood outside his warrant and shown as such to anyone who checked. The other name was a retired senior officer with broad grants, absent in another city, shaped conveniently for a suspicion that could not be put to him in person. Neither name explained a signature that belonged to nobody.

His name sat below the line she had drawn, in the margin she had left for names that did not fit the mechanism but touched it. His desk sat at the junction. That was the plain fact of the building, not a suspicion. Every requisition that moved between the lower floors and the upper registry passed the corner where Dall worked, not because he controlled it, but because the floor had been built that way long before either of them had come to Carrowmere. Trays waited there when the lifts were slow. A form could rest on his desk for an hour without anyone thinking the rest meant anything. A form could also be read there, copied there, or sent onward with a second paper clipped behind it that had not been there when it arrived. She had no proof that any of that had happened. She had a desk read taken at dawn after a floor read that had already spent more of her than she had planned to spend, and what the desk had given her was relief.

Relief, and a year of waiting folded under it.

She had stood with her hand on the edge of that desk and felt the residue settle into her the way weight always did, not as a picture and not as a voice, but as an affect that became hers for as long as it took her body to pay for it. The wood had held patience worn thin. It had held the particular tiredness of a man who had watched something go wrong in small increments and had kept his objection in the correct channel and had been told, in the correct language, that the channel would carry it. It had held the loosening that comes when a person who has waited for an inquiry finally hears the inquiry begin. Relief. She had taken her hand away and known that relief could belong to an innocent man who wanted the chain exposed, and it could belong just as easily to a guilty man who believed the inquiry would miss him or who wanted a rival part of the chain cut away. Weight did not identify a person. Weight did not detect truth. It gave her what the place had kept, and the place had kept relief, and relief explained nothing by itself.

Leyden read the margin name and looked up at her.

She told him the evidence was thin. She used that word, thin, because any other word would have dressed it. She told him she could not prove which kind of relief she had read. She told him the desk sat at the one junction the chain must pass, whether its keeper helped it pass or merely watched it pass a hundred times without understanding what he watched. She told him that a sweep that left that desk untouched would leave a hole in the middle of the floor, and anything the chain had left behind would walk out through that hole while the Office was congratulating itself on the doors it had locked. She told him that if Dall was innocent, the arrest would cost him a morning and cost her more than a morning, and she would carry both costs without asking to set either down. She told him that if Dall was part of it and they left him in place, the sting would close on clerks and miss the junction entirely.

Leyden did not argue. He did not nod either. He turned the list slightly toward himself and studied the margin as if the handwriting might change under attention.

She picked up the pen herself. She wrote Dall's name into the sweep list in her own hand, below the retired officer's name and above the clerks who would be held in their rooms for questioning, and she initialed the addition so that no one later could say the name had drifted there by clerical error. It was her decision. She wanted the paper to say so. Leyden witnessed the initial without comment and sealed the orders with the rest, and the seal made the thin evidence into an instruction that other people would now act on in four cities at the same hour.

By the time she reached the requisition floor her reserve was already reduced, and she felt the reduction in specific, unglamorous ways that had nothing to do with error. The dawn floor read had taken the first layer, the cold residue of the empty registry before the staff arrived, when she had put her palm flat to the boards beside the night cabinet and let the room give her the accumulated weight of months of forms passing in the dark hours when the early logins were used. That read had cost her the briskness she usually carried into an operation. The desk read on Dall's junction had taken the second layer. What remained was enough. She had told Leyden it was enough and she had been accurate, but enough was not full, and the difference showed in the half second it took her eyes to settle when she turned her head quickly, in the care she had to take on the stairs, in the way the noise of the floor arrived a fraction late, as if her body were processing the room through cloth. She did not mistake the slowness for doubt. She knew its source and its price, and she set her feet carefully and kept her hand off the rail because the rail would have things to tell her that she could not afford to hear that morning.

The sweep began without a signal anyone outside the Office would have recognized. In Carrowmere the doors of the requisition floor opened at the usual minute and the clerks came in with their coats and their tea, and the officers placed the night before as temporary registry staff stood up from desks they had pretended to work at for two days and showed their authority in the quiet voice the Office preferred. In the other three cities the same thing happened on floors she would never see, and she held those floors in her mind only as reports that would come later. Her junction was the corner desk where Henrik Dall was removing his coat with the unhurried movements of a man who had done it ten thousand times.

She watched his face when the officer reached him. She made herself watch it, because she had written the name and she owed the name its witnessing. Dall looked up with mild annoyance first, the annoyance of a man whose tray order has been interrupted, and then the annoyance gave way to comprehension, and comprehension gave way to something she could not use. He did not blanch. He did not glance toward a drawer or a door. He stood when he was told to stand and he placed his hands where he was told to place them and his eyes found her across the floor, and in them she saw recognition and a question, and she could not answer the question because she did not know its answer herself. He was taken toward the side room with the others, walking under his own power, his coat still over his arm because no one had thought to take it from him, and she turned away only when the door closed behind him, because the floor was not finished with her.

The junction office was the small room behind Dall's desk where the senior clerks kept the day books, the bound volumes in which the floor recorded by hand what the system recorded by stamp, a redundancy older than anyone working that morning and therefore the one record the chain had been forced to respect. A stamp could be explained. The day book, kept in ink in a rotating hand, was harder to bend after the fact, and the Office wanted that morning's book and the two before it. Reni Vosk went in first because Reni had the warrant list and the quickest eye for shelf order, and Mira followed with the second officer, and Joren Dray was already inside.

She registered him in pieces, the way the reduced reserve forced her to register everything that morning, a fraction behind the event. Dray at the stove. The stove brazier open, its small fire lit for the morning chill as it always was, a convenience no one had thought to remove from a room full of paper because it had been there longer than the paper. Dray with the day book in both hands, not the current book but the middle one, the one whose dates covered the borrowed logins she had traced to Mave Ottoline's name. Dray with his face set in the expression of a man who has rehearsed a destruction and now finds the rehearsal shorter than he expected. He was the floor's deputy supervisor, the widest reaching of the nine who could only process, keeper of the junction room and its day books, and he had used the brazier's ordinary presence the way the chain had used every ordinary presence in the building, as cover that did not look like cover.

Reni spoke first. She told him to put the book down. Her voice stayed level and she held the warrant where he could see it, and for a moment the room balanced on the possibility that he would obey, that the authority in her hand would weigh more than the fire in his. Dray looked at the warrant. He looked at the brazier. He moved.

The movement was not skilled. It was committed, which was worse. He swung the book toward the open stove as a man swings a thing he wants gone, and Reni went to meet the book rather than the man, because the book was the mission and her training and her instinct agreed on that order. She got her left hand to the binding. Dray kept his grip on the other end and pulled, and the pull turned them both, and the book hung between them over the brazier with its pages stirring in the heat. Mira saw Reni's right hand come up to secure the spine and saw it fail to close with the strength the grip required, and she understood the failure at once and without surprise. The stitched forearm from the previous case, healed on the surface and sound for ordinary work, had a limit that ordinary work rarely found. Sustained grip against a resisting weight found it. Reni's fingers held and then loosened by degrees she was visibly fighting, and the cost of the fight showed in her shoulder and her stance, in the way she shifted her weight to compensate with her whole body for what one forearm could not give her. She did not let go. She paid for not letting go in the coin of position, step by step, as Dray dragged the contest closer to the fire.

Mira moved. She did not run. The room was too small for running and running would have turned control into collision. She stepped inside Dray's reach on the side away from the stove and took his nearer wrist with both hands and turned it, not with force against his strength but with the angle the Office taught, the angle that made a wrist into a lever and a lever into an instruction the body obeyed before the mind had finished deciding whether to obey it. He resisted. Men in the grip of a committed act usually did, and his resistance had the clumsy power of a clerk who had never been trained to fight and therefore fought with everything at once. She gave him the step his resistance demanded and used the giving of it, pivoting so that his own momentum carried his shoulder past her, and she followed the shoulder down with her forearm across the back of it and her weight, her ordinary bodily weight, settled through the hold in the way she had been taught when she was younger and quicker and fuller in reserve than she was that morning. It cost her. The reduced state took its payment in the effort of precision. Where a full reserve would have let her feel the room and the man and the distances all at once, the reduced state made her choose, moment by moment, what to attend to, and she chose the wrist and the shoulder and the distance to the brazier, and let everything else go unheard. The stove radiated against her leg. She noted the heat as information and moved her leg by the width of a hand.

Dray tried to turn back toward the fire. He still held the book in his far hand, stretched now at an angle that weakened him, and Reni, freed of half the pull, hauled backward with her good arm wrapped through the binding and her compromised hand pressed flat to the cover, holding by pressure what she could not hold by grip. The pages had begun to darken at one corner. A thin line of scorch ate the edge of the cover where it had hung lowest over the brazier, and the smell of hot leather and singed paper filled the small room with an acrid insistence that made Mira's eyes water, which she resented, briefly and uselessly, because her eyes were needed. She shortened her hold. She brought Dray's wrist across his body and set her knee behind his knee, not as a strike but as a presence, a fact his leg would have to reckon with, and when he shifted his weight to free the wrist she let the shift happen and guided it, and the knee's presence turned the shift into a descent. He went down on one knee on the office boards with a sound that was mostly breath. The book came free in the same movement, not torn from him but released, because a man going down opens his hands to catch himself unless he has trained the reflex out, and Dray had never trained anything out.

Reni took the book clear. She cradled it against her body with her good arm and turned her back to the stove in the same motion, shielding it with herself, and her breathing was audible and her face was set and she did not look at her forearm, which Mira noted and approved of in the part of her mind that kept working while the rest of her finished the hold. The second officer had come in behind them and now took Dray's other arm, and between them they brought him fully under control without haste, wrist to wrist, in the restraints the Office used for men it wanted talking. Nobody had struck him. Nobody had needed to. He knelt on the boards of the room where he had worked for years and looked at the brazier as if it had promised him something and withdrawn the promise, and his breathing slowed by stages, and when he spoke it was to ask whether the book had burned, which the second officer did not answer.

Mira let go last. Her hands, when she opened them, took a moment to open fully, and she recognized the delay as the reduction again, the body spending carefully after spending fast. She stood up and the standing cost her a hand's breadth of steadiness that she concealed by the simple act of not moving until it returned. She looked at the book in Reni's arms. The scorch marked one corner of the cover and the edges of perhaps a dozen pages beneath it, darkened and curled but legible where she could see them, the ink intact under the singeing. Saved, then. Scorched at one corner and saved. She told Reni to log it exactly that way, scorched at one corner, condition otherwise sound, and Reni nodded and carried it out past Dall's empty desk without looking toward the side room where Dall sat under arrest on Mira's initialed instruction.

The rest of the floor took longer and made less noise. One room held the morning's violence and the other rooms held men and women sitting very still at their desks while officers worked through drawers and trays and cabinets in an order written down the night before. Two more clerks on her floor were arrested without resistance, both of them processors, both named in the chain by papers rather than by authority, and she watched each arrest with the same attention she had given Dall's. A drawer in the junction office, not Dall's desk but the shared cabinet beside it, held a bundle of early hour slips in a hand she recognized from the specimen books of the previous case, the hand that belonged to nobody, practicing its loops on requisition paper, and she stood over that drawer for a long moment, because the practice slips settled something in the architecture of the case even as they settled nothing about a name. She had the drawer sealed as it stood.

By midday the floor was secured. The trays sat where they had stopped. The lifts ran for officers only. The day books rested in sealed boxes in the registry with their condition noted, and the staff who had not been arrested stood in the corridor in the particular silence of people recalculating their understanding of their own workplace, and did not meet her eyes or met them too deliberately. The telephone reports came in the order the cities had been listed in the sealed orders, each voice flattened by the line into the same official calm. Two arrests in the second city, taken at their desks. One arrest in the third city, a woman who had kept a second set of slips and surrendered them from her own bag when the officers entered, as if she had been waiting to be relieved of them. One arrest in the fourth city, at a sub registry desk, the man found with his coat on and a packed case beneath the desk. With Dray and the two processors from her own floor the count stood at seven. Seven arrests inside Accord buildings in one morning, across four cities, reported by telephone and by runner, the runner arriving last with the written confirmations in a sealed pouch, and she signed for the pouch with a hand that had recovered its steadiness.

## The Silence

They gave her the small room off the registry because she asked for ten minutes and Leyden, without looking at her directly, had said to give her twenty. The room had no desk work in it. It held a chair, a narrow table, a window that faced the inner court, and the kind of quiet that a building produces in the middle of a working day when everyone beyond the door has agreed, without speaking of it, to lower their voices near the place where the arrests were being written up.

She sat down because standing had a price that morning and she was learning, again, to spend where spending bought something. Through the wall she could hear the soft sounds of the floor reordering itself, a tray set down too carefully, a voice beginning a sentence and deciding against it. The day book with the scorched corner rested in its sealed box two rooms away. She did not need it in her hands. She had the shape of the case in her mind, and for the first time since dawn she let the shape sit there without pushing it toward the next task.

The early login under Mave Ottoline's name. It came back to her the way it had come in the registry weeks of the investigation, a small fact with clean edges. Someone had entered the system before the floor opened, under a name that belonged to a woman whose own hours were regular to the point of legend, and had raised what the day would later process as if it had always existed. Mave had not done it. Mave's life, examined gently and then examined again, contained no room for it. But the name had been borrowed with knowledge, by someone who knew whose hours would make an early entry look like diligence rather than intrusion.

The nine who could only process. She saw them at their desks as she had seen them that morning, hands that moved paper onward. Trained hands. The chain had not needed them to know. It had needed them to be exactly what the Accord had made them, people for whom a form that existed was a form to be moved, and whose training had quietly removed the question of whether it should exist at all. Some of them had been arrested that morning for the shape of that obedience. Some of them would be released. The distinction would matter enormously to them and very little to the architecture that had used them.

The signature that belonged to nobody. The practice slips in the drawer beside Dall's desk. A hand rehearsed until it could sign authorizations in a script that no living clerk owned, so that the paper trail, followed honestly to its end, arrived at a person who did not exist.

And Dall, relieved rather than afraid. The desk had told her that before the sweep told her anything, and the telling had not changed when she watched him stand and place his hands where he was told. A guilty man at the junction might have been relieved too. She held that alongside the other reading and refused, in the quiet of the small room, to let either one swallow the other yet.

She did not speak the conclusion. It was there, assembled in the order the evidence had built it, and she could feel the pressure of it the way she felt weight in a room, as a presence with a cost. Outside the window the court lay empty in the midday light. A clerk crossed it with a tray held high against his chest like a shield, not looking up at the registry windows. She watched him reach the far door and go in, and she stayed in the chair, and the quiet held, and the case held inside it, complete and unspoken.

## Close

The sting, when she reported it to Leyden that afternoon, was stated as completed, because it was. Seven arrests inside Accord buildings in one morning across four cities. No one killed. No one escaped between the sealing of the orders and the closing of the doors. The day books secured, the practice slips secured, the early hour records pulled from four registries and laid side by side on the registry table until the pattern they made could no longer be described as coincidence by anyone willing to use the word honestly.

She laid the chain out in the order it operated, and Leyden let her lay it without interruption, which was his way of marking that the work had earned its hearing. Forms were raised on borrowed early logins, in the hour before the floors opened, under names chosen for their regularity. The raising was done by hands with authority, or by hands using the one rehearsed signature that belonged to nobody, and once a form existed the chain no longer needed authority at all. It needed obedience, and the Accord supplied obedience in depth. Clerks who had never held raising authority processed what was put in front of them. They had been trained not to ask, not in the manner of a conspiracy, which would have required them to know, but in the manner of a well run floor, which required only that they trust the paper. The paper moved through the junction desks. The paper gathered its stamps. The paper arrived at its destination carrying the full appearance of an ordinary requisition, and by the time anyone outside the chain handled it, its origins had been laundered by the simple passage of honest hands.

That was the result, assembled. The chain was real. It was not a clerical drift and it was not a single clerk's invention. It was a method, repeatable and repeated, for bringing requisitions into existence outside the authority the Accord believed it had assigned, and for moving them through the Accord's own buildings on the Accord's own paper until they were indistinguishable from the work the buildings existed to do.

She reported the limit of the result in the same voice she had used for the result itself, because Leyden would have heard any change in it and because the limit was part of the result. The chain stopped two steps short of the Ninth Office. She had followed it link by link through logins and junctions and signature practice, through the two names with any raising grant and the nine who could only process, through four cities and one morning of arrests, and the trail, followed as far as paper and weight and testimony could follow it, ended at an authorizing step taken by someone using the chain from outside the Office's own floor. Two steps remained between the last link she could prove and the Office itself. Who took the step across those two, she could not say. Weight could not identify a person. It had never been able to, and she would not pretend on this afternoon of all afternoons that a residue had given her a name. Nobody arrested that morning had confessed to that step. Dray, who had fought for a day book with a fire lit beside him, asked for counsel and then answered questions about his own countersign and the day books for two hours without once placing any other person at the authorizing desk. The woman in the third city who had surrendered her slips from her own bag wept and named the clerk who had trained her and no one above him. The man with the packed case said he had packed it for a family visit and then said nothing further at all, which his counsel advised and which the Office could not compel him to improve upon. The authorizing step stood empty. The chain reached toward the Office and stopped, two steps short, with its last visible link held by people who could describe the paper they received and not the hand that had sent it.

Henrik Dall was released from arrest the same day, in the late afternoon, when his private note was brought up from the junction office and laid beside her desk read and the two agreed in every particular that could be checked. The note was not a diary. It was the record of a man who had used the correct channel the way the channel asked to be used. Dates on which a requisition had rested at his junction with a second paper behind it that had not been listed in the tray count. Dates on which he had queried the discrepancy in writing, through the registry, in the language the registry preferred. The replies he had received, correct in form and empty in substance, filed behind the queries in order. A final entry, from the week before the investigation reached his floor, recording that he had begun to keep the note at home because the office copy of one query could no longer be found in the file where he had placed it. He had waited a year with that note growing in a drawer, and the relief his desk had given her at dawn was the relief of a man whose waiting had finally been answered by someone arriving to ask. He was a witness. He was not a member of the chain. The release papers were drawn before the evening shift, and he was brought out of the side room with his coat still over his arm, and she stood where he would pass because she had written his name onto the list and she would not be elsewhere when the writing was undone.

He stopped in front of her. He looked older in the corridor light than he had looked at his desk, or perhaps the morning had simply spent him in the visible places. She told him the arrest had been her decision and her initial stood on the order, and that the evidence for it had been his desk's position and a reading she could not prove in either direction, and that she would make the same accounting to Leyden in writing before the day ended. She did not ask him to understand it. She did not dress it as necessity, although she believed it had been necessary, because a man arrested in front of his floor on thin evidence was owed the thinness stated plainly and not the comfort of her reasoning. Dall listened with the attention he had given forms for years. He said that he had wondered, sitting in the side room, whether the relief he had felt when the officers stood up would look to her like guilt, and that he had decided, somewhere in the second hour, that if she was the kind of officer who arrested on relief alone she would not have taken a year to arrive. He said it without warmth and without rancor, as a finding. Then he asked whether she wanted the note copied for the registry or the original, and she said the original, and he gave her the name of the drawer at his home where the copy lay, in case the original joined the office copy of his query in whatever place such papers went. He walked on down the corridor with his coat over his arm, and the clerk at the registry desk watched him pass and then looked at Mira and then looked down at the desk with a care that had not been there the day before, and in that small sequence of glances she read the first installment of the cost.

The cost was named in her report before the arrests were summarized, because she wanted it on the record ahead of the success. Seven Accord colleagues arrested, some of whom she had known for years. She had shared floors with two of them. She had eaten in the refectory at the long table with the woman from the third city on the one occasion their duties had brought them to the same building, years before, and had liked her. The success of the morning did not reduce that cost and she did not allow the report to arrange the sentences so that it seemed to. From this day, the Office treated her differently. She wrote that too, not as a complaint, which would have been out of place in a report, but as an operational fact that Leyden would need. It showed in small behavior first, the way a change in a building always showed small before it showed structural. A corridor that had always made room for her now required her to make her own room, not by obstruction but by the absence of the small yielding that colleagues perform without thinking. A desk clerk who had called her by name for six years addressed the air slightly to the left of her shoulder and called her by her title. An officer she had trained beside left the lift when she entered it, with his papers gathered too quickly, as if he had remembered a floor he needed, and perhaps he had. She recorded none of those moments as grievances. She recorded what they meant. The Office had watched her arrest its own, on evidence she herself described as thin in one case and on paper in the others, and the Office, which was a collection of people who processed what was put in front of them, had begun to process her differently.

Leyden kept his distance by procedure. Her report was received, logged and answered in the correct form within the correct interval, and the interview she would ordinarily have expected, the hour in his room with the chain drawn on the board and the next steps argued aloud, did not take place. His door, which stood open during the working afternoon as a matter of his own long habit, was closed when she passed it with the release accounting, and it remained closed when she passed it again an hour later, and a clerk with a tray waited outside it in the corridor rather than knocking, which told her the closed door was being respected as a signal by people who read signals for a living. She left the accounting with the clerk. She did not knock either.

Harrand kept his distance by candor, which was his different way and not a kinder one. He found her by the registry stairs as the building was emptying, and told her plainly that the rollup was good work and that good work of this kind made a floor quiet around the person who had done it, and that she should expect the quiet to last longer than she expected. He said the chain had been cut where the Office could reach it and that what lay two steps beyond the reach would now know the reach had been measured, and that this knowledge had its own consequences which no report could file away. He did not offer her the refectory. He did not offer her reassurance. He wished her a good evening with exact courtesy and went down the stairs ahead of her, and she let the distance between them grow to a full flight before she followed, because following closely would have asked him to shorten a distance he had chosen, and she had spent the day learning the price of overriding what people chose.

By the time she filed the last paper the sealed boxes had gone to the deep registry and the floor had been released back to its ordinary keepers for the morning. The chain was broken where she could break it. The arrests stood. The witness had gone home with his coat over his arm and his note in her evidence pouch. The authorizing step stood empty, two steps short of the Office, unclaimed by confession and unreached by weight, and the report said so in plain language under her name.

## The Notch

In the days after the sweep the building performed its normality with great care, and Mira performed hers alongside it, and between the two performances the one fact that had changed sat undisturbed, the way a notch cut into a beam sits undisturbed while the house goes on being lived in around it.

The signature sat in the Ninth Office's own requisition chain, two steps short of the Office itself.

She turned that sentence over in the course of ordinary work and found that it did not soften with handling. The chain was not a rumor from an outside floor or a method practiced in a distant registry that the Office could regard as another city's failure. It ran on Office paper. It borrowed the names of Office clerks in the Office's own early hours. It rehearsed its authorizing hand on slips kept in a cabinet beside an Office junction desk, and it moved through honest Office hands until honest hands had carried it almost home. Almost. Two steps remained, and the two steps were the whole of it now. Everything before them could be arrested, filed, released, reported. The steps themselves could only be stated.

She did not fill them with a face. That discipline, which had cost her Dall's morning and Dall's corridor, held here too, and held more easily for having been paid for once already. Weight had not named the authorizing hand. Testimony had not named it. Paper, which had named so much else with such patience, stopped at the same place paper had always been going to stop, at the edge of the step that someone had taken without leaving the form of it behind. She could describe the shape of the absence the way she could describe the shape of a room by the furniture arranged around a space no one used. A step existed. It was taken regularly. It was taken by someone positioned to use the chain from outside the Office's own floor while reaching into the heart of the Office's own process, and the position was all she had. A position was not a person. She kept it filed as a position, in the part of her mind where she kept things that would wait years if they had to, alongside Dall's relief, now resolved, and the memory of a packed case in a fourth city, unresolved and probably unresolvable, and the woman who had surrendered her slips as if surrendering a weight she had carried in her bag each morning like a second set of keys.

Leyden filed her report without annotation, which she had learned to read as his way of letting a record stand exactly as its writer had left it, neither endorsed nor softened. Harrand, passing her in the registry a week later, asked after the day books and nothing else, and she answered about the day books and nothing else, and the exchange had the clean efficiency of two people who had agreed where the floor between them now lay. The clerks who remained at their desks on the requisition floor did their work in the new quiet. The junction desk had a new keeper, a young woman transferred from the upper registry, who arranged her trays in a different order than Dall had used and who looked up, the first time Mira passed, with the frank curiosity of someone who had been told a story about a floor and was matching the story to the woman walking across it. Dall himself had been moved, at his own request, to a records post in another wing, where the paper was old and its origins were no longer anyone's urgent question. His note stayed in the evidence pouch in the deep registry. His copy stayed in the drawer he had named. She thought that arrangement suited the case, two copies of an honest record kept in two places, neither of them at a junction.

At night the case returned to her in its reduced form, stripped of the morning and the corridor and the reports, and what remained was the count of steps. One step from the last proven link to the authorizing hand. One step from the authorizing hand to the Office. Two steps short, and nobody could say who took the third.

The registry clerks sent up the inventory of the requisition floor at the close of the session week, as they sent it every week, a plain list of desks in use and desks vacated, lamps accounted for, chairs accounted for, the small furniture of a working floor rendered as numbers for a file no one expected to be read as anything but a file. She read it at her desk after the building had emptied, because the inventory was part of the case papers and she finished what was put in front of her, even now, especially now. Seven desks stood vacated by the arrests, their keepers in cells or in counsel rooms or in the first days of a suspension that would decide, slowly, what their obedience had made them. The floor keeper had not yet reassigned them. The floor keeper had not yet cleared them either, because clearance required an order and the order required a signature and the signature, for once, was being taken slowly. So the inventory recorded what anyone walking that floor after hours would have seen, and she saw it through the plain words of the list as clearly as she had seen anything in the case, and it stayed with her after she had filed the list and put out her own lamp and gone home through the darkened corridors of the building that now made its own room around her.

Seven empty desks on one floor with their lamps still on.

## Case File

### Q: How is a requisition raised and processed inside the Accord?

A requisition begins when a requesting desk completes the standard form in triplicate, stating the goods or services required, the file to which the cost will fall, and the name of the officer who will receive the item. The form passes first to the desk registrar, who checks the file reference and enters the request in the day book before any approval is sought. It then moves to the authorising officer for signature, and only a signature enrolled in the specimen book has effect. Once signed, the requisition travels to the requisition floor, where clerks price the request, assign a supplier from the standing lists, and record the commitment against the relevant allocation. A copy returns to the requesting desk, a copy remains with the floor, and the third copy enters the archive sequence. No stage may be skipped, and an unsigned or unrecorded requisition creates no obligation for the Accord.

### Q: What arrest powers does the Accord hold internally, and where do those powers end?

Within Accord premises and in matters touching Accord files, designated officers may arrest a member of staff, a contractor, or a visitor where there is recorded cause to believe an offence against the Accord has occurred. The arrest must be entered in the custody register without delay, the arrested person must be told the file reference under which they are held, and a senior officer unconnected with the case must review the detention within the prescribed interval. The power does not extend to private dwellings, to premises of a member administration, or to persons holding a current safe conduct, unless a separate warrant has been issued through the proper channel. It also confers no power to seize private property unconnected with the file. An arrest made outside these limits is void, the detained person must be released, and the arresting officer becomes subject to inquiry.

### Q: Why does simultaneous action across four cities present a distinct coordination problem?

Simultaneous action in four cities requires four separate desks to move at an agreed hour while each operates under local conditions that the others cannot observe directly. Messages between the cities travel at different speeds, clerks work to local rosters, and a delay in one place cannot be corrected from another without breaking the agreed sequence. The coordinating desk therefore issues a single timetable, names a single officer to declare the start, and requires each city to confirm readiness through two independent channels before the hour is fixed. Fallback times are agreed in advance, so that silence is read as postponement rather than permission to proceed alone. Records from all four cities are later collated into one file, with discrepancies in timing noted rather than reconciled after the fact. Without this discipline, an early move in one city warns subjects in the others and defeats the purpose of acting together.

### Q: What does shared counsel signify during an internal investigation?

When two or more persons under inquiry are represented by the same counsel, the investigating desk records the fact in the shared counsel register and treats the arrangement as a material circumstance. Shared representation suggests that the persons concerned have communicated about the inquiry, that their accounts may have been compared, and that advice given to one may shape the position of another. It does not establish guilt, collusion, or any improper act, and no adverse finding may rest on the arrangement alone. Its practical effect is procedural. Interviews are scheduled separately, questions are framed so that one person's answers are not disclosed to another through counsel, and any joint submission is marked as joint on the file. Investigators must also consider whether counsel faces a conflict if the interests of the persons represented begin to diverge, and record that consideration even where no conflict is found.

### Q: How is the Carrowmere requisition floor organised, and what work happens there?

The requisition floor at Carrowmere occupies a single long room divided by function rather than by rank. At the entry end, receiving clerks log incoming forms, check signatures against the specimen books, and return defective forms to the sending desk with the defect noted. The central tables handle pricing and supplier assignment, working from standing lists and recording each commitment against its allocation in ink. At the far end, dispatch clerks prepare the outward orders, seal them, and enter the dispatch in the floor register. A raised gallery allows the floor supervisor to observe the whole room, settle disputes between tables, and authorise urgent handling where the file justifies it. No money changes hands on the floor. Its product is paper: a complete, ordered record showing who asked, who approved, what was ordered, and when the order left the building.

### Q: What is the login register, and how are credentials kept in custody?

The login register records every occasion on which an officer signs into an Accord system, room, or strong store, noting the officer, the time, the purpose stated, and the credential presented. Credentials themselves, whether seals, keys, or countersigns, are held in individual custody and may not be lent, pooled, or left in a shared drawer. When an officer is absent, the credential is surrendered to the custodian against a receipt and reissued only to the named holder on return. A discrepancy between the register and the credential issue book is treated as a security matter, not a clerical error, and both books are impounded pending comparison. Retired credentials are cancelled in the presence of two witnesses, the cancellation is entered in the register, and the physical item is destroyed or defaced according to its kind. The register is open to auditors at any time without prior notice.

### Q: How are authority warrants issued, and by what process are they revoked?

An authority warrant is issued when a senior officer certifies, on a written application, that a specified act cannot lawfully be performed under standing powers alone. The application states the act, the file, the persons covered, and the period for which authority is sought. The issuing officer may grant a narrower warrant than the one requested, and the granted terms alone have effect. Each warrant receives a unique number, is entered in the warrant book before it is used, and must be produced on demand to any person directly affected by its exercise. Revocation may occur at any time, either because the purpose is complete, because circumstances have changed, or because the issuing officer loses confidence in the grounds. Revocation is recorded in the same book, notice is sent to every holder of a copy, and any act performed after revocation is treated as unauthorised from the outset.

### Q: What do processing stamps record, and how are their dies controlled?

A processing stamp records that a document passed a particular desk at a particular stage, and its impression carries the desk mark, the stage mark, and space for the clerk to add the date and initials by hand. The stamp is evidence of handling, not of approval, unless the stage mark is one reserved for decisions. Dies are numbered, issued to named clerks against signature, and counted at the close of each working day. A missing die suspends the work of its desk until the loss is reported, searched for, and recorded, because an uncontrolled die could manufacture a false history for any document. Worn dies are withdrawn and replaced under supervision, and the old die is defaced before disposal. Test impressions from a new die are filed so that later impressions can be compared where the authenticity of a stamp is questioned in proceedings or in an internal inquiry.

### Q: What is the day book, and what legal status does it hold?

The day book is the chronological record in which desks enter transactions, receipts, decisions, and movements of files as they occur during the working day. Entries are made in ink, in sequence, without gaps, and corrections are made by a further entry rather than by alteration of the original line. Because of this discipline, the day book is accepted in Accord proceedings as a primary record: where a later summary conflicts with a contemporaneous day book entry, the entry prevails unless it is shown to have been falsified. Clerks sign the book at the close of business, and the supervising officer countersigns to confirm that the day is complete. A missing day, an unsigned day, or a page removed from the binding must be reported the same evening. Copies made for working purposes carry no independent status and are marked as copies on every page.

### Q: How are simultaneous arrest warrants handled across member jurisdictions?

Where arrests must occur in several member jurisdictions at once, the coordinating office prepares a separate warrant for each jurisdiction, drawn in the form that jurisdiction requires and naming only the persons to be arrested within its territory. No warrant crosses a boundary, and an officer acting outside the issuing territory acts without authority. The warrants are held sealed until the coordinating office releases the agreed hour, and each local commander confirms receipt and readiness through the prescribed channel. If one jurisdiction cannot proceed, the coordinator decides whether the remaining arrests still serve their purpose or whether the whole action is postponed, and that decision is recorded with reasons. After execution, certified copies of each warrant and each custody record are gathered into the central file, so that a court or inquiry examining one arrest can see the full shape of the coordinated action without relying on recollection.

### Q: What interview rights do Accord staff hold when questioned in an inquiry?

A member of staff interviewed in an internal inquiry has the right to be told whether they are interviewed as a witness or as a person under suspicion, and that status must be restated if it changes during the interview. They may have a colleague or counsel present, may ask for a question to be repeated or clarified, and may decline to answer a question that would require them to disclose material covered by a separate duty of confidence, recording the ground for refusal. Interviews are noted contemporaneously, and the person interviewed receives the note to read, correct for accuracy, and sign, or to refuse to sign with reasons entered. No interview may continue beyond the prescribed length without a break and a senior officer's recorded approval. A person under suspicion must also be told, before questioning begins, what use may be made of their answers in later proceedings.

### Q: When may an officer be suspended pending inquiry, and what follows from suspension?

Suspension pending inquiry is a precaution, not a punishment, and may be imposed only where the officer's continued presence would risk evidence, influence witnesses, or repeat the conduct under examination. The suspending officer must record the ground relied on, and a general statement that suspension is routine is not sufficient. A suspended officer retains pay and standing unless a separate decision, made through the disciplinary channel, directs otherwise. During suspension the officer may not enter Accord premises except by appointment, may not access files or systems, and must surrender credentials to the custodian against a receipt. The inquiry must proceed without avoidable delay, and the suspension is reviewed at fixed intervals by an officer outside the investigating desk. If the inquiry clears the officer, the suspension is lifted in writing, access is restored, and the record of the period is confined to the inquiry file.

### Q: How does the shared counsel register work in practice?

The shared counsel register is maintained by the inquiries desk and lists every case in which one counsel acts for two or more persons connected with the same matter. An entry is made as soon as the arrangement becomes known, whether counsel declares it, a client discloses it, or correspondence reveals it. Each entry records the counsel, the persons represented, the matter reference, and the date the arrangement was identified. Investigators consult the register before arranging interviews, before sending correspondence that might reach several clients at once, and before accepting a joint statement. Access is restricted to those working on the matter and to auditors, because the register itself reveals connections that may not yet be public within the building. An entry is closed when the shared arrangement ends, and the closure is dated and signed. Closed entries are retained, since a past connection may become relevant if the matter is reopened.

### Q: How is a Weigher's read recorded when it forms part of an internal case?

When a Weigher's read contributes to an internal case, the read is recorded as an observation with defined limits, not as a conclusion about any person. The recording officer notes the conditions of the read, its duration, what was asked, and the result in the plain terms the Weigher supplies, without converting uncertainty into certainty for the convenience of the file. The Weigher signs the record, may add a qualification, and may withdraw cooperation if the record misstates the result. A read may support a decision to investigate further, to preserve material, or to seek corroboration, but no finding against a person may rest on a read alone. The file must show what independent material was obtained and how it bore on the question. Where a second read is obtained, both records are kept, including any difference between them, and the difference is addressed openly in the case summary.

### Q: What chain of custody applies to a document damaged by fire?

A document damaged by fire is handled as both evidence and a fragile object, and the two duties are recorded together. The first officer to take possession photographs or sketches its condition where facilities allow, notes where it was found and by whom it was handled, and places it in a rigid container without flattening, cleaning, or separating fused pages. Each transfer thereafter is signed for by giver and receiver, with the condition restated at every handover so that later damage cannot be attributed to an earlier holder. Examination is carried out only by officers appointed for the task, who record every test, however small, and preserve any fragment detached during handling in a labelled envelope within the same container. Copies and transcripts are marked as derived material. The original remains in the strong store when not under examination, and its custody history accompanies it whenever it is produced.

### Q: How long are requisitions kept, and how is the requisitions archive arranged?

Completed requisitions are retained in the archive according to the class of expenditure they record, with routine supply requisitions kept for the shorter prescribed period and requisitions touching property, long contracts, or disputed accounts kept for the longer period. The archive is arranged by year sequence within each class, then by requisition number, so that a document can be found from its number alone without knowledge of the requesting desk. A requisition under inquiry, litigation, or audit is withdrawn from the ordinary destruction cycle and marked as held, and no held document may be destroyed until the hold is lifted in writing. Destruction of time expired material is carried out in batches, listed in advance, and certified by two officers after sampling the batch against the list. Researchers and auditors work in the archive room under supervision and may not remove originals, though certified copies are provided on request through the registrar.

### Q: What recorded behaviour do desk officers note under the headings of relief and waiting?

In a desk read of recorded behaviour, relief and waiting are treated as observable patterns rather than as states of mind. Relief is noted where a person's recorded actions change after a pressure is removed: correspondence resumes its ordinary pace, deferred tasks are completed, or enquiries that had stopped are renewed. Waiting is noted where actions are suspended in a pattern that tracks an expected event, such as replies held, visits postponed, or decisions left unsigned without other explanation. The recording officer describes the pattern, the period it covers, and the event it appears to track, and does not assign a motive. Such notes have value only in comparison, against the same person's ordinary pattern and against the patterns of others in the same matter. A single instance proves little. The file must therefore show the baseline relied on, the source of each observation, and any innocent explanation that was considered and why it was set aside.

### Q: What happens to the record of a person who is cleared in an internal matter?

When a person is cleared, the decision is recorded in terms that state the matter examined, the material considered, and the conclusion reached, and a copy of that record is given to the person concerned. References to the suspicion are removed from general indexes and from the person's ordinary service file, so that a later reader of that file encounters no unproven allegation. The inquiry file itself is retained, sealed, under the retention rule for investigations, because the Accord must be able to show how the conclusion was reached if the matter is later questioned. Access to the sealed file requires senior authorisation and is logged. The cleared person may submit a short statement to be kept with the inquiry file, and may require the clearance to be communicated to any desk that was formally notified of the suspicion. No officer may revive the allegation informally in references or postings discussions.

### Q: How are other offices notified when an arrest takes place inside a member building?

When an arrest occurs inside a building belonging to a member administration, the arresting desk notifies the building's liaison officer as soon as the arrested person is secured, and in any event before the party leaves the premises. The notice states that an arrest has occurred, the authority relied on, and the place to which the person will be taken, but it does not disclose evidence or the wider shape of the investigation. A written confirmation follows within the same working day, signed by the officer in charge, and the liaison officer acknowledges receipt on the copy retained for the file. The member administration may appoint an observer to confirm that its property and uninvolved staff were respected, and the observer's report is filed without alteration. Failure to notify does not invalidate a lawful arrest, but it is recorded as a procedural fault and considered in any later review of the operation.

### Q: What is the two step gap problem in chain diagrams, and how do clerks address it?

A chain diagram sets out a sequence of transfers, decisions, or movements so that gaps in a record become visible. The two step gap problem arises when two consecutive links are both missing, because a single missing link can often be bridged by inference from its neighbours, while two missing links remove the fixed points that such an inference needs. Clerks are instructed not to bridge a two step gap by assumption. Instead, the gap is drawn openly on the diagram, marked with its own reference, and treated as a question requiring material from outside the chain, such as a day book entry, a register, or a witness account. If no external material is found, the diagram remains incomplete and any summary based on it must state that the sequence is unproven at that point. A diagram with an honestly marked gap is considered more reliable than a complete diagram built on conjecture.
