## How a drama replaced a biography

A play opened at the National Theatre in London in the winter of 1979, and five years later a film carried its story around the world. Between them, Peter Shaffer's Amadeus installed a version of Mozart in the general mind that no archive supports: a giggling innocent murdered by a jealous rival, a genius too pure for the commercial world that starved him. The replacement happened with remarkable speed. Within a decade of the film, the envious Salieri was the version schoolchildren met first, the version concert programmes had to correct before they could begin, and the version against which every documentary account had to argue its way back. The Mozart myths and legacy that most listeners carry are therefore not a slow sediment of two centuries of biography. They are, in large part, the residue of one dramatic work and its sources, laid over a documented life that looks very different. Almost every popular belief about Mozart originates in a dramatic work rather than a document, and the serious argument about his standing cannot begin until that layer is removed, at which point the case for him changes shape entirely.

The documentary record itself is generous. Few composers of the eighteenth century left so much paper: thousands of family letters, contracts, account entries, a thematic catalogue in the composer's own hand, and an estate inventory drawn up within weeks of his death in 1791. The life those papers describe, the Salzburg childhood, the Vienna decade of freelance work, the marriage, the debts and the unfinished final score, is narrated in full in [the account of Mozart's life and career](/2010/10/21/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-life-and-career/), and this article takes that narration as read. What concerns this article is not the life but the argument laid over it. Five beliefs dominate the popular picture: that Salieri destroyed him, that the ability arrived untouched by training, that composition cost him no labour, that he died in poverty, and that his early death is itself part of the greatness. Each belief has a traceable origin, and in every case the origin is a stage, a page of fiction or a piece of romantic biography rather than a document from the man's lifetime.

One question runs underneath all five, and it is worth stating plainly at the start because the article will not answer it until the poisoning story has been fully traced. Why does a story the documents do not support feel so true? A reader who has seen the film does not merely believe the rivalry; he remembers it, with faces, voices and a deathbed scene attached. A claim encountered once in a footnote has no such weight. The contest between the drama and the documents is therefore not a fair fight between two accounts of equal form, and understanding why the weaker evidence wins is the first task. The answer, when it comes in section five, will turn out to say more about audiences than about either composer.

## Where the Salieri story actually comes from

The poisoning story has a genealogy, and every link in it can be dated. Mozart died in Vienna in December 1791, aged thirty-five, of an illness his doctors named in the vocabulary of their period and which no document of the time connected to any crime. Rumour began early. Within a few years, talk circulated in Vienna that jealous rivals, Italians at the opera, had somehow encompassed his death, and the early biographies carried a report that Mozart himself, in his final illness, had feared he might have been poisoned. That report reached print through the recollections of his widow Constanze and her family, published in the biographical tradition that begins with Franz Xaver Niemetschek in 1798 and continues in the biography assembled by Georg Nikolaus von Nissen in 1828. A dying man's fear, recorded at second hand years later, is evidence about the fear. It is not evidence about a poisoner, and it names nobody.

The story acquired its villain by slow accretion rather than by accusation. Nobody in the 1790s charged Antonio Salieri with anything. No authority investigated him, no court entertained a claim, and no contemporary letter from Mozart accuses him of harm. The allegation, in the strict vocabulary this series insists on, was alleged in gossip, dramatised in fiction, and never charged, never tested and never proved in any forum at all. Its transformation into literature came in 1830, when Alexander Pushkin wrote a short verse drama, Mozart and Salieri, one of his Little Tragedies, in which the older composer murders the younger out of metaphysical envy. Pushkin's play is a meditation on genius and mediocrity, and it needs Salieri the way a parable needs a sinner. Rimsky-Korsakov turned the play into an opera in 1898, and the pairing of the two names as murderer and victim passed into the European repertory. Shaffer inherited the pairing from that lineage, not from any Viennese archive. The rivalry at the centre of the popular story has no documentary basis and can be traced to a specific dramatic lineage, running from a dying man's reported fear, through Pushkin, to the London stage.

### Did Salieri kill Mozart?

No document accuses him, no authority charged him, and no investigation ever tested the claim. The accusation exists only as rumour repeated after Mozart's death and then as drama built on that rumour. Salieri was never formally accused of any crime, and the men and women who knew both composers in Vienna left no testimony that supports the story.

The rumour's one brush with apparent corroboration came more than thirty years later, and it is worth describing precisely because it shows how thin the material is. In 1823 Salieri, by then seventy-three and in failing health, was taken into care after a collapse, and reports spread that in his confusion he had accused himself of the murder. A self-accusation attributed to a sick old man by unnamed intermediaries is not a confession in any evidentiary sense, and the musician and writer Giuseppe Carpani, who knew Salieri, published a public rebuttal setting out that the attendants who were actually present heard no such thing. Ludwig van Beethoven's conversation books, written by visitors because of his deafness, show the rumour reaching him as gossip to be weighed, not as news to be believed. That is the whole file. Set beside the feuds examined in [the survey of rivalries that shaped music history](/2026/03/21/rivalries-that-shaped-music-history/), where documented enmities left letters, lawsuits and public insults, the Salieri case is striking for what is absent from it: everything.

Maynard Solomon, the biographer who reads the Mozart legends as constructions laid over gaps in the record, treats this pattern as the point. Where the documents fall silent, and they fall silent precisely at the moments of highest drama, the culture supplies a scene. A death at thirty-five with no settled medical explanation is a gap. An unfinished Requiem delivered by messenger is a gap. The legends grow in the gaps, and they grow in the shape of stories, because stories are what fit into silences. The poisoning tale is the largest of these growths, and its pedigree, traced above, is theatrical from Pushkin onward. Nothing in the pedigree passes through a Viennese courtroom, a police file or a physician's report, because no such documents exist.

## What Salieri's career was really like

The man the story requires to be a mediocrity was, on the record of appointments and performances, one of the most successful musicians in Europe. Antonio Salieri was born in Legnago in 1750, six years before Mozart, and was brought to Vienna at sixteen by the composer Florian Leopold Gassmann, who trained him and introduced him at court. In 1774, at twenty-four, he was appointed court composer to the Emperor Joseph II. His operas were performed from Naples to Paris; his Armida opened the new Italian opera in Vienna, and his Axur, re d'Ormus was one of the great Viennese successes of 1788, performed for years while other men's works closed in a week. In 1788 he became Imperial Kapellmeister, the most senior musical office in the Habsburg capital, and he held it until 1824, through four decades of changing taste, war and revolution. Institutions do not retain mediocrities in their most visible musical post for thirty-six years.

The fact a reader repeats at dinner is this one, and it deserves its place in the middle of the section rather than as a footnote. Salieri taught Beethoven, Schubert and Liszt, and held the most senior musical post in imperial Vienna. Beethoven came to him for instruction in setting Italian words, Schubert studied counterpoint with him as a boy and remained his protégé, and the young Liszt received lessons from him in Vienna in the early 1820s. The supposed patron saint of mediocrity was, in the documented world, a teacher entrusted with the formation of three of the composers who define the generation after Mozart. He also championed other men's music in his official capacity, directing performances and supporting the institutions, including the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde, that kept Viennese musical life running. His own operas faded after his death in 1825, as most operas of his generation faded, and the fading left his name available for other uses.

This is where the popular story inverts the record rather than merely erring about it. Salieri was a successful, respected composer and teacher, which makes the villain role not merely wrong but backwards. The historical problem is not that Salieri envied Mozart into the grave; it is that a man with a full career, an imperial office and a roster of famous pupils was posthumously reduced to a single fictional emotion. Restoring him does not require inflating him into a neglected master, and this article will not do that. His music's eclipse has ordinary causes: Italian opera seria fell from fashion, his works were tied to libretti and occasions that aged quickly, and French and German repertories displaced the Viennese Italian stage he served. A composer can be genuinely accomplished, genuinely honoured in his lifetime, and genuinely eclipsed afterward, and all three are true of Salieri. The stance this article takes toward him is the stance it takes toward its main subject: the documents first, the strongest version of every claim, and no counter-myth built to replace the myth it removes.

His own sense of his standing survives in one eloquent document. In 1804, at the height of his official career and two decades before his death, Salieri composed a Requiem in C minor intended for his own funeral, writing for himself the solemn leave-taking that his office and reputation seemed to promise. The work was duly performed when he died in 1825. A man plotting posthumous obscurity does not compose his own Requiem; a man secure in a great career does, and the manuscript is a measure of the distance between the historical Salieri's self-understanding and the figure the drama later constructed out of his name. The eclipse that followed had witnesses at the time: pupils who had sat in his classroom, Beethoven among them, dedicated works to him and treated the association as an honour worth printing on a title page. The dedication copies survive. The villain of the popular story was, to the musicians who knew the actual man, a teacher whose name improved a pupil's prospects, which is perhaps the plainest verdict the documentary record offers on what Antonio Salieri really was.

## The relationship as the documents describe it

Against the dramatic version, set the paper trail of two working composers in a small court city. Vienna in the 1780s had one imperial opera establishment, a handful of aristocratic patrons, and a freelance market in teaching, subscription concerts and publication. Mozart and Salieri competed inside that economy for the same commissions, the same singers and the same imperial favour, and competition of that ordinary kind is fully documented. What the documents do not contain is enmity of the murderous, or even the personal, kind. Mozart's letters complain about Italian cabals at the opera house in general terms, in a letter to his father in 1783 he describes the intrigues he believed were blocking a German opera project, and Salieri, as a senior Italian official of that establishment, was plausibly among the people he suspected of obstructing him. Suspicion of professional obstruction, in a letter about a stalled project, is a long way from the relationship the drama requires, and it is also the most hostile note the surviving correspondence strikes.

The positive evidence points the other way, and it is unusually concrete. In 1785, when the singer Nancy Storace returned to the Vienna stage after an illness had threatened her voice, a cantata celebrating her recovery was performed whose music was composed jointly by Mozart, Salieri and a third colleague, Cornetti, to words by Lorenzo Da Ponte. Rivals in a death feud do not write joint cantatas for a colleague's comeback. Six years later, in October 1791, Mozart took Salieri and his companion to a performance of The Magic Flute and wrote to Constanze that Salieri had listened with the closest attention and applauded with genuine warmth from the overture to the final chorus. The letter is a private communication to a wife, with no audience to perform for, and its tone is that of a man pleased by a senior colleague's approval. After Mozart's death, the supposed murderer taught the dead man's younger son, Franz Xaver Mozart, in composition, an entrustment by the widow that is impossible to square with suspicion inside the family.

Cliff Eisen, the source scholar who tests the letters and the surviving paper against each other, represents the method this article follows on all such claims: a letter is evidence for what its writer wanted its reader to believe on that day, and no more, but a pattern across hundreds of letters is evidence of a world. The pattern here is of professional proximity, occasional friction over posts and projects, and public courtesy that was, on the two occasions it can be checked in detail, warmer than courtesy required. Both men belonged to a tiny professional circle in which everyone reviewed everyone else's prospects. Mozart also benefited from the establishment Salieri served: the emperor who employed Salieri gave Mozart his own chamber appointment in 1787, and the court that staged Salieri's operas staged Figaro, Don Giovanni on its return from Prague, and Cosi fan tutte. The relationship as the documents describe it is not a friendship, and the article does not claim one. It is the ordinary, documented coexistence of two ambitious men in one trade, which the drama found unusable and replaced.

## Why the poisoning story took hold

Here the first open question can be closed. Why does a story the documents do not support feel so true? Begin with the shape of the event it explains. A composer of unmatched gifts dies at thirty-five, in harness, leaving the largest of his works unfinished on the desk. That shape is intolerable to narrative instinct, because the effect is enormous and the cause, a fever recorded in the terminology of eighteenth-century Viennese medicine, is small and faceless. A villain restores proportion. If Salieri killed him, then the death was as significant as the life, and the universe of the story balances. The medical record, which offers no face and no motive, loses to the drama every time the two are placed side by side, not because audiences are foolish but because the drama answers a question the record refuses to ask: what does this death mean?

The story also flatters its audience in a precise way. Salieri in the drama is the patron saint of everyone who has done everything correctly and been outshone anyway. His confession in the play is addressed, through the priest, to the mediocrities of the world, and audiences recognised themselves in the confessional rather than in the victim. That identification is the engine of the story's spread. A biography of Mozart asks the reader to contemplate a gift he does not have. The Salieri story asks the reader to contemplate a resentment he may well have had, and the second contemplation is far more comfortable. Pushkin built that comfort first: his Salieri reasons, almost syllogistically, that a man who wastes divine gift on comic opera forfeits his right to it, and the argument is seductive because it dresses envy as justice. Each retelling sharpened the dressing. By the time the film carried the story worldwide, the envy had acquired a theology, a confession and a deathbed, and the compound felt like knowledge.

There is a further absolution buried in the tale, and it may be the deepest reason for its hold. If Mozart was murdered, then nobody neglected him. The Viennese public that let his subscription concerts thin out in his final years, the posterity that let the operas sleep for decades, the readers who prefer the film to the letters: all are innocent, because the crime belongs to one Italian in a black coat. The documented alternative, that a great composer can be under-rewarded by ordinary market indifference and die of an ordinary illness, distributes a mild guilt across everyone, including the audience. Faced with the choice, the audience acquits itself. That is why the story feels true: it is the version in which the listener has done nothing wrong. A claim repeated inside a great work of theatre acquires the memory of having been witnessed, and witnessed events do not feel like claims at all. The loop closes there. The documents were never competing on evidence; they were competing against absolution.

The spread has a linguistic residue that measures the story's victory more exactly than any box office figure. The name Salieri passed into educated usage as a common noun and an adjective, a shorthand in reviews, essays and ordinary speech for the envious also-ran, the competent man soured by a greater talent in the next room. Headline writers reached for it whenever a resentment between an established figure and a prodigy needed a label in one word, and dictionaries of allusion gave the usage their entries, defining the man by the crime the drama assigned him. A name that becomes a common noun has escaped the jurisdiction of biography altogether; correcting the record then requires correcting the language, one usage at a time, against the resistance of everyone who finds the word convenient. Few libels achieve that depth of settlement. It is the final stage of the process this article has been tracing, in which a rumour becomes a play, a play becomes a film, a film becomes a fact, and a fact becomes a word.

## The Amadeus portrait and its real sources

Shaffer's play reached the National Theatre in London in November 1979, directed by Peter Hall, and the film followed in 1984, directed by Milos Forman from Shaffer's own screenplay, winning eight Academy Awards, including Best Picture, with F. Murray Abraham's Salieri at its centre. Both works are accomplishments of a high order as drama and as cinema, and this article treats them as cultural objects deserving analysis, not as errors to be corrected with embarrassment. But a work's quality is exactly what makes its inventions durable, so the portrait it draws has to be sourced as carefully as a document. Shaffer was transparent about his method: he called the play a fantasia on the theme of Mozart and Salieri, and he built it from the Pushkin lineage, from the early biographies by Niemetschek and Nissen, from the published family letters, and from the accumulated anecdote stock of two centuries, the emperor's complaint about too many notes, the grey messenger at the door, the child exhibited at courts. His Salieri is a narrator invented for the frame, an old man in an asylum in 1823 confessing to a priest, and the invention exploits the one genuinely documented peg in the whole structure, the rumours of self-accusation that had circulated around the real Salieri's breakdown in that year.

Even the name in the title is a legacy effect. The composer was baptised Joannes Chrysostomus Wolfgangus Theophilus Mozart, signed himself most often Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart or playful variants of Amade, and used Amadeus, the Latin form of Theophilus, only occasionally and usually in jest. Posterity standardised the Latin form, and the film engraved it. The point is small but diagnostic: the culture's Mozart is assembled from conventions layered over the man, and the assembly began long before Shaffer. What Shaffer added, and what distinguishes his version from the older legends, is a narrator with a grievance and a theory. The earlier myths were local; they attached to single episodes, a burial, a letter, a commission. Amadeus unified them into one psychology, mediocrity confronting genius, and gave every older anecdote a character to happen to. That unification, more than any single invention, is why the portrait displaced its predecessors.

## What the film gets right

Fairness to the film requires listing its accuracies with the same care as its inventions, because several of them are substantial. The Vienna it depicts is recognisably the working environment the documents describe: a court economy in which an emperor functioned as the single largest gatekeeper of taste, a German opera project could be created and strangled by administrative decision, and a composer's income depended on assembling teaching, subscription concerts, commissions and publication into a living that no salary underwrote. The film's sense that Mozart was a professional in a marketplace, hustling for pupils and copying out parts against deadlines, is truer to the letters than the marble bust tradition ever was. Its treatment of the music is likewise serious. The score of the film is not decoration; long passages are allowed to run as argument, and the sequence in which the dying man's music is effectively reviewed piece by piece gives a mass audience something close to the experience of studying the late works in order.

The Requiem commission, which the film turns into its central device, has a documented core that is strange enough without embellishment. In the summer of 1791 an unknown messenger brought Mozart a commission for a Requiem mass from a patron who refused to be named. The patron was Count Franz von Walsegg, a widowed aristocrat who wished to perform the work as his own composition at memorial services for his wife, and who used an agent precisely to keep his authorship scheme intact. Mozart accepted, took a partial fee, and was interrupted by other work, including the Prague coronation opera and The Magic Flute, before turning back to the score in his final weeks. The unfinished state of the manuscript at his death, and the fact that another hand completed it, are documented facts, and the film's instinct that this episode is the natural climax of the life was sound. What the film changed was the personnel and the meaning: the completer, the setting, and the supposed relationship between the work and the death are its inventions, and the next section takes them in turn.

The film is also right about one thing the biographies sometimes soften: the final year was financially hard. Subscription income had fallen from its mid-decade heights, the Turkish war had emptied aristocratic purses and concert rooms, and the letters to Michael Puchberg asking for loans are genuine documents of a man managing a cash shortage with increasing desperation. Where the film errs is one of degree, converting a liquidity crisis in a high-earning household into destitution, and that error belongs to the poverty myth, which sections fifteen and sixteen examine on the ledgers. A fair summary of the film's accuracy is that its Vienna is right, its economics are exaggerated, its music is honoured, and its central relationship is invented. That is a better record than most historical drama achieves, and it is also exactly the mixture most dangerous to memory, because the true parts credential the invented ones.

## What it invents and why it matters

The inventions cluster around the death, and they are load-bearing. The film ends with Salieri at the deathbed, taking dictation of the Requiem from the failing composer, in a scene that collapses composition, confession and murder into a single night. No such night occurred. The completion of the Requiem after Mozart's death was a business transaction managed by his widow, who needed the balance of the fee: Joseph Eybler, a respected younger colleague, began the work and returned the manuscript unfinished, after which Franz Xaver Sussmayr, Mozart's pupil and copyist, produced the completion that Walsegg received, copying the whole score in his own hand so the join would not show. Salieri had no part in any of it. By substituting him for Sussmayr, the film gives its narrator a physical presence at the death, and it converts an untidy story about money and workmanship into a sacrament of envy. The substitution is dramatically perfect and historically empty.

### Is the film Amadeus accurate?

Its Vienna, its court politics and its account of a freelance composer's market are broadly faithful, and its treatment of the music is serious. Its central relationship is invented: the murder, the deathbed dictation and the asylum confession are drama built on rumour, and its portrait of a destitute, infantile composer contradicts the letters and accounts.

Other inventions work more quietly. The film's Mozart is infantile, giggling, obscene and incapable of adult management, a character assembled from the earthier passages of the family letters and raised to a total personality. The letters do contain scatological humour, which was a family idiom and a period taste, but the same correspondence shows a man negotiating fees, planning concert series, managing a publishing strategy and writing to his father as a professional equal about professional matters. The film needs the infant because its theology requires the gift to be unearned: if the vessel is empty, the music must have come from God, and Salieri's grievance becomes cosmic rather than professional. That is a coherent dramatic argument. It is also the effortless-genius legend in costume, and it is why the film matters beyond its own inventions. Amadeus did not merely spread the old myths; it fused them into a single explanatory system, in which the murder, the poverty, the infantilism and the effortlessness all prove one another. Dismantling the system therefore takes longer than correcting any of its parts, which is the business of the sections that follow, beginning with the question the film assumes away: whether the ability was a gift at all, or a product.

## The prodigy question stated properly

The argument about the child Mozart is usually conducted as a single question, genius or fraud, miracle or manufacture, and conducted that way it cannot be answered because it is actually three questions wearing one coat. The first question is whether the boy displayed musical ability far outside the ordinary range of children: on that, the contemporary evidence is overwhelming and neither side seriously disputes it. The second question is how much of the mature achievement that ability explains, and how much is explained by the most intensive musical education any child of the century is known to have received: on that, honest opinion divides, and sections ten and eleven give each side its strongest form. The third question is how far the image of the prodigy was a commercial construction, assembled by a father who needed courts to open their doors: on that, the documents are again clear that construction happened, though construction of a real phenomenon is not fabrication of a false one. Keeping the three questions separate is the whole method of the next four sections.

### Was Mozart a genius or a trained child?

The evidence supports both halves of the question and refutes the choice between them. The ability was documented by independent witnesses before the training could explain it, and the training was the most systematic any child of the period received. The surviving argument is about proportion, not about which single cause to credit.

The second open loop of this article can now be stated in its proper form. Was the ability born or built? The question will not be answered by accumulating marvels on one side and lesson schedules on the other, because both accumulations are genuine. It turns on a comparison that history, by an accident of family management, half performed and half withheld: the same teacher, the same household and the same touring stage were given to two children, a brother and a sister, and the experiment was then altered mid-course in ways that make its result endlessly arguable. Section twelve examines that sister's career as the closest thing to a control the eighteenth century provides. First, each case deserves to be made as strongly as its best advocate can make it, without the other case interrupting. The innate case rests on witnesses who had no reason to inflate, on feats recorded close to the event, and on the brute asymmetry that the same training, applied elsewhere, never again produced this. The training case rests on the father's profession, the surviving exercises, the hands of two men visible in the earliest manuscripts, and the consideration that everything exceptional about the output was preceded by something exceptional about the input. Neither case is the property of cranks. Both are held, in different proportions, by the named authorities this article uses, and the proportions are the argument.

## The case that the ability was innate

The strongest form of the innate case begins not with family publicity but with a hostile witness. Daines Barrington, a Fellow of the Royal Society in London, visited the Mozart children in 1764 precisely to expose a suspected fraud, since the boy's advertised age was doubted and a small adult was rumoured to be hidden behind the child prodigy act. Barrington tested him rigorously: he set the eight-year-old to sight-read a newly written duet, to improvise on a given theme, to play with the keyboard covered, and to identify notes sounded out of his sight, and he verified the child's age against the Salzburg baptismal record before reporting to the Royal Society. His published account concluded that the abilities were real, extraordinary and musical in kind, not tricks of memory or parental coaching. A scientist who arrived as a debunker and left as a witness is the innate case's best exhibit, because his testimony cannot be discounted as the family's advertising.

The second exhibit is a documented feat of memory and ear from the Italian journey of 1770. In Rome during Holy Week, the fourteen-year-old heard Gregorio Allegri's Miserere performed in the Sistine Chapel, a work the chapel guarded as its exclusive property, and wrote it out from memory afterward, returning a second time to correct details. The family's letters report the episode close to the event, and the feat impressed Rome enough that the Pope made the boy a Knight of the Golden Spur. Later writers inflated the story, copies of the Miserere did circulate outside the chapel, and memory is not composition; the innate case does not need the inflation. What the episode documents is a musical memory operating at a level that training refines but does not obviously create, since identically trained contemporaries are not recorded doing anything comparable.

The third exhibit is a judgement rather than a feat. In February 1785, Joseph Haydn, then the most respected living composer of instrumental music, heard the boy, now twenty-nine, play the set of string quartets dedicated to him, and told Leopold Mozart directly that his son was the greatest composer Haydn knew, by reputation or in person. Leopold reported the remark home in a letter, and Haydn had no commercial reason to say it; he was surrendering a title he might have claimed himself. The innate case rests its argument on the accumulation: precocity appearing before tuition could produce it, feats of ear and memory that tuition has never reliably reproduced in others, and the considered verdict of the era's best-placed judge. Its advocates add one asymmetry they treat as decisive. The machinery of training, which the next section describes, was applied by the same father to pupils and to a daughter in the same house, and the outcome, in the son's case, has no parallel among them. If training were sufficient, they argue, the eighteenth century, which trained thousands of children intensively, would be littered with Mozarts. It contains one.

## The case that it was manufactured by training

The strongest form of the training case begins with the father's profession. Leopold Mozart was not a stage parent who stumbled on a talented child; he was one of Europe's leading music pedagogues, whose violin method of 1756 was the standard treatise of its generation, translated and reprinted across the continent. He recognised his son's aptitude in a household where both children were being educated as professional musicians from the start, and he then did something no other father of the century managed: he converted the whole family into a travelling conservatory. The grand tour of 1763 to 1766 and the journeys that followed were exhibitions, certainly, but they were also a curriculum. The boy heard every important orchestra and opera company in Europe, studied the styles of Paris, London, Mannheim and the Italian houses from inside their theatres, and absorbed the international language of the galant style at its sources rather than from provincial copies.

The named stages of that curriculum are documented in detail. In London in 1764 and 1765 the eight-year-old was taken up by Johann Christian Bach, the youngest son of Johann Sebastian, whose keyboard sonatas the boy played, studied and imitated until the influence is audible in his own earliest concertos. In Bologna in 1770 he worked through counterpoint exercises under Giovanni Battista Martini, the most learned contrapuntist in Italy, and the exercise books with Martini's corrections survive as paper evidence of instruction at the highest technical level. Between those poles ran a daily discipline of performance, copying, composition and correction that began before the child's fifth birthday. Cliff Eisen, the source scholar who has spent a career separating hands in the early manuscripts, has shown that Leopold's hand appears in the notation and correction of the earliest works, which is exactly what a training model predicts: the first scores are collaborative products of a workshop, and the pupil's independent hand emerges gradually across the 1760s. Nothing in that finding diminishes the pupil; it simply relocates the miracle from birth to method.

Ruth Halliwell, the scholar of the Mozart family correspondence, supplies the construction side of the case at its strongest. The letters that document the childhood were written by a father managing an enterprise: they solicit engagements, report successes to Salzburg patrons, frame the children as a heaven-sent marvel for audiences whose doors the family needed opened, and they systematically omit failures, illnesses of morale, and the commercial calculations Halliwell reconstructs from the pattern of the whole correspondence. The prodigy, on this reading, was partly a product designed for a market that paid for marvels and not for competence. The training case then generalises: everything exceptional in the adult output was preceded by something exceptional in the childhood input, an input no rival composer received in that concentration, at that age, across that geography. Where the innate case points to one Mozart among thousands of trained children, the training case replies that no other child was trained like this one, so the experiment the innate side demands has never actually been run. Both sides, it should be noted, are now arguing about the same facts with different weightings, which is the mark of a genuine historical problem rather than a legend.

## What Nannerl's career tells us about both cases

Maria Anna Mozart, called Nannerl, was four and a half years older than her brother, and for the first decade of the family enterprise she was its co-star. She received the same father's tuition in the same household, played the same tours from 1762 onward, and was received by the press of the 1760s as a keyboard player of the first rank in Europe while still a young teenager, praised in terms that did not treat her as the lesser half of the act. The siblings were exhibited together, billed together and reviewed together. If the innate case required a control, a second child raised inside the identical machine, the family supplied one, and her early record is precisely why her later record is so uncomfortable for both sides of the argument.

For after 1769 the experiment was discontinued on one side only. When Leopold took Wolfgang alone to Italy in that year, and twice more afterward, Nannerl remained in Salzburg. The official reasons were practical: the son's career was the family's investment, a daughter's touring life raised problems of propriety as she reached marriageable age, and two careers cost more than the enterprise could fund. The consequences were structural and permanent. The brother received the Italian opera houses, Martini's counterpoint, the academies and the commissions; the sister received pupils in Salzburg, which she taught for the rest of a long life, and a marriage in 1784 to a widowed magistrate. She composed, according to her brother's own letters, which praise a song she sent him and ask for more; none of her music survives, because nothing in her position caused it to be copied, published or preserved. The archive of her talent is therefore a set of reviews, a father's early letters, and an absence.

Her career is the best available control on the prodigy question, and it points at training as much as at innate gift, because the sibling who shared the gift's environment but was denied its advanced stages plateaued exactly where the provision stopped. That is the third rung of this article's ladder, and it cuts both ways, which is why it settles nothing by itself. The training side reads Nannerl as proof that the machine made the musician and that her brother is simply the child the machine was allowed to finish. The innate side replies that the machine was never aimed at composition in her case, that no record shows her attempting large-scale composition and being refused, and that a control in which the crucial variable was never tested cannot decide the question. Both readings are honest. The loop that opened in section nine therefore closes here in the only way the evidence permits: structurally. For the training account to be wholly right, the finished input must be shown sufficient, and somewhere in Europe a comparably finished product of comparably total training should exist; none has been produced. For the innate account to be wholly right, the gift must be shown independent of the machine, and the machine's fingerprints are on every stage of its delivery. The sister's silenced career is the fact each side must digest, and neither digests it comfortably. The question of proportion stays open, and any writer who closes it for you is selling something.

## The effortless composition legend

The most durable picture of Mozart at work comes from a single letter, and the letter is the problem. In 1815 the German critic Friedrich Rochlitz published in a music journal a letter purporting to be Mozart's own account of his method: the composer describes whole works arriving complete in his mind during walks and carriage rides, so that writing them down afterward is a mere transcription of something already perfect, requiring no more effort than taking dictation. The picture is beautiful, it flatters a particular theory of genius, and it has been quoted ever since by writers who need Mozart to be a passive vessel. The difficulty is that the letter's authenticity collapsed under scrutiny. Later scholars, comparing its language, its circumstances and Rochlitz's known habits as an editor who improved the anecdotes he published, concluded that the letter is, at best, a heavily worked construction and, on the sterner judgement, a fabrication built to satisfy a market hungry for testimony about how genius operates.

Maynard Solomon, the biographer who reads the Mozart legends as constructions laid over gaps in the record, treats the Rochlitz material as the clearest case of the process at work. Rochlitz had published a stream of Mozart anecdotes in the years around 1800, several of which fail independent checking, and the letters he attributed to the composer served the cultural needs of the generation that invented the romantic genius: a creator who does not labour, whose work arrives whole, and whose poverty and early death can then be read as the world's ingratitude toward a being too fine for commerce. The effortless legend is not a stray error inside that ideology; it is the ideology's load-bearing wall. Remove labour from composition and the music becomes a visitation, the composer a medium, and criticism a form of worship. Keep the labour in, and the music becomes what the scores show it to be: the product of a trained professional working at speed under commercial pressure, which is a less mystical and more interesting story.

It matters to be precise about what is being rejected, because speed itself is documented and is not the legend. Mozart did compose quickly by any standard, and quickly in a way his contemporaries remarked on: the Linz Symphony written in a matter of days in 1783 because he arrived in that city owing his host a concert, the final three symphonies of 1788 produced within a single summer, opera scores delivered against opening nights. Speed, however, is a property of a craftsman working fluently in a language he has spoken since childhood; it is not the absence of process. The legend requires the stronger claim, that the process happened invisibly, in the head, before the pen moved, and that the manuscripts should therefore be clean first thoughts. The manuscripts are the test. If the legend were true, the autographs would show transcription; what they show, as the next section sets out, is composition in the act of writing, with all the false starts, deletions and second thoughts that composition involves. The distinction between fast and effortless is the distinction between a professional and a miracle, and the evidence supports the professional.

## What the sketches and corrections show

The paper evidence against effortlessness is extensive, and its scale is the first thing to grasp. Ulrich Konrad, the scholar who catalogued Mozart's surviving sketch material, counts more than three hundred sketch leaves and fragments: openings tried and set aside, themes drafted for works that were never completed, passages worked over in pencil and ink before they reached a fair copy. The fragment, not the perfect whole, is the characteristic Mozart manuscript form after the autograph scores themselves. He abandoned works at every stage of his career and in every genre, concertos begun and dropped, quartets left as movements, an opera abandoned when a commission collapsed. A composer whose works arrived complete in his head would not leave this residue. A composer who worked on paper, testing material against the resistance of actual notes, leaves exactly this.

### Did Mozart really never revise his music?

He revised constantly, and the paper survives to prove it. More than three hundred sketch leaves and fragments are catalogued, the autographs carry deletions, crossings-out and rewritten passages, and works were abandoned unfinished at every stage of his career. The six quartets he dedicated to Haydn occupied him, on and off, for three years.

The Haydn quartets make the case in a single documented instance. The six string quartets Mozart published in 1785 with a dedication to Haydn are among his most finished works, and his own dedication letter to the older composer describes them, in the composer's words, as the fruit of long and laborious study. The surviving materials confirm the description: three years of intermittent work, revisions between the earliest versions and the published text, and an artistic ambition, to match and honour the acknowledged master of the form, that raised the cost of every movement. Set beside that dedication, the Rochlitz letter's account of effortless dictation is not a rival description of the same man; it is a description of a different being, invented for a readership that wanted one. The manuscripts show drafting, revision and abandonment, which removes the effortlessness that the legend depends on, and with it goes the theological version of the prodigy as well. What remains after this section is a worker: faster than his rivals, better trained than any of them, and working. That reallocation of credit, from miracle to labour, is the pattern the rest of this article repeats, next on the subject of money, where the ledgers survive in unusual completeness.

## The poverty claim and the financial record

The poverty story asserts that Mozart died destitute, neglected and buried as a pauper, and it dissolves on contact with the numbers, provided the numbers are taken from the documents rather than the biographies. The reconstruction used here is the one built by Volkmar Braunbehrens, the historian who rebuilt Mozart's Viennese finances from the surviving accounts, contracts and correspondence, and its shape is consistent. In December 1787 Mozart was appointed Imperial Chamber Composer at a salary of eight hundred florins a year, a post whose duties consisted largely of supplying dance music for the court balls. The fee for The Marriage of Figaro at the court opera was four hundred and fifty florins. In the strong middle years of the decade, when his subscription concerts, his aristocratic pupils and his publications all paid at once, his annual income ran to several times the salary, placing him among the better-paid professionals of the city. He spent at that level too: the apartment in the Domgasse, where Figaro was completed, cost four hundred and sixty florins a year in rent, and the household kept servants, took cures at Baden for Constanze's health, and maintained the social front a fashionable teacher required. Anyone reconstructing a freelance income of this shape from fees, loans and irregular payments has to keep the scraps in one place, which is what [the download page for the offline encrypted notes app](https://vaultbook.net/download/) exists for in a modern freelancer's version of the same problem.

The deterioration after 1788 is equally documented and equally specific. War with the Ottoman Empire emptied Vienna of the aristocratic subscribers on whom the concert economy depended, commissions thinned, and the gap between a spending pattern set in fat years and an income set in lean ones was bridged by borrowing. The begging letters to his fellow Freemason Michael Puchberg survive, and the loans they obtained added up to some fourteen hundred florins across the final three years. The estate documents drawn up after his death in December 1791 record debts of roughly nine hundred florins against an inventory listing respectable furniture, a fortepiano, a billiard table and a large music library: the balance sheet of an embarrassed professional, not of a pauper. His burial, finally, was in a common grave at St Marx cemetery under the Josephine burial regulations then in force, an arrangement applied to the respectable middle class generally, intended by the reforming emperor to economise on ground and ceremony; it marked neither disgrace nor destitution, and the storm-lashed pauper's funeral of the biographies is a later atmospheric invention, contradicted by the mild weather Vienna recorded that December. The financial record shows a high earner with a cash-flow problem, so the poverty story is doing emotional rather than factual work, and the next section asks what that work is.

One comparison, often quoted by the poverty story's defenders, deserves to be returned to its context, because Braunbehrens's reconstruction handles it precisely. The chamber composer's salary of eight hundred florins was indeed far below the two thousand florins a year that Christoph Willibald Gluck had drawn in a comparable dignity, and the contrast is real paper, not legend. But Gluck's payment had rewarded a lifetime of imperial service and a European fame of forty years' standing, in a fuller appointment with wider duties, while Mozart's post was created for a man of thirty-one whose assigned labour was largely the supply of dance music for the redoute balls. A salary gap between a junior appointment and a veteran's is evidence about the Viennese court's pay scales. It was converted by the biographies into evidence about the world's contempt for genius, a conversion the full ledger, salary and fees and rents and loans together, does not support.

## Why the poverty story is attractive

If the ledgers refute the poverty story, its persistence needs an explanation in the same register as the poisoning story's persistence, and the two explanations rhyme. The poverty story converts a commercial outcome into a moral verdict. A freelance composer whose income fell when war emptied his concert rooms is a victim of business conditions, and business conditions make a dull villain. A genius starved by an ungrateful world is an indictment, and indictments are satisfying to deliver, especially for listeners who arrive two centuries later and can join the prosecution at no cost to themselves. Believing the story lets its teller stand on the right side of the trial: we, who play the records and fill the halls, would have recognised him. The belief is a loyalty oath disguised as a fact, and it is sworn by people who have never been asked to pay a subscription in a war year.

The story also performs a second service, certifying the value of the music by the price the world refused to pay for it. In a market culture, an artist's commercial failure needs explaining away if his artistic rank is to stay intact, and neglect by fools is the explanation that protects both the rank and the audience's self-image as the fools' opposite. The nineteenth century, which built the romantic biography trade, needed this service urgently: its middle-class readers were the market, and a story in which the market murdered genius by indifference would have been an accusation against the readership itself. Poverty by villainy, by cabal, by the malice of rivals and the stinginess of emperors, kept the blame on individuals and off the system of buying and selling that the readers embodied. Otto Jahn's great biography of the 1850s, the foundation of modern Mozart scholarship and a work of real documentary labour, still frames the final years in the pathos its century required. The frame survived the scholarship that should have dissolved it, because the frame was the part readers were buying. A starving genius is a story a culture tells about itself: that it knows, now, what things are worth. The ledgers tell a plainer tale, of a household that earned well, spent freely, borrowed when the market turned, and was overtaken by a death that turned a temporary embarrassment into a permanent-looking wreck. Embarrassment, unlike destitution, cannot be statuesque. That is the whole of its disadvantage as legend, and the whole of its advantage as truth.

## Dying at thirty-five and what it does to reputation

Mozart died in 1791 in his thirty-sixth year, and the question for this section is not what that cost him, which is unanswerable, but what it does to the machinery of reputation. The mechanics are several and they pull in different directions. The first is the absence of a decline. Every long career supplies its critics with a late period to quarrel over, works that can be enlisted as evidence of failing powers or hardening manner; a reputation built on a catalogue that stops at thirty-five never has to survive its owner's old age. The curve of the output, judged by the works of the final year alone, The Magic Flute, the Clarinet Concerto, the Requiem fragment, points upward at the moment it stops, and posterity has consistently mistaken a rising truncation for a trajectory. The second mechanism is the counterfactual premium. Every estimate of Mozart silently includes the forty unwritten years: the operas of a man in his fifties, the symphonies of a master with Haydn's longevity. No other major composer is so routinely credited with works he did not write, and the credit is unfalsifiable, which is precisely its value to the reputation.

The third mechanism is loss of control. A composer who lives manages his own afterlife in advance: he revises, destroys, publishes, dedicates, feuds and writes memoirs. Mozart's afterlife was managed by others, a widow with a business to run, a pupil with an interest in the completion story, publishers who bought the manuscripts, and biographers who built the legend this article has been dismantling. The management was, on the whole, commercially brilliant, and the legend was one of its products. Set against these mechanisms, however, stand the costs of early death, which the inflation thesis usually ignores. An early death risks the works themselves: unpublished manuscripts scatter, perishable repertory is forgotten before it can be printed, and no advocate with the composer's authority survives to insist. Mozart escaped that fate because Constanze treated the estate as an enterprise and because the music was already lodged in the memories of a generation of performers. The honest conclusion is narrower than the myth. Dying at thirty-five did not inflate the music's quality, which is fixed in the scores. It altered the shape of the reputation around the music, removing the complications of age, adding the glamour of the unwritten, and handing the story to managers. Inflation happens to the narrative. The notes are untouched by it.

## Comparing careers cut short across the series

The comparison with other short careers tests whether Mozart's case is special or merely the most famous instance of a general pattern, and the series this article belongs to supplies the comparisons from within its own pages. Franz Schubert died in 1828 at thirty-one, leaving hundreds of works unpublished and a reputation that had to be constructed afterward by advocates: Schumann publicising the Great C major Symphony, Liszt transcribing the songs for piano, publishers issuing the catalogue in arrears for decades. There, early death plainly risked oblivion rather than guaranteeing inflation; the inflation, when it came, was the work of the advocates, and it arrived a generation late. Giovanni Battista Pergolesi, dead in 1736 at twenty-six, shows the pattern's pathological form: his tiny genuine catalogue was swollen after his death by dozens of works falsely attributed to his name, because a dead prodigy's name was a marketable brand and no living composer existed to object. Attribution itself became the inflation mechanism. Felix Mendelssohn, dead at thirty-eight in 1847, had the opposite machinery: wealth, an institution, a devoted sister and a family archive, and his reputation was curated from inside the household before being attacked from outside it for reasons that had nothing to do with his music's survival.

Frédéric Chopin died at thirty-nine, Henry Purcell at about thirty-six, in each case leaving a catalogue whose completeness is an editorial construction assembled by others from manuscripts in private hands. Across all these cases, the constant is not the early death but the transfer of power that follows it: reputation passes from the composer to a changing cast of widows, pupils, publishers, editors and movements, and the resulting fame reflects the competence and interests of that cast at least as much as the contents of the catalogue. Mozart's cast was unusually able and unusually motivated, since Constanze's income depended on the value of the name, and the publishers who bought the autographs had capital riding on the same asset. The completeness of his surviving catalogue, the early complete edition, the numbered works that let a public navigate six hundred compositions: these are achievements of the transfer, not of the short life. A comparison that credits the death with what the survivors did misreads the mechanism in every case, Mozart's included. Death is the occasion of the transfer. It is not the author of the reputation.

The control cases, the long careers, confirm the reading from the other side. Haydn lived to seventy-seven and spent his final creative decade writing the two oratorios that crowned his fame, managing his reputation with a shrewdness Mozart never had the years to practise, and his standing rose with his age rather than despite it. Beethoven lived to fifty-six, through deafness, a catastrophic public premiere record in his last years and a body of late work his contemporaries found unintelligible, and his reputation absorbed all of it, because the composer survived to insist, publish and outlast his critics. Longevity did not dilute either man; it gave each the chance to become his own most determined advocate. The variable that separates the inflated short career from the ruined one, and the honoured long career from the forgotten one, is never the age at death itself. It is whether someone, the composer or his survivors, does the unglamorous work of preservation, publication and advocacy in the decades when the music cannot speak for itself. Early death merely decides whose hands that work falls into.

## The greatest-composer claim stated on criteria

With five beliefs dismantled, the article reaches the claim they were all standing guard over, and the third open loop can be posed in its full form. What is left of him once every myth has been removed? The question will be answered in section thirty-two, after the claim has been tested on ground the mythology never visits. The claim itself, that Mozart is the greatest composer, is usually asserted as if greatness were a measurable property of a man, like his height. It is not. It is a verdict delivered under criteria, and the verdict changes when the criteria change, which is why partisans argue past each other: the Bach partisan has silently chosen contrapuntal architecture as the test, the Beethoven partisan has chosen developmental span, the Mozart partisan has chosen finish and fertility, and each then discovers, with triumph, that his candidate wins his own examination. An honest conduct of the argument therefore begins by fixing the criteria in advance and accepting their verdicts in advance. The series' own comparison of [the greatest composers of all time](/2025/04/07/greatest-composers-of-all-time/) works the wider field on stated measures; this article narrows the field to the three criteria on which the Mozart case is most often and most seriously made.

The three criteria chosen here are melodic invention, dramatic characterisation and structural command. They are not arbitrary. Melodic invention is the criterion ordinary listeners apply without knowing its name, since a melody is what survives of a work in a person who cannot read a score. Dramatic characterisation is the criterion his operas force on the discussion, because no composer of his century put so many distinct human beings, thinking incompatible thoughts at the same time, inside a single musical span. Structural command is the criterion his detractors choose, the one on which the case is most vulnerable, and a defence that refused it would be worthless. Each of the next three sections takes one criterion, states the strongest available form of the Mozart claim under it, and then states, without rescue, where the claim fails under it. The summing-up in section twenty-three is then arithmetic rather than advocacy: the greatest-composer title is worth exactly what the weighting of three such verdicts is worth, and the reader who finishes those sections will know what his own verdict, if he reaches one, is made of. Nothing in the test requires the murder, the miracle, the destitution or the deathbed. That is the point of the exercise. The mythology was a substitute for this examination, and the examination is more favourable to its subject than the substitute ever managed to be, though favourable in a more complicated shape.

## Melodic invention as the criterion

State the claim at full strength: across a working life of roughly twenty-five years, Mozart produced a supply of distinct, memorable and structurally useful melody larger than any composer of his era and arguably of any era, spread across every genre he touched, opera, concerto, symphony, quartet, mass, serenade and solo sonata, with no genre treated as a place to spend leftovers. The test of a melody, for these purposes, is not beauty in the abstract; it is survival under transplantation. A Mozart theme can be moved into another key, another tempo, another composer's hands, and it remains itself, which is why later musicians kept stealing the raw material in public. Beethoven, the least sentimental of borrowers, wrote variation sets on themes from The Magic Flute and Don Giovanni. Tchaikovsky, a century later, built his orchestral suite Mozartiana as an act of homage around Mozart themes, arranging them the way a jeweller resets stones he did not cut. Melody that other masters treat as currency is melody whose invention is not in dispute.

The criterion also has a quantitative face that partisans rarely use because it can be checked. Set the catalogue out by the Köchel numbering, more than six hundred works, and count how many contain at least one theme that an ordinary concertgoer can recognise after two hearings. For Mozart the count runs into the hundreds, distributed from the famous serenade written as functional evening music to the slow movements of concertos composed for his own subscription series. Invention at that rate is not a visitation; it is a habit of mind, and it connects this section to the labour argument of sections thirteen and fourteen. A gift that produces this volume on this schedule, through illness, debt, travel and bereavement, is indistinguishable from a supremely practised professional faculty, and it becomes stronger evidence the more carefully the mythology of effortlessness is cleared away from it. The honest limit of the criterion must also be stated. Melody is the most portable element of music and the least sufficient: a composer of tunes alone would be a tunesmith, and the prettiness charge examined in section twenty-four is precisely the accusation that Mozart's melodic gift is the whole of him. The next criterion answers that accusation on its strongest ground, because characterisation is melody put to work in the service of persons.

## Dramatic characterisation as the criterion

Julian Rushton, the scholar of Mozart's stage works, represents the line of criticism on which this criterion rests: in the operas, character is not described by the music but enacted by it, so that what a person is can be heard in how his or her musical material behaves. The clearest laboratory is the ensemble. In the Act Two finale of The Marriage of Figaro, the action moves through a sequence of confrontations in which the Count's authority, the Countess's dignity, Susanna's quickness and Figaro's improvisation are maintained simultaneously, each with its own melodic profile, while the plot advances at the speed of farce and the emotion keeps deepening underneath it. A listener always knows who is winning an exchange and who is losing face, and knows it from the notes rather than the words. No composer before him had built so long a span of music out of so many incompatible viewpoints held in balance, and the feat is technical before it is poetic: it requires a melodic invention fluent enough to give every character a voice, and a structural control firm enough to keep the voices in one argument.

The criterion extends beyond the stage, which is what raises it from a specialism to a claim on the title. The piano concertos of the Vienna years behave like dramas for two characters, soloist and orchestra, whose relationship changes by movement: partners, rivals, a solo voice teasing an ensemble that answers in kind, an orchestra that introduces a theme the piano then treats as a confidence to be developed in private. Even the instrumental works carry the dramatist's habit of giving material a personality and then testing it against opposition. The limit of the criterion is real and should be recorded beside the claim. Characterisation of this kind depends on contrast among defined persons; it does not supply the single overwhelming subjective voice that romantic music would later prize, and a listener who wants the composer himself to be the protagonist of every work will find Mozart withholding, present everywhere as intelligence and nowhere as confession. Under the criterion as stated, though, the verdict is not close within his century. On dramatic characterisation, the removed-myth Mozart does not merely survive the examination. He sets its standard.

## Structural command as the criterion

The third criterion is the one the defence must not fudge. Structural command means the management of musical argument across spans: the pacing of tension and release over a movement, a work, an evening. Within the movement and the act, Mozart's command is close to unmatched in his period. His development sections do not merely process themes; they re-function them, so that material introduced as decoration returns as argument, and his recapitulations characteristically adjust rather than repeat, as though the music had learned something in the intervening pages. The finale of the Jupiter Symphony, composed in the summer of 1788, builds its closing pages from the combination of five themes in invertible counterpoint, a display of learned technique worn so lightly that audiences applaud it as brilliance rather than scholarship. In opera the same faculty works at the scale of the act: the long finales of Figaro and Don Giovanni are engineered like machines whose pressure never drops while their emotional register keeps changing. At the scale he worked in, the command is real, precise and demonstrable in any score.

At larger spans, the concession must be made cleanly. Mozart left no cycle that binds many works into one architecture, no equivalent of the Bach Passions' theological span or of the long developmental journeys Beethoven would build across whole symphonies and sonatas; his forty-one numbered symphonies are each self-contained occasions, and the greatest of them is twenty-five minutes long. His sacred music, though it contains movements of the first rank, is a set of commissions fitted to liturgical slots rather than a single sustained structure, and the Requiem, the one work that might have stood as a large-span summation, is the work death interrupted. A partisan who claims the whole of structural command for Mozart is claiming territory the catalogue does not occupy. The defensible claim is bounded: at the scale of the movement, the act and the single work, his structural command is as complete as any composer's of the century; at the scale of the career-spanning edifice, the title belongs elsewhere. Bounded claims of this kind are what the mythology made unnecessary, because a miracle does not have to specify its dimensions. A professional reputation does, and this article's defence is content with the dimensions the evidence supports.

## Where he wins and where he does not

The three criteria can now be summed, and the summing is deliberately unglamorous. On melodic invention, Mozart wins outright: the volume, the distribution across genres and the adoption of his themes as currency by later masters make the verdict the least contestable in this article. On dramatic characterisation, he wins outright within his century and set a standard the next century's opera composers measured themselves against, as the line from Rossini's ensembles to Verdi's crowd scenes attests in the repertory itself. On structural command, he shares the title at the scale of movement and act, and yields it at the scale of the large edifice to Bach behind him and Beethoven ahead of him. On criteria this article did not select, the yielding is plainer still: for contrapuntal architecture considered as an end in itself, Bach stands alone; for the single-minded development of small material across huge spans, Beethoven stands alone; for the reinvention of what music could be asked to do in a generation, several names precede him. The greatest-composer question, pursued honestly, dissolves into a schedule of particular supremacies, and the schedule is more informative than the crown.

That dissolution is not a defeat for the Mozart case; it is the Mozart case finally stated in checkable form. What the examination shows is that the deflated, documented Mozart, the trained professional of sections nine to fourteen, the high earner of section fifteen, holds two outright titles and a share of a third against the strongest field the art form can assemble. The mythology, by contrast, claimed everything and could defend nothing, because a murdered miracle has no criteria, only pathos. There is a reason the debunking exercise of this article has repeatedly left its subject stronger than it found him: claims with dimensions can be verified, and his verify. The remaining sections of the argument take up the two live charges that survive the criteria test in the modern repertory, that the music is merely pretty, and that the man is not overrated but overexposed, before the article returns to the open loop and closes it. The charges matter because they are the forms in which the debate is actually conducted in concert halls and record shops, and they deserve the same strongest-form treatment as the legends already dismantled.

## The charge of surface prettiness

Every defence of Mozart eventually meets the charge that the music is decorative: graceful, finished, charming, and finally shallow, a porcelain art for a powdered century. The charge has an institutional history. The nineteenth century domesticated him for the drawing room, issued the sonatas as accomplishments for daughters, arranged the operas into potpourris, and built the music-box Mozart whose tinkling serenades could stand for the whole output. Against that manufactured figure the charge is accurate, which is why it survives: it describes the packaging, and most listeners meet the packaging first. In the twentieth century the charge acquired a formidable named advocate. Glenn Gould, the Canadian pianist and broadcaster whose Mozart recordings were famously unsympathetic, argued in print and in broadcast that the later style hardens into mannerism, and delivered the verdict that Mozart, unlike the composers who died too soon for their promise, had if anything died too late for his. Gould's is the strongest form of the case because it comes from inside the keyboard literature and from a musician whose Bach was beyond dispute: the music, on his account, becomes glib, its gestures repeated until elegance curdles into facility.

The charge also draws strength from a real feature of the music that defenders should concede before answering. Mozart's harmony characteristically resolves its tensions within the phrase and the period; his dissonances are prepared, appraised and discharged with a courtesy that can sound, to ears trained on later music, like a refusal of depth. A listener formed by Wagner or by Mahler, for whom truth lives in the unresolved, will hear resolution itself as evasion. That hearing is not stupid, and the counter-case cannot simply call it deaf. What the counter-case can do is direct the listener to the parts of the catalogue where the courtesy fails on purpose, where the tensions are not discharged but sustained, contradicted or left standing at the curtain. The charge of prettiness is best answered not by argument but by assignment: the operas first, where the elegance is constantly made to carry cruelty, lust for power, humiliation and forgiveness; then the minor-key instrumental works, where the surface is not pretty at all. The next two sections give the assignment in detail, since a debate article earns its conclusions by producing its evidence rather than by summarising it.

## The counter-case from the operas

Begin where the prettiness charge goes to die: the end of Don Giovanni. The opera's final scene assembles the whole surviving cast to sing a tidy moral about the wages of wickedness, and the music Mozart gives them is correct, bright and strangely weightless, as though the opera itself did not believe the tidy moral were adequate to what the audience has watched. For two acts the score has taken its anti-hero's vitality seriously, given the wronged women music of genuine anguish and fury, and built the stone guest's arrival into a scene of terror that the orchestral writing, trombones and a key of D minor reserved throughout the opera for the supernatural, makes physical. A composer of surfaces does not construct an evening whose official moral feels inadequate in his own setting of it. The gap between the moral and the music is the meaning, and it is available only to a dramatist working at full stretch.

The Marriage of Figaro makes the complementary point about elegance as a weapon. The opera's surface is the most polished in the repertory, and under it runs a plot about a servant out-thinking his master, a countess confronting the collapse of her marriage, and a household in which everyone is deceiving someone for reasons the music keeps sympathetic. The ensembles, examined in section twenty-one as feats of characterisation, are also feats of moral attention: Figaro's humiliation and Susanna's steadiness are rendered with the same melodic generosity as the Count's authority, and the opera famously ends not with punishment but with a plea for forgiveness that the Countess's music makes enormous. Idomeneo, the opera seria Mozart composed for Munich in 1781 at twenty-five, attempts a sterner register altogether: a king who has promised to sacrifice the first living thing he meets on shore, and meets his son. The storm music, the oracle's subterranean pronouncement and the queen's derangement are the work of a composer reaching for terror inside the most formal genre of his century. Julian Rushton, the scholar of the stage works, draws the general conclusion this section needs: across the operas, the musical surface is a medium, not a message, and the medium carries whatever the drama loads onto it, including cruelty, class rage, erotic obsession and grace. Prettiness is what the charge calls the medium because it has not stayed for the message.

## The counter-case from the minor key works

The instrumental catalogue contains its own refutation, filed under a handful of Köchel numbers. The Symphony No. 40 in G minor, K. 550, opens with a figure of restless agitation that the movement never allows to settle, and its finale sustains a contrapuntal ferocity that the drawing-room image cannot accommodate. Its smaller companion in the same key, the Symphony No. 25, K. 183, was written when the composer was seventeen and already sounds like a young man kicking the furniture of the galant style apart. The two great minor-key piano concertos, No. 20 in D minor, K. 466, and No. 24 in C minor, K. 491, put the soloist in genuine contention with the orchestra, the first with a turbulence that made it Beethoven's favourite among the concertos to perform, the second with a scale and wind writing that point directly at the next century. The String Quintet in G minor, K. 516, composed in the spring of his father's final illness and death, grieves in public form; the Mass in C minor, K. 427, unfinished like the Requiem, shows what his sacred writing did when it was not trimmed to a Salzburg service. A listener who works through those six works in sequence has heard the counter-case entire.

Honesty requires the concession that completes the section, because the counter-case is often overstated by its own side. The minor-key works are a minority of the catalogue, a few dozen works among six hundred, and the darkness in them is rationed by a composer whose professional life required serenades, dances, teaching pieces and divertimenti by the yard. The rationing, however, is itself the artistic fact. Contrast is a structural resource, and a composer who writes darkness constantly spends its value; Mozart reserves the minor mode and the full tragic register for chosen occasions, which is why their arrival registers as an event rather than a climate. The prettiness charge, set against the whole catalogue rather than its packaging, reduces to the observation that most of his music is in major keys and beautifully finished, which is true, and the inference that beauty of finish excludes depth of content, which the six works above refute by existing. Between the charge and the refutation stands only the listener's willingness to leave the dozen famous pieces and enter the catalogue. That leaving is harder than it sounds, for reasons of exposure that the next section examines.

## Is he overexposed rather than overrated

The two charges are habitually confused, and separating them is the most useful service this article can perform for the ordinary listener. Overrated is a claim about the estimate: that informed opinion credits the music with more than the music contains. Overexposed is a claim about the delivery system: that the music reaches listeners so constantly, so casually and in such a narrow selection that the encounter rate exceeds any listener's capacity to hear it freshly. The first claim was tested in sections nineteen to twenty-three and failed on two criteria of three. The second claim is true, and demonstrably so. A serenade composed as evening entertainment for a patron in 1787, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, has become hold music, advertising music and lift music across the planet. The opening of the C major piano sonata, K. 545, meets most of its hearers as a beginner's exercise, the piece the teaching tradition feeds to children, so that millions encounter one of the subtlest sonata movements ever written as homework. Salzburg sells his face on chocolate, first manufactured in 1890 by the confectioner Paul Furst, and the anniversary industries examined in section twenty-nine have twice in a generation carpeted the world with his name for a calendar year at a time.

### Is Mozart overrated?

On the criteria this article fixed in advance, no: the estimate survives audit on melodic invention and dramatic characterisation, and shares the honours on structural command. The irritation the question usually expresses is real but misaddressed. It belongs to the exposure machine, the programming, packaging and merchandising that deliver a fraction of the catalogue as wallpaper.

The distinction matters because the remedies are opposites. If a composer is overrated, the remedy is demotion, and the listener is licensed to stop listening. If he is overexposed, demotion punishes the wrong party, and the remedy is curation: different works, whole works, works heard in the foreground with the attention they were built to command. The backlash mechanism deserves a sentence of its own, because it manufactures false evidence for the overrated claim. A listener who has heard the same serenade movement four hundred times as background develops a genuine fatigue, and then misreads the fatigue as a property of the music rather than of the delivery, and reports the composer as lightweight with the full sincerity of first-hand experience. Glenn Gould's verdict, treated in section twenty-four as the strongest form of the prettiness charge, is partly vulnerable to the same analysis: a pianist who spent a career in recording studios, building an idealised Bach of total clarity, heard Mozart's fluency through an exposure history few musicians share. The verdict stands as criticism. But it is criticism aimed at an encounter pattern quite as much as at a catalogue, and the difference decides what a fair listener should do next.

## Familiarity and the cost of ubiquity

Ubiquity taxes music in ways that are worth specifying, because the tax falls hardest on the effects this particular composer builds most carefully. Mozart's scores are engineered around surprise administered inside perfect manners: an entry delayed by a bar so that its arrival lands as wit, a development that wanders one room too far from home so that the return feels earned, a phrase that repeats with a single note altered so that recognition itself becomes the joke or the pang. Every one of those effects is destroyed by over-familiarity, because an effect that depends on expectation being wrong-footed cannot survive being heard four hundred times. The wallpaper Mozart of the public world is therefore not merely a reduced Mozart; he is an inverted one, since the music's principal devices are precisely the ones repetition disarms. A Beethoven symphony survives background hearing better, because its rhetoric is built from insistence; an art of surprise has no such armour.

The recording and programming industries compound the damage by concentration. Of more than six hundred works, the public catalogue in practice consists of perhaps twenty: two symphonies, four concertos, one serenade, one unfinished Requiem, the same three operas in rotation. The remaining nineteen twentieths of the output, the church music complete, the divertimenti and serenades written as functional craft, the early operas, the concert arias, the chamber music with piano, sit unplayed, and the public judgement of shallowness is delivered on the played fraction by listeners who reasonably believe they have heard the composer. The cost of ubiquity is thus double: the famous works are worn smooth by repetition while the unknown works that would complicate the picture stay silent. There is a practical remedy, and it belongs in a debate article because the debate is sustained by the disease it would cure. Hear the operas whole and in sequence, from Idomeneo to The Magic Flute, and the dramatist replaces the tunesmith. Hear the serenades as what they were, superb functional music for named occasions, and the craftsman replaces the porcelain figure. Hear the liturgical music and the chamber works written for friends, and a working life replaces a brand. None of this requires new scholarship; it requires only a redistribution of attention across a catalogue that has been sitting open for two centuries, waiting for the exposure machine to widen its playlist by one shelf.

The economics of recording explain why the widening is slow, and they are worth a paragraph because they convert the problem from taste into structure. A record company amortises a famous work across a famous name with minimal risk, while a first recording of an unknown serenade or a concert aria must sell the work and the discovery together. Broadcasters face the same arithmetic in miniature: a movement everyone recognises holds an audience through an advertisement break, and an unknown quartet, however fine, is a wager. Each institution, acting rationally on its own ledger, renews the same twenty-work playlist, and the aggregate of all that private rationality is a public Mozart a thirtieth of the size of the real one. No conspiracy keeps the rest of the catalogue silent. Arithmetic does, which is why the remedy lies with the institutions' customers as much as with the institutions: every complete opera bought, every unfamiliar work requested and attended, is a small counter-entry in the ledger that decides what the next generation will be overexposed to.

## What the commercial afterlife does to the music

The name became an industry early, and the industry's history explains much of the modern debate, since most listeners argue about the industry while believing they are arguing about the music. Constanze Mozart, widowed at twenty-nine with two children and debts, understood the asset she held: she staged benefit performances of the Requiem, promoted the completion story that raised the work's value, and between 1799 and 1800 negotiated the sale of the bulk of her husband's autograph manuscripts to the Offenbach publisher Johann Andre, who issued works in editions for decades afterward. The first complete edition, published by Breitkopf and Hartel in Leipzig between 1877 and 1883, fixed the corpus as a monument; Ludwig von Kochel's catalogue of 1862 had already given every work its number, turning a chaotic estate of manuscripts into a navigable product line that any amateur could order from. The Neue Mozart-Ausgabe, the scholarly complete edition begun in 1955, replaced monument with critical text, while the Salzburg Festival, founded in 1920 by men including the composer Richard Strauss and the director Max Reinhardt, built the pilgrimage economy that the chocolate Mozartkugel, a confectioner's product of 1890, came to symbolise. Two anniversary floods, the bicentenary of the death in 1991 and the two hundred and fiftieth birthday year in 2006, each carpeted the world's shops, stamps, concerts and broadcasts with the name for a full calendar cycle.

Commercial success on this scale does the music two opposite services. It preserves: the catalogue's survival rate is extraordinary for the eighteenth century, and it survives because generations of publishers could sell it. And it distorts: the market rewards the serenade over the Mass, the excerpt over the act, the recognisable over the demanding, so that the commercially fittest Mozart and the artistically central Mozart drift apart, and criticism of the first is forever being mistaken for criticism of the second. What later musicians, as distinct from the market, took from him is a different and cleaner story: Beethoven studying the scores and writing variations on the opera themes, Rossini calling himself a Mozartian, Tchaikovsky building a suite in homage, each taking identifiable techniques rather than an aura. That transmission, technique by technique, is traced in [the account of Mozart's influence on music](/2010/12/07/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-influence-on-music/), and it matters to this debate because it is the one reputational channel the myths cannot contaminate: composers do not build on wallpaper. The industry argues with its own shadow in anniversary years. The influence record, kept by the only jury whose verdicts are made of work, sits quietly underneath, still returning the same judgement it returned in 1785.

## Which contemporaries he is unfairly measured against

The debate's collateral damage falls on the men who shared his century, because a mythology needs a supporting cast, and his contemporaries have been cast ever since as foils: the pedants, the also-rans, the mediocrities against whom the miracle shines. The arrangement is unjust in specific, checkable ways. Joseph Haydn, twenty-four years his senior, invented the string quartet as a serious medium and rebuilt the symphony across a career twice as long; he then lived to write The Creation and The Seasons after Mozart was dead, works of a man in his sixties still expanding. To read Haydn as a John the Baptist figure, a forerunner whose function was to prepare the way, is to invert the chronology of influence in the very genre where the two men exchanged it most equally, and Haydn's own verdict on the younger man, delivered to Leopold in 1785, was the generosity of a master who did not experience the comparison as theft. Antonio Salieri's case has been made in section three: judged for two centuries against a murder he did not commit, in place of the operas and the teaching that constituted his actual career.

The wider bench suffers the same flattening. Muzio Clementi, the pianist-composer who contested a famous keyboard duel with Mozart before the emperor in 1781, built a piano technique and a publishing empire that Beethoven admired and used; his sonatas were still being studied when the foil treatment had reduced him to an anecdote about a contest. The anecdote is worth pausing on, because it contains its own refutation: the emperor staged the contest as an entertainment between the two most formidable keyboard players available to his court, and Mozart's private letters about the evening, dismissive of his rival in the way of young competitors writing home, have been quoted ever since as though a partisan's match report were a judgement of history. Clementi lived to seventy-nine, out-earned every musician in this article, manufactured the pianos his rivals played on, and left a body of sonatas that fed directly into Beethoven's. A foil with that biography is not a foil; he is a parallel career that the mythology had to shrink before it could use him. Vicente Martin y Soler, a Spaniard working in Vienna, wrote the opera Una cosa rara, which in the very season of Figaro was the larger Viennese success, quoted inside Don Giovanni by Mozart himself as a compliment between professionals; posterity kept the quotation and discarded the opera. Leopold Kozeluch and Johann Baptist Vanhal wrote the symphonies and quartets that filled the same salons and subscription lists, competently, inventively and in Vanhal's case with an emotional range his modern revival keeps surprising listeners with. Ruth Halliwell, the scholar of the family correspondence, shows in her work on the Mozarts how much of the foil structure originates in Leopold's own letters, where rival musicians appear as obstacles in a sales narrative; the father's marketing categories became history's cast list. A comparison in which the contemporaries must lose for the subject to shine is not a comparison at all. It is the mythology wearing the costume of judgement, and section thirty-one sets out what the genuine article would require.

## What a fair comparison would require

The conditions for comparing composers fairly can be stated as plainly as the criteria were, and they disqualify most of the comparisons in circulation. Compare within genre and function first: a Mass against the Masses its composer was commissioned to write under the same liturgical limits, a subscription concerto against the concertos its rivals offered the same subscribers in the same season. Second, count the surviving corpus and price its preservation luck, since a reputation can be built on nine tenths of an output or on the lucky tenth of another man's, and the two cases are not parallel. Third, price the working conditions: patronage or market, deadlines or leisure, a court orchestra on salary or an ad hoc band rehearsed once, because music written for known players by Friday is a different athletic event from music written for posterity at leisure. Fourth, separate the documented achievement from the biography's entertainment value, so that no candidate draws marks for his death, his poverty or his murderer. A teacher laying such a comparison out for a class, criterion by criterion and condition by condition, can open the material in [the browser-based presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and keep each axis of the comparison on its own screen, which is roughly the discipline the whole exercise demands of the mind in any case.

Applied to the field this article has surveyed, the conditions redistribute praise without confiscating it. Haydn gains when longevity and invention across a whole art form's rebuilding are priced in. Salieri gains his actual career back. Clementi gains the piano. The forgotten professionals of the Viennese subscription lists regain the context that made Mozart's achievement legible as an achievement, since a standard can only be measured against the level of the room. Mozart loses the miracle and keeps the titles established in sections twenty to twenty-three. Nothing in the redistribution requires his demotion; it requires only that the supporting cast be restored from foil to colleague, which is what the documents describe in the first place. The fair comparison turns out to be not a threat to the central reputation but the first setting in which that reputation is fully visible, because a summit is measured by the range around it, and the range around this one was higher, busier and more accomplished than the mythology, which needed a desert, ever allowed.

## Mozart myths and legacy: what survives when the myths are removed

The third loop can now be closed, and the answer is plainer than the mythology and stronger than the debunking usually leaves behind. Remove the murderer, and there remains a professional relationship between two colleagues that the record shows functioning, in public, to the end. Remove the miracle, and there remains a child whose ability was independently verified at eight and whose education was the most thorough of the century, the two facts in harness rather than in competition. Remove the effortlessness, and there remains a worker whose sketches, corrections and abandoned fragments document a process, working at a speed his rivals remarked on and at a standard they could not reach. Remove the destitution, and there remains a high earner whose household outran a collapsing market, a story with the dignity of ordinary economic truth. Remove the deathbed dictation, and there remains an unfinished masterpiece, a widow's commercial problem, and a pupil who finished the job and copied it out fair so the seam would not show. What is left of him once every myth has been removed is, in short, everything that was ever actually his.

The sixth and final rung of the ladder can be set down in the terms the criteria sections earned. With every myth removed, the case for him narrows to two criteria, dramatic characterisation and melodic invention, and on those it is stronger than the mythology ever made it. Stronger, because the mythology's case depended on events that did not happen, and a case that depends on a murder collapses with the murder. The narrowed case depends on evidence any listener can test: open the Figaro ensembles and count the simultaneous viewpoints held in balance; take the themes other masters borrowed as raw material and ask what made them currency; set the minor-key concertos beside the serenades and watch the porcelain charge dissolve. The wider question of durability, of [what makes music last for centuries](/2026/10/21/what-makes-music-last-for-centuries/), is its own problem and is treated elsewhere in this series; within this article the survival is explained without residue by the two criteria. A reputation that needs no invention is the rarest kind. Stripped of five beliefs, four of them flattering, the subject of this debate does not shrink. He becomes, for the first time in the popular telling, a colleague of the men around him and a worker in a trade, and the music keeps the dimensions it always had.

## What would have to be true for the claim to fail

A case that cannot state its own defeat conditions is a creed, so this article closes its argument by stating them. The claim for Mozart's standing, as reconstructed here, rests on melodic invention and dramatic characterisation. For the claim to fail on the first criterion, the themes would have to be shown, at scale, to be adaptations of named models by other composers, transferred without transformation; individual borrowings exist and are documented, as they are for every composer of a century that treated themes as common stock, but the demonstration would need the pattern, hundreds of cases deep, and no scholar has produced it. For the claim to fail on the second criterion, the characterisation heard in the ensembles would have to be shown to be a projection of performers and productions rather than a property of the scores; that demonstration would need to explain away the notation itself, the differentiated writing for each voice in the Figaro finales, which sits on the page independent of any performance tradition. Neither demonstration has been made. Until one is, the claim stands on the evidence described.

The same discipline applied to the article's two open questions keeps them honestly open rather than rhetorically closed. The Salieri question would be settled by a contemporary document: an accusation in a dated hand, an official record, a letter from the supposed victim naming his fear of a person rather than of poison in the abstract. Researchers have had two centuries and the full run of the family's correspondence, and the document has not appeared; absence of that order, in an archive this dense, is itself evidence, though of the careful, negative kind. The prodigy question would be settled by a true control: a second pupil given Leopold's complete programme, including the Italian finishing years, whose adult output could then be weighed against his son's. Nannerl, for the reasons in section twelve, is the control history started and stopped. The matters that are open remain open in the structural sense this series requires: each side knows exactly what it needs, neither side has it, and the argument can resume the day the paper turns up. That is not an evasion. It is what an unsolved historical problem looks like when it is not being used as a stage set.

## The argument the myths were standing in for

Underneath the whole debate sits the discomfort the myths were invented to relieve, and naming it ends the article's work. The documents show a man of no visible extraordinariness in the conduct of his life: a professional who bargained over fees, cadged loans from a friend, met deadlines, quarrelled with an archbishop employer, wrote family jokes in his letters and kept a pet starling whose death he mourned in verse. The works show an extraordinariness that has never been matched for fertility within a single short life. The gap between the man in the letters and the mind in the scores is the true subject of the Mozart mythology, and every myth examined here is a bridge thrown across that gap: a murderer makes the death commensurate, a miracle makes the childhood commensurate, effortlessness makes the method commensurate, poverty makes the world's response commensurate. Each bridge spares the audience the work of accepting that the commensurateness is not there, that a life can be ordinary in every documentable respect and the work can still be what it is.

The serious argument about his standing, the one this article has tried to conduct, is what remains when the bridges are taken down. It is an argument about criteria and evidence, about what the Figaro ensembles demonstrably do and what the sketches demonstrably show, and it can be lost by its subject in a way a myth never can, which is exactly why winning it means something. The culture that prefers the bridges is not wicked; it is expressing, in story form, a real judgement that the phenomenon is disproportionate to its visible causes. The judgement is right about the disproportion and wrong about the remedy. Training, market, deadlines, a father's method and a widow's enterprise are the visible causes the record actually supplies, and they are not small causes merely because they are unglamorous. Mozart's standing does not need a villain, a pauper's grave or a voice from heaven. It needs what this debate, conducted on documents and criteria, has given it: two titles held outright, one shared, a catalogue that paid its own way through two centuries of other men's commerce, and the standing invitation, extended to every skeptic, to open the scores and check.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the most common myths about Mozart?

Five beliefs dominate the popular picture. The first is that Antonio Salieri murdered him out of envy, a story that originates in Pushkin's verse drama of 1830 rather than in any document. The second is that he composed effortlessly, whole works arriving complete in his mind, a picture drawn from a letter printed in 1815 whose authenticity collapsed under scrutiny. The third is that he died destitute and was buried as a pauper, refuted by account evidence showing high earnings, modest debts and a standard middle class burial. The fourth is that the prodigy needed no teaching, when in fact his pedagogue father gave him the most systematic musical education of the century. The fifth is that the Requiem was dictated to Salieri in a deathbed scene invented by a film. Each belief replaces a document with a drama, and the detailed evidence against each is set out in the sections above.

### Q: Where did the Salieri poisoning story come from?

It grew in stages, and every stage can be dated. After Mozart's death in 1791, rumour in Vienna spoke vaguely of jealous rivals, and the early biographies reported that the dying composer himself had feared he might have been poisoned, a fear recorded at second hand through his widow's family and naming nobody. No contemporary accused Salieri, no authority charged him, and no investigation ever tested the idea. The villain was supplied by literature: in 1830 Alexander Pushkin built a short verse tragedy, Mozart and Salieri, on the envy theme, and Rimsky-Korsakov's opera of 1898 carried the pairing across Europe. Rumours of an elderly Salieri confessing during an illness in 1823 were publicly rebutted by Giuseppe Carpani, who knew him. Peter Shaffer's play and the film that followed inherited the story from that dramatic lineage, not from any Viennese archive, and gave it its modern form.

### Q: Who was Antonio Salieri really?

He was one of the most successful musicians in Habsburg Europe. Born in Legnago in 1750 and brought to Vienna at sixteen, he became court composer to Joseph II in 1774 and Imperial Kapellmeister, the most senior musical office in the capital, in 1788, holding the post until 1824. His operas were staged from Naples to Paris, and his Axur, re d'Ormus was a Viennese success performed for years. He also stood at the centre of the city's musical teaching: Beethoven came to him for instruction in setting Italian words, Schubert studied counterpoint with him and remained his protégé, and the young Liszt received lessons from him in the early 1820s. His own operas faded after his death in 1825 for ordinary reasons of fashion, as Italian opera seria left the repertory. The jealous mediocrity of the drama is an inversion of this record, and restoring the man requires no inflation: an accomplished, honoured professional, eclipsed afterward.

### Q: Did Mozart and Salieri actually work together?

Yes, on the record, and the occasions are documented in detail. In 1785 the two men, with a third colleague named Cornetti, composed a joint cantata for the singer Nancy Storace, celebrating her return to the Vienna stage after an illness that had threatened her voice; the words were by Lorenzo Da Ponte. In October 1791 Mozart invited Salieri to a performance of The Magic Flute and wrote to his wife Constanze that the older composer had listened with close attention and applauded warmly from the overture to the final chorus, a report made privately, with no public to impress. The two also competed inside the same court economy for commissions, singers and imperial favour, which the letters reflect as ordinary professional friction. After Mozart's death, Salieri taught the composer's younger son, Franz Xaver, in composition, an entrustment by the widow that sits impossibly beside any family suspicion of murder.

### Q: How accurate is the Amadeus portrayal?

It is accurate about the setting and invented about the story. The Vienna of the play and film, a court economy ruled by an emperor's taste, a freelance composer assembling teaching, concerts and commissions into a living, is faithful to the letters and accounts. The treatment of the music is serious, and the anonymous Requiem commission at the heart of the plot has a documented core: Count Franz von Walsegg did send an unnamed messenger in 1791, intending to pass the work off as his own. The inventions are the load-bearing ones. Salieri neither murdered Mozart nor took dictation at his deathbed; the Requiem was completed by the pupil Sussmayr after Eybler withdrew. The asylum confession is a frame built from rumours of 1823. The infantile, helpless composer contradicts a correspondence full of fees negotiated, series planned and business conducted. Enjoy it as a great drama about envy. Do not cite it as biography.

### Q: Was Mozart's ability innate or trained?

The honest answer is that the evidence documents both, in unusual strength, and the surviving argument is about proportion. For the innate side: a Royal Society fellow named Daines Barrington tested the eight-year-old in London in 1764, intending to expose a fraud, verified his age from the baptismal record, and reported abilities of sight-reading, improvisation and ear that coaching could not explain; the boy also wrote out Allegri's Miserere from memory in Rome at fourteen. For the training side: his father Leopold was a leading pedagogue whose violin method of 1756 was a European standard, the grand tours functioned as a curriculum through every musical capital, Johann Christian Bach mentored him in London, and Padre Martini corrected his counterpoint exercises in Bologna. No other child of the century received that input. The question cannot be closed, because the one natural control, his sister, was withdrawn from the experiment halfway, as the next answer explains.

### Q: What does Nannerl's career tell us about the prodigy question?

Maria Anna Mozart received the same father's tuition in the same household, toured as co-star of the family enterprise from 1762, and was praised across Europe as a keyboard player of the first rank while still a teenager. She is therefore the closest thing the prodigy debate has to a control: same teacher, same stage, same start. After 1769 the provision diverged. Her brother was taken to Italy three times, into the opera houses, the academies and Martini's counterpoint lessons; she remained in Salzburg, later teaching pupils there for decades. She composed, her brother's letters praise a song she sent him, but none of her music survives because nothing in her position preserved it. Her career points toward training as a decisive factor, since she plateaued where the advanced provision stopped, yet it cannot settle the question: composition, the variable that matters most, was never demanded of her, so the experiment was never completed on the control subject either.

### Q: Did Mozart compose without drafts?

No, and the surviving paper is the refutation. More than three hundred sketch leaves and fragments are catalogued by the scholar Ulrich Konrad: themes tried and set aside, openings for works never finished, passages worked over before reaching a fair copy. The autograph scores carry deletions, crossings-out and rewritten passages. Works were abandoned at every career stage, a concerto here, a quartet movement there, an opera when its commission collapsed. The source of the contrary belief is a letter printed by Friedrich Rochlitz in 1815 describing whole compositions arriving finished in the composer's mind; its authenticity is rejected by modern scholarship, which reads it as a construction fitted to the romantic market for genius testimony. Mozart's own dedication of the six quartets to Haydn calls them the fruit of long and laborious study, and the three years those quartets occupied him bear the description out. He composed at remarkable speed. Speed is fluency, not effortlessness.

### Q: Was Mozart poor?

He was, in his final years, short of money, which is a different thing. The financial record rebuilt by the historian Volkmar Braunbehrens from accounts, contracts and letters shows an Imperial Chamber Composer's salary of eight hundred florins a year from 1787, a fee of four hundred and fifty florins for The Marriage of Figaro, and peak years in the middle 1780s when concerts, pupils and publication brought in several times the salary. He spent at that level: a large apartment at four hundred and sixty florins a year, servants, medical cures for his wife. After 1788, war emptied the subscription rooms and income fell while spending habits held; he borrowed some fourteen hundred florins from his friend Michael Puchberg across three years, and the estate papers at his death record debts of roughly nine hundred florins against respectable furniture, a fortepiano and a large library. That is a cash-flow crisis in a high-earning household, not destitution, and his common grave was standard middle class practice under the burial law of the time.

### Q: Why do people prefer the poverty version?

Because it does emotional work that the ledger version cannot. A starving genius turns a business outcome into a moral verdict: the world failed to recognise worth, and the modern listener, playing the records and filling the halls, gets to stand on the innocent side of that verdict, one of the people who would have recognised him. The story also certifies the music by the market's refusal of it, as though the price a war-emptied city would not pay were a measurement of value. Nineteenth-century biography, which built the romantic image of the artist, needed the story for its readers, who were themselves the market and preferred the blame placed on rivals, emperors and cabals rather than on the indifference of ordinary buyers. The documented alternative, a prosperous household overtaken by a downturn and a death, offers embarrassment instead of martyrdom, and embarrassment cannot be carved into a statue. Preference here is not an error of information; it is a choice of feeling, which is why information alone so rarely displaces it.

### Q: Does dying young inflate a musical reputation?

It changes the reputation's shape rather than its size. A composer who dies at thirty-five leaves no late period for critics to quarrel over, no decline to discount, and an output curve that points upward at the moment it stops, in Mozart's case with The Magic Flute, the Clarinet Concerto and the Requiem commission in a single final year. Posterity also credits him silently with the unwritten decades, an unfalsifiable premium no long-lived rival receives. Against that, early death endangers the works themselves: manuscripts scatter and unpublished music can vanish, as nearly happened to Schubert, dead at thirty-one with hundreds of works unprinted. Mozart escaped that fate because his widow ran the estate as a business and publishers bought the autographs. The comparison across short careers shows the constant is not the death but the transfer of power that follows it, to widows, pupils, publishers and editors. Inflation happens to the narrative around the music. The scores are unaffected.

### Q: Is Mozart the greatest composer?

The question has no answer until its criteria are fixed, and under the three criteria this article fixed in advance the verdict is mixed and strong. On melodic invention he wins outright: hundreds of themes across more than six hundred works that survive transplantation into other composers' hands, Beethoven's variation sets and Tchaikovsky's Mozartiana among them. On dramatic characterisation he wins within his century: the Figaro ensembles hold five incompatible viewpoints in a single musical span, a feat no contemporary matched. On structural command he shares the honours at the scale of the movement and the act, and yields the large edifice to Bach behind him and Beethoven ahead of him; for contrapuntal architecture as an end in itself, and for development across huge spans, those two stand apart. So the crown dissolves into a schedule of particular supremacies, two held outright and one shared, against the strongest field the art form offers. That is a more defensible, and finally more impressive, claim than the unexamined superlative.

### Q: Should I watch Amadeus before studying Mozart?

Watch it, but in the right order and with the right label attached. The play and film are serious artworks about envy, and the film's treatment of the music, long passages allowed to run as argument, has sent generations of listeners to the scores, which is a genuine service. The risk is sequence: a viewer who meets the invented Salieri first will spend his early study unlearning a murder, a deathbed dictation, an asylum confession and an infantile composer, none of which survive contact with the letters. The safer order is to read a documentary biography first, or the family letters themselves, and then watch the film as what its author called it, a fantasia on a theme, appreciating how its drama is built from rumour, Pushkin and the anecdote stock of two centuries. Students who reverse the order usually still arrive at the record; they simply pay a toll in confusion that the record never charged. Treat the film as the debate's most famous exhibit, not as its evidence.

### Q: Is it possible to hear Mozart freshly after so much exposure?

Yes, but freshness has to be engineered, because the exposure machine works against it. The famous fraction of the catalogue, perhaps twenty works of more than six hundred, is delivered as background, in lifts, advertisements and teaching studios, until effects built on surprise, the delayed entry, the altered repeat, the development that wanders too far from home, are worn smooth. The remedy is curation rather than abstinence. Hear the operas whole and in sequence, so the dramatist replaces the tunesmith. Hear the works the playlist ignores: the church music, the concert arias, the chamber music with piano, the serenades understood as superb functional craft for named occasions. Hear even the famous works in the foreground, complete, with the attention a concert of his own subscription series would have demanded. Listeners who run that programme consistently report the same discovery, that the composer they had filed as pretty and familiar was neither. The fatigue was real, but it belonged to the delivery system, not to the catalogue.

### Q: How do I check a biographical claim about a composer?

Work through four tests, in order. First, date the claim's earliest appearance: a story that surfaces decades after the event, as the poisoning confession and the effortless-composition letter did, carries a burden a contemporary document does not. Second, ask who recorded it and what they gained: a father soliciting engagements, a widow selling an estate, an editor feeding a market for genius anecdotes, a dramatist building a play. Third, check the claim against the hard paper of the life, the letters written at the time, the contracts, the account books and the estate inventories, which were made for purposes other than fame and therefore lie less fluently. Fourth, classify what survives the tests with the vocabulary the claim deserves: what was alleged, what was charged, what was proved, what was settled, and what was never tested at all. A claim that began as gossip does not become history by being repeated in hard covers. These tests are not pedantry; they are how the documented Mozart was recovered from the dramatised one.

### Q: How do I separate a film's version from the record?

Start by identifying the film's sources, because a dramatist always has them. Amadeus descends from Pushkin's verse tragedy of 1830, from the early biographies by Niemetschek and Nissen, and from two centuries of anecdote; knowing the lineage tells you which parts are inherited legend and which are the author's own welding. Then list the film's load-bearing scenes, the ones the whole structure hangs on, and check each against the documentary record: the deathbed dictation fails, since Sussmayr completed the Requiem as a business transaction managed by the widow; the asylum confession fails, since the rumours of 1823 were rebutted by a man who knew Salieri; the anonymous messenger passes, since Count Walsegg really did commission the work in secret. Finally, notice what the drama needed: a narrator with a grievance, a theology of genius, a single night in which everything resolves. Whatever serves those needs belongs to the film. Whatever the letters and accounts show belongs to the man.

### Q: Which works make the strongest case for Mozart?

The case is strongest where the two winning criteria operate together, in the operas and the great concertos. The Marriage of Figaro makes it in its ensembles, where five characters hold incompatible positions inside one musical argument without losing their separate voices. Don Giovanni makes it in the gap between its tidy sung moral and the terror and vitality the score has actually delivered. The Magic Flute makes it by placing a simple folk-like tune and a stratospheric aria of vengeance inside a single act and making both belong. Among the concertos, the D minor, K. 466, and the C minor, K. 491, show the soloist and orchestra in genuine contention, drama for two characters without words. The Symphony No. 40 in G minor and the String Quintet in the same key, K. 516, refute the charge of prettiness in purely instrumental terms. A listener who knows those works knows the case at its full strength, and the case no longer needs a legend to carry it.

### Q: What should a skeptical listener hear first?

Hear the works the exposure machine does not play, in the order that answers the skepticism directly. Begin with the String Quintet in G minor, K. 516, twenty minutes in which the porcelain image cannot survive the first movement. Follow it with the Piano Concerto No. 24 in C minor, K. 491, where the scale, the wind writing and the soloist's embattled relationship with the orchestra point straight at the century to come. Then take an opera whole, Don Giovanni if the skeptic doubts the drama, The Marriage of Figaro if he doubts the humanity, and listen for the ensembles rather than the famous tunes. Finish with the first movement of the Symphony No. 40 in G minor, which he will think he knows, and count how much of its agitation had been edited out by familiarity. The sequence is designed as a cross-examination: each work testifies against a different count of the indictment, prettiness, slightness, decoration and facility, and the verdict is left where it belongs, with the listener.

### Q: Which contemporaries deserve more attention?

Begin with the man the mythology wronged. Salieri's best operas, Axur, re d'Ormus among them, repay hearing as the polished professional article they were, and his historical position as teacher of Beethoven, Schubert and Liszt makes his own music a document of the era's standards. Haydn needs no rescue, but he deserves release from the forerunner role: the London symphonies and the late oratorios were written after Mozart's death by a master still expanding in his sixties. Clementi's piano sonatas, admired and used by Beethoven, show a keyboard imagination that the famous duel anecdote has buried. Martin y Soler's Una cosa rara, the Viennese hit of Figaro's own season, lets a listener hear what the same audience actually rewarded. Kozeluch and Vanhal, the working professionals of the subscription lists, restore the level of the room against which Mozart's achievement was measured at the time. Hearing the contemporaries does not diminish the central figure. It gives him back his century.

### Q: What changes about Mozart once the myths are removed?

Everything except the music, which is the point. The murderer goes, and in his place stands a colleague who collaborated on a cantata, applauded at The Magic Flute and taught the widow's son. The miracle goes, and in its place stands a verified child prodigy harnessed to the most systematic education of the century, verified at eight by a skeptic from the Royal Society and finished in the opera houses of Italy. The effortlessness goes, and three hundred sketch leaves, a dedication calling the Haydn quartets the fruit of long and laborious study, and a desk full of abandoned fragments take its place. The pauper goes, and a high earner with a cash-flow crisis stands in the ledgers instead. What remains is a reputation resting on testable ground: melodic invention and dramatic characterisation held outright, structural command shared, and a catalogue that paid its way through two centuries of other men's commerce. The figure who emerges is smaller than the legend and larger than the debunking, a worker whose work survived every story told about it.
