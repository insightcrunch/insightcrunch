The distance between a reform report and the statute it produces is usually invisible, because only the statute survives in the law reports and only the statute is ever read. The Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013 is the rare Indian amendment whose full trail survives in public: a committee report written in thirty days under street pressure, an ordinance promulgated eleven days after that report, and an Act passed by Parliament seven weeks later which differed from both. The three documents can be laid side by side, recommendation by recommendation, and the differences measured. That measurement is the subject of this article, and it supports a claim worth naming at the outset. The narrowing sequence is this: at each step from report to ordinance to Act, the recommendations that carried the highest political cost were the ones most likely to be dropped, softened, or reversed, while the recommendations that cost the political system little, the new offences and the higher punishments, passed through almost intact and were in one case strengthened beyond what the committee had proposed. The 2013 amendment is therefore not only a change in the law of sexual offences. It is a calibrated record of what Indian legislative reform can absorb when public pressure is at its maximum, and of what it cannot absorb even then.

![The Parliament of India and the passage of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013 after the Verma Committee report - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-73.webp)

A reader who finishes this article will be able to perform that measurement unaided. The article traces the state of the law before December 2012, the constitution and method of the Justice J S Verma Committee, the full spread of its recommendations, the respects in which the ordinance of February 2013 departed from the report it claimed to implement, and the further respects in which the Act of March and April 2013 departed from the ordinance. It then works through the enacted law itself: the new offences in Sections 354A to 354D of the Indian Penal Code, the acid attack and trafficking provisions, the rewritten definition of rape in Section 375, the punishment ladder in Sections 376 to 376E, the procedural and evidentiary changes in the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act, and the sequel in the courts and in the amendment of 2018. The refusals receive the same space as the enactments, because in an amendment trail the refusals are data of the same quality as the text. The marital rape exception that the committee asked Parliament to delete, the sanction shield for armed forces personnel that the committee asked Parliament to lower, the command responsibility it proposed, and the death penalty it expressly advised against and Parliament enacted anyway, together describe the outer boundary of the possible in 2013 more precisely than any list of new sections can.

## The Law as It Stood Before December 2012

The law of sexual offences that the protests of December 2012 confronted was old, narrow, and in places archaic in its vocabulary. Rape was defined by Section 375 of the Indian Penal Code as sexual intercourse by a man with a woman in five described circumstances, against her will, without her consent, with consent obtained by fear or by impersonation of her husband, with consent given through unsoundness or intoxication when she could not understand what she was consenting to, and with or without consent when she was under sixteen years of age. The definition reached one act only. A range of penetrative violations that any modern code would treat as equivalent in gravity fell outside it and had to be prosecuted, if they were prosecuted at all, under provisions designed for lesser wrongs. Section 354 punished assault or criminal force to a woman with intent to outrage her modesty, a phrase the courts had construed for generations and which carried a maximum of two years. Section 509 punished words, gestures, and acts intended to insult the modesty of a woman, with a maximum of one year. Between the single grave offence of rape and these two modesty offences there was almost nothing: no offence of stalking, no offence of voyeurism, no offence of sexual harassment as such in the Penal Code, no offence of disrobing, and no specific offence of acid attack, which had to be charged as grievous hurt under provisions written for a different kind of violence.

The punishment structure matched the definitional narrowness. Rape carried a minimum of seven years under Section 376, extendable to life, but the provision allowed a court, for adequate and special reasons recorded in the judgment, to impose less than the minimum, a proviso that sentencing courts had used in ways that reformers had criticised for decades. Custodial and aggravated categories existed within Section 376(2), but the graded ladder of distinct aggravated offences, gang rape as a separately defined crime with its own minimum, and offences built around abuse of authority were absent or compressed. Trafficking for sexual exploitation was not an offence of the Penal Code at all in its modern sense: Section 370 addressed the buying or disposing of a person as a slave, a nineteenth century provision aimed at a different trade, while trafficking was handled by a separate statute addressed to prostitution and by general provisions on kidnapping and procuration that did not describe the offence that recruiters, transporters, and brothel keepers actually committed.

The deeper inheritance was a body of doctrine that placed the complainant's character and conduct at the centre of the trial. The acquittal in the Mathura case, Tukaram v State of Maharashtra in 1978, in which the Supreme Court treated the absence of injuries and the complainant's sexual history as reasons to disbelieve a young tribal girl who said she had been raped inside a police station, had produced an open letter from four law professors, a public campaign, and the Criminal Law (Second Amendment) Act of 1983, which introduced custodial rape categories and a presumption of absence of consent in Section 114A of the Evidence Act for specified prosecutions. That earlier amendment trail matters here because it established the pattern the 2013 trail would repeat at greater speed: a scandal, a campaign, a legislative response that accepted the punishment and procedure recommendations and declined the structural ones. By 2012 a further reform bill, the Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill of that year, was already before Parliament and under examination by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs, proposing modest increases in some punishments. Its existence is a useful corrective to the idea that reform began in December 2012. What began in December 2012 was not reform but the collapse of the ordinary timetable of reform.

### What offences against women existed in the Penal Code before 2013?

Before 2013 the Penal Code's offences against women were rape under Section 375, assault with intent to outrage modesty under Section 354 carrying up to two years, and insulting modesty under Section 509 carrying up to one year, plus kidnapping and cruelty provisions for particular situations. There was no offence of stalking, voyeurism, sexual harassment, disrobing, or acid attack.

The procedural law offered a complainant little protection against the process itself. Her statement would be recorded at a police station in the ordinary way, with no statutory requirement that a woman officer record it; the medical examination regime carried the discredited two finger test in many hospitals, under which a doctor recorded an opinion on whether the woman was habituated to sexual intercourse, an inquiry with no forensic value that functioned in court as a comment on her credibility; and the Evidence Act permitted cross-examination directed at her general immoral character in the situations Section 155(4) allowed, so that a rape trial could become an examination of the complainant's life. The conviction figures that circulated in public argument were contested then and remain contested, and this article asserts none of them. What is not contested is the structure: a narrow central offence, archaic satellite offences, no statutory architecture for the conduct in between, and a trial process in which the complainant's character was formally in issue. It was this structure, and not only the facts of one crime, that the Verma Committee was asked to rebuild.

## The Earlier Trail: Mathura, the 1983 Amendment, and the Shape of Reform to Come

An amendment trail is read correctly only when the statute's earlier trails are kept in view, because the 2013 amendment was the second time the Indian Parliament rewrote the law of rape under the pressure of a single case and a public campaign, and the parallels discipline any claim that 2013 was unprecedented. The earlier case was that of Mathura, a young tribal girl who was raped by two policemen inside a police station in Maharashtra in 1972. The sessions court acquitted, reasoning in terms of her being habituated to sexual intercourse; the Bombay High Court convicted; and the Supreme Court, in Tukaram v State of Maharashtra in 1978, acquitted again, treating the absence of injuries, the absence of an alarm raised, and her supposed sexual history as grounds to characterise the encounter as a peaceful affair. Four professors of law, Upendra Baxi, Raghunath Kelkar, Lotika Sarkar, and Vasudha Dhagamwar, published an open letter to the Chief Justice of India protesting the judgment's reasoning, and the letter became the organising document of a national campaign that is generally taken to mark the beginning of the modern Indian women's movement's engagement with criminal law.

The legislative product of that campaign, the Criminal Law (Second Amendment) Act 1983, is worth itemising because so much of the 2013 argument was conducted in its shadow. The 1983 Act created aggravated categories of custodial rape, covering rape by police officers, public servants, and the staff of jails, remand homes, and hospitals, a design the 2013 ladder inherited and extended. It inserted Section 114A into the Evidence Act, creating the presumption of absence of consent where the woman stated she had not consented, in prosecutions for the custodial and aggravated categories, the presumption the 2013 Act would widen. It provided for in camera trials of rape cases and strengthened Section 228A of the Penal Code, which punishes the disclosure of a victim's identity. It shifted the burden in custodial cases in part and raised the punishment floor, while its accompanying debate already contained, in early form, the argument about consent within marriage, which the campaign of that period raised and the Parliament of that period declined, exactly as its successor would thirty years later.

The lesson the 1983 trail teaches is not that reform fails. The custodial categories and the presumption were genuine and durable changes, and both are still recognisable inside the 2013 text. The lesson is that scandal driven amendment has a characteristic shape in India: it responds to the case that caused the scandal with provisions addressed to that case's configuration, custodial power in 1983, stranger violence and public space in 2013; it enacts the procedural protections that the campaign's lawyers drafted; and it defers, each time, the question that implicates the ordinary household rather than the aberrant official or the stranger, because that question's constituency is not in the street but in the chamber. The Verma Committee understood this history and wrote against it, which is why its report insisted on treating the home, the marital bedroom, and the chain of command as sites of the same legal problem as the street. The 2013 Act's departures from the report are partly explained by the older pattern reasserting itself over a committee that had tried to legislate outside it.

One further inheritance deserves mention because it conditions the evidence chapter of 2013. Between the two amendment trails, the courts had developed, without Parliament, a body of protective doctrine: directions on the anonymity of victims, on the recording of evidence, and on compensation through the victim compensation schemes that the Code of Criminal Procedure acquired in 2008 with Section 357A. The Sakshi organisation's litigation and the Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum case of 1995 had produced guidelines on the conduct of rape trials and the assistance of complainants. By 2012, therefore, a reformer did not start from the bare code of 1860 plus 1983. She started from a code supplemented by three decades of judicial patching, and the committee's task, which it performed, was in large part one of codifying and correcting that accumulated patchwork rather than inventing new law. Several of the Act's least noticed provisions, the extension of Section 228A's identity protection to the new Section 376E offence among them, are tidying operations of exactly this kind.

## The Crime and the Collapse of the Ordinary Timetable

On 16 December 2012 a young woman was gang raped and fatally injured in a moving bus in Delhi. This article follows the discipline of the brief that governs it: no case detail, no identification of the victim beyond what the public record of the legislation requires, and no graphic description, because the legislative trail does not need them. What the trail does need is the political sequence that followed. Protests began within days, centred in Delhi at India Gate and Raisina Hill and repeated in cities across the country, and they were unusual in composition and demand. The crowds were not organised by political parties, they persisted through police action against them, and their demand was not exhausted by punishment of the accused in the case itself. They demanded a changed law and a changed state response, and they demanded them immediately.

The government's first responses were administrative: fast track courts were announced for sexual offence trials in Delhi, committees were promised, and policing measures were listed. They did not meet the demand, because the demand was legislative and the ordinary legislative calendar was empty. Parliament's winter session ended without any vehicle for instant amendment, and the standing reform bill was still in committee. On 23 December 2012, a week after the crime, the government constituted a three member committee under Justice J S Verma, a former Chief Justice of India, with Justice Leila Seth, a former Chief Justice of the Himachal Pradesh High Court, and Gopal Subramanium, a former Solicitor General of India, charged with recommending amendments to the criminal law to provide for quicker trial and enhanced punishment for criminals accused of committing sexual assault against women. The committee's own timetable, thirty days, was set by its chairman against the date of the budget session. The committee reported on 23 January 2013. The ordinance followed on 3 February. The Act received assent on 2 April. From crime to enacted statute took under sixteen weeks, a speed without real parallel in Indian criminal legislation, and the speed is itself one of the causes of the narrowing this article traces: documents produced at this pace borrow their contents from whatever is already on the table, and what was on the table was a report, an earlier bill, and a set of political red lines that the pressure never dislodged.

## The Verma Committee: Method as Argument

The committee's composition did analytical work before it wrote a word. A former Chief Justice known for the Vishaka judgment on sexual harassment at the workplace, a former Chief Justice of a High Court who had served on the Law Commission, and a former Solicitor General gave the panel a profile that made its report difficult to dismiss as activist literature. Its method reinforced that profile. The committee invited public submissions and received, on its own count, around eighty thousand of them, from women's organisations, lawyers, academics, students, and ordinary correspondents in India and abroad, and it states that they were read and considered. It worked for twenty nine days from constitution to submission and produced a report of some six hundred and thirty pages that ranged far beyond the punishment clauses its terms of reference might have suggested: into the definition of the offences, the duties of police and hospitals, the law of evidence, the governance of the armed forces in disturbed areas, the disqualification of electoral candidates, the reform of medical examination protocols, and the content of school education.

The report's intellectual frame matters for everything that follows, because the narrowing sequence is best understood as the fate of that frame. The committee treated sexual violence as a failure of governance and of constitutional equality, not merely as a deficit of punishment. Its most quoted sentence of principle was that a marital or other relationship between the perpetrator and the victim is not a valid defence against the crimes of rape or sexual violation, endorsing the conclusion of the European Commission of Human Rights in C R v United Kingdom that a rapist remains a rapist regardless of his relationship with the victim. It treated deterrence claims for the death penalty as empirically unproven, noting the evidence placed before it, including that of the Working Group on Human Rights, that homicide rates had declined across two decades in which executions had slowed. It treated the state's own agents as part of the problem to be legislated for, recommending offences for police officers who refuse to register complaints and for the chain of command that tolerates sexual violence by subordinates. And it treated the complainant's dignity inside the process, in the recording of her statement, in her medical examination, and in cross-examination, as a subject for binding law rather than for circulars. A report of that breadth could not survive contact with an ordinance deadline and a parliamentary majority intact. The question this article answers is which parts of it did.

### How fast did the Verma Committee work, and on what evidence?

The committee was constituted on 23 December 2012 and submitted its report on 23 January 2013, twenty nine days later. It received around eighty thousand public submissions, heard experts and organisations, and produced a report of some six hundred and thirty pages covering offences, punishments, police duties, medical protocols, evidence, armed forces accountability, and electoral reform.

One feature of the report should be flagged because later sections return to it: the committee appended a draft Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill of its own. It did not merely recommend principles and leave drafting to the government. It proposed text: a widened Section 375, graded sexual assault provisions, new sections for the conduct offences, and punishment clauses calibrated, in its judgment, to what the evidence on deterrence and proportionality supported. The existence of committee drafted text is what makes the later comparison exact. Where the ordinance or the Act departs from the committee's text, the departure is a choice between two available formulations, not the absence of an alternative.

## What the Committee Recommended: The Full Map

The committee's recommendations fall into six groups, and the groups had different fates, so they are worth separating before the ordinance is reached. The first group concerned the offences themselves. The committee recommended that rape be redefined to cover non-consensual penetration of a sexual nature in its several forms, rather than the single act the old Section 375 described, and that consent be defined as an unequivocal voluntary agreement, so that submission could not be read as consent. It recommended new offences for the conduct that the old code had missed: voyeurism, stalking, intentional sexual touching and sexual harassment, assault with intent to disrobe, and acid attacks, each with graded punishments. It recommended that trafficking be brought into the Penal Code as a defined offence directed at recruitment, transport, and harbouring for exploitation. And it recommended a graded punishment structure in which simple rape carried seven years to life, rape of a minor and gang rape carried higher minimums, and rape causing death or a persistent vegetative state carried a minimum of twenty years extendable to imprisonment for the remainder of natural life.

The second group concerned what the committee refused to recommend, and a refusal by a committee of this standing is a recommendation in itself. It refused the death penalty. It examined the demand for capital punishment, which was then at its loudest, and concluded that there is no substantial evidence that the death penalty deters sexual violence, that the deterrent claim made for it is a myth on the record available, and that the graded ladder of long imprisonment, including imprisonment for the whole of natural life in the gravest cases, was the proportionate answer. It also refused to lower the age of criminal responsibility and addressed the juvenile justice demands of the moment by declining them. And on the age of consent it recommended sixteen years, the threshold the Penal Code had carried since before independence, in preference to the eighteen that the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012 had just enacted for its own offences, a position it reached by weighing the criminalisation of consensual adolescent relationships that a higher threshold produces.

The third group was structural, and it is the group that fared worst. The committee recommended the deletion of Exception 2 to Section 375, the marital rape exception, so that marriage would cease to be a defence. It recommended that the requirement of prior sanction for prosecuting members of the armed forces for sexual offences be removed, by amending the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Acts and the allied sanction provisions, so that a soldier accused of rape would face the ordinary criminal law rather than an internal process shielded by governmental permission. It recommended a doctrine of command responsibility, under which a superior officer who knew or ought to have known of sexual offences by those under his command and failed to prevent or report them would himself incur liability. And it recommended electoral consequences: changes to the law governing elections so that persons facing charges of sexual offences could not convert public office into a shield, together with directions addressed to the conduct of political parties in fielding candidates. Each of these four recommendations imposed a cost on an organised constituency: husbands as a class, the security establishment, the officer corps, and the political class itself. None of the four survived.

The fourth group concerned the state's duties to the complainant. The committee recommended that a police officer who refuses to register a complaint of a sexual offence should commit an offence, that hospitals should be bound to provide free immediate treatment, that the two finger test be abandoned and medical examination be governed by a protocol that records injury and collects forensic material without opining on the woman's sexual history, that statements of complainants be recorded promptly and, it proposed, with videography in the scheme it drafted, and that a state funded compensation mechanism, of the kind the report described through a Criminal Injuries Compensation Board, should stand behind the fine imposed on a convict. The fifth group concerned trial process: fast track courts, day to day trial, and the removal from the Evidence Act of the complainant's character as a subject of inquiry. The sixth group was social rather than legal: sexuality education, police and judicial sensitisation, and the enforcement of the constitutional promise of equality in public life. A government reading the report for ordinance purposes in the first days of February 2013 therefore faced a document in which the strictly penal recommendations were a minority of the whole. The ordinance took most of the first group, some of the fourth and fifth, and none of the third. The narrowing had begun before Parliament met.

## The Ordinance: Eleven Days After the Report

The Cabinet approved an ordinance on 1 February 2013, and the President, Pranab Mukherjee, promulgated the Criminal Law (Amendment) Ordinance on 3 February 2013. The government had reasons for the ordinance route that were genuine as far as they went: Parliament was not in session, the budget session was weeks away, public order in the capital had been strained by weeks of protest, and every day without a visible legislative response was a day in which the state's failure to act was the story. An ordinance is law the moment it is promulgated, and the government could say, truthfully, that the new offences and punishments were in force. What the route cost was scrutiny. An ordinance is drafted inside the executive, examined by no committee, debated by no House, and amended by no opposition. The document that emerged showed all three absences.

Its best known departure from the report was verbal and structural at once. The ordinance substituted the offence of sexual assault for the offence of rape across the relevant provisions, and it framed the perpetrator in gender neutral terms, so that the penetrative offence could in law be committed by any person against any person. The committee had not proposed this. Its draft had retained rape as the name of the penetrative offence and a gendered frame for it, while extending new protective offences more widely. The ordinance's renaming and neutralising were presented as modernisation, but they landed, within days, in a storm of objection from the very constituency the law was for. Women's rights organisations, among them national federations and Delhi based groups that had campaigned on the issue for decades, wrote against the ordinance and held it to be a betrayal of the report; left parties, including the Communist Party of India (Marxist), attacked both the content and the ordinance route itself and demanded a parliamentary bill. The objections to gender neutrality were specific, and they deserve statement in their own terms rather than summary dismissal. A gender neutral perpetrator, the objectors argued, converts every complaint into a potential counter complaint: the accused in a rape case could file a reciprocal allegation of sexual assault against the complainant, deploy it as leverage for withdrawal, and raise the cost of complaining in a system in which complainants already face pressure to settle. Whether that risk justified a gendered perpetrator is a question taken up in the section on the gender thread below. What matters here is that the objection was made, that it came from organisations with long records on the subject, and that it identified a real mechanism rather than a slogan.

The ordinance departed from the report in the opposite direction on punishment. Where the committee had refused the death penalty after examining it, the ordinance introduced it: for the offence corresponding to rape causing death or a persistent vegetative state, and for repeat offenders, the ordinance made death an available sentence. It adopted the age of consent of eighteen rather than the committee's sixteen. It left the marital exception standing. It did not touch the sanction requirement for the armed forces, command responsibility, or electoral disqualification, none of which reappeared at any later stage. It did carry the new conduct offences, the widened definition, the duties on police and hospitals, and enhanced punishments, so it cannot be described as an empty gesture. It is better described as the report passed through a filter: everything in the report that punished offenders harder came through, everything that redistributed power, between wife and husband, citizen and soldier, voter and candidate, complainant and accused in the matter of who may be accused, was strained out or, in the death penalty's case, inverted.

The ordinance's defenders, speaking for the government, made a case that should also be recorded, since the trail is not well served by treating the executive's document as merely a dilution. The Home Minister and the Law Minister of the day presented the ordinance as the fastest available faithful implementation of the committee, pointed to the new offences and the punishment ladder as proof of fidelity, and treated the renaming of the offence and its neutral framing as a drafting improvement of the kind comparative codes had adopted. On the death penalty, the government did not pretend the committee had recommended it; it defended the addition on the ground of public demand and the exceptional gravity of the two categories chosen. The disagreement, in other words, was not concealed by either side. It was a disagreement about whose judgment the statute should embody, the committee's on the questions it had examined or the executive's on the questions it regarded as political, and the ordinance resolved it by the executive's authority to legislate alone for a season. That is the constitutional design of ordinance power, and the episode is among the clearest modern demonstrations of both its utility and its price: the utility of law in force within eleven days of a report, and the price of a text that its intended beneficiaries disowned within the week.

### What did the ordinance change about the name and framing of the offence?

The ordinance renamed the penetrative offence sexual assault in place of rape and drafted it in gender neutral terms, so that in law any person could commit it against any person. The Verma Committee had proposed neither change. Parliament reversed both, restoring the name rape and a male perpetrator and a female victim in Section 375.

The filter metaphor should not be pushed past its usefulness. Some of what the ordinance dropped it dropped because an ordinance is a blunt instrument for structural reform: electoral disqualification and armed forces sanction sit in other statutes and other ministries, and command responsibility was a novel doctrine that cautious drafters would resist under any timetable. But the sequence matters for the trail. By the time the bill reached Parliament in March, the ordinance text, and not the report, was the working draft. Parliament's argument was conducted over the executive's filtered version, and several of the committee's structural recommendations were never moved as amendments at all, because the organisations and parties that might have moved them were spending their limited parliamentary capital on reversing the ordinance's own departures: the name of the offence, the gender of the perpetrator, and the design of the new offences. Pressure is a finite resource in a legislature, and the ordinance spent much of it on repairs.

## The Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013 in Parliament: Two March Weeks

The Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill was taken up in the budget session. An all party meeting before the debate settled the two most contested design questions in the government's favour and against one of them: the age of consent would remain eighteen, the figure the ordinance had chosen and the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act had normalised, rather than the sixteen the committee had recommended and the Standing Committee examining the earlier 2012 bill had favoured; and the offence would once again be named rape, with a male perpetrator and a female victim, reversing the ordinance. The Lok Sabha passed the bill on 19 March 2013 and the Rajya Sabha on 21 March 2013. The President assented on 2 April 2013, and the resulting Act, Act 13 of 2013, was deemed to have come into force on 3 February 2013, the date of the ordinance it replaced, so that the legal sequence from ordinance to Act is continuous and no act done under the ordinance fell into a gap.

Parliament's changes to the ordinance text, as distinct from its ratification of it, were concentrated in the new offences and in the restoration of the gendered frame. The first conviction for stalking, and the first conviction for voyeurism, were made bailable, where the ordinance's scheme had been sterner, after members argued that open textured conduct offences carrying non bailable status on a first complaint invited misuse against young men in consensual relationships that families disapproved of. The voyeurism provision was refined to reach the capturing and dissemination of images of a woman engaged in a private act. The definition of stalking was qualified by a proviso protecting conduct that was reasonable and justified, including acts done under law or in prevention and detection of crime. The Act also dropped the ordinance's generalised sexual assault ladder and rebuilt the scheme inside the Penal Code's existing architecture: Section 375 for the redefined rape, Sections 376 to 376E for its punishment grades, and Sections 354A to 354D for the new conduct offences, alongside an enhanced Section 354 and Section 509. On the structural questions Parliament did not reopen what the ordinance had closed. No amendment to delete the marital exception was carried; the sanction shield and the electoral recommendations were not in the bill and were not added; and the death penalty, which the ordinance had introduced against the committee's advice, Parliament retained, so that the final Act contains, in Sections 376A and 376E, a punishment its own originating committee had examined and refused. The trail from report to Act is thus not a story of simple dilution. It is a sequence of three distinct decisions, by a committee, by an executive drafting alone, and by a legislature under time pressure, and each decision is separately attributable.

The debate itself, compressed into two sitting days across the two Houses, had a texture the bare dates conceal. The bill was piloted by the Home Minister, Sushilkumar Shinde, and was supported in principle by every major formation, including the principal opposition, whose leader in the Lok Sabha, Sushma Swaraj, spoke for severity and for the death penalty in the gravest cases with a directness that matched the government's own position and exceeded the committee's. The contested ground was narrower than the public argument outside: the age of consent, where a body of members favoured sixteen on the committee's reasoning and were answered by the argument that eighteen was the age the child protection statute had settled and the age below which the state would not treat consent as possible; the bail design of stalking and voyeurism, where the apprehension of misuse in consensual relationships was pressed by members from several parties; and isolated voices on the marital exception, which was defended on the floor in the language of family stability and not seriously pressed to a vote by its critics. No division on the bill's core was ever in prospect. What the debate demonstrates, for trail purposes, is the difference between a legislature scrutinising a text and a legislature ratifying a settlement reached elsewhere: the all party meeting had fixed the questions on which concession was possible, the two Houses adjusted the conduct offences at the margin, and the structural items, being absent from the bill, were absent from the debate as well, because parliamentary time follows the text before the House and the text before the House was the ordinance's.

## The New Offences: Sections 354A to 354D and the Conduct Gaps

The most durable achievement of the Act, on any assessment, is the set of offences it wrote into the Penal Code for conduct that had previously been punishable, if at all, under the modesty provisions. Section 354A created the offence of sexual harassment, defined by four kinds of act: physical contact and advances involving unwelcome and explicit sexual overtures; a demand or request for sexual favours; showing pornography against the will of a woman; and making sexually coloured remarks. The first three carry rigorous imprisonment of up to three years, or a fine, or both; the fourth carries up to one year. The drafting deserves a moment's attention, because it located the offence in the Penal Code as a crime against the woman rather than inside the workplace framework of the Vishaka guidelines and the legislation on workplace harassment that was separately in progress, and it graded the offence by the intrusiveness of the act, treating a sexually coloured remark as serious but less serious than a demand backed by a position of power.

Section 354B punished assault or use of criminal force to a woman, or abetment of such an act, with the intention of disrobing her or compelling her to be naked, with imprisonment of not less than three years extending to seven, and a fine. The offence addressed a form of public humiliation of women that had featured in communal and caste violence and in crowd assaults, and which the old code could reach only as assault or as an outrage to modesty, descriptions that did not name what was done. Section 354C created voyeurism: watching, or capturing the image of, a woman engaging in a private act in circumstances where she would usually have the expectation of not being observed, and disseminating such an image. A first conviction carries one to three years and a fine; a subsequent conviction three to seven years and a fine. The explanation to the section supplies the definition of a private act, including acts in a place which would reasonably be expected to provide privacy, and extends to a victim using a lavatory or engaged in a sexual act not of a kind ordinarily done in public. Section 354D created stalking: following a woman and contacting or attempting to contact her to foster personal interaction repeatedly despite a clear indication of disinterest, or monitoring her use of the internet, email, or any other form of electronic communication. A first conviction carries up to three years and a fine, a subsequent conviction up to five years and a fine, subject to the proviso that conduct is not stalking if the man proves it was pursued for the purpose of preventing or detecting crime by a person entrusted with that responsibility, or under a requirement of law, or was reasonable and justified in the particular circumstances.

Around these new sections the Act tightened the old ones. Section 354 itself, the assault provision, which had carried a maximum of two years, was amended to a minimum of one year and a maximum of five, with a fine, converting a provision the courts had treated as minor into a middle ranking offence. Section 509, the word and gesture provision, moved from a maximum of one year to a maximum of three. The changes read as housekeeping beside the new sections, but their practical effect is considerable, because Sections 354 and 509 are the provisions under which the everyday run of street and workplace harassment is actually charged, and trebling a maximum alters charging and sentencing practice across thousands of cases that will never cite the Verma report.

### Which entirely new offences did the 2013 Act create?

The Act created sexual harassment in Section 354A, assault with intent to disrobe in Section 354B, voyeurism in Section 354C, and stalking in Section 354D of the Penal Code, and free standing offences of acid attack and attempt in Sections 326A and 326B and a defined offence of trafficking in Section 370.

## Acid Attacks, Trafficking, and Two Changes of a Different Kind

Sections 326A and 326B are grouped with the sexual offences in most accounts of the Act, and the committee had recommended an acid attack offence, but analytically they belong to a different family: they are offences of permanent injury whose distinctive feature is the punishment and compensation design. Section 326A punishes voluntarily causing grievous hurt by throwing or administering acid, or by any means with the intention of causing permanent or partial damage, deformity, burns, maiming, disfigurement, disability, or grievous hurt, with imprisonment of not less than ten years extending to imprisonment for life, and a fine which the section directs shall be just and reasonable to meet the medical expenses of the treatment of the victim and shall be paid to the victim. That direction, tying the fine to the victim's treatment costs and routing it to her, was a deliberate break with the ordinary fine, which is paid to the state and calibrated to the offender. Section 326B punishes the attempt, throwing or attempting to throw acid with the requisite intention, with five to seven years and a fine. The Act simultaneously amended Section 100 of the Code, the right of private defence of the body, to add a Seventhly clause covering an act of throwing or administering acid, or an attempt, which may reasonably cause the apprehension that grievous hurt will otherwise be the consequence, a small insertion that tells the reader the drafters were thinking about the victim's resistance and the bystander's intervention as well as the attacker's sentence.

The trafficking substitution was the Act's largest single piece of offence drafting. The old Section 370, which punished buying or disposing of any person as a slave, was replaced wholesale by a definition of trafficking built on the international model: recruiting, transporting, harbouring, transferring, or receiving a person, by threat, force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power, or inducement, for the purpose of exploitation, with exploitation defined to include prostitution and other forms of sexual exploitation, forced labour or services, slavery or practices similar to slavery, servitude, and the forced removal of organs, and with an explanation that the consent of the victim is immaterial. The punishment ladder is graded by the victim and the offender: seven to ten years for trafficking one person, ten years to life where more than one person is trafficked or the victim is a minor in specified configurations, fourteen years to life for trafficking more than one minor, and imprisonment for life for the remainder of natural life where the offender is a public servant or a police officer. Section 370A punished the exploitation of a trafficked person, engaging a trafficked minor for sexual exploitation with five to seven years and a trafficked adult with three to five. The substitution matters to the amendment trail because it shows the filter at work in reverse: trafficking reform had been urged for years by a different constituency than the one protesting in December 2012, it imposed no cost on any organised interest in the chamber, and it passed into the Act in full, carried by a vehicle built for another subject.

## The Fourth Statute: The Child Protection Overlap

The Act's long title named a fourth statute that accounts of the amendment habitually forget, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012, and its treatment there exposes a structural problem the trail created even as it solved others. The 2012 Act, in force for only a few months when the Verma Committee sat, defined a child as any person below eighteen and made penetrative sexual assault on a child an offence regardless of consent, with its own aggravated categories, its own special courts, and its own presumptions. Once the Penal Code's age of consent also rose to eighteen, the two statutes covered the same ground for every complainant below eighteen, and a single act could be charged under either or both. The 2013 Act addressed the overlap in the only way the 2012 Act's scheme allowed: it amended Section 42 of the child protection statute so that where an act constitutes an offence under both that Act and specified provisions of the Penal Code, the offender is liable to the punishment that is greater in degree, and it inserted Section 42A, declaring that the child protection statute's provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law.

The overlap's consequences run in two directions, and honesty requires both. For child victims, the combined scheme is genuinely protective: the special statute supplies child friendly procedures, presumptions, and special courts that the ordinary code lacks, and the greater punishment rule prevents the overlap from becoming a discount. For older adolescents in consensual relationships, the combined scheme is the engine of the problem the committee had warned about when it recommended sixteen. A relationship between a seventeen year old and a young adult is, in law, penetrative sexual assault on a child and rape, both strict in the matter of consent, and the process is characteristically set in motion not by the young woman but by her family, sometimes in the setting of an eloping couple and sometimes across a caste or community boundary the family wishes to police. High Courts across the country have repeatedly remarked on this class of case in bail and quashing proceedings, and the Law Commission's 283rd report of 2023, examining the age of consent under the 2012 Act, recorded the pattern while declining to recommend lowering the age, proposing instead a guided judicial discretion in sentencing for cases close in age and consensual in fact. The 2013 Act cannot be charged with creating the child protection statute, which predated it by months. It can be charged with choosing, against its own committee's advice, to align the Penal Code upward to eighteen rather than to hold the code at sixteen and let the special statute govern children, and that choice, taken in an all party meeting under the pressure of being seen to protect, is the single most consequential refusal in the Act after the marital exception.

## Section 375 Rewritten: Penetration, Consent, and the Seven Circumstances

The redefinition of rape is the Act's centre of gravity. The new Section 375 provides that a man commits rape if he penetrates his penis, to any extent, into the vagina, mouth, urethra, or anus of a woman or makes her do so with him or any other person; or inserts, to any extent, any object or a part of the body, not being the penis, into the vagina, urethra, or anus of a woman or makes her do so; or manipulates any part of the body of a woman so as to cause penetration into the vagina, urethra, anus, or any part of her body; or applies his mouth to the vagina, anus, or urethra of a woman or makes her do so, under seven described circumstances. The circumstances preserve the structure of the old law while widening it: against her will; without her consent; with consent obtained by putting her or any person in whom she is interested in fear of death or of hurt; with consent given because she believes the man to be another man to whom she is or believes herself to be lawfully married; with consent given at a time when, by reason of unsoundness of mind or intoxication or the administration of a stupefying substance, she is unable to understand the nature and consequences of what she consents to; with or without consent when she is under eighteen years of age; and when she is unable to communicate consent.

Two of the section's explanations carry the doctrinal weight. The first provides that penetration is sufficient to constitute the sexual intercourse necessary to the offence, closing the door on argument about degree. The second defines consent as an unequivocal voluntary agreement when the woman by words, gestures, or any form of verbal or non verbal communication communicates willingness to participate in the specific sexual act, with a proviso that a woman who does not physically resist the act shall not by reason only of that fact be regarded as consenting to it. That proviso is the committee's clearest single victory in the text. It writes into the definition the principle that submission is not consent, the principle whose absence from the old law had allowed judgments to reason from a complainant's passivity to her agreement, and it does so in the section that police, prosecutors, and trial courts read first. The exceptions qualify the whole. Exception 1 excludes a medical procedure or intervention. Exception 2 provides that sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under fifteen years of age, is not rape. The age in that exception, fifteen, in a statute that had just set the age of consent at eighteen, created the internal contradiction that the Supreme Court would be asked to repair four years later.

The age of consent change requires separate statement because it was the point on which the committee, the ordinance, the Standing Committee's view of the earlier bill, and the final Act were most visibly at odds, and because its consequences have been the most litigated in practice. Before 2013 the Penal Code's threshold was sixteen. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012 had made eighteen the threshold for its offences, producing an overlap in which a seventeen year old was a child for one statute and above the age of consent for the other. The committee recommended a single threshold of sixteen for the Penal Code offence, reasoning in part from the criminalisation of consensual relationships between older adolescents that an eighteen threshold produces, where the criminal process is set in motion by disapproving families and the complainant herself denies any offence. The ordinance chose eighteen. The all party process in March 2013 confirmed eighteen, and the Act enacted it as the Sixthly circumstance. The Law Commission of India, in its 283rd report of 2023 on the age of consent under the 2012 Act, would return to the problem the committee had identified, declining to lower the age while recommending guided judicial discretion in sentencing for cases involving older adolescents in consensual relationships, which is a measure of how durable the committee's underlying analysis proved even where its recommendation was refused.

### How did the 2013 Act change the legal meaning of consent?

The Act wrote a definition of consent into Section 375 as an unequivocal voluntary agreement communicated by words, gestures, or other verbal or non verbal communication of willingness to participate in the specific act, and added that a woman who does not physically resist an act is not, for that reason alone, to be regarded as consenting to it.

The marital exception carries a history of its own that explains some of its durability, and the trail within the trail is instructive. When the Penal Code was enacted in 1860, the exception protected a husband where the wife was not under ten years of age, the same age as the general age of consent the code then set. The death of Phulmoni Dasi, a girl of about eleven, from injuries inflicted by her adult husband, who could not be convicted of rape because his wife was above the excepted age, produced the Age of Consent Act 1891, which raised the protected age to twelve. Amendments in 1925 raised the marital age to thirteen and the general age of consent to fourteen, and amendments in 1940 raised them to fifteen and sixteen respectively, the figures the code then carried for over seventy years. Two features of that history matter for 2013. The first is that the exception was never, across a century and a half, debated as a question of sexual autonomy; it was debated always as a question of the age at which a wife's incapacity to consent should be irrebuttably presumed, so that the institution of the exception itself passed from generation to generation unexamined while its number was adjusted. The second is that the 2013 amendment, which raised the general age of consent from sixteen to eighteen, left the exception's age at fifteen, breaking the alignment between the two figures for the first time and in the wrong direction, so that the statute simultaneously treated a seventeen year old as incapable of consenting to any man and a fifteen year old as incapable of refusing her husband. The anomaly was visible on the face of the enacted text, it was pointed out in commentary immediately, and it is what made the exception, rather than any other part of the Act, the inevitable subject of the next round of litigation.

## The Punishment Ladder: Sections 376 to 376E

The Act rebuilt the punishment provisions as a ladder of distinct offences rather than a single section with aggravated clauses. Section 376(1) punishes rape with rigorous imprisonment of not less than seven years extending to imprisonment for life, and a fine, and the Act removed the former proviso that had allowed a court to go below the minimum for adequate and special reasons, so that the floor became a genuine floor. Section 376(2) collects the aggravated categories, among them rape committed by a police officer within his station or on a woman in his custody, by a public servant taking advantage of his official position, by a member of the armed forces in a deployed area, by the management or staff of a jail, remand home, or hospital, by a relative, guardian, teacher, or person in a position of trust or authority, upon a woman during communal or sectarian violence, upon a woman knowing her to be pregnant, upon a woman under sixteen years of age, upon a woman incapable of giving consent, by a person in a position of control or dominance, upon a woman suffering from mental or physical disability, while causing grievous bodily harm or endangering her life in the course of the offence, and repeatedly on the same woman. These carry not less than ten years extending to imprisonment for life, which the Act specifies shall mean imprisonment for the remainder of that person's natural life.

Above the aggravated tier the Act built three offences that carry the trail's central controversy inside the statute. Section 376A punishes a person who, in committing rape, inflicts an injury which causes the death of the woman or causes her to be in a persistent vegetative state, with not less than twenty years extending to life for the remainder of natural life, or with death. Section 376D punishes gang rape, where a woman is raped by one or more persons constituting a group or acting in furtherance of a common intention, each being deemed to have committed rape, with not less than twenty years extending to life for the remainder of natural life, and a fine directed to be just and reasonable to meet the medical expenses and rehabilitation of the victim and to be paid to her. Section 376E punishes a person previously convicted of an offence under Sections 376, 376A, or 376D who is subsequently convicted of any of them, with imprisonment for life for the remainder of natural life, or with death. Between the aggravated tier and these stand two relational offences: Section 376B, punishing sexual intercourse by a husband upon his wife during separation, under a decree of separation or otherwise, without her consent, with two to seven years, an offence whose cognizance the Code of Criminal Procedure restricts to the wife's own complaint; and Section 376C, punishing sexual intercourse by a person in a position of authority, a public servant, a superintendent or manager of a jail or institution, or the management or staff of a hospital, who abuses that position to induce or seduce a woman in his custody or charge to have intercourse not amounting to rape, with five to ten years.

The ladder's shape is the committee's shape with its top rung replaced. The committee had proposed minimums of the same general order, twenty years for gang rape and for the death and vegetative state cases, extendable to imprisonment for the remainder of natural life, and it had stopped there, deliberately. The ordinance added death at the top, for the gravest offence and for the repeat offender, and Parliament kept it. The consequence for reading the Act is that its punishment chapter cannot be described as the Verma scheme enacted. It is the Verma scheme with its ceiling raised past the point its authors set, at the demand of a politics that neither the committee nor, in the event, the government's own draft bill of the following session could have resisted.

## Discretion Removed: Sentencing After the Floors Hardened

A change in the punishment chapter that drew little comment at the time deserves separate treatment, because it altered the daily working of the rape trial more than either capital provision did. The former Section 376 had allowed a court, for adequate and special reasons to be mentioned in the judgment, to impose a sentence below the seven year minimum. Sentencing courts had used that proviso in a line of cases that reformers cited with anger, in which compromise, the victim's subsequent marriage, the passage of time, or the accused's family circumstances were treated as reasons to descend below the floor, and the Supreme Court had more than once corrected the practice while the statutory door stood open. The 2013 Act closed the door. Section 376(1) as substituted states a minimum of seven years without a proviso, the aggravated tier states ten, gang rape and Section 376A state twenty, and the fine under Sections 326A and 376D is tied to the victim's medical and rehabilitation expenses rather than left at large. The ladder is therefore not only higher than the old law; it is harder, in the sense that its rungs are fixed points rather than presumptions.

The case for hardening was the committee's own: it had documented sentencing inconsistency as one of the system's failures and had recommended enhanced and certain punishment as the answer to the deterrence question, in place of the death penalty's uncertain terror. Certainty and celerity, in the deterrence tradition running from Beccaria through the modern empirical literature the committee cited, do the work that severity advertises. The case against hardening, made by sentencing scholars and by judges speaking off the bench in the years since, is that rigid floors transfer discretion rather than abolish it: to prosecutors choosing charges, to police recording the first information, and to trial judges at the conviction stage, where a court persuaded of guilt but unwilling to impose a twenty year floor on the facts before it may resolve its doubt in favour of acquittal, and where complainants in borderline cases face pressure to settle precisely because the consequences of conviction have become inelastic. Both mechanisms are real, and the Indian data does not exist in a form that would let an honest writer say which dominates. What can be said is that the 2013 settlement chose its instrument deliberately: it distrusted the sentencing judge, on a record the committee had assembled, and it expressed that distrust in the text. Readers of the punishment chapter who miss the deleted proviso miss the amendment's theory of the judiciary.

## Procedure Rebuilt Around the Complainant

The Penal Code changes are the ones that attract commentary, but a complainant meets the criminal law through the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the Act's procedural chapter is where several of the committee's recommendations survived with their force intact. The first is the duty to record. A proviso structure added to Section 154 of the procedure code requires that information relating to the specified offences against a woman be recorded by a woman police officer or any woman officer; where the victim is temporarily or permanently, mentally or physically, disabled, the information is to be recorded at her residence or at a place of her choice, in the presence of an interpreter or a special educator, and the recording is to be videographed, with a statement before a Judicial Magistrate to follow. Section 164(5A), inserted by the Act, obliges the Judicial Magistrate to record the statement of a victim of the specified offences as soon as the commission of the offence is brought to notice, with the assistance of an interpreter or special educator where the victim is disabled, and provides that such a statement shall be treated as a statement in lieu of examination in chief, so that the complainant may be cross examined on it without repeating her account in full. Section 273 received a proviso directing that where the evidence of a woman below eighteen alleged to have been subjected to rape or another sexual offence is recorded, the court may take appropriate measures to ensure she is not confronted by the accused, while preserving his right of cross examination. Section 327's in camera scheme was extended to the new Section 376E. Section 309(1) was substituted to require day to day proceedings and, for trials relating to the Section 376 offences, completion as far as possible within two months of the filing of the charge sheet. Section 198B restricted cognizance of the Section 376B offence to proceedings on the wife's complaint, upon the court's prima facie satisfaction. And Section 197, the sanction provision, received a proviso of real significance to the sanction debate: no sanction is required where a public servant is accused of the specified sexual offences, a change that lowered the shield for officials generally while leaving the armed forces' distinct statutory shield, which the committee had targeted, exactly where it stood.

The two Penal Code provisions that belong to this procedural story are Sections 166A and 166B. Section 166A makes it an offence, punishable with rigorous imprisonment of not less than six months extending to two years and a fine, for a public servant knowingly to disobey a direction of law governing investigation, or to fail to record any information given under Section 154(1) of the procedure code in relation to a cognisable offence under the specified sections, which include the acid attack sections, Section 354, Section 354B, the trafficking sections, the Section 376 series, and Section 509. The provision converts the most common point of failure in a sexual offence case, the police station counter, into a point of criminal liability for the officer, which is precisely what the committee had asked for. Section 166B punishes whoever, being in charge of a hospital, public or private, contravenes Section 357C of the procedure code, with imprisonment of up to one year, or a fine, or both; and Section 357C itself obliges all hospitals, public or private, whether run by the Central Government, a State Government, local bodies, or any other person, to provide immediately, free of cost, first aid or medical treatment to victims of the specified offences and to inform the police immediately of the incident. Section 357B completes the triangle by providing that compensation payable by the state under the victim compensation scheme is in addition to the fine payable to the victim under Sections 326A and 376D, so that a convict's fine and the state's compensation do not cancel each other.

Two quieter provisions of the same chapter complete the procedural picture, and both show the Act working at the level of the courtroom rather than the headline. The Act amended Section 26 of the procedure code to provide that an offence under the Section 376 series shall be tried, as far as practicable, by a court presided over by a woman, and it carried the corresponding direction into the in camera scheme of Section 327, a measure that was debated as symbolism and defended as substance: the identity of the presiding officer changes the management of a trial in which the complainant's evidence is the centre of the record, the control of improper cross examination is the judge's daily work, and the committee had recorded complainants' accounts of courtrooms that replicated the indifference of the police station. The Act also extended Section 228A of the Penal Code, the provision that makes it an offence to print or publish the name or any matter that may make known the identity of a victim of specified sexual offences, so that its protection covers the new Section 376E offence alongside the rest of the series. Identity protection is the least discussed element of the 2013 settlement and among the most consistently used, because it operates in every reported case and every newsroom without requiring a prosecution, a committee, or a campaign to activate it.

## Evidence Recalibrated: Sections 53A, 114A, and 146

The Evidence Act changes are three, and each removes a distinct forensic advantage that the old law had given the defence. Section 53A, inserted by the Act, provides that in a prosecution for the specified offences, where the question of consent is in issue, evidence of the character of the victim or of such person's previous sexual experience with any person shall not be relevant on the issue of such consent or the quality of consent. The proviso added to Section 146, the section governing questions lawful in cross examination, provides that in such a prosecution it shall not be permissible to adduce evidence or to put questions in cross examination of the victim as to her general immoral character or previous sexual experience, for the purpose of proving consent or its quality. Between them, the two provisions close the route by which a rape trial had historically been converted into an inquiry into the complainant's life, the route the Mathura judgment had exposed in 1978 and which Section 155(4) of the old Act had kept open in terms.

Section 114A works in the opposite direction, on the burden of proof. Substituted by the Act in a form wider than the 1983 original, it provides that in a prosecution for rape under the specified aggravated clauses of Section 376(2), where sexual intercourse by the accused is proved and the question is whether it was without the consent of the woman, and she states in her evidence before the court that she did not consent, the court shall presume that she did not consent. The presumption is rebuttable, and it does not relieve the prosecution of proving the act or the identity of the accused. What it does is allocate the evidential burden on the single issue that the complainant's own testimony directly addresses, and it confines the allocation to the aggravated categories, a calibration that tracks the committee's approach of strengthening the complainant's position inside the trial without disturbing the presumption of innocence on the elements of the offence. The Act also amended Section 119 of the Evidence Act to replace the archaic treatment of witnesses unable to speak with a scheme for witnesses unable to communicate verbally, whose statements, given by signs or writing with the help of an interpreter or special educator and videographed, are deemed oral evidence. Read together, the evidence chapter of the Act is the least controversial and arguably the most professionally consequential part of it: it changed what may be proved and how, in every trial under the amended code, without raising a single political constituency against it, which by the logic of the narrowing sequence is exactly why it passed whole.

## Fast Track Courts: The Recommendation That Travelled by Administration

The committee's demand for speedier trials was implemented, but not by the Act, and the distinction illustrates a recurring feature of the trail: recommendations that could be executed administratively were, and only those requiring statutory text or political surrender were not. Within days of the December protests, the Delhi High Court and the government moved to designate fast track courts for sexual offences in the capital, and the Union government pressed every state to do the same and offered central assistance for additional courts. The Act itself contains no court creating provision; its contribution to speed is disciplinary rather than institutional, the day to day trial rule and the two month direction in the substituted Section 309(1), the magistrate recorded statement that shortens the complainant's evidence, and the in camera and non confrontation measures that reduce the occasions for adjournment. The committee had wanted both the institutions and the discipline, together with the judges and prosecutors to staff them. It received the discipline in full and the institutions in the form of designations laid over an existing and burdened district judiciary, a pattern familiar from the earlier fast track court experiment of the preceding decade, in which centrally funded courts were created for a term and then wound into the ordinary system. The consequence for the trail is that the part of the committee's scheme most visible to the public, the promise of a fast trial, rested on the weakest of its three foundations: administrative enthusiasm, statutory discipline without new capacity, and a two month direction qualified by the words as far as possible. The committee had identified certainty and speed, not severity, as the variables that deter. The settlement of 2013 delivered severity by statute and speed by exhortation, and the imbalance between those two deliveries is the practical form the narrowing sequence took inside the courtroom.

## The Medical Examination: The Reform the Act Did Not Carry

One committee recommendation failed in a manner unlike the others, and its afterlife shows a route by which refused recommendations can still enter the law. The committee had condemned the two finger test, the practice under which a doctor examining a rape complainant inserted two fingers to assess the laxity of the vaginal muscles and recorded an opinion on whether she was habituated to sexual intercourse, and it had recommended a medical protocol confined to recording injuries, collecting forensic evidence, and treating the patient. The ordinance and the Act enacted no such protocol. The Penal Code and procedure code changes the Act did make, free treatment under Section 357C, prompt magistrate recorded statements, and the bar on character evidence, addressed the complainant's treatment at the police station and in the courtroom, but the examination room was left to a provision the Act did not touch, Section 164A of the procedure code, inserted in 2005, which governs medical examination of the victim with her consent and says nothing about the test.

The correction came from the courts and the health administration instead. In Lillu v State of Haryana, decided in 2013, the Supreme Court held that the two finger test and its interpretation violate the right of rape survivors to privacy, physical and mental integrity, and dignity, and that even a report that a victim was habituated to sexual intercourse could not be used against her testimony. The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare followed with medico legal guidelines for the care of survivors of sexual violence that excluded the test and prescribed the protocol approach the committee had sought: a standardised examination kit, documentation of injuries, treatment without conditioning care on a police requisition, and the express instruction that opinion on habituation has no place in the record. The episode complicates the narrowing thesis in a way the thesis needs. The filter that stopped the committee's structural recommendations operated on the legislature. It did not operate with the same force on institutions that could act without a majority: a two judge bench applying constitutional privacy doctrine, and a ministry issuing clinical guidelines, between them enacted a recommendation that Parliament had ignored, within roughly a year of the Act. The trail of a refused recommendation does not always end at the refusal, and a complete account of the 2013 reform has to follow the ones that found another door.

## The Refusals: What the Trail Left Behind

The three structural refusals deserve to be stated as carefully as the enactments, because each had a reasoned case on both sides and the trail is falsified if the refusals are treated as mere failure of will. On marital rape, the committee's case was one of principle fortified by comparative law: consent is the organising idea of the redefined Section 375, an exception that withdraws a class of women from that idea solely because of their relationship to the perpetrator contradicts the definition it sits inside, and the European Commission of Human Rights in C R v United Kingdom, followed by the House of Lords, had held that a husband's immunity was incompatible with the fundamental objectives of the offence. The case against criminalisation, made in Parliament and by successive governments, runs on different ground: that marriage as an institution would be destabilised by admitting a rape complaint inside it, that the provision would be misused in matrimonial disputes, that evidentiary difficulties of proving absence of consent within a continuing sexual relationship are of a different order from stranger cases, and that other remedies, cruelty, domestic violence legislation, and the Section 376B offence for separated spouses, occupy the field proportionately. The first set of arguments is about what the offence definition means. The second is about what enforcement would do. They do not meet, which is why the question has remained open for a decade: it is a value dispute about the legal meaning of marriage carrying a factual dispute about misuse inside it, and neither an ordinance nor a two day debate could close it.

On the armed forces, the committee's recommendation was narrower than its critics sometimes allow. It did not propose the repeal of the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Acts. It proposed that where the allegation is a sexual offence, the ordinary criminal law should govern: the sanction requirement that obliges a prosecutor to obtain the previous permission of the Central Government before proceeding against a soldier for an act done in exercise of the Act's powers should not stand in the way, and allegations should be tried in ordinary courts rather than absorbed into military process. The counter case, made by the security establishment in every iteration of this debate, is that forces deployed in disturbed areas act under operational pressure and legal exposure to motivated complaints, that sanction is the filter separating the two, and that removing it for one category of offence invites its removal for all. The Act's handling of the neighbouring question shows the line the legislature drew: it removed the sanction requirement for public servants accused of sexual offences in the ordinary civil administration through the proviso to Section 197 of the procedure code, and it left the armed forces' shield, which rests on the Special Powers Acts themselves rather than on Section 197, untouched. A reform that applied to a police officer in Delhi did not apply to a soldier in a disturbed area, and that distinction was a decision, not an oversight.

Command responsibility failed for a different reason: it had no domestic foothold. The doctrine, developed in international criminal law for commanders who knew or should have known of crimes by forces under their effective control and failed to prevent or punish them, would have required the Penal Code to absorb a mode of liability it has never carried, attaching guilt for an offence of this gravity to omission and position rather than to act and intention. The committee, two of whose members had deep experience of international and constitutional law, proposed it as the necessary complement to the sanction recommendation, since removing the shield without a doctrine reaching the chain of command would leave accountability resting on the lowest ranking accused. Parliament was not asked to vote on it; it was simply absent from both the ordinance and the bill, an idea that travelled no further than the report. The electoral recommendation travelled the same distance. The committee had proposed that the law disqualify or at least expose candidates facing sexual offence charges, building on the disclosure regime the Supreme Court had constructed a decade earlier. In a chamber a significant number of whose members across parties faced criminal charges of some description, a proposal to attach electoral consequences to charges in one category asked the political class to legislate against its own exposure, and it was not taken up by the executive that would have had to move it.

## Independent Thought and the Exception That Shrank

The exception that survived 2013 carried its contradiction on its face: a wife of sixteen was old enough, under Exception 2, to be beyond the protection of Section 375 in intercourse with her husband, and under the Sixthly circumstance and the child protection statute she was a child incapable of consenting to intercourse with any other man. In Independent Thought v Union of India, decided in 2017, the Supreme Court was asked to resolve the contradiction in a petition directed at the overlap between the Penal Code and the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012, which treats every person below eighteen as a child and contains no marital exception. The Court read the exception down. It held that Exception 2 must be read as providing that sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under eighteen years of age, is not rape, harmonising the Penal Code with the child protection statute and with the constitutional rights of the girl child, and it expressly confined its judgment to that question, declining to address the position of adult wives, on which it said nothing.

The reading down is a study in what judicial correction can and cannot do to a legislative refusal. It eliminated the anomaly at the boundary of childhood, and it did so by interpretation rather than by striking down, leaving the exception's structure standing. It left the substantive question, whether marriage is a defence to rape between adults, exactly where Parliament had left it, and the Court said so. The question then travelled the route the Act's history predicts for it: to a constitutional challenge against the exception itself. In proceedings before the Delhi High Court, a Division Bench delivered a split verdict in 2022, Justice Rajiv Shakdher holding the exception unconstitutional as violative of the equality and liberty guarantees, and Justice C Hari Shankar upholding it, reasoning in part that the legislature's classification rested on the distinct character of the marital relationship and that the court should not supply the offence Parliament had withheld. With the bench divided, the matter stands before the Supreme Court. This article describes that posture and predicts nothing about it, because the trail's lesson cuts both ways: a question that Parliament has refused three times, in a report response, an ordinance, and an Act, may be answered by a court on constitutional principle, or a court may conclude, as one of the two judges below concluded, that the refusal is itself a legislative choice the Constitution permits.

The reasoning by which the Court reached the reading down repays attention, because it both vindicated and confined the committee's logic. The bench reasoned that the classification the exception created between married and unmarried girls between fifteen and eighteen had no rational relation to any legitimate purpose: a married girl of sixteen stood outside the protection of the rape law in her husband's hands while an unmarried girl of the same age stood inside it against the whole world, and the child protection statute, enacted by the same Parliament, recognised no such distinction. It treated the exception, in its unamended reach, as an artificial distinction destructive of the girl child's rights to bodily integrity and reproductive choice under the equality and liberty guarantees, and it chose harmonious construction over invalidation as the instrument of repair, partly because striking the exception down altogether would have decided the adult question that the petition had not fully argued. The committee had proposed the legislative version of the same conclusion five years earlier and had been refused; the Court adopted the minimum of it that constitutional interpretation would bear. The distance between the two documents on this point is the distance between a legislature's freedom to decline a reform and a court's duty to refuse an anomaly, and the adult exception sits precisely in the space between them, too principled for the Court to have blessed and too political for Parliament to have surrendered.

## The Gender Thread: Counter Accusation, Male Victims, and the Missing Section 377

The gender neutrality question is actually two questions wearing one label, and the 2013 trail is unintelligible until they are separated. The first question is whether the perpetrator of the penetrative offence should be gender neutral, so that any person may commit it. The ordinance answered yes. Women's organisations answered no, on the counter accusation ground set out earlier: in the conditions in which Indian rape complaints are actually investigated, a neutral perpetrator definition hands the accused a symmetrical weapon, and the threat or fact of a counter case is among the pressures most likely to force a complainant out of the process. That argument is an empirical claim about litigation behaviour under asymmetric social conditions, and it persuaded Parliament. The second question is whether the victim of the penetrative offence should be gender neutral, so that adult men and transgender persons are protected as women are. It was barely argued in 2013, because the ordinance's single drafting move had fused the two questions into one clause and the political fight consumed the fused version. The committee's own scheme had protected a wider class through the new conduct offences while keeping the penetrative offence gendered, but the Act as passed wrote even the conduct offences, voyeurism and stalking and disrobing, around a woman victim, so that an adult man subjected to the same conduct had no recourse under them at all.

For a decade the gap left by the second question was partly covered, in practice and at a price, by Section 377 of the Penal Code, the unnatural offences provision, which after the reading down in [the Section 377 litigation that culminated in the Navtej Singh Johar judgment](/2011/03/01/section-377-navtej-johar-judgment/) no longer reached consensual acts between adults but continued to punish non consensual acts of the kinds it described, whoever the victim was. Prosecutors used it for male victims of sexual assault because there was nothing else to use. The successor code of 2023 then deleted Section 377 entirely and enacted no equivalent, a choice examined in the article on that code, with the consequence that the question which was not settled in 2013 returned in a harder form: conduct against an adult male victim that Section 377 would once have punished, and that the 2013 Act declined to bring within Section 375, now sits outside the penetrative offences of the new code altogether. The men's rights argument for a gender neutral rape law, which is usually debated as though it were the same claim as the one defeated in 2013, is in substance the second question returning: its strongest form is not that women complainants should face counter accusations, which its serious proponents do not need and many do not seek, but that a code which grades sexual violation by gravity has no principled basis for grading it instead by the sex of the person violated, and its hardest case is the victim for whom no offence now exists. Its opponents answer that the conditions of Indian litigation have not changed since 2013 and that the counter accusation risk attaches to victim neutrality in practice less than to perpetrator neutrality, a distinction the argument rarely observes. The thread is drawn here because connecting the ordinance debate of 2013 to the omission of 2023 is the only way either event is fully explained: the first refusal created the dependence on Section 377, and the second removed it.

The transgender dimension of the second question has its own documentary trail, and it sharpens the point. The Supreme Court's recognition of transgender persons as a third gender in National Legal Services Authority v Union of India in 2014 postdated the Act by a year, but the population it recognised had been visible to the 2013 debate, and the committee's remit of sexual assault against women, as its terms of reference were drawn, did not extend to redesigning the offence for victims outside that frame. When Parliament legislated for transgender persons in the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019, it punished sexual abuse of a transgender person with imprisonment of six months to two years under Section 18 of that Act, a maximum below the minimum for the least grave of the 2013 conduct offences against a woman. Whatever view is taken of the ordinance's fused drafting of 2013, the statute book it left behind contains the disparity in plain figures: the gravity of a sexual violation in Indian law is still sorted first by the legal category of the victim and only then by the character of the act. That ordering is the unexamined residue of the choice Parliament made in March 2013, when it settled the perpetrator question, which the ordinance had forced, and never reached the victim question, which it had not.

## The Death Penalty the Committee Refused and Parliament Enacted

No part of the trail states the narrowing sequence more cleanly than the death penalty, because here the sequence is not a dropping but a reversal. The committee had the question directly before it, in a season when the demand for hanging was the loudest single demand in the streets, and it answered with reasons: the deterrent effect claimed for capital punishment on serious crime is not borne out by the evidence, including the long decline in the homicide rate across a period in which executions in India had become rare; sentencing in capital cases is demonstrably inconsistent, a criticism the Supreme Court's own capital jurisprudence, with its confession of judge centric variation in the application of the rarest of rare test from Bachan Singh, had made available to any reader; and the proportionate response to aggravated rape was a ladder of imprisonment reaching, at the top, confinement for the remainder of natural life, a punishment the committee regarded as severe by any honest measure. The Law Commission of India, reporting in 2015 in its 262nd report on the death penalty, reached a cognate conclusion for offences generally, recommending abolition for all crimes other than terrorism related offences and waging war, while recording the constitutional court's framework within which the question would continue to be argued.

Parliament, following the ordinance, answered otherwise, and its reasons also deserve statement. The case made for the two capital provisions was that there exists a class of rape, that which kills or destroys the victim's conscious life, and a class of offender, the repeat rapist, for whom the community's condemnation cannot be expressed by any term of years, and that a legislature answerable to the public mood after December 2012 was entitled to mark that class with the ultimate sentence. Between these two positions there is a factual dispute, about deterrence and about sentencing consistency, on which the committee had the evidence and Parliament did not seriously engage it, and a value dispute, about whether any crime short of murder or mass killing warrants death, which evidence cannot settle. The factual dispute has a direction: no Indian data produced in the debate showed a deterrent effect specific to the new provisions, and the provisions were not accompanied by the investigation and trial reforms that the deterrence literature identifies as the operative variables, certainty and speed of punishment, which the committee's procedural recommendations had targeted instead. The value dispute remains genuinely open, and the article's verdict is confined to the factual limb: on the record of 2013, the death penalty entered the sexual offence code as an expressive act, against the advice of the body appointed to examine the evidence, and the 2018 amendment extended it further in the same expressive register.

The neighbouring argument, that the 2013 reforms are widely misused, requires the same separation and a stricter discipline, because the statistics traded in it are contested on both sides and this article asserts none of them. One side, represented in public argument by men's rights organisations and by some members in the parliamentary debate itself, points to acquittals in rape trials and to cases in which allegations collapse, and argues that stringent offences with high minimums invite false or exaggerated complaints, particularly in disputes over relationships and property. The other side, represented by women's organisations and by the research tradition running from the Mathura open letter onward, answers that an acquittal is not a false case, that trials fail for reasons of investigative quality, delay, witness hostility under pressure, and the ordinary standard of proof, and that the proportion of complaints shown to be fabricated is small beside the proportion of offences never reported at all, a reporting gap every official committee that has examined the question has recorded. Both positions agree on one operational point, which is where the argument is usable: the quality of the first investigation decides these cases, and the Act's own machinery, Section 166A's liability for the officer who will not record, the magistrate recorded statement under Section 164(5A), the two month trial direction, is aimed at exactly that point. A reader who wants the general position applied to a live matter needs advice on that matter, and this account, which is legal information about a statute's history and text, is not a substitute for it.

## The 2018 Sequel: The Pattern Repeating

Five years later the sequence ran again, faster and with less deliberation, and the comparison is the strongest evidence that 2013 taught the system a repeatable script. The rape and murder of a child in Kathua in 2018 produced public outrage of the same character as December 2012. This time there was no committee. The response moved directly to an ordinance and then to the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2018, which raised the minimum punishment under Section 376(1) from seven years to ten, created Section 376AB punishing rape of a woman under twelve years of age with a minimum of twenty years extending to life for the remainder of natural life or with death, created Sections 376DA and 376DB for gang rape of a woman under sixteen and under twelve respectively, the latter carrying death, prescribed timelines for investigation and appeal in the procedure code, and restricted anticipatory bail for the new child offences. Every element of the 2018 package sits on the punishment side of the line the Verma report had drawn. Nothing in it touched the structural questions of 2013, none of which had moved in the interval. The script that 2013 wrote, in which a horrifying case produces a punishment amendment within weeks and the structural agenda waits, had by 2018 become the standard response of the Indian legislature to sexual violence, and the distance between the report and the statute, which in 2013 could still be measured with surprise, had become a fixed feature of the landscape.

The provisions of 2013 were in time carried, with the 2018 additions, into the successor penal code, [the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023](/2010/12/15/bharatiya-nyaya-sanhita-2023-explained/), whose chapter on offences against women and children reproduces the Section 375 definition, the consent explanation, the graded punishments, and the new conduct offences in substantially the terms the two amendments settled, while retaining the marital exception with the age that Independent Thought had written into it. The wider lineage of legislation for women's rights, of which this amendment forms one episode, is traced in [the guide to women's rights laws in India](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/), and a structured comparison of the amendment exercises across the years is maintained in [the comparison of criminal law amendments on women's offences](/2016/03/15/criminal-law-amendments-women-compared/), so that what follows can confine itself to the verdict on this trail alone. The amended codes themselves, in their full extent, are treated in [the complete guide to the Indian Penal Code](/2010/11/01/indian-penal-code-complete-guide/) and in [the guide to the Indian Evidence Act 1872](/2010/12/01/indian-evidence-act-1872-explained/), to which the reader is pointed for the operative text as it stood before the codes were replaced.

## What the Trail Predicts About the Next Amendment

Template and prediction are the uses of an amendment trail, and this trail supports three predictions about any future amendment of the sexual offence law enacted under crisis conditions. The first is that its punishment provisions will be its least debated and most severe feature, because severity is the one legislative act that every participant in a crisis debate can support at no institutional cost, and because the 2013 precedent shows that even a committee's evidence based refusal of severity can be reversed without political penalty. The second is that its procedural protections for complainants will be enacted in proportion as they are drafted in advance by an expert body, since the provisions of 2013 that survived whole, the recording duties, the statement mechanism, the character evidence bar, were the provisions that arrived in Parliament as finished text with a committee's authority behind them, while every proposal that required the legislature to design a new institution, a compensation board, a command liability doctrine, an electoral disqualification, died of its own novelty. The third prediction concerns the questions that will not be answered: any recommendation whose cost falls on the legislature's own composition, on the security establishment's immunities, or on the legal privileges of marriage will be deferred to the courts or to a later politics, because the three filters of 2013, executive drafting, parliamentary triage, and constituency cost, remain in place and operated identically in 2018.

Against those predictions stands one counterweight the trail also documents. The questions deferred in 2013 did not disappear. The marital exception was narrowed by a court within five years and stands under constitutional challenge; the two finger test was abolished by a judgment and a clinical protocol within one; the age of consent was re examined by the Law Commission within ten, on the committee's reasoning if not to its conclusion; and the victim neutrality question was forced back onto the agenda by the successor code's omission of Section 377, in a form its 2013 participants had not foreseen. A refused recommendation in a published report of this authority becomes part of the permanent brief against the settlement that refused it, available to every later court, commission, and campaign. The committee lost the votes of 2013. It has been winning a slower argument ever since, and an amendment trail that ends with the Act, without following the refusals into their afterlives, understates what the report achieved.

## The Verdict: A Measurable Distance

The namable claim of this article can now be stated with the evidence assembled. Between the Verma report of 23 January 2013 and the Act that received assent on 2 April 2013 there is a measurable distance, and it is not randomly distributed. Of the committee's penal recommendations, the widened definition, the consent standard, the new conduct offences, the acid attack provisions, the trafficking substitution, and the enhanced minimums were enacted substantially as proposed. Of its procedural recommendations, the recording duties, the magistrate statement, the free treatment obligation, the liability of the defaulting police officer, the character evidence bar, and the time bound trial were enacted substantially as proposed. Of its structural recommendations, marital rape, the armed forces sanction, command responsibility, and electoral disqualification, none was enacted in any form. And on the one question where the committee had taken a reasoned negative position after examining evidence, the death penalty, the legislature enacted the opposite. A filter that passes punishment and procedure and stops structure, and that adds severity where severity was refused, is not a failure of drafting or of time. It is the political system's revealed preference under maximum pressure, and it predicts, as the 2018 sequel confirmed, how the next crisis amendment will behave before it is written.

Two qualifications keep the verdict honest. The first is that the enacted two thirds of the report was real. A complainant in a police station, a hospital casualty ward, or a witness box is differently placed because of Sections 166A, 357C, 164(5A), and 53A than she was in 2012, and the conduct offences filled gaps through which actual violence had passed unpunished. The narrowing thesis is about the boundary of reform, not about the worth of what crossed it. The second qualification is that the refused third was not refused by one actor. The executive filtered the report into the ordinance; Parliament, given the chance to restore what the ordinance had dropped, spent its amendments on the ordinance's own design instead; the political class as a whole declined the electoral recommendation that touched itself; and the courts, when the exception reached them, corrected its edge and left its centre to the legislature. The statute, as this series maintains across its amendment trails, is the end of a process and not the process itself, and the 2013 amendment is the proof that repays closest reading, because its process is the best documented in modern Indian legislation: a report that can be read, an ordinance that can be compared with it, an Act that can be compared with both, and a distance between the first and the last that any reader can now measure without assistance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013 change?

The Act amended the Indian Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the Indian Evidence Act, together with a consequential change to the child protection statute. It redefined rape in Section 375, created the offences of sexual harassment, disrobing, voyeurism, and stalking in Sections 354A to 354D, added acid attack offences in Sections 326A and 326B, substituted a trafficking offence in Section 370, rebuilt the punishments in Sections 376 to 376E, made a police officer's refusal to record a complaint an offence under Section 166A, required free medical treatment for victims, barred evidence of a victim's character and past sexual experience, and directed that rape trials be completed within two months of the charge sheet as far as possible.

### Q: Why was the Verma Committee set up and how fast did it report?

The government constituted the committee on 23 December 2012, a week after the Delhi gang rape of 16 December 2012 and in the middle of sustained public protests demanding legislative change, and charged it with recommending amendments to the criminal law for quicker trial and enhanced punishment for sexual assault against women. Its members were Justice J S Verma, a former Chief Justice of India, Justice Leila Seth, and Gopal Subramanium. It received around eighty thousand public submissions, worked for twenty nine days, and submitted a report of some six hundred and thirty pages on 23 January 2013.

### Q: Which Verma Committee recommendations were rejected?

Four structural recommendations were rejected outright: deleting the marital rape exception in Section 375, removing the prior sanction requirement for prosecuting armed forces personnel for sexual offences, introducing command responsibility for superior officers, and attaching electoral disqualification to sexual offence charges. Two calibrated positions were also refused: the committee's recommendation that the age of consent be sixteen, where the Act chose eighteen, and its reasoned refusal of the death penalty, where the Act introduced death for rape causing death or a persistent vegetative state and for repeat offenders, punishments the committee had examined and advised against.

### Q: Why did women's groups criticise the 2013 ordinance?

Women's organisations criticised the ordinance of February 2013 on several grounds at once. It replaced the offence of rape with a gender neutral offence of sexual assault, which the Verma Committee had not proposed, and they argued that a gender neutral perpetrator would expose complainants to counter accusations by the accused and pressure to withdraw. It introduced the death penalty the committee had refused. It retained the marital rape exception. It kept the age of consent at eighteen against the committee's advice. And it was promulgated without parliamentary debate, so groups that had contributed submissions to the committee found the filtered result enacted before any legislature had examined it.

### Q: Did the 2013 Act criminalise marital rape?

No. Exception 2 to Section 375, which provides that sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife is not rape, was retained by the ordinance and by the Act, against the Verma Committee's recommendation that it be deleted. As enacted, the exception protected a husband where the wife was not under fifteen years of age, an age that sat uneasily beside the Act's own age of consent of eighteen. The Supreme Court in Independent Thought, in 2017, read the exception down so that it does not protect a husband where the wife is below eighteen, but the exception for adult wives remains in the statute.

### Q: Which new offences did the Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013 add?

The Act added sexual harassment in Section 354A, assault or criminal force with intent to disrobe in Section 354B, voyeurism in Section 354C, and stalking in Section 354D of the Penal Code. It added voluntarily causing grievous hurt by acid in Section 326A and throwing or attempting to throw acid in Section 326B. It substituted a defined offence of trafficking of persons in Section 370 and exploitation of a trafficked person in Section 370A. It added Section 166A, punishing a public servant who disobeys directions of law in investigation or fails to record specified complaints, and Section 166B, punishing a hospital in charge that refuses the free treatment Section 357C of the procedure code requires.

### Q: Why did the government use an ordinance in 2013?

Parliament was not in session when the Verma Committee reported on 23 January 2013, the budget session was weeks away, and the government faced continuing protests in the capital demanding immediate legislative action after the December 2012 gang rape. An ordinance allowed the new offences and punishments to take legal effect at once, on 3 February 2013, instead of waiting for a bill to pass both Houses. The Cabinet approved the ordinance on 1 February. The cost of the route, as critics pointed out at the time, was that the text was drafted inside the executive without committee examination or parliamentary debate, and several of its choices had to be revisited when the bill reached Parliament in March.

### Q: Did the Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013 introduce the death penalty for rape?

The Act introduced the death penalty at two points in the punishment ladder, and it did so against the Verma Committee's advice. Section 376A makes death an available sentence where a person committing rape inflicts an injury that causes the death of the woman or causes her to be in a persistent vegetative state, the alternative being imprisonment of not less than twenty years extending to imprisonment for the remainder of natural life. Section 376E makes death available for a repeat offender previously convicted under Sections 376, 376A, or 376D and convicted again. Ordinary rape under Section 376(1) and gang rape under Section 376D do not carry death; their maximum is imprisonment for the remainder of natural life.

### Q: Can a police officer be prosecuted for refusing to record an FIR in a sexual offence case?

Yes. Section 166A of the Penal Code, inserted by the 2013 Act, makes it an offence for a public servant to fail to record any information given under Section 154(1) of the Code of Criminal Procedure in relation to a cognisable offence under the specified provisions, which include the acid attack sections, Section 354, Section 354B, the trafficking sections, the Section 376 series, and Section 509. The punishment is rigorous imprisonment for not less than six months extending to two years, and a fine. The same section also punishes a public servant who knowingly disobeys a direction of law governing the conduct of an investigation, to the prejudice of any person.

### Q: Are hospitals required to treat victims of rape and acid attack free of cost?

Yes. Section 357C of the Code of Criminal Procedure, inserted by the 2013 Act, requires all hospitals, public or private, whether run by the Central Government, a State Government, local bodies, or any other person, to provide immediately, free of cost, first aid or medical treatment to victims of the offences specified in it, which include the acid attack offence in Section 326A and the Section 376 series, and to inform the police immediately of the incident. A person in charge of a hospital who contravenes that duty commits an offence under Section 166B of the Penal Code, punishable with imprisonment of up to one year, or a fine, or both.

### Q: Why did Parliament fix the age of consent at eighteen when the Verma Committee favoured sixteen?

The committee recommended sixteen, the age the Penal Code had long carried, partly because a threshold of eighteen criminalises consensual relationships between older adolescents and is frequently set in motion by disapproving families rather than by the young woman herself. Parliament chose eighteen for three connected reasons: the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012 had just fixed eighteen as the boundary of childhood for its offences, and a lower Penal Code threshold would have set the two statutes against each other; members across parties treated a higher threshold as the more protective position in the political conditions of March 2013; and the all party consultation before the debate settled on eighteen, from which the bill did not move.

### Q: What does Section 114A of the Evidence Act presume about consent?

Section 114A, which the 2013 Act substituted in a wider form, provides that in a prosecution for rape under the specified aggravated clauses of Section 376(2) of the Penal Code, where sexual intercourse by the accused is proved and the question is whether it was without the consent of the woman, and the woman states in her evidence before the court that she did not consent, the court shall presume that she did not consent. The presumption is rebuttable. It does not relieve the prosecution of proving the sexual act itself or the identity of the accused; it allocates the evidential burden on the single issue of consent once the complainant has testified to its absence.

### Q: Can the accused question the victim about her past sexual history in a rape trial after 2013?

No, where consent is the issue. Section 53A of the Evidence Act, inserted by the 2013 Act, makes evidence of the character of the victim or of her previous sexual experience with any person irrelevant on the issue of consent or the quality of consent in a prosecution for the specified offences. The proviso the Act added to Section 146 bars questions in cross examination of the victim as to her general immoral character or previous sexual experience for the purpose of proving consent. Before 2013, provisions of the old law had allowed a rape trial to examine the complainant's sexual history, a practice whose exposure in the Mathura case of 1978 had driven an earlier round of reform.

### Q: What punishment does Section 376A prescribe where rape causes death or a persistent vegetative state?

Section 376A, inserted by the 2013 Act, applies where a person committing an offence punishable under Section 376(1) or Section 376(2) inflicts in the course of it an injury which causes the death of the woman or causes her to be in a persistent vegetative state. The punishment is rigorous imprisonment for a term not less than twenty years, which may extend to imprisonment for life, meaning imprisonment for the remainder of that person's natural life, or death. The Verma Committee had proposed the twenty year minimum and the natural life maximum for this offence but had advised against adding the death penalty, which the ordinance introduced and Parliament retained.

### Q: Is a first conviction for stalking bailable under the 2013 Act?

Yes. Section 354D of the Penal Code, which created the offence of stalking, punishes a first conviction with imprisonment of up to three years and a fine, and the offence on a first conviction is bailable; a second or subsequent conviction carries up to five years and a fine and is non bailable. The bail position was a parliamentary choice: the ordinance's scheme had been sterner, and during the March 2013 debate members pressed the concern that an open textured conduct offence, non bailable on a first complaint, would be turned against young men in relationships of which families disapproved, so the first offence was made bailable while the definition and the repeat offence were kept strict.

### Q: How did the Supreme Court change the marital exception in Independent Thought in 2017?

In Independent Thought v Union of India, decided in 2017, the Supreme Court read down Exception 2 to Section 375 to resolve its conflict with the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012, which treats every person below eighteen as a child and knows no marital exception. The Court held that the exception must be read as if it said that sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under eighteen years of age, is not rape. The judgment was an act of interpretation rather than a striking down, it harmonised the two statutes at the boundary of childhood, and the Court expressly declined to pronounce on the exception's operation between adults.

### Q: What did Section 370 of the Penal Code punish before the 2013 Act replaced it with trafficking?

Before the substitution, Section 370 punished buying or disposing of any person as a slave: whoever imports, exports, removes, buys, sells, or disposes of any person as a slave, or accepts, receives, or detains against his will any person as a slave, faced imprisonment of up to seven years and a fine. It was a nineteenth century provision aimed at the slave trade, and it did not describe modern trafficking, which works by recruitment, deception, coercion, and abuse of power rather than by formal sale. The 2013 Act replaced it with a definition of trafficking built on those means and on the purpose of exploitation, and added Section 370A for the exploitation of a trafficked person.

### Q: Why does the Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013 take effect from 3 February 2013 when it was passed in April?

Because the Act replaced an ordinance that was already in force. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Ordinance was promulgated on 3 February 2013 and created the offences and punishments from that date, while Parliament was not in session. When Parliament enacted the bill in March, with modifications, the resulting Act was deemed to have come into force on 3 February 2013, the ordinance's date, so that there is no gap in the legal sequence and nothing done under the ordinance, including proceedings begun for offences committed after that date, falls between the two instruments. The President assented to the Act on 2 April 2013, and its commencement was retrospective to the ordinance by the Act's own terms.

### Q: What offence does Section 376B create for a husband who is separated from his wife?

Section 376B punishes a husband who has sexual intercourse with his own wife, who is living separately whether under a decree of separation or otherwise, without her consent, with imprisonment of not less than two years extending to seven years, and a fine. The offence occupies a deliberate middle position in the 2013 scheme: the marital exception in Section 375 continues to protect a husband within a subsisting cohabiting marriage, but once the wife is living separately the protection falls away and non consensual intercourse becomes an offence, though a lesser one than rape itself. The procedure code adds a safeguard against misuse of the provision: under Section 198B, a court may take cognizance only on the wife's own complaint and upon its prima facie satisfaction with the facts.

### Q: How quickly must a rape trial be completed under the amended Section 309 of the Code of Criminal Procedure?

Section 309(1) of the Code of Criminal Procedure, as substituted by the 2013 Act, requires proceedings in every inquiry or trial to continue from day to day until all witnesses in attendance have been examined, with adjournments beyond the following day only for recorded reasons. Its proviso adds that where the inquiry or trial relates to an offence under Sections 376, 376A, 376B, 376C, or 376D of the Penal Code, it shall, as far as possible, be completed within a period of two months from the date of filing of the charge sheet. The two month direction is qualified by the words as far as possible, so it operates as a statutory discipline on adjournment practice rather than as a limitation period after which a trial fails.
