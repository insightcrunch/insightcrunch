Gatsby and modernist American fiction have an awkward relationship in the classroom. The novel is taught as a modernist text, appears on modernism syllabuses, and shares a decade and a set of methods with the movement's central works, yet almost nobody finds it hard. Students who need three weeks and a guide to get through the era's celebrated experiments read this one over a weekend and enjoy it. That mismatch, between the movement it belongs to and the ease with which it goes down, is the placement's real subject. Resolving it produces an argument about what modernism actually required and what it merely happened to look like.

![Gatsby and modernist American fiction of the 1920s and its place in the movement explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-39.webp)

The stakes are not merely taxonomic. How a reader files the novel determines what they look for in it. Filed as a social novel about the 1920s, it becomes a document of Prohibition, parties, and new money, and the analysis runs toward history. Filed as a modernist work, it becomes a book about fragmented time, unreliable telling, symbolic compression, and the collapse of inherited meaning, and the analysis runs toward method. Both files contain real material. The second is the one most often skipped, because the prose is so hospitable that readers stop noticing how strange the construction underneath it is.

## What This Placement Sets Side by Side

The comparison here is a novel against a movement, which requires more care than a novel against another novel. Movements are retrospective constructions; no group of writers in 1925 filled out a membership form. What existed was a cluster of shared problems and a set of overlapping solutions, and placement means asking which problems the novel took up and which solutions it adopted, refused, or modified.

The problems were these. Inherited narrative order, the confident third-person chronicle that carried the nineteenth-century novel, had come to feel false to writers who had watched a war dismantle the civilization that produced it. Inherited moral certainty had gone with it. Language itself had come under suspicion, since the vocabulary of honor, glory, and sacrifice had been spent on the war and returned devalued. And the city, the crowd, the machine, and the advertisement had produced a texture of experience that older forms could not register.

The solutions clustered too. Fragmentation of chronology. Narration that could not be fully trusted. Symbol used as compression rather than as decoration. Myth laid under contemporary material as a structural frame. Interior time given priority over clock time. A refusal of the summarizing conclusion. Difficulty accepted as a cost of accuracy.

Fitzgerald's novel takes up nearly all the problems and adopts most of the solutions. What it declines is the last one. That declining is the whole story of its placement, and it is why the craft-side companion on [modernist technique in the novel](/2026/08/03/gatsby-modernist-technique/) can analyze the methods in isolation while this article has to argue about the company the book keeps.

### What is literary modernism?

Literary modernism is the early twentieth-century movement that broke with inherited narrative order and moral certainty after they stopped feeling adequate to modern experience. Its characteristic methods include fragmented chronology, unreliable or limited narration, compressed symbolism, mythic underlay, and a refusal of tidy resolution. Difficulty was frequently accepted as the price.

## What 1925 Looked Like

Placing the novel means naming the year it landed in, and 1925 was a dense one.

The movement's foundational documents were three years old. The long poem that mapped a post-war spiritual wasteland and the enormous Irish novel that rebuilt a single Dublin day on the frame of an ancient epic had both appeared in 1922, and both had done the thing modernism became notorious for: they had made the reader work. In the same year Fitzgerald published his novel, Virginia Woolf published a book that compressed a London day into consciousness rather than plot, Theodore Dreiser published a vast naturalist account of ambition and murder, John Dos Passos published a novel that assembled New York out of disconnected fragments and headlines, and Hemingway published the collection of stories that established the stripped style he would carry into his novels. Faulkner had not yet produced the books that would matter; his experiments arrive at the end of the decade.

The company is worth stating precisely because it shows the range inside the movement. Dos Passos and Woolf were experimenting with form directly, breaking the chapter and the paragraph. Dreiser was working in an older naturalist mode that modernism was reacting against. Hemingway was doing something modernist by subtraction, stripping the sentence rather than complicating it. Fitzgerald sat between the subtractors and the complicators, and the position was deliberate.

The war stands behind all of it. Fitzgerald trained as an officer and did not reach France; Hemingway drove ambulances and was wounded; the writers who returned brought back a vocabulary that had stopped working. The novel registers this glancingly rather than centrally, which is one reason its modernism gets overlooked. Nick's war service is mentioned and dropped. Gatsby's is a plot device that supplies the separation from Daisy and the decorations he can produce as evidence. But the disillusion the war produced is everywhere in the book's texture, and the [Lost Generation context](/2026/12/21/lost-generation-great-gatsby/) article traces the connection between that historical experience and the literary posture it produced.

## Gatsby and Modernist American Fiction: The Movement Table

The findable artifact for this article is a movement table setting the novel against its 1920s contemporaries on the methods that define the period, with a column for what each book asks of its reader. Call it the modernist accessibility audit.

| Work and year | Chronology | Narration | Symbol and myth | Demand on the reader |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The long 1922 poem of the wasteland | Fragmentary by design; no continuous sequence | Multiple unattributed voices | Myth as the organizing structure | Very high; notes required |
| The 1922 Dublin novel | Single day, radically expanded | Shifting techniques chapter by chapter | Epic frame under domestic material | Very high; a guide is standard |
| Woolf, 1925 | Single day; interior time governs | Free indirect movement between minds | Recurring images carry the structure | High; plot recedes |
| Dos Passos, 1925 | Deliberately discontinuous | Multiple detached third-person threads | The city itself as the organizing figure | High; no central character |
| Hemingway, mid-1920s | Linear; scenes in sequence | First person, flat, withholding | Meaning carried by omission | Moderate; the difficulty is in what is absent |
| Dreiser, 1925 | Linear and exhaustive | Omniscient, documentary | Little symbolic compression | Low in method; high in length |
| Fitzgerald, 1925 | Disrupted; backstory withheld and reordered | First person, limited, self-undermining | Compressed symbol; mythic register in the close | Low; the surface carries the reader |

The final row is the anomaly and the argument. Read across it and every methodological box is checked except the last. The novel does what its difficult contemporaries do and asks almost nothing of the reader in exchange.

## The Methods It Shares

Four modernist methods operate in the novel and each can be shown at the level of the page.

Chronology is disrupted, systematically and with purpose. The narration begins after everything has ended and is delivered by a man writing at a distance of at least a year. Gatsby's origins are withheld until the sixth chapter, so the reader spends five chapters constructing a figure out of rumor before receiving the facts, and the facts then retroactively rewrite everything already read. The Louisville courtship arrives in chapter four as a story told at a party by a third party. The Dan Cody years arrive out of sequence with an announcement that they are being inserted. The events of a single summer are delivered in an order that a straight chronicle would never use, and the order is doing interpretive work: the reader meets the invented man before the inventor, which is the sequence the invention was designed to produce.

Narration is limited and undermines itself. The book has no access to the mind of its central figure, offers its most important psychological moment as speculation, and is delivered by a narrator who claims a virtue in terms designed to be doubted and promises impartiality about a man he has already exempted from judgment. That configuration is squarely modernist. The nineteenth-century chronicle assumed a narrator who could be trusted about facts and values alike; this book supplies one who is unreliable about both and tells you so. The placement of Nick in the longer [witness-narrator tradition](/2028/08/14/nick-witness-narrator-tradition/) shows how far that arrangement predates the movement, but the self-undermining is the modernist part.

Symbol operates by compression rather than illustration. The green light is not an allegory with a fixed meaning attached; it changes register across three appearances and ends up carrying more than any paraphrase holds. The valley of ashes is presented in a mode closer to vision than to description, a "fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens" populated by "ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air." That is not a place a train passes; it is a landscape of the spirit dressed as a location, which is exactly what the era's poetry was doing at the same moment. The eyes on the billboard supply a defunct divinity in an image an advertiser paid for, and the novel is careful that the meaning is projected by a broken man rather than emanating from the sign, which is a modernist move about the loss of shared symbols rather than a symbolist one about their power.

Meaning is refused rather than delivered. The book declines to rule on whether its central figure's enterprise was worth undertaking, ends on a widening meditation rather than a summing-up, and leaves its most-argued questions permanently open. A Victorian novel closes accounts. This one closes with a sentence about being carried backward.

## The Nine Chapters as Modernist Architecture

The novel's structure is the least discussed piece of evidence for its placement and one of the strongest, because a book this short with this much rearrangement in it did not arrive at its shape by accident.

The design is symmetrical around a center. Chapters one and nine are both set at the narrator's house and both end on the dock with the green light, so the book closes a circle rather than running to a stop. Chapters three and six are the two parties, the first before Daisy and the second with her present, and the pairing is engineered so the same spectacle reads oppositely depending on who is watching. Chapter five, the reunion, sits at the exact midpoint and is the only chapter in which nothing is destroyed. Everything before it builds toward the meeting; everything after it disassembles what the meeting produced.

That is a designed structure rather than a chronicle, and design of this kind is a period marker. The nineteenth-century novel accumulates; it adds incident until the material is exhausted and then resolves. Modernist construction arranges: elements are placed for their relation to each other rather than for their sequence in time, and the reader is expected to feel the arrangement even without articulating it. Woolf builds a day around recurring images that return at intervals. The era's long poem is held together by placement rather than by narrative. Fitzgerald's nine chapters work the same way, which is why readers report a sense of inevitability about a plot that is, examined coldly, a series of coincidences.

The withholding follows the same architectural logic. Three separate accounts of the central figure's past are distributed across the book at calculated intervals: his own version in the car in chapter four, delivered in a manner that invites disbelief; Jordan's Louisville account later that same afternoon, at second hand; and the authorial correction in chapter six, arriving only after the reader has settled into a picture. A chronological novel would supply the origin first and let the reader watch the invention proceed. This one supplies the invention first and the origin last, which makes the reader complicit in the illusion before revealing it as one.

The final architectural decision is the placement of the death. It arrives at the end of chapter eight, reported as an aftermath rather than shown, leaving an entire chapter afterward for consequences. A conventional tragedy would end near the death. Giving the aftermath a full ninth of the book is a structural statement that the interesting material is not the killing but the silence that follows it, and the silence is where the novel's judgment on its society gets made.

## The Emptied Symbol

Modernism's most consistent preoccupation was the loss of shared meaning, the sense that the symbols a culture had inherited no longer connected to anything, and the novel takes that preoccupation up more directly than its reputation suggests.

The billboard is the clearest case. Eyes described as "blue and gigantic," with retinas "one yard high," which "look out of no face, but, instead, from a pair of enormous yellow spectacles which pass over a nonexistent nose," brood over the industrial waste between the suburbs and the city. The novel gives them a mundane history: an optician put them up to build a practice and then either moved away or, as Nick puts it, "sank down himself into eternal blindness." They have no meaning. They are a commercial object that outlived its commercial purpose.

Then Wilson looks at them in the worst hours of his life and tells his neighbor that God sees everything. The neighbor's reply, correcting him, is that it is only an advertisement. That exchange is the modernist problem in miniature: the appetite for transcendent meaning survives, the objects that used to carry it are gone, and what remains available for the appetite to fasten on is advertising. Fitzgerald is careful that the novel does not endorse Wilson's reading. The eyes do not watch. A grieving man projects onto them, and the projection is what the book is about.

The green light works the same way from the other end. It begins as a symbol with almost unbearable charge, a distant point the central figure reaches toward in the dark. Once he stands beside the woman it belongs to, the narrator records that "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." The symbol was never in the light; it was in the distance. That is a modernist account of how meaning works, and it is delivered in eight words in the middle of the book's happiest chapter.

The valley of ashes completes the set. Presented as "a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens," inhabited by "ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air," it is not a description of an industrial district. It is a landscape of spiritual exhaustion given a location on a commuter line, and it belongs to the same imaginative operation as the era's poetry of sterility and waste.

### Why does the billboard matter to the novel's modernism?

Because it dramatizes the loss of shared meaning rather than asserting it. The eyes are a dead advertisement with no significance of their own, and the significance arrives only when a broken man projects it onto them. The appetite for transcendence survives its object, which is the movement's central preoccupation compressed into one image.

## The Party Chapter as Collage

The third chapter contains the novel's most formally adventurous writing and it passes unnoticed because it is enjoyable.

The chapter opens in a mode that has no fixed moment. Nick describes the parties in general, using habitual constructions that cover a whole season at once: on weekends the car ran as a bus, on Mondays the servants repaired the damage, every Friday five crates of oranges arrived. Then, without any signal, the tense shifts into the present and the party assembles in real time, with the orchestra arriving and the lights growing brighter as the earth lurches away from the sun. Then it shifts back into a specific Saturday night at which Nick was, as he notes, one of the few guests who had actually been invited.

Three temporal registers inside a few pages, moving without transition. That is a modernist handling of time, and it does exactly what the era's fragmentors were doing with more visible seams: it presents the party as a recurring condition rather than an event, so the individual evening the reader is attending is felt as one instance of something continuous.

The chapter then does something closer to collage. Nick delivers a catalogue of guests, a run of names and fragments of gossip with no narrative connection between them, and he tells the reader he compiled it from a disused timetable, marking the list as a found document rather than a memory. Names arrive attached to nothing, some of them absurd, some of them followed by a scrap of biography, some of them admitted to be forgotten. The technique produces a social panorama by accumulation without organizing it, which is what the documentary fragmentors of the same year were building whole novels out of.

Set against Dos Passos, the difference is one of scale rather than kind. He assembled a city from such fragments across an entire book. Fitzgerald performs the operation for a few pages and then returns to his narrative. The method is present, used deliberately, and contained.

## Publication and the Slow Recognition

The novel's placement in the movement was not obvious in 1925 and the delay is part of the story.

Reviews were mixed rather than dismissive, and the book sold well below what Fitzgerald's earlier novels had done. He had expected it to establish him as a serious artist and it did not, at least not commercially, and his disappointment is documented in his correspondence with his editor. He continued to be paid far better for magazine stories than for the novel he considered his best work, and he died in 1940 with the book largely out of general circulation.

The recognition arrived afterward. The wartime years put the novel into wide distribution among American servicemen, and the critical revival that followed in the late 1940s and the 1950s established it in the position it now holds. That sequence matters to the placement for a specific reason: the novel was absorbed into the modernist canon retrospectively, by critics working with a fully formed idea of what modernism had been. Fitzgerald did not write into an established category. The category was assembled later and the book was placed in it.

That is worth keeping in view because it explains the persistent unease about the label. The difficult works were canonized as modernist while their authors were alive and arguing for the program. This one was canonized as modernist by readers who noticed, decades on, that the methods were all there. The classification is sound and it is also a piece of literary history rather than a fact about the author's intentions.

The working titles Fitzgerald considered supply a last piece of evidence about how he saw the book. Among them were one that named a figure from Roman satire, a byword for vulgar ostentation, and one that used the colors of the flag. Both point toward a novel of social diagnosis rather than a romance, and both suggest a writer thinking about what his book was arguing rather than what its story was. The [reception history of the novel](/2029/12/17/gatsby-critical-reception/) covers the full arc from mixed reviews to canonization.

## Against European Modernism

A brief look across the Atlantic clarifies what was specifically American about the novel's version of the movement.

European modernism was substantially preoccupied with consciousness: the interior monologue, the texture of thought, the disparity between what a mind contains and what a body does. Woolf's method is built on it. The Dublin novel's most celebrated passages are made of it. Fitzgerald has almost none. There is no interior monologue in this book, no stream of any character's consciousness, and the one mind the reader occupies is a narrator who is describing rather than experiencing.

What Fitzgerald has instead is social surface read symbolically. The book's method is to look hard at objects, houses, cars, shirts, lawns, a light, a billboard, and to make them carry the weight that European modernism put into thought. That is an American solution and it connects the novel backward to a native tradition of symbolic object-reading and forward to a great deal of American fiction that followed.

The consequence for placement is that the novel is modernist by a different route than the European monuments. Both refuse the confident third-person chronicle. The European route replaces it with interiority; the American route here replaces it with a compromised observer reading a world of things. Neither is more legitimate, and a student who has only been taught European modernism will find this book anomalous for the wrong reason: not because it is easy, but because its center of gravity is outside the mind.

## The Difficulty It Refuses

Having adopted the methods, the novel declines the cost that its contemporaries paid, and understanding how it manages that is the heart of the placement.

The difficult modernist works make their difficulty structural. The reader cannot proceed without doing interpretive labor, because the surface itself is discontinuous: voices are unattributed, techniques change without warning, syntax is disassembled, and the connective tissue that normally tells a reader where they are has been removed on purpose. The labor is the point. These books argue, by their form, that modern experience does not arrive in orderly sentences and that a form pretending otherwise would be lying.

Fitzgerald keeps the connective tissue. Every scene has a stated location and a stated cast. Every chapter follows the previous one with a clear temporal marker. The prose is grammatical, the sentences are complete, and the vocabulary is common. A reader is never uncertain about who is speaking or where the action stands. The modernist operations are all performed inside a surface that behaves.

That is why the book can be read at speed and still be doing what its difficult peers do. The disruptions are semantic rather than syntactic. Withholding Gatsby's origins until chapter six is a structural disruption that costs the reader nothing in comprehension and everything in assumption. Nick's unreliability is signaled in plain sentences that a fast reader can process and a slow reader can catch out. The symbols are presented as objects a character looks at, so they can be taken at face value on the first pass and figuratively on the second.

Length is part of the same strategy. The novel runs to roughly fifty thousand words, which is short for its ambitions and short for its era; the contemporaries it is filed with are two to ten times its size. Compression forced choices. There is no subplot that does not feed the main line, no scene that does not either advance the reunion or supply the material that will destroy it, and no descriptive passage that is not also doing symbolic work. The book is dense rather than long, which is a modernist virtue achieved by an unmodernist means: instead of expanding a day into hundreds of pages, Fitzgerald contracted a summer into a novella's length and let the pressure do the work.

The result is a book that produces modernist effects at conventional reading speed. That combination is rare and it explains the novel's position in the curriculum. It is the movement's ambassador: the text that can be assigned before the difficult ones because it teaches the methods without requiring the apparatus.

### What makes a novel modernist rather than realist?

Not subject matter but method and stance. A realist novel assumes a reliable narrator, a continuous chronology, and a world whose meanings can be reported. A modernist novel treats narration as partial, chronology as arrangeable, symbol as compression, and meaning as something the form withholds rather than delivers.

## The Eliot Connection

The relationship between the novel and the era's most influential poem is the most productive single comparison available here, and it can be made without speculation.

The poem, published three years before the novel, opens on a landscape of spiritual sterility, moves through fragments of contemporary urban life, layers mythic material under modern scenes, and treats the modern city as a place where meaning has drained out of inherited symbols. The novel's second chapter does a compressed version of the same operation. The desolate stretch between West Egg and New York is presented as a farm growing ashes, its inhabitants crumbling as they move, its only presiding figure a commercial advertisement whose enormous eyes brood over a "grey land and the spasms of bleak dust which drift endlessly over it." Wilson's mistaking the billboard for God is the poem's central problem in one gesture: a culture that has kept the form of religious attention and lost the object.

The connection has a documented biographical side. Eliot read the novel and wrote to Fitzgerald about it in strongly appreciative terms, and Fitzgerald valued the response enough that it stayed with him. That correspondence is genuine and often cited, and it matters here for a specific reason: it establishes that the movement's central figure recognized the book as belonging to the same project, which is external evidence for a placement that some readers still resist.

The differences are as instructive as the resemblance. The poem fragments its surface; the novel keeps its surface intact and fragments its chronology. The poem uses myth as scaffolding a reader must reconstruct; the novel uses mythic register only in its final pages, where the Dutch sailors and the fresh, green breast of the new world arrive as a widening rather than as a structural frame the reader has to decode. The poem requires notes. The novel requires attention.

## Beside Hemingway

The comparison with the other American writer of the moment sharpens the placement further, because Hemingway represents the opposite solution to the same problem.

Both writers concluded that the inflated diction of the pre-war novel had become unusable. Hemingway responded by stripping: short declarative sentences, concrete nouns, dialogue that carries the weight, and emotion signaled by what is left out. Fitzgerald responded by keeping the lyric register and undercutting it. His sentences retain the cadence and the adjectives that Hemingway threw away, but they are placed in the mouth of a narrator whose reliability is in question, and the beautiful formulations arrive attached to things the book is simultaneously exposing. The service of a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty is a magnificent phrase about a cheap object, and the magnificence and the cheapness are both in the sentence.

That is a genuinely different modernist strategy. Hemingway distrusted the beautiful sentence and removed it. Fitzgerald distrusted it and kept it, framed. Which strategy is more modernist is not a useful question; that both are responses to the same crisis of language is the useful observation. The head-to-head reading of the two novels is developed in the comparison with [The Sun Also Rises](/2028/05/29/gatsby-vs-sun-also-rises/), and the stylistic contest itself is the subject of [Fitzgerald versus Hemingway as stylists](/2028/10/23/fitzgerald-vs-hemingway-style/).

One further contrast belongs here. Hemingway's difficulty is invisible to a fast reader in a different way: nothing on the surface is hard, but the meaning is in the omissions, so a reader who takes the surface at face value gets a story about fishing or drinking and misses the wound underneath. Fitzgerald's surface is not withholding in that way. His prose tells the reader what to feel and then complicates the instruction. The two books are equally accessible and unequally forthcoming, and students who find Hemingway flat and Fitzgerald rich are responding to a real difference in method rather than a difference in quality.

## Beside Faulkner and the Fragmentors

The comparison with the writers who broke the surface entirely clarifies what the novel gave up.

Faulkner's major experiments arrive at the end of the decade and go where Fitzgerald declined to go: consciousness rendered without punctuation or chronological anchoring, narrators who cannot be understood on a first pass, the same events told from incompatible positions with no arbitration. That method buys something real. It produces an experience of confusion that is itself the content, and it can render mental states that orderly prose cannot reach.

What Fitzgerald bought instead is transmissibility. A book that behaves on the surface can be read by a seventeen-year-old, taught in a single unit, quoted from memory, and carried into a culture that does not read difficult books. Whether that is a gain or a compromise is a live critical question and a good essay topic. The comparison of [Fitzgerald and Faulkner on the American Dream](/2028/10/30/fitzgerald-vs-faulkner/) takes up the thematic side of the pairing.

Dos Passos in the same year offers the sharpest contrast on structure. His New York novel refuses a central character altogether, assembling the city from disconnected lives and documentary fragments so that no single consciousness organizes the material. Fitzgerald's New York is organized entirely by one consciousness, and the organizing is the book's method. The two books demonstrate that the modernist city could be rendered either by dispersal or by concentration, and that concentration, in this case, produced the more durable object.

## The Counter-Reading: Not Modernist At All

The strongest objection holds that the novel is a well-made romantic tragedy in a modernist decade and that the label is applied by association rather than by analysis. The case deserves a full statement.

On this reading, the book has a hero, a heroine, a rival, a love plot, a death, and a moral, which is the equipment of the nineteenth-century novel rather than of the movement that repudiated it. Its prose is lyrical in a register the modernists were reacting against. Its symbols are legible enough for schoolchildren, which is the opposite of compression. Its ending delivers a resounding statement about human aspiration, which is precisely the summarizing conclusion modernism refused. And its author was a commercial magazine writer producing popular fiction for money, not a member of the avant-garde. Filed honestly, it is a late Romantic novel with modern furniture.

The objection has real force and two things answer it.

The first is that the equipment is present and disabled. There is a love plot, and it fails in a manner that makes its own conventions look ridiculous: the reunion the reader has waited five chapters for happens offstage, and the great romantic confrontation at the Plaza is conducted in a rented suite in unbearable heat over a bottle of whiskey while a wedding band plays downstairs. There is a hero, and he is a criminal whose grandeur is asserted by an unreliable observer rather than demonstrated. There is a moral, and it is delivered by a narrator the book has taught us to doubt. Deploying the old machinery in order to show it failing is a modernist procedure, not an evasion of one.

The second is that the resounding ending is not a summing-up. Read it closely and it resolves nothing about the plot; it changes scale instead, moving from a dock to a continent and ending on an image of unstoppable regression. The last sentence tells the reader that the effort described is permanent, general, and unsuccessful. That is a refusal of conclusion wearing the clothes of one, and mistaking it for a moral is the most common misreading of the book's final page.

Neither answer requires denying the objection's core observation. The novel is more hospitable, more shapely, and more emotionally direct than its difficult contemporaries. The argument is that hospitality is not the criterion.

## Against Naturalism: The 1925 Test Case

The most exact way to isolate what is modernist about the novel is to set it against a book published the same year that takes the same subject and handles it by an older method.

Dreiser's account of a poor young man who attaches himself to a wealthy world, acquires an obstacle in the form of a woman, and is destroyed by his own ambition covers the same social ground as Fitzgerald's. Both books are about class, aspiration, money, and a death by water. Both were published in 1925. The two treatments could hardly be less alike, and the difference is entirely a difference of method rather than of subject.

Dreiser proceeds by accumulation and documentation. His narration is omniscient and does not doubt itself. His chronology is continuous and complete: the reader is given the protagonist's childhood, adolescence, and adulthood in order, at length, with the social conditions laid out as causes. His symbolism is minimal because his argument does not need it; the sociology is stated. The book is enormous and its power comes from the weight of the accumulated evidence.

Fitzgerald proceeds by compression and inference. His narrator is limited, personally involved, and demonstrably partial. His chronology is arranged for effect. His social argument is never stated as sociology; it is carried by a landscape of ashes, a billboard, a light on a dock, and a narrator's closing meditation. The book is short and its power comes from what has been left out.

The comparison settles the question of whether subject matter can make a book modernist. Both novels describe a poor man destroyed by the American promise. One is a naturalist document and the other is a modernist construction, and the difference lives entirely in narration, chronology, and symbol. The related placement of both books within the [American Dream novel tradition](/2028/07/31/gatsby-american-dream-novels/) handles the thematic overlap, and the head-to-head reading is developed in the comparison with [An American Tragedy](/2028/06/19/gatsby-vs-american-tragedy/).

### Is Gatsby closer to naturalism or modernism?

Modernism, and the 1925 comparison proves it. Dreiser handles nearly identical material through omniscient narration, continuous chronology, and documented social causation. Fitzgerald handles it through a compromised observer, rearranged time, and compressed symbol. The subject is shared; only the method separates them, which is where the classification lives.

## The Sentences Where the Method Shows

A placement argument should be testable at the smallest scale, and three sentences in this novel carry the whole case.

The first is the conditional that introduces the central figure. Nick writes that "if personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him, some heightened sensitivity to the promises of life." Examine the construction. The elevation arrives inside a hypothetical, so the reader is offered a definition and asked to accept it before the praise can land. A confident nineteenth-century narrator would state that the man was remarkable. This one proposes a criterion, notes that under that criterion the man qualifies, and leaves the criterion standing there available for rejection. Greatness has become conditional on a premise the reader may decline. That is the epistemic uncertainty of the period built into a single sentence.

The second is the recognition at the pool. Nick writes that his subject "must have felt that he had lost the old warm world, paid a high price for living too long with a single dream." The modal verb converts the book's central psychological event into an inference. No omniscient narrator would write it this way, because omniscience does not need to guess. No confident first-person narrator would write it this way either, since he would either know or say he did not know. The sentence performs a stance: the interior of another person is unavailable, and the best any account can do is a well-founded reconstruction offered as such.

The third is the last. "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past." The grammar is worth slowing over. The verb is present and continuous, so the action never completes. The subject has widened from a single man to everyone, including the reader. The direction is backward while the effort is forward, so the sentence describes a permanent failure rather than a completed one. Nothing is resolved, nobody is judged, and no lesson is drawn. It is the refusal of conclusion executed in a cadence so satisfying that generations of readers have mistaken it for a conclusion.

Three sentences, three period markers: conditional stature, inferred interiority, refused resolution. Any of them could be quoted in an essay as direct evidence that the novel's method belongs to its moment rather than to the century before.

## The Epigraph and the Frame

The book opens with an epigraph attributed to a poet who does not exist, and the invention is a modernist gesture that almost every reader walks past.

The verse instructs a lover to wear a gold hat and bounce high if that is what it takes to win the woman, and the attribution is to a name Fitzgerald had used before as a character in his first novel. The poem is his own, presented as a quotation from someone else. That is a small act of literary impersonation of a kind the period specialized in, and it establishes the book's relation to authority before the first sentence: what looks like an inherited voice is a constructed one, and the reader who checks will find nothing behind it.

The gesture rhymes with everything the novel does afterward. A man presents an invented self and the reader accepts it for five chapters. A narrator presents an assertion of greatness and the reader accepts it for nine. A billboard presents a watching face and a grieving man accepts it. An epigraph presents an authority and the reader accepts it. The book is built out of attributions that do not hold up when examined, and it opens with one.

The frame closes at the other end with the final chapter's return to the empty house, where Nick erases an obscene word a boy has scratched on the white steps and then walks down to the beach. The novel's last physical action is the deletion of graffiti from the front of a mansion nobody lives in. A tidier book would end at the funeral. This one adds a scene in which the narrator performs a small, futile act of maintenance on a monument to a project that failed, and then generalizes it into a statement about a continent. The gap between the pettiness of the action and the scale of the meditation that follows is the last of the novel's controlled instabilities.

## Using the Placement in Practice

The argument has a practical payoff for anyone writing about the book, and it is worth stating plainly.

Once the novel is understood as a modernist construction with a hospitable surface, a whole class of essay questions becomes easier to answer well. Questions about narration stop being questions about Nick's personality and become questions about what a limited narrator lets a book claim. Questions about symbolism stop being decoding exercises and become questions about compression and the loss of shared meaning. Questions about structure stop being plot summaries and become arguments about arrangement. Questions about the ending stop being about whether the book is optimistic and become about the difference between a widening and a conclusion.

The placement also supplies a defense against the most common weakness in essays on this novel, which is treating the beautiful writing as the point. A student who calls the prose lyrical and quotes something lovely has said nothing an examiner can reward. A student who observes that the lyricism is systematically attached to false or cheap objects, and that the gap is the method, has an argument. The vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty formulation is the single best demonstration, because the sentence contains its own deflation.

One caution belongs with the payoff. The placement is a lens rather than a verdict on the whole book, and a reading that sees only method will miss what the novel does emotionally. The methods are in service of something: a man who wanted an impossible thing, a narrator who could not decide what he thought about him, and a society that closed over the gap without noticing. An essay that demonstrates modernist technique and forgets to say what the technique achieves has passed one test and failed a more important one.

## The Systematic Anti-Climax

One further pattern belongs in the placement, because it runs through the whole book and has no equivalent in the fiction the movement was reacting against: every set piece the novel builds toward is deliberately spoiled at the moment of arrival.

The reunion is the clearest case. Four chapters of preparation, an afternoon of rain, flowers bought and a house borrowed, and then the meeting itself begins with a clock nearly knocked off a mantelpiece and a host who looks, Nick says, as though he has passed through two states and is entering a third. The scene the reader has been promised does not deliver a scene; it delivers embarrassment, then a shift into an intimacy the narrator is excluded from and cannot report. The payoff is withheld at the exact point a conventional romance would supply it.

The confrontation is spoiled by its setting. Two men fight over a woman in a rented suite at the Plaza on the hottest afternoon of the summer, with the windows open, mint and ice being sent for, and a wedding march audible from a ballroom below. The novel could have staged the scene anywhere. It chooses a hired room, oppressive heat, and the sound of someone else's celebration, which drains the dignity out of the confrontation while the confrontation is happening.

The party chapters are spoiled by scale. The reader is shown magnificence and then shown the host standing apart from it, sober, watching, unattended. The second party is spoiled by Daisy's presence, which is what it was arranged for: she is appalled rather than delighted, and the spectacle that had been impressive when she was absent becomes vulgar the moment she looks at it.

The death is spoiled by its narration. It happens offstage, is reported as an aftermath, and is delivered to the reader through a chauffeur's uncertainty and a disturbance in the surface of the water. The most consequential event in the plot receives no scene at all.

And the funeral is spoiled by absence, which is the pattern's endpoint. The novel builds an entire social world across two hundred pages and then declines to bring any of it to the grave.

Read together, these are not lapses of construction. They are a consistent method, and the method is a period marker. The nineteenth-century novel delivers its set pieces; the payoff is the contract. Modernist fiction breaks the contract on principle, on the argument that experience does not arrive shaped and that a form supplying shapely climaxes is falsifying. Fitzgerald applies the principle six times in a short book, and applies it so smoothly that most readers register disappointment as a feeling about the characters rather than as an effect the author engineered.

The systematic anti-climax also explains something about the novel's emotional afterlife. Readers report that the book leaves an ache rather than a catharsis, and that the ache is disproportionate to its length. Withholding six payoffs in a row does that. Nothing in the plot completes, the one moment of arrival is immediately marked as a subtraction from the count of enchanted objects, and the final page states that the arriving was never available in the first place. The feeling the book produces is the formal method converted into an emotion, which is as good a definition of a successful modernist construction as the period offers.

One caveat keeps the pattern honest. Not every deflation in the book is doing formal work; some of it is character. Gatsby is a genuinely awkward man in private, and the clock and the wet hair in chapter five are as much characterization as method. What makes the pattern legible as method is its consistency across scenes that have nothing else in common. A single spoiled reunion is a novelist observing that reunions are awkward. Six spoiled set pieces in a row, covering romance, confrontation, spectacle, death, and burial, is a policy.

The policy has one exception, and the exception proves it. The closing meditation on the dock delivers everything it promises. It is the only passage in the book that arrives at full scale without being undercut, and it is placed after all the plot has been exhausted, where no character is present to spoil it and nothing further can happen. Fitzgerald withholds the payoff from every scene and grants it once, to a paragraph with no events in it. That distribution is a statement about where meaning was available to a writer in 1925: not in what people do to each other, but in what one of them can say about it afterward, alone, looking at water in the dark.

## What the Placement Illuminates in the Novel

Reading the book as a modernist work rather than as a period romance changes three specific things.

It changes the reading of chapter two. Treated as scene-setting, the valley of ashes is background: a poor industrial district between the suburb and the city where Wilson has a garage. Treated as a modernist landscape, it becomes the book's argument in compressed form, a place where the byproduct of the wealth on display in chapter one is shown accumulating, presided over by a dead advertisement that a grieving man will later mistake for God. The chapter is not a stop on the way to New York; it is the spiritual condition the rest of the novel takes place inside.

It changes the reading of the withheld biography. Treated as suspense, the delay in revealing Gatsby's origins is a storytelling trick that keeps the reader curious. Treated as method, the delay is an argument about how identity works in the period: the reader constructs Gatsby out of rumor exactly as the party guests do, believes several contradictory things about him at once, and then receives the facts too late to undo the construction. The novel makes the reader perform the process it is describing.

It changes the reading of the prose. Treated as beautiful writing, Fitzgerald's lyricism is a pleasure and a period style. Treated as modernist practice, it is a controlled instability: gorgeous sentences delivered by a compromised speaker about false objects, so that the reader is never permitted to enjoy the beauty without also registering what it is attached to. The essential feature of the style is not its music but the gap between the music and its occasion. The dedicated study of [Fitzgerald's prose style](/2026/02/09/fitzgerald-prose-style/) works through the sentence-level mechanics.

## What the Novel Illuminates in the Movement

The traffic runs the other way, and this direction is where the placement becomes an argument.

The novel demonstrates that difficulty was a strategy rather than a requirement. Every modernist problem it takes up gets a modernist solution, and none of the solutions requires the reader to struggle. That is a substantial claim about the movement, and it is contestable in the productive way: someone can reasonably answer that the difficult works were registering a disorder that a well-behaved surface necessarily falsifies. But the burden shifts. If a book can fragment chronology, undermine its narrator, compress its symbols, and refuse its conclusion while remaining readable in an afternoon, then difficulty has to be defended as doing something extra rather than assumed as intrinsic.

The novel also demonstrates the durability advantage of concentration. Modernism's characteristic move was expansion: a day stretched across hundreds of pages, a consciousness followed at length, a city assembled from dozens of threads. Fitzgerald moved the other way and contracted a summer into a book most readers finish in two sittings. A century on, the contracted version is the one general readers know, and the reason is not simply that it is easier. Compression made every element multi-functional, and multi-functional elements are what survive translation into a culture's common stock. The green light works as an image, a plot object, a psychological state, and a national argument at once, and it fits in a phrase.

Finally, the novel shows what modernism looks like when it is not defensive. The difficult works often carry a polemical edge, an insistence that the reader who wants comfort should look elsewhere. Fitzgerald's book has no quarrel with its reader. It performs the movement's operations without announcing them and without demanding credit for them, which is why generations of readers have absorbed a thoroughly modernist experience while believing they were reading a love story about the twenties.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict this article defends: the novel is fully modernist in method and deliberately unmodernist in demand, and the combination is the reason it occupies the position it does.

The deciding factor is the treatment of narration. Everything else could be argued around. Fragmented chronology appears in novels long before modernism. Symbolic compression has a nineteenth-century pedigree. A refused conclusion could be read as artistic reticence rather than as a movement's principle. But a narrator who is limited by design, who supplies the evidence against his own reliability in the opening pages, and who delivers the book's most important psychological event as an admitted inference is doing something the pre-modern novel does not do and does not want to do. The Victorian chronicle rests on the assumption that a story can be told correctly. This book rests on the assumption that it cannot, and builds the assumption into the telling.

Name the criterion and use it. Call it the epistemic test: a novel is modernist when its form embodies a claim that reliable knowledge of its own material is unavailable. Difficulty is one way to embody the claim and not the only one. Fitzgerald embodies it through a narrator, which costs the reader nothing and concedes nothing.

## Building a Comparative Essay From This Placement

The predictable failure here is the checklist essay. An essay that lists modernist features and finds each one in the novel will be accurate and inert, because the conclusion is contained in the introduction and each paragraph performs the same operation.

The productive thesis argues about the relationship between method and demand. Something like "Fitzgerald adopts modernism's methods while refusing its difficulty, and the refusal is why the novel became the movement's most widely read text" gives every paragraph work to do and permits a genuine verdict at the end.

Choose two contemporaries and go deep. A survey of five books produces five thin paragraphs. The strongest pairings are the era's central poem, for the wasteland landscape and the emptied symbol, and Hemingway, for the opposite response to the same crisis of language. A third comparison should only be added if it changes the argument rather than confirming it.

Quote for method, not for beauty. The passages that carry weight are the ones where the technique is visible: the valley of ashes, the reserved-judgment opening, the withheld biography's arrival in chapter six, the pool scene's conditional inference, and the final page's refusal to conclude. A quotation chosen because it is lovely will not prove a formal claim. ## Closing Verdict

The novel belongs to modernist American fiction on the evidence of its methods and belongs to the general reading public on the evidence of its surface, and there is no contradiction between the two facts. Fitzgerald identified which parts of the movement's program were load-bearing and which were incidental, kept the first set, and declined the second.

What he kept: a narrator who cannot be trusted and knows it, a chronology arranged rather than followed, symbols that compress instead of illustrate, a landscape that reports a spiritual condition, and an ending that widens instead of concluding. What he declined: the broken surface, the unattributed voice, the syntax under strain, and the demand that the reader assemble the book before understanding it.

That is why it is the modernist novel most readers meet first, and why meeting it first works. A reader who has finished this book has already experienced unreliable narration, disrupted time, compressed symbolism, and a withheld conclusion, and has experienced them as pleasure rather than as labor. The difficult books become legible afterward because the operations are already familiar. Fitzgerald wrote the movement's introduction without setting out to, by proving that its methods survive being made hospitable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Where does Gatsby sit in modernist American fiction?

It sits at the accessible edge of the movement, sharing the period's methods while declining its characteristic difficulty. Fragmented chronology, a narrator limited and self-undermining, symbols that compress rather than illustrate, and a conclusion that widens instead of resolving are all present. What is absent is the broken surface: every scene has a stated location and cast, the syntax is orderly, and the vocabulary is common. That combination is unusual enough to make the novel the movement's ambassador, the text assigned before the harder ones because it teaches the methods without requiring an apparatus.

### Q: What modernist American novels is Gatsby among?

Its immediate company is the fiction of the middle 1920s. In 1925 alone, Dreiser published a vast naturalist account of ambition and murder, Dos Passos published a novel assembling New York from disconnected lives and documentary fragments, and Woolf, on the other side of the Atlantic, compressed a London day into consciousness. Hemingway's early stories appeared the same year and his first novel the next. Faulkner's major experiments follow at the end of the decade. The range inside that company matters: Dos Passos was breaking form directly, Hemingway was stripping the sentence, and Fitzgerald sat between them.

### Q: What modernist methods does Gatsby share?

Four, each demonstrable at the level of the page. Chronology is disrupted, with the narration delivered retrospectively and the central figure's origins withheld until the sixth chapter. Narration is limited and undermines itself, offering the book's key psychological moment as admitted speculation. Symbol operates by compression, with the green light shifting register across three appearances and the valley of ashes presented as vision rather than description. And meaning is refused rather than delivered, since the novel declines to rule on whether the enterprise it describes was worth undertaking.

### Q: How does Gatsby differ from more radical modernist experiments?

It keeps the connective tissue those works remove. In the difficult books the surface itself is discontinuous: voices go unattributed, technique changes without warning, and syntax is disassembled, so the reader cannot proceed without interpretive labor. Fitzgerald performs his disruptions underneath an orderly surface. The withheld biography costs nothing in comprehension and everything in assumption. The unreliability is signalled in plain sentences. The symbols can be taken at face value on a first pass and figuratively on a second. The disruptions are semantic rather than syntactic, which is why the book reads quickly and still does the work.

### Q: Is Gatsby fully experimental or not modernist?

Neither, and the either-or framing is what causes the confusion. The novel is not formally experimental in the way the era's celebrated difficult works are; nothing about its surface requires reconstruction. But it is thoroughly modernist in stance, because its form embodies a claim that reliable knowledge of its own material is unavailable. A narrator who supplies the evidence against himself, a chronology arranged rather than followed, and an ending that widens instead of concluding are all commitments to that claim. Method and difficulty came apart in this book, and reading it well means holding them apart.

### Q: Why is Gatsby the modernist novel many readers meet first?

Because it delivers the movement's effects at conventional reading speed. A reader who finishes it has already experienced unreliable narration, disrupted chronology, compressed symbolism, and a withheld conclusion, and has experienced them as pleasure rather than as labor. The harder books become legible afterward because the operations are already familiar. Length helps too: at roughly fifty thousand words the novel is a fraction of the size of the works it is filed with, and the compression made every element multi-functional, which is what survives passage into a culture's common stock.

### Q: What is literary modernism?

Literary modernism is the early twentieth-century movement that broke with the confident narration and moral certainty of the nineteenth-century novel after a war and a rapidly industrialising urban world made those inheritances feel false. Its recurring methods include fragmented chronology, unreliable or limited narration, symbolic compression, mythic material laid under contemporary scenes, priority given to interior over clock time, and a refusal of the summarizing conclusion. Difficulty was frequently accepted as the price of accuracy, though this novel demonstrates that the price was optional rather than intrinsic.

### Q: What makes a novel modernist rather than realist?

Method and stance rather than subject matter. A realist novel assumes that its world can be reported: the narrator is reliable, the chronology is continuous, and meaning is available to be stated. A modernist novel treats narration as partial and possibly compromised, chronology as something to be arranged, symbol as compression rather than illustration, and meaning as something the form withholds. Both can describe the same social world. Dreiser and Fitzgerald both wrote about a poor young man's fatal ambition in 1925, and the difference between the books is entirely a difference of method.

### Q: How does Gatsby compare with Ulysses in difficulty?

They are at opposite ends of the same movement. The Dublin novel changes technique from chapter to chapter, expands a single day across hundreds of pages, buries an epic frame under domestic material that the reader is expected to excavate, and is routinely read with a companion guide. Fitzgerald's novel can be finished in an afternoon by a reader with no preparation. What is worth noticing is that both books disrupt chronology, both use myth, and both refuse a settled conclusion. The difference is where the disruption is placed: in the surface, or underneath a surface that behaves.

### Q: What does Fitzgerald owe to T. S. Eliot?

The debt is best described as a shared operation rather than a borrowing. The era's central poem, published three years before the novel, opens on a landscape of spiritual sterility, layers myth under modern urban material, and treats the contemporary city as a place where inherited symbols have been drained of meaning. The valley of ashes chapter performs a compressed version of the same operation, complete with a commercial advertisement standing in for a departed divinity. Eliot read the novel and wrote to Fitzgerald in strongly appreciative terms, which is external evidence that the movement's central figure recognised the book as part of the same project.

### Q: Why is Gatsby so short compared with other modernist works?

Because Fitzgerald solved the era's problems by contraction rather than expansion. The characteristic modernist move was to stretch: a day across hundreds of pages, a city across dozens of threads. He compressed a summer into roughly fifty thousand words and let the pressure do the work. The consequence is that nothing in the book is single-purpose. There is no subplot that does not feed the main line, no scene that does not either advance the reunion or supply the material that destroys it, and no descriptive passage that is not also carrying symbolic weight. Density replaced length.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald think of himself as a modernist?

He did not use the vocabulary the way later critics do, and the movement was not a club with a roster. What the record does support is that he was working consciously at the level of method rather than merely telling a story: he revised heavily, thought hard about structure and point of view, and was ambitious for the book in artistic rather than commercial terms even while earning his living from magazine fiction. Attributing a settled self-classification to him would overstate the evidence. Attributing deliberate formal ambition to him is straightforwardly supported by how the novel is built.

### Q: How does the novel handle time compared with other modernist books?

It rearranges rather than expands. Woolf compresses a day and dilates it internally so that clock time becomes almost irrelevant beside remembered time. Fitzgerald keeps clock time intact within scenes and disturbs the order in which scenes reach the reader. The account is written retrospectively, the origins arrive in the sixth chapter, the courtship arrives at second hand in the fourth, and the Dan Cody years are inserted with an announcement that they are being inserted. The past in this novel is not a stream flooding the present; it is material the narrator has arranged for effect.

### Q: Which modernist novel should I read after Gatsby?

The natural next step depends on which operation interested you. If the unreliable narration was the draw, move to Conrad, whose reconstructive tellers are the direct ancestors. If the compressed symbolism and the emptied urban landscape were the draw, move to the era's long poem of the wasteland, which does the same work with the surface broken. If the stripped alternative appeals, Hemingway's first novel offers the opposite solution to the same crisis of language. If you want the full difficulty, the 1922 Dublin novel is the standard destination and rewards a companion guide.

### Q: Is Gatsby a Lost Generation book or a modernist book?

Both terms apply and they name different things. Lost Generation is a historical and biographical category covering the writers who came of age around the First World War and carried its disillusion into their work. Modernism is a formal category covering the methods they and others developed. Fitzgerald belongs to the first by biography and to the second by technique. Confusing the two produces a common classroom error, in which the presence of post-war disillusion in the novel is treated as evidence of its modernism. The disillusion is the content; the modernism is in how the content is delivered.

### Q: How do I write about modernism in a Gatsby essay?

Argue about the relationship between method and demand rather than listing features. A thesis claiming that Fitzgerald adopts the movement's methods while refusing its difficulty, and that the refusal explains the novel's position, gives every paragraph work. Choose two contemporaries and develop them fully rather than surveying five. Quote passages where technique is visible, the valley of ashes, the reserved-judgment opening, the pool scene's conditional inference, rather than passages chosen for beauty. Then state the counter-case that this is a late Romantic novel with modern furniture and answer it.

### Q: Does Fitzgerald use symbols the way Faulkner does?

Not quite. Fitzgerald's symbols are attached to objects a character can look at, which lets them be taken at face value on a first pass and figuratively on a second: a light on a dock, a billboard, a stretch of industrial waste. The difficult fragmentors more often distribute symbolic weight across a broken surface, so the reader must assemble the pattern before it can be seen at all. The practical consequence is transmissibility. A symbol that fits in a phrase and sits on a visible object passes into common cultural currency, which is why the green light is known to people who have not read the book.

### Q: Why do exam boards teach Gatsby as modernist?

Because it demonstrates the movement's methods in a form students can actually handle, and because the methods are unusually easy to point at. A teacher can show unreliable narration in the second paragraph, disrupted chronology in the arrival of the sixth chapter, symbolic compression in the valley of ashes, and a refused conclusion on the final page, all within a book that a class can read in a fortnight. The novel also connects cleanly to the historical unit on the 1920s, which lets a syllabus cover the period and the movement together. The risk is that students learn to recite the features without understanding what they were for.

### Q: Why does the epigraph credit a poet who does not exist?

The verse about the gold hat and the high bouncing is Fitzgerald's own, attributed to a name he had used for a character in his first novel. The invention is a small act of literary impersonation of a kind the period specialized in, and it sets the book's relation to authority before the opening sentence: what looks like an inherited voice turns out to be a constructed one, and a reader who checks finds nothing behind it. The gesture rhymes with the novel's whole procedure, since the book is built from attributions that do not survive examination.

### Q: Does the beautiful prose work against the novel's modernism?

Only if the lyricism is read as decoration. The period's other American solution to the exhausted pre-war diction was Hemingway's, which stripped the sentence down. Fitzgerald kept the cadence and the adjectives and disabled them another way, by placing them in a compromised narrator's mouth and attaching them to objects the book is simultaneously exposing. Describing the service of a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty is a magnificent phrase about a cheap thing, and both halves are in the sentence. The essential feature of the style is the gap between the music and its occasion.
