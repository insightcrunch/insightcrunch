## Reading the Novel by Counting: Quantitative and Digital Humanities on Gatsby

Quantitative and digital humanities on Gatsby means reading the novel by counting. It means treating the text as data without ceasing to treat it as literature, and asking what happens when stylometry, word-frequency analysis, and network analysis are set loose on Fitzgerald's prose. The claim of this article is plain: these computational methods open a genuine new angle on the novel, surfacing patterns of style and structure that unaided close reading can miss, while running into limits that no amount of processing power can cross. The quantitative does not replace interpretation. It disciplines it, checks it, and sometimes surprises it.

The misunderstanding that shadows this field takes two opposite forms, and both need naming before the methods can be described. One camp treats a numerical result as decisive, as though a frequency table could settle what a passage means. The other camp treats every number as worthless, as though counting words were inherently hostile to literary understanding. Both positions misunderstand what the methods actually do. A count is not a verdict. It is a measurement, and measurements become meaningful only inside an argument that says what was measured, why it matters, and where the measurement stops being informative. This article keeps the methods exact, keeps their limits explicit, and keeps every quantitative claim at the level the evidence supports.

Digital humanities is a broad label for scholarship that uses computation to ask humanistic questions. When applied to a single novel, it usually takes four forms. Stylometry compares word-choice patterns between texts or passages, asking whether two stretches of writing share the same verbal fingerprint. Word-frequency analysis counts how often words and phrases occur, asking which terms dominate the novel's vocabulary and which are conspicuously rare. Network analysis maps relationships, usually among characters, by recording who appears with whom, and then studies the resulting structure. Text mining is the umbrella term for the automated extraction of patterns from large amounts of text, including techniques such as topic modeling, which sorts vocabulary into clusters of co-occurring words. Each of these is a real method with real procedures, real assumptions, and real boundaries. None of them reads the novel for you.

![Word frequency patterns visualized across the chapters of The Great Gatsby - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-113.webp)

Why should a reader of Gatsby care? The novel is short, roughly fifty thousand words, and it has been read closely for a century. One might assume there is nothing left that counting could add. But close reading has its own blind spots. A reader notices what the prose makes noticeable: the famous images, the striking sentences, the scenes engineered for emphasis. What a reader cannot reliably notice is the background texture of the book, the thousand small habits of diction and syntax that operate below the threshold of attention. Does the narration lean on certain abstract nouns? Does one character's dialogue carry a different rhythm of short words than another's? Does the vocabulary of money cluster in particular chapters? These are questions a careful reader can guess at and a computer can test, provided the test is designed with enough care to mean something.

The temptation to overclaim begins the moment the first table appears. A number on a screen carries an authority that a paragraph of interpretation does not, and readers transfer that authority to the conclusion drawn from the number. This is the central discipline of the field: separating what the computation shows from what the scholar infers. A frequency count shows that a word occurs a certain number of times. It does not show that the word matters, or why the author chose it, or what a reader should feel about it. The gap between the measurement and the meaning is where scholarship happens, and no method closes it automatically. The honest digital humanist spends more time on that gap than on the code.

The questions are older than the computers. Long before software could count a novel in a fraction of a second, scholars counted by hand: tabulating Shakespeare's vocabulary to argue about authorship, tallying biblical word frequencies to date passages, compiling concordances of major authors line by laborious line. What the computer changed was not the curiosity but the scale and the checkability. A hand count of a fifty-thousand-word novel takes weeks and cannot easily be redone; a scripted count takes seconds and can be re-run by any colleague with the same file. This continuity matters because it refutes the idea that quantification is foreign to literary study. Readers have always noticed that some words recur and some voices differ; the digital methods only make those notices explicit, complete, and auditable. The novelty is procedural, not philosophical, and the standards by which the work should be judged are the old standards of evidence, honestly stated and open to inspection.

A further complication comes from the size of the object. Gatsby is, by computational standards, a small text. Many of the methods were developed for corpora of millions of words, where statistical patterns are robust and noise averages out. Applied to a single novel, the same methods become sensitive to small choices: which edition supplies the text, how hyphenated words are handled, whether dialogue is separated from narration. This sensitivity is not a reason to abandon the methods. It is a reason to understand them, because the scholar who knows where the fragility lies can design around it, while the scholar who treats the software as a black box cannot tell a genuine pattern from an artifact of preparation.

This article proceeds in four movements. First, a compact table lays out the principal computational approaches alongside what each can reveal and what each cannot. Second, the core methods are described one by one, each at the level of its actual procedure, with worked walkthroughs that show how the logic applies to Gatsby-relevant material. Third, the limits are stated plainly, including the preparation decisions that shape every result before the first count is made. Fourth, the article argues for the middle position the field actually occupies: quantitative results as neither decisive nor worthless, but as checks, prompts, and scaffolding for interpretation. Throughout, no finding is invented, no study is claimed that cannot be verified, and every quantitative statement stays at the level of method rather than pretending to report results no one has produced.

## The Methods at a Glance: A Compact Table

Before the procedures are unpacked in prose, the whole landscape can be surveyed in one table. Each row names a computational approach, states what it can genuinely reveal about the novel, and states what it cannot reveal no matter how carefully it is applied. The table is the article's findable artifact: the quick reference for a reader deciding which method fits a research question, and the honest boundary map the rest of the article defends in detail.

| Computational approach | What it can reveal | What it cannot reveal |
|---|---|---|
| Stylometry | Whether two passages share similar patterns of word choice, useful for comparing narration with dialogue or one chapter's prose with another's | Whether a passage is good, what it means, or who wrote it with certainty, since similar patterns can arise from genre, era, or imitation |
| Word-frequency analysis | Which words dominate the novel's vocabulary and which are rare, and how the distribution shifts across chapters or speakers | Why a word was chosen, what it signifies in context, or whether its frequency reflects intention rather than topic |
| Concordance and keyness | Every occurrence of a word in its surrounding context, and which words are unusually frequent compared with a reference collection of other novels | The significance of any single occurrence, which only close reading of the surrounding passage can establish |
| Network analysis | The structure of character co-occurrence, showing who shares scenes with whom and which figures occupy central or peripheral positions | The quality of any relationship, since co-occurrence records presence, not affection, hostility, or moral judgment |
| Topic modeling | Clusters of words that tend to appear together, suggesting the novel's recurring vocabularies of money, place, time, or social life | The names of those clusters, which the researcher assigns, or whether a cluster corresponds to a deliberate theme |
| Text reuse and collation | Precise differences between editions, drafts, or printings, down to the level of individual words and punctuation | Which variant represents the author's final intention, a judgment that requires documentary and editorial evidence |

The rightmost column is the more important one. Every method in the left column has been oversold somewhere, and the overselling always takes the same form: the silent promotion of a measurement into a meaning. The table resists that promotion row by row. What follows gives each row its full procedural description, so the reader can see exactly where the measurement ends and the interpretation begins.

## Stylometry and the Fingerprint of a Voice

### What does stylometry actually measure in a novel like Gatsby?

Stylometry measures the relative frequencies of common words, especially the small function words such as articles, prepositions, and pronouns, and compares these frequency profiles between texts or passages. In Gatsby it can test whether Nick's narration and a given character's dialogue share the same underlying word-choice habits, without claiming anything about quality or meaning.

That paragraph deserves unpacking, because stylometry is the most misunderstood of the four core methods. It does not analyze style in the literary sense. It does not detect beautiful sentences, or irony, or rhythm as a reader hears it. It measures something far more mechanical: the rates at which a writer uses the words that carry grammar rather than content. The words that do the grammatical work of English, the, of, and, to, in, that, with, are used almost unconsciously, and their relative proportions turn out to be remarkably stable within a writer's output and remarkably distinctive between writers. This is the empirical foundation of the whole method. Content words change with topic. Function words change with habit.

The procedure, stripped to its logic, runs in four stages. First, the texts to be compared are prepared as clean digital files, a step whose importance the later section on preparation will belabor. Second, the researcher selects the features to count, usually the most frequent words in the combined texts, often the top fifty or hundred, or sometimes sequences of two or three words. Third, each text is converted into a frequency profile: a list of numbers recording how often each selected feature occurs, adjusted for the text's total length so that a long chapter and a short passage can be compared fairly. Fourth, a statistical measure of distance or similarity compares the profiles, and the researcher examines which texts cluster together and which stand apart. Nothing in this pipeline understands English. It counts, normalizes, and compares.

Applied to Gatsby, the natural comparisons are internal. The novel offers a built-in set of contrasting voices: Nick's retrospective narration, Gatsby's dialogue, Daisy's dialogue, Tom's dialogue, Jordan's, Myrtle's, Wilson's, Wolfsheim's. A stylometric comparison asks whether these voices are measurably distinct in their function-word profiles, or whether the novel's famous unity of voice means that Fitzgerald's habits show through every speaker equally. Either outcome is interesting. If the voices separate cleanly, the method has quantified something readers sense intuitively about characterization through speech. If they do not separate, the method has put a number on the observation that Gatsby's dialogue is stylized rather than mimetic, that all its speakers talk a little like Fitzgerald. The method cannot decide which of these literary judgments is correct. It can only report the distances and leave the judgment to the scholar.

A worked walkthrough makes the logic concrete. Suppose a researcher wants to test whether the famous final pages of the novel, the closing meditation that shifts into a more lyrical register, differ stylometrically from the narration of the early chapters. The researcher divides the digital text into the two passages, counts the hundred most frequent words across both, and computes each passage's relative frequencies. If the distance between the two profiles is larger than the distances among randomly chosen passages of similar length from within the early chapters, the researcher has evidence that the closing pages are stylistically distinct at the level of function-word habit, not merely at the level of imagery that any reader can see. The key move is the internal control: comparing the observed distance against the background variation within the novel itself. Without that control, any difference is uninterpretable, because all passages differ from all other passages to some degree. The control is what turns a number into an argument.

The limits of stylometry are as instructive as its powers. Short texts are its weakness. A fifty-word passage does not contain enough function words for a stable profile, so stylometric claims about single sentences are statistical noise dressed as science. Genre and era confound the signal: two writers of the same period writing in the same mode can resemble each other more closely than either resembles a writer from a different tradition, which is why stylometry works best on comparisons within a controlled set rather than across the whole of literature. Imitation defeats it by design: a parodist or a forger who has internalized the function-word habits of a target can produce a profile that matches, because the method measures habit, not identity. And most importantly for Gatsby, the method is blind to everything that makes the novel worth reading. It cannot see irony, cannot hear rhythm, cannot distinguish a sincere sentence from a sarcastic one. Two passages with identical profiles can mean opposite things.

The responsible way to report a stylometric result is therefore hedged in a specific vocabulary. The researcher reports distances, not identities. The researcher says that passage A is closer in profile to passage B than to passage C, not that A and B share an author. The researcher states the feature set, the normalization, and the distance measure, so that another scholar can reproduce the computation and test whether the finding survives different choices. This reproducibility is the method's genuine contribution to literary study: not certainty, but checkability. A close reader's impression that two voices sound alike cannot be re-run by a colleague. A stylometric comparison can.

A second walkthrough shows the method applied to characterization rather than to large-scale structure. Suppose the question is whether two characters' dialogue is measurably distinct: the clipped, declarative speech of one figure against the meandering, qualified speech of another. The researcher extracts each character's spoken lines into a separate file, using the quotation-mark separation described later, and computes the function-word profile of each. The comparison then asks not only whether the profiles differ, but where they differ: which function words drive the distance. A divergence concentrated in pronouns suggests a difference in self-reference versus other-reference. A divergence in conjunctions suggests a difference in sentence complexity, the paratactic against the hypotactic. A divergence in articles and prepositions suggests a difference in descriptive density. Each of these is a stylistic hypothesis that close reading can then investigate in the actual lines. The method does not discover that one character is terse and another expansive; any reader knows that. It locates the grammatical substrate of the terseness, and it tests whether the impression holds across the character's entire dialogue or rests on a few memorable speeches. When the profile confirms the impression, the characterization gains an empirical anchor. When it complicates the impression, showing for instance that a supposedly plain speaker shares the narrator's function-word habits, the scholar has a finding worth writing about: the plainness is a matter of diction and topic, not of underlying verbal habit, which says something precise about how the novel constructs its voices.

One more caution belongs here, because it recurs across all the methods. The choice of features is itself an interpretive act. Counting the top hundred words is a convention, not a law of nature, and different feature sets can produce different clusterings. A scholar who runs the analysis once, with one feature set, and reports the result as the answer has mistaken a single measurement for a robust finding. The careful practitioner varies the parameters, reports the range of outcomes, and treats only the stable patterns as worth interpreting. This is slower and less dramatic than the single striking chart, and it is the difference between scholarship and demonstration.

## Word Frequency: From Lists to Patterns

### How does word-frequency analysis differ from manual counting?

Manual counting finds what the counter already suspects, while computational frequency analysis produces a complete inventory of every word in the text, ranked by occurrence, which lets the researcher notice dominant and rare terms that no hypothesis had predicted. The difference is exhaustiveness: the machine counts everything, including the words nobody thought to look for.

The foundation of frequency analysis is the frequency list: every distinct word in the text, ordered from most to least common, with its count and its relative frequency. For any English text of novel length, the list has a characteristic shape. A tiny number of words account for a large share of all tokens. The word the alone typically accounts for several percent of a novel's total words, and the top ten words together account for roughly a quarter. Then comes a long middle range of ordinary vocabulary, and finally a long tail of words that occur once or twice. This shape is a general property of language, not a discovery about Gatsby, and the researcher who understands it will not be surprised that the novel's most frequent words are grammatical workhorses. The interest lies further down the list.

The first genuinely informative operation is filtering. The researcher removes or sets aside the function words and examines the content words that remain: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs. Here the novel's preoccupations begin to surface in quantitative form. Words belonging to the vocabularies of wealth, of vision and eyes, of time and the past, of parties and crowds, will rank higher in Gatsby's content-word list than they do in the corresponding list for a novel about, say, farming or seafaring. This comparison is the move that turns a raw list into an argument, and it requires a reference point. A frequency is only high or low relative to something.

That reference point is the second key concept: the reference corpus. To say that a word is unusually frequent in Gatsby, the researcher needs a baseline drawn from comparable texts, perhaps a collection of American novels from the same decade, or a large sample of Fitzgerald's other fiction. The comparison produces a keyness score for each word, a statistic that measures how much more frequent the word is in the target text than in the reference. Words with high keyness are the novel's distinctive vocabulary, the terms that mark this book off from its neighbors. The procedure is straightforward, but the choice of reference corpus is interpretive through and through. Compare Gatsby with other Fitzgerald novels and the key words will mark what is distinctive about this book within his oeuvre. Compare it with a broad sample of the period's fiction and the key words will mark what is distinctive about Fitzgerald's milieu and subject. Neither comparison is wrong. They answer different questions, and the honest researcher states which question is being asked.

A worked walkthrough shows how the logic applies to a concrete Gatsby question. Consider the novel's vocabulary of vision: eyes, see, look, watch, gaze, stare. A reader senses that seeing is thematically loaded in this novel, from the billboard eyes to the repeated scenes of characters watching one another. The frequency analyst can test the shape of that intuition. First, assemble the set of vision-related terms, a step that requires judgment about which words count. Second, count their occurrences across the novel and compute their combined relative frequency. Third, count the same set in a reference corpus of comparable novels and compute the same figure. If the novel's rate substantially exceeds the reference rate, the analyst has quantitative support for the claim that vision is a distinctive preoccupation of this book's diction, not merely a memorable motif. If the rates are similar, the analyst has learned something equally valuable: that the motif's power comes from placement and emphasis rather than from sheer repetition. Both outcomes inform interpretation. Neither outcome interprets itself, because the analyst must still argue why the pattern matters, and must still read the individual occurrences to see how the words are actually used.

The third operation is diachronic: tracking frequency across the novel's own structure. Dividing the text into chapters and computing the relative frequency of a word or word set in each chapter produces a distribution profile, a curve showing where the vocabulary concentrates. Money words may peak in the chapters of display and collapse in the chapters of aftermath. Time words may gather density as the narrative drives toward its end. These profiles are among the most immediately suggestive products of frequency analysis, because they map vocabulary onto the reading experience: the reader feels certain chapters as saturated with a particular atmosphere, and the profile shows the lexical substrate of that feeling. But the suggestion must be checked against the text, because a chapter can feel saturated by a word that appears only twice if both appearances are placed at moments of maximum emphasis. Frequency measures exposure. Emphasis is a different quantity.

Between the dominant words and the rare ones lies the long tail, and the tail deserves its own discussion, because it behaves differently from the rest of the list. In any novel-length text, a large fraction of the distinct words occur only once. These single-occurrence words, the hapax legomena, are individually negligible and collectively significant: they are where the author's specific choices live, the precise nouns and unusual adjectives that give a passage its particular texture. A frequency analysis that attends only to the top of the list misses the level at which much of the novel's verbal art operates. The practical techniques for studying the tail are comparative rather than absolute. The researcher can compute the proportion of single-occurrence words in each chapter, a rough measure of lexical variety, and ask whether descriptive chapters are lexically richer than dialogue-heavy ones. The researcher can extract the tail words belonging to a semantic field, the rare words of color or of weather, and read them as a set, asking what work the unusual choices do. The researcher can compare the tail across the novel's sections to see whether the vocabulary contracts as the plot tightens, a quantitative trace of the narrowing that readers feel in the final chapters. None of these operations requires exotic statistics. Each requires the complete inventory that only computation provides, and each returns the scholar to the text, because a rare word earns its interpretation from its single context, which must be read closely.

The limits of frequency analysis cluster around three points. First, it is blind to context by construction. A count of a word's occurrences treats every occurrence as identical, which means it cannot distinguish the sincere use from the ironic, the literal from the figurative, the narrator's word from a character's quoted word. The concordance, which displays each occurrence with its surrounding words, is the standard corrective, and the responsible analyst moves constantly between the table and the text. Second, frequency is sensitive to text length in ways that can mislead. Raw counts cannot be compared between passages of different lengths; only relative frequencies, counts per thousand or per ten thousand words, are comparable, and even those become unstable in very short passages. Third, the method inherits every decision made during text preparation. Whether contractions are split, whether hyphenated compounds are joined, whether dialogue tags are included: each choice shifts the numbers slightly, and the analyst who does not know the preparation cannot evaluate the result.

A realistic practice task brings these abstractions down to the desk. Take a clean digital text of the novel and divide it into its nine chapters. For each chapter, compute the relative frequency of first-person pronouns against third-person pronouns. The hypothesis to test is whether the narration's balance of self-reference and other-reference shifts as the novel moves from exposition to crisis: does the narrator say I more often when the events become personal, or does the prose turn outward toward the other characters? The procedure is simple enough for a student with basic scripting skills, and the interpretive payoff is real either way. A shift in the ratio is a fact about the text's surface that close reading can easily miss, and it becomes evidence for an argument about the narrator's changing stance. No shift is equally informative, because it constrains the arguments a scholar can make. The exercise teaches the central lesson of the method: the count is the beginning of the argument, never its end. Readers interested in the measurable features of Fitzgerald's sentences, including the rhythmic patterns that frequency analysis alone cannot capture, will find the companion discussion of syntax and rhythm a natural next step ([Fitzgerald's syntax and rhythm](/2026/02/23/gatsby-syntax-rhythm/)).

A second practice task develops the keyness operation, the comparison against a reference corpus. Assemble two small reference collections: several chapters from other American novels of the same period, and several chapters from Fitzgerald's own short fiction. Compute the content-word frequency lists for Gatsby and for each reference collection, then identify the words whose relative frequency in Gatsby most exceeds their rate in each reference. Compare the two keyness lists. Words that are key against both references mark what is distinctive about this novel in any company. Words that are key against the period reference but not against the Fitzgerald reference mark the author's general preoccupations rather than this book's. Words key against Fitzgerald but not against the period mark what the novel borrows from its moment. The three lists tell three different stories, and the discipline of the exercise is to keep them distinct: each answers a different question, and conflating them is the most common error in published keyness work. The student who can articulate which question each list answers has understood the method at the level that matters.

## Network Analysis: The Novel as a Social Graph

### What does a character network show that a chapter summary misses?

A chapter summary tells what happens, while a character network shows the structure of who shares scenes with whom across the whole novel, revealing which figures are structurally central to the social world and which are peripheral, a pattern that cuts across the sequence of events. The network makes visible the architecture of relationships that the plot's chronology can obscure.

Network analysis begins with a simple translation: the novel's social world becomes a graph, a mathematical structure of nodes and edges. Each character is a node. An edge connects two characters when they appear together under some defined condition: in the same scene, in the same chapter, in the same conversation, or within a fixed window of words. The researcher defines the condition, extracts the co-occurrences from the text, and builds the resulting structure. Software then computes structural measures and draws the graph, placing tightly connected characters near one another and peripheral figures at the margins. The picture that emerges is the novel's social geometry rendered visible.

The measures deserve plain-language definitions, because their names are borrowed from mathematics and their literary meanings must be constructed with care. Degree counts how many direct connections a character has: the number of other figures with whom the character co-occurs. A high-degree character is socially embedded, present across many relationships. Betweenness measures how often a character lies on the shortest path between other characters: a high-betweenness figure is a bridge, someone through whom different parts of the social world connect. Closeness measures how few steps it takes a character to reach everyone else: a high-closeness figure sits near the center of the web. These are structural facts about the graph. Their literary significance has to be argued in each case, because a central position in the graph and a central position in the reader's experience are two different things, and the interesting cases are the ones where they diverge.

Consider what the method can reveal about Gatsby's particular social world. The novel's cast is small and its parties are large, which creates a distinctive structural signature. A handful of figures, the narrator, the host, the central couple, recur across many scenes and accumulate high degree. Around them orbits a cloud of party guests, names that appear once or twice, contributing edges that connect to the host but to almost nothing else. The graph of such a novel looks like a dense core with a sparse periphery, and that shape is itself an interpretation-relevant fact: it quantifies the novel's social stratification, the difference between the inner circle and the crowd. A reader feels this stratification intuitively. The network gives it a measurable form and lets the researcher compare its strength across the novel's sections, asking whether the core tightens or loosens as the plot moves from the summer of parties to the autumn of consequences.

A worked walkthrough shows the procedure at the level of actual decisions. The researcher begins by defining the cast list, which is already an interpretive act: does the man in the library with the owl-like glasses count as a character, do the unnamed party guests count individually or as a group, does a character mentioned but never present count at all? Next, the researcher defines co-occurrence. The simplest definition is scene co-presence: two characters share an edge if they appear in the same scene. A stricter definition requires interaction: dialogue exchanged, or one character acting upon another. The choice changes the graph. Scene co-presence produces a denser network that captures the social world as the narrator presents it. Interaction produces a sparser network that captures the actual traffic of agency. Neither is correct in the abstract. Each answers a different question, and the researcher must state the question before defending the choice.

Then comes extraction, the most labor-intensive stage. For a short novel, a researcher can code co-occurrences by hand, reading through the text and recording the pairs. For larger projects, automated methods identify character names and record their proximity. Either way, judgment calls accumulate: a pronoun referring to a character must be resolved, a scene boundary must be placed, a mention in narration must be classified as presence or absence. Each decision is small, and their accumulation is the analysis. The researcher who documents these decisions produces work another scholar can audit. The researcher who does not has produced a picture whose foundations cannot be inspected.

The resulting graph can then be read against the novel's own emphases. The most instructive comparisons are the mismatches. A character who dominates the reader's memory may turn out to be structurally peripheral, appearing in few scenes but with enormous weight in each. A character the reader barely registers may turn out to be structurally central, a bridge connecting otherwise separate groups. These mismatches do not correct the reader's experience. They complicate it, and complication is the method's gift. When the graph says a figure is central and the prose treats the figure as marginal, the scholar has a genuine puzzle: why does the novel invest structural position and narrative attention differently? The answer will come from close reading, not from the graph, but the graph posed the question that close reading alone had not asked.

The limits of network analysis are severe and should be stated without softening. Co-occurrence is not relationship. Two characters who share a scene may be lovers, enemies, strangers, or master and servant, and the edge connecting them records none of this. The graph cannot distinguish affection from hostility, intimacy from obligation, or a long marriage from a brief encounter. It flattens every mode of human connection into presence, which means it is blind to exactly the qualities that make the novel's relationships worth analyzing. A network of Gatsby's characters can show that two figures never share a scene, which is a real structural fact, but it cannot say whether their separation is a matter of class, of plot, of psychology, or of accident. The interpretation of the structure is entirely the scholar's work.

Direction and weight add nuance but not meaning. A directed edge can record that one character speaks to another rather than merely appearing alongside, and a weighted edge can record how often the co-occurrence happens. These refinements make the graph more informative, but they do not cross the fundamental boundary: the graph remains a record of arrangement, not of significance. The scholar who forgets this begins to write sentences in which the network "shows" that a relationship is important, when all it shows is that it is frequent. Frequency of contact and importance of contact are correlated in life and in fiction, but they are not the same thing, and the novel is full of relationships whose importance is inversely proportional to their frequency.

Directed edges open a further dimension that suits this novel's particular dynamics. Instead of recording mere co-presence, the researcher records the direction of address: who speaks to whom, who acts upon whom, who is spoken about in whose presence. The resulting directed graph distinguishes the characters who initiate from those who receive, the talkers from the talked-about. In a novel organized around a figure who is constantly discussed and rarely confronted directly, this directionality captures something the undirected graph misses: the asymmetry between being the object of the social world's attention and being a participant in it. The researcher can compute, for each character, the balance of outgoing and incoming edges, and ask whether the figures the novel presents as powerful are the ones who direct traffic or the ones toward whom traffic flows. The measure is still structural, still blind to tone and motive, but it is a finer structure, and the comparison between the directed and undirected versions of the same graph is itself informative: the relationships that change position between the two are the ones where presence and agency diverge, which in this novel is a thematically loaded divergence.

There is also the problem of the narrator's mediation, which network analysis of a first-person novel must confront directly. Every co-occurrence in the graph is filtered through the narrator's presence and attention. Characters who never meet the narrator can only enter the graph through report, and characters the narrator dislikes may be systematically underrepresented in scenes the narrator chooses to render fully. The graph therefore maps the narrator's social world as much as the novel's, and a sophisticated analysis says so explicitly. This is not a flaw in the method. It is a fact about the object, and the method is honest enough to make it visible. The naive reading of the graph as an objective map of the fictional world is the error, not the graph itself.

A realistic practice task for this method stays deliberately small. Choose three consecutive chapters and construct the co-occurrence network by hand, defining an edge as shared presence in a scene and recording every pair. Compute the degree of each character in this subgraph and compare the ranking with your own sense, before you began, of who matters most in these chapters. Where the ranking matches your sense, articulate what in the prose creates the structural centrality you already felt. Where it diverges, investigate: is the divergence an artifact of your edge definition, or does it reveal a genuine difference between structural position and narrative emphasis? The exercise takes an afternoon, requires no software beyond paper, and teaches the method's core discipline: the graph is a prompt for reading, not a substitute for it.

A natural extension compares the network's shape across the novel's own divisions. Build two subgraphs, one from the chapters of the summer's height and one from the chapters of the aftermath, using identical edge definitions for both. Compute the same structural measures on each and compare. Does the core of recurring figures tighten or loosen? Do peripheral characters drop out or persist? Does any figure's betweenness collapse, marking the loss of a bridging position as the social world contracts? These comparisons quantify the novel's structural arc in a way that complements the plot summary: the story of who is present with whom, told as changing geometry rather than as sequence. The comparison also tests the robustness of any single-graph finding, because a structural claim that holds in the full graph but dissolves in both halves may be an artifact of aggregation rather than a feature of the novel. As with every method in this article, the computation proposes and the reading disposes, but the proposal is precise enough to be checked, which is more than can be said for most impressions of a novel's social structure.

## Before the First Count: Preparing the Text

### What does a worked frequency walkthrough look like on a Gatsby passage?

A worked walkthrough starts with a chosen passage, normalizes it by lowercasing and stripping punctuation, counts each word, and converts the counts to rates per thousand words so passages of any length can be compared fairly. The walkthrough below applies this logic to the novel's closing pages, showing each decision point where preparation shapes the numbers that follow.

Text preparation is the least glamorous and most consequential stage of any computational analysis, and it is where most published errors originate. Every method described in this article operates not on the novel but on a digital surrogate of the novel, and the distance between the two is the sum of a dozen small decisions. The scholar who understands these decisions can evaluate any quantitative claim about Gatsby. The scholar who does not is at the mercy of whoever prepared the file.

The first decision is the source text. Gatsby exists in multiple editions with small but real differences in wording and punctuation, the products of a century of reprinting, correction, and occasional silent emendation. A frequency count performed on one edition will differ slightly from a count performed on another, and a comparison of results across studies is meaningless unless the studies used the same text. The responsible researcher names the edition, and where possible works from a text whose provenance is documented rather than from an anonymous file downloaded without questions. The novel's entry into the public domain made clean digital texts widely available, which democratized this kind of work, but it also multiplied the number of undocumented files in circulation. Availability is not the same as reliability.

The second decision is cleaning. Optical character recognition, the process that converts scanned pages into digital text, introduces errors: a damaged letter becomes a different character, a ligature becomes two letters, a page number intrudes into a sentence. Even a professionally prepared digital text contains artifacts of its production: running heads, chapter headings, front matter, advertisements, all of which must be identified and handled. The researcher decides what counts as the novel for the purposes of the analysis. Does the analysis include the author's introduction if the edition carries one? Does it include the chapter titles? Each inclusion shifts the counts, and the honest study documents the boundaries of its corpus.

The third decision is tokenization: the rules by which the continuous stream of characters is divided into countable words. This sounds mechanical and is anything but. Consider the contractions that pepper the dialogue. Is "don't" one word or two? The answer changes the frequency of "do" and "not" and the total word count, which changes every relative frequency in the analysis. Consider hyphenated compounds and the novel's occasional French phrases. Consider the possessives, the em dashes that join clauses, the ellipses that mark hesitation. Each of these must be resolved by a rule, and the rule must be applied consistently, and the rule must be reported. Two researchers who tokenize differently will produce different numbers from the same novel, and both numbers can be correct relative to their own rules. Comparability requires shared rules, which is why the field's mature studies publish their preprocessing steps in full.

The fourth decision is normalization: lowercasing, the handling of punctuation, and the treatment of variant spellings. Lowercasing is nearly universal, but it destroys the distinction between a word at the start of a sentence and the same word elsewhere, and between proper nouns and common nouns that share a form. Lemmatization, the reduction of inflected forms to their base, groups "runs," "ran," and "running" under a single headword, which is useful for studying vocabulary but destructive for studying morphology. Stemming does the same thing more crudely. Stop-word removal discards the function words to focus on content, which is exactly the wrong move for stylometry and exactly the right move for topic modeling. Every one of these operations is a trade, and the researcher must know what is being traded away.

The fifth decision, specific to fiction, is the separation of narration from dialogue. Many of the most interesting questions about Gatsby concern the difference between how the narrator writes and how the characters speak, and answering them requires a text in which the two are reliably distinguished. Quotation marks provide the mechanical basis, but the novel's dialogue is not always tidily marked, and interrupted speech, interior monologue, and free indirect passages blur the boundary. Automated separation gets most of it right and some of it wrong, and the errors concentrate exactly where the literary interest lies: at the moments when narration and speech interpenetrate. The researcher who needs a clean dialogue corpus for stylometric comparison must either accept this noise or correct it by hand, and must say which was chosen.

Gatsby presents its own preparation challenges, and they are worth naming because they generalize to much early twentieth-century fiction. The novel's dialogue is rich in contractions, interruptions, and broken-off sentences, all of which stress any tokenization rule. Its narration moves between scenic presentation and reflective summary, and the boundary between the two is a matter of readerly judgment that no regular expression can fully capture. Period typography introduces its own artifacts: the spaced em dashes of the first edition, the inconsistent hyphenation of compound words, the occasional British spelling that survived American copyediting. A digital text derived from a scan of an early printing will carry these features faithfully, including the errors, while a text derived from a modern reprint will carry a different set of silent corrections. Neither is automatically preferable. The early-printing text is closer to what the first readers saw but further from what the author finally intended; the modern text is cleaner but mediated by decades of editorial decisions. The researcher chooses, documents, and proceeds, aware that the choice is part of the analysis. This is textual scholarship, not data janitorial work, and it deserves to be described with the same care as the statistics that follow it.

A concrete walkthrough ties these abstractions to the page. Imagine the analyst wants to compare the vocabulary density of the novel's opening chapter with that of its closing pages, asking whether the famous ending is lexically richer or sparer than the beginning. The steps run as follows. Obtain a documented digital text and record its source. Strip front matter and back matter, keeping only the novel's chapters, and record the byte ranges removed. Tokenize with an explicit rule for contractions and hyphenation, and record the rule. Lowercase everything, and record that choice. Divide the text into the two passages by chapter boundaries, and record the boundaries. Count the distinct words and the total words in each passage, compute the ratio, and normalize by passage length. The result is a number for each passage. Now the interpretive work begins: the analyst checks whether the difference survives alternative tokenization rules, whether it is driven by a handful of unusual words or by a broad pattern, and whether the passages are long enough for the comparison to be stable. Only then does the analyst write the sentence reporting the finding, and the sentence includes the caveats, because the caveats are part of the finding.

The moral of preparation is not despair but discipline. Every quantitative result about Gatsby carries the fingerprints of these five decisions, and a result presented without them is not a result but a rumor. The reader of digital-humanities work should develop the habit of asking, before anything else: which text, cleaned how, tokenized by what rule, normalized how far, and with narration and dialogue separated by what criterion? The studies that answer these questions are the ones worth trusting. The studies that do not answer them are the ones to set aside, no matter how striking their charts.

## Topic Models and the Sorting of Vocabulary

### What does topic modeling sort, and what does it miss?

Topic modeling sorts a text's vocabulary into clusters of words that tend to appear near one another, revealing the novel's recurring word neighborhoods, but it misses everything about why those words cluster, whether the clustering reflects authorial design or the accidents of subject matter, and what any individual occurrence means.

Topic modeling is the most ambitious and the most easily misread of the text-mining methods. Where frequency analysis counts words one by one, topic modeling looks for structure among them: which words keep company with which. The underlying idea is that a text's vocabulary is not a random mixture but an organized one, and that the organization can be recovered statistically. The method divides the text into segments, examines the distribution of words across those segments, and identifies groups of words that co-occur more often than chance would predict. Each group is called a topic, a term of art that has caused endless confusion, because these statistical topics are not themes in the literary sense.

The procedure, described without mathematics, works like this. The researcher chooses the number of topics to find, a decision with no uniquely correct answer, and the algorithm assigns every word occurrence in the text to one of those topics, then iteratively reassigns words to improve the fit, converging on a stable arrangement. The output is a set of word lists: the words most strongly associated with each topic. One list might contain money, rich, wealth, expensive, diamonds. Another might contain eyes, look, see, watch, stare. A third might contain time, past, future, years, ago. The researcher then inspects these lists and gives each topic a label: the wealth topic, the vision topic, the time topic. That labeling step is interpretation, not computation, and it is where the method's honesty is tested. The algorithm found co-occurrence. The scholar supplied the meaning.

Applied to Gatsby, topic modeling offers a distinctive kind of overview. The novel is short enough that a careful reader can hold its themes in mind, but the model can still surprise, because it is indifferent to the novel's emphases and attends only to distribution. A topic the reader had not consciously registered, a cluster of words around a minor but persistent vocabulary, can emerge from the lists and prompt a return to the text with fresh attention. Conversely, a theme the reader considers central may fail to cohere as a statistical topic, which is itself informative: it suggests that the theme is carried by imagery and placement rather than by a repeated vocabulary, a finding about how the novel achieves its effects. The model is most useful not when it confirms what readers already know but when it diverges from expectation in ways that send the scholar back to the page.

The method's parameters are its interpretive levers, and they must be understood as such. The number of topics is the most consequential choice. Too few, and distinct vocabularies are forced together into muddy clusters. Too many, and coherent vocabularies shatter into fragments. There is no statistical test that identifies the right number for literary purposes; the researcher tries several values and judges the results by their interpretability, which means the outcome is guided by the scholar's judgment at the most fundamental level. The size of the text segments matters too: whole chapters, paragraphs, or fixed windows of words each produce different clusterings, because co-occurrence at the chapter level captures different structure than co-occurrence at the paragraph level. Preprocessing choices, stop-word removal, lemmatization, the minimum frequency threshold, all shift the results. A topic model is not a photograph of the text's hidden structure. It is one of many possible organizations, produced by a particular configuration of choices.

The limits, stated plainly, are these. The model knows nothing of order, irony, negation, or syntax. It treats a text as a bag of words, and a bag of words is a drastic simplification of a novel. The topics it finds are statistical artifacts until a scholar argues otherwise, and the history of the method includes celebrated cases where the most prominent topic in a collection turned out to track a formatting quirk or a preprocessing error rather than any literary reality. The labels are the scholar's, not the text's, and two scholars can label the same word list differently without either being wrong. The method is exploratory, a machine for generating hypotheses, and hypotheses generated by a topic model require the same confirmation as hypotheses generated any other way: a return to the text, a close reading of the passages, an argument that the pattern matters.

Consider, as an illustration of the labeling problem, a hypothetical topic list containing the words money, rich, house, party, car, expensive, and white. A researcher might label this the wealth topic, and the label feels natural. But the same list could be labeled the display topic, emphasizing the performative aspect, or the surface topic, emphasizing the visual register, and each label steers the subsequent interpretation in a different direction. The algorithm is indifferent among these labels; it found only the co-occurrence. Now consider a second list: eyes, look, see, watch, window, stare, glasses. The vision label suggests itself, but so does surveillance, and the choice between them is already an interpretive commitment about the novel's moral atmosphere. The honest write-up acknowledges the alternatives and explains why one label was chosen, citing the passages where the clustered words actually appear. The dishonest write-up presents the label as the algorithm's discovery and builds an argument on the unexamined choice. The difference between these two write-ups is the difference between scholarship and theater, and it is visible in a single paragraph: does the author discuss the labeling decision, or does the label arrive without a history?

A realistic practice task keeps the scale human. Take the novel's nine chapters as segments and, without any software, attempt the labeling step by hand: for each chapter, list the ten content words you judge most characteristic, then group the chapters by the similarity of their lists. Compare your groupings with a partner's. The disagreements you find, over which words are characteristic and which groupings are natural, are exactly the disagreements that the algorithm's parameters encode. The exercise demystifies the method by showing that its core operation, grouping by vocabulary similarity, is something readers do intuitively, and that the algorithm's contribution is scale and consistency rather than a fundamentally different kind of insight.

## The Limits the Count Cannot Cross

### Why do computational results still need an interpretive frame?

Computational results need an interpretive frame because every number the methods produce is a measurement of textual surface, while meaning lives in context, intention, and reception, which no count can observe. The frame supplies the argument that connects the measurement to a claim about the novel, and without it the number is mute.

The limits of quantitative reading fall into four families, and each deserves its moment, because the field's credibility depends on its practitioners stating their boundaries as clearly as their powers. The first family is linguistic. All of the methods described here, with minor exceptions, treat the text as a sequence of words detached from the structures that give words their literary life. Syntax is flattened or ignored. Irony is invisible: the methods cannot distinguish a sincere statement from its sarcastic inversion, because the words are the same and only the frame differs. Negation is mishandled by the simpler techniques, which count the words around a not as though the not were absent. Figurative language is literalized: a metaphor is counted as its vehicle, not its tenor. Point of view is erased: the same word in narration and in quoted speech is the same token. These are not bugs to be fixed by better software. They are consequences of the decision to count, and they define the permanent boundary of what counting can know.

The second family is statistical. A novel is a small dataset, and small datasets are noisy. The patterns that emerge clearly from millions of words can dissolve into randomness at fifty thousand, which means that findings about Gatsby are inherently more fragile than findings about large corpora. Significance testing, the machinery that tells a researcher whether an observed difference could plausibly be chance, was designed for experiments with controlled conditions, not for single texts where every observation is entangled with every other. A p-value computed on word frequencies within one novel does not mean what it means in a laboratory, and the researcher who reports it without qualification is borrowing an authority the number does not possess. The honest quantitative scholar of a single novel works with effect sizes and robustness checks rather than with the ritual language of significance: does the pattern survive different parameter choices, different segmentations, different preprocessing? Stability across choices is the relevant standard, not a threshold borrowed from another discipline.

The third family is interpretive, and it is the most frequently violated. A measurement becomes a claim only through an argument, and the argument can fail in ways the measurement cannot detect. The most common failure is the confusion of correlation with design: the observation that two things co-occur in the text is treated as evidence that the author placed them together deliberately. But co-occurrence can arise from subject matter, from genre convention, from the accidents of plot, or from the researcher's own grouping decisions. A cluster of money words in the party chapters may reflect authorial emphasis, or it may reflect the simple fact that parties in this novel are expensive. The method cannot distinguish these, and the scholar must supply the distinguishing argument from outside the numbers. The second common failure is the reification of the researcher's categories: the topics the model found are discussed as though they were features of the novel rather than products of the analysis, and the labels the scholar assigned are quoted as though the algorithm had produced them. The third is the selective reporting of the striking result: the one chart that confirms the hypothesis is published, while the dozen alternative analyses that showed nothing are forgotten. Against all three, the discipline is the same: state the choices, report the range, and mark clearly where the numbers end and the argument begins.

The fourth family is ethical and practical. Digital texts of the novel circulate in many versions of varying quality, and a finding computed on a corrupted text is a finding about the corruption. Editions differ, and the differences, though small, are exactly the kind of small variation that statistical methods can amplify into apparent patterns. The researcher has a duty of textual care that precedes the duty of analysis: know your text, document your source, and never let the convenience of a downloaded file substitute for the question of what the file contains. There is also a subtler ethical point about the rhetoric of objectivity. Numbers persuade, and the scholar who presents a quantitative result borrows the cultural authority of science. That authority is legitimate only insofar as the methods are scientific in the relevant sense: transparent, reproducible, and hedged at the limits of their evidence. The moment the numbers are used to bludgeon rather than to clarify, the field betrays its own standards.

A fifth family of limits, adjacent to the ethical one, concerns software literacy: the black-box problem. Much quantitative literary work is performed with software whose internal operations the user does not fully understand, and the defaults of that software then become silent methodological choices. A topic-modeling package ships with default values for its smoothing parameters; a network-visualization tool ships with a default layout algorithm that places nodes according to aesthetic criteria, not analytical ones; a concordancer ships with default definitions of word boundaries. The researcher who accepts every default has made a dozen methodological decisions without knowing it, and the resulting findings inherit those decisions invisibly. The remedy is not to write all software from scratch, which is impractical, but to learn what the defaults do and to vary the consequential ones. A finding that appears under one layout algorithm and vanishes under another was never a finding about the novel. The literate practitioner treats software as an instrument to be understood, not an oracle to be consulted, and says so in the method section. Readers evaluating quantitative work should look for this literacy: does the author discuss the tools' assumptions, or do the tools appear as magic?

### What vocabulary distinguishes honest quantitative claims from inflated ones?

Honest quantitative claims use the language of measurement, reporting distances, frequencies, and distributions with their conditions attached, while inflated claims use the language of proof, promoting a count into a verdict about meaning, intention, or value. The reader can audit any digital-humanities argument by checking which vocabulary its key sentences employ.

This diagnostic is worth developing into a working checklist, because it gives the non-specialist reader a way to evaluate quantitative literary claims without mastering the statistics. The honest study says "the frequency of X in passage A is higher than in passage B under the following preprocessing choices." The inflated study says "the analysis proves that the author intended X." The honest study says "the network places this character at the center of co-occurrence." The inflated study says "the network shows that this character is the most important." The honest study says "the topic model groups these words together." The inflated study says "the model discovered the novel's hidden themes." In each pair, the first sentence stays inside the method's competence and the second leaps beyond it. The leap is always the same: from arrangement to significance, from pattern to meaning, from the measurable to the literary. Learning to spot the leap is the single most useful skill a reader of this field can develop.

A further test concerns the handling of negative results. The honest study reports the analyses that showed nothing, because the absence of a pattern is information: it tells the field which questions this method cannot answer and constrains the claims that can be made. The inflated study reports only the positive findings, creating the false impression that the method succeeds wherever it is tried. Relatedly, the honest study discusses its parameter choices and shows that the finding survives reasonable alternatives. The inflated study presents a single configuration as though it were the only way to run the analysis. These are not technical niceties. They are the difference between scholarship and advertising, and they apply with equal force to traditional close reading, which has its own versions of selective quotation and suppressed counterevidence. The standard is one standard: show your work, state your limits, and let the reader check.

## Neither Decisive nor Worthless: Using Results Honestly

The complication this article promised to address can now be stated as a practical doctrine. Quantitative results are neither decisive nor worthless. They are evidence of a particular kind: evidence about the distribution of textual features, carrying the strengths and weaknesses of all distributional evidence. Like any evidence, they are decisive only within a well-constructed argument and worthless only when severed from one. The two extreme positions both make the same mistake, treating the number as though it had a fixed epistemic value independent of its use. It does not. A frequency count is decisive for the question it answers, which is how often a feature occurs under specified conditions, and it is silent on every other question until an argument connects them.

The middle position has a constructive program, and it is worth spelling out as a set of legitimate uses. First, quantification can test impressions. A reader's sense that a certain vocabulary saturates a chapter is an impression, and impressions are fallible: memory overweight striking instances and underweights the ordinary. A count checks the impression against the text's actual distribution, confirming it, refining it, or overturning it, and each outcome improves the resulting interpretation. Second, quantification can ensure exhaustiveness. A scholar arguing that a motif runs through the novel needs to know about every occurrence, not just the memorable ones, and a concordance supplies the complete inventory that memory cannot. Third, quantification can reveal the unnoticed. The background texture of function-word habits, the distribution profiles across chapters, the structural position of a minor character: these are patterns that unaided reading systematically misses, and the methods bring them into view. Fourth, quantification can discipline comparison. Claims about what is distinctive in Gatsby's style require a baseline, and the methods supply baselines that are explicit and inspectable rather than impressionistic. Fifth, quantification can support pedagogy. Students who count learn to attend to the text's surface with a care that transfers to their close reading, and the demystification of the methods teaches a broader lesson about evidence.

Each of these uses respects the boundary between measurement and meaning. Testing an impression does not replace the impression's interpretation. Ensuring exhaustiveness does not interpret the occurrences. Revealing the unnoticed does not explain the pattern. Disciplining comparison does not choose the comparators. Supporting pedagogy does not do the students' reading for them. In every case the method serves the scholarship rather than supplanting it, which is exactly the relationship the field's serious practitioners describe. The fantasy of a criticism without interpretation, a reading performed entirely by machine, belongs to manifestos, not to practice. The practice is humbler and more useful: better-checked arguments, more complete evidence, and occasional surprises that send the scholar back to the text.

The worthless camp deserves a direct answer too, because its objection is not entirely without force. The objection runs: literature is about meaning, tone, and value, and these are precisely what the methods cannot measure, so the methods are irrelevant to literary study. The answer is that literary study has always depended on facts about the text's surface, and that better facts make better arguments. No critic ever argued that counting syllables was irrelevant to the study of meter, though meter is a quantitative fact about verse. The digital methods extend the same logic to prose: they establish the distributional facts within which interpretation operates. A critic who scorns all counting while relying on claims like "the novel repeatedly associates wealth with coldness" is already making a quantitative claim, just an unchecked one. The methods do not introduce quantity into criticism. They make the quantity that was already there explicit and checkable.

There remains a legitimate version of the skeptical worry, and it concerns opportunity cost. Time spent counting is time not spent reading, and a field that rewards the production of charts may draw energy away from the slow work of interpretation. This is a real institutional concern, and the answer to it is not defensive but practical: the methods earn their place when they produce the goods, when a count changes an argument or opens a question that close reading had missed. The burden of proof is on the quantification, in every study, to show that it mattered. The field's best work meets this burden routinely. Its weakest work, the chart in search of a thesis, does not, and deserves the skepticism it receives. The standard cuts both ways, which is as it should be.

An illustration, explicitly hypothetical, shows the middle position in action. Imagine a scholar who has long argued, from close reading, that the novel's final chapter represents a turn inward, a withdrawal of the narration from the social world into solitary reflection. The impression is plausible; the closing pages feel contemplative. The scholar tests it by computing, for each chapter, the ratio of first-person singular pronouns to proper names of other characters, a rough index of self-reference against social reference. Suppose the ratio in the final chapter is indeed the highest in the novel. The impression is confirmed, but the confirmation adds something the impression lacked: a precise location of the shift, which the chapter-by-chapter profile reveals to begin two chapters earlier than the scholar had thought, during the aftermath rather than at the very end. The argument is revised: the withdrawal is gradual, not sudden, and the close reading that follows attends to the transitional chapters with new care. Now suppose instead that the ratio shows no such elevation. The scholar does not discard the impression but refines the question: perhaps the inward turn is carried by sentence length, or by abstract diction, or by the placement of reflective passages rather than their density. Each alternative is testable, and each test, positive or negative, sharpens the eventual interpretation. This is the middle position as a working method: the count as a sparring partner for the reading, valued as much for its resistance as for its agreement. Nothing in the illustration reports a real finding. Everything in it describes how a real finding would earn its keep.

## Counting and Close Reading at the Same Desk

The productive relationship between the methods and traditional interpretation is best described not as a division of territory but as a cycle. Close reading generates hypotheses: a sense that the narration's diction shifts, that a character's speech has a distinctive texture, that a vocabulary clusters in a particular stretch. Quantification tests and refines those hypotheses against the complete text, confirming some, complicating others, and occasionally refuting them outright. The refined hypotheses return to close reading, which examines the individual passages with new attention, noticing placements and ironies the numbers cannot see. The cycle repeats, each turn producing a more precise account of how the novel works. Neither partner in this cycle is dispensable. Without the hypothesis, the count has no question to answer. Without the count, the hypothesis has no check against the text's full extent.

This cycle has a concrete implication for how quantitative work on Gatsby should be written. The numbers should appear inside an interpretive argument, not alongside it as an appendix of authority. The reader should be able to see, at every step, what was counted, why it was counted, what the count showed, and what the author infers from the showing, with the inferences marked as inferences. The prose that surrounds the tables matters as much as the tables, because it is there that the measurement becomes scholarship. A study that presents its charts without this surrounding argument has not finished its work. A study that weaves the quantitative and the interpretive together, each checking the other, exemplifies the field at its best.

The relationship also clarifies what each side owes the other. Quantification owes interpretation its honesty about limits, stated up front and not buried in a footnote. It owes reproducibility: the data, the code, and the preprocessing decisions made available so that another scholar can verify or challenge the result. It owes restraint in its rhetoric, the vocabulary of measurement rather than the vocabulary of proof. Interpretation owes quantification its seriousness: the willingness to let a count overturn a cherished impression, to follow the evidence of distribution even when it complicates a favored reading. It owes the methods their proper questions, the ones about surface and structure that the methods can actually answer, rather than demanding that they settle questions of meaning they were never designed to address. When both sides meet these obligations, the result is scholarship that is both more precise and more humble than either approach achieves alone.

The workflow of a mixed-methods study can be stated as a sequence, not because research follows a script but because the sequence names the checkpoints where honesty is tested. It begins with a question that close reading has made interesting and that counting can address: a distributional question, not a question of meaning. It continues with text selection and preparation, documented in full. It proceeds through exploratory analysis, where the researcher looks at the data without a fixed hypothesis and notes what stands out, marking these observations as exploratory. It narrows to confirmatory analysis, where specific hypotheses are tested against the complete text with stated parameters. It subjects every finding to robustness checks, varying the choices that could have produced it. It returns to the text, reading the passages behind the numbers closely. And it ends in writing where the quantitative and the interpretive are woven together, each claim carrying its conditions. A study that skips the exploratory marking, or the robustness checks, or the return to the text, has not failed technically; it has failed scholarly, because the missing step is where the argument earns its trust. This sequence is the practical form of everything this article has argued.

The cycle described here also suggests a research program, a set of questions that the methods are well placed to address and that close reading alone has left open. How stable is the novel's narrative voice across its chapters at the level of function-word habit, and do the moments of greatest lyrical intensity coincide with measurable shifts in that habit? How does the distribution of concrete versus abstract vocabulary map onto the novel's movement between social scenes and reflective passages? Which characters' dialogue is most distinct from the narration's profile, and does that distinctness correlate with the reader's sense of each character's otherness? What does the co-occurrence structure look like when the edges are defined by interaction rather than mere co-presence, and which relationships change their structural position between the two definitions? These are genuine questions, answerable in principle, and each would require the full discipline this article has described: a documented text, explicit preprocessing, stated parameters, robustness checks, and an interpretive frame that says what the answer would mean. They are offered not as findings but as invitations, the kind of work the field does when it is done well.

## Verdict

Reading the novel by counting is a legitimate and limited enterprise, and this article has tried to describe it exactly as it is. Digital-humanities methods apply stylometry, frequency analysis, and network analysis to Gatsby's text, and they reveal patterns of style and structure that unaided reading can miss: the fingerprint of function-word habits, the distribution of vocabulary across chapters, the geometry of character co-occurrence, the clustering of co-occurring terms. These are real discoveries when they are produced with care, and they deserve their place in the scholarship. But every one of them is a measurement of surface, and the journey from surface to significance runs through interpretation, which the methods cannot perform and should not be asked to perform. The methods table near the start of this article remains the compact statement of that boundary: each row pairs a genuine power with the limit that contains it, and any future quantitative claim about the novel can be audited by asking which row it belongs to and whether it respects the row's stated limit.

The complication with which this article began, the temptation to treat quantitative results as either decisive or worthless, dissolves once the methods are understood at the level of their procedures. A result is decisive for the question it answers and silent on the rest. It is worthless only when it is severed from an argument, and it is valuable whenever it checks an impression, ensures exhaustiveness, reveals the unnoticed, disciplines a comparison, or teaches a student to attend. The honest practitioner states the text, the preprocessing, the parameters, and the limits, reports the range of outcomes rather than the single striking chart, and writes the surrounding prose in the vocabulary of measurement rather than the vocabulary of proof. Held to that standard, the quantitative study of Gatsby is not a rival to interpretation but its most demanding ally: the partner that checks the work, extends the evidence, and keeps the scholarship honest about what the text actually contains.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What digital-humanities methods are applied to Gatsby?

The principal digital-humanities methods applied to Gatsby are stylometry, word-frequency analysis, network analysis, and text mining. Stylometry compares the relative frequencies of common words between passages, which lets researchers test whether the narration and the various characters' dialogue share the same underlying word-choice habits. Word-frequency analysis produces complete ranked inventories of the novel's vocabulary and compares them against reference collections of other novels to find the book's distinctive terms. Network analysis converts the cast into a graph of co-occurring characters and studies its structure, revealing who is central and who is peripheral to the novel's social world. Text mining covers the broader automated extraction of patterns, including topic modeling, which sorts vocabulary into clusters of co-occurring words. Each method is described at the level of its actual procedure in this article's main sections, with its limits stated alongside its powers.

### Q: What computational methods are used?

The computational methods used on the novel are the standard toolkit of quantitative text analysis, adapted to a single short book. Frequency counting with normalization by text length produces comparable rates per thousand or per ten thousand words. Statistical comparison of frequency profiles measures the distance between passages or between the novel and a reference corpus. Concordance software displays every occurrence of a word in its surrounding context, bridging the gap between the count and the passage. Graph computation builds character co-occurrence networks and calculates structural measures such as degree and betweenness. Probabilistic clustering, in the form of topic modeling, groups co-occurring vocabulary into topics. Collation software aligns different editions word by word to expose textual variants. None of these methods understands the novel. Each performs a well-defined mechanical operation, and the scholarship lies in designing the operation, checking its robustness, and interpreting its output within an argument.

### Q: What do the methods reveal about the novel?

At the level of method, the techniques reveal four kinds of pattern that unaided reading tends to miss. Stylometry can reveal whether the novel's many voices are measurably distinct in their function-word habits or whether Fitzgerald's own habits show through every speaker. Frequency analysis can reveal which vocabularies dominate the book and how their distribution shifts across chapters, mapping the lexical substrate of effects readers feel intuitively. Network analysis can reveal the structure of character co-occurrence, including mismatches between a figure's structural centrality and narrative prominence that pose genuine interpretive puzzles. Topic modeling can reveal clusters of co-occurring vocabulary that the reader had not consciously registered, prompting a return to the text with fresh attention. This article deliberately states these as capacities rather than findings, because no specific result is claimed here. What any particular analysis reveals depends on its design, its text, and its parameters.

### Q: What are the limits of quantitative reading?

The limits fall into four families. Linguistic limits: the methods treat text as word sequences and are blind to syntax, irony, negation, figurative language, and point of view, so they cannot distinguish a sincere sentence from its sarcastic inversion. Statistical limits: a single novel is a small dataset, so patterns are fragile, and significance testing borrowed from experimental science does not mean here what it means in a laboratory. Interpretive limits: a measurement becomes a claim only through an argument, and the common failures are confusing co-occurrence with authorial design, reifying the researcher's own categories as features of the text, and reporting only the striking results. Textual limits: every result inherits the edition chosen, the cleaning performed, and the tokenization rules applied, so an undocumented text produces untrustworthy numbers. None of these limits is a reason to abandon the methods. Each is a reason to practice them with stated procedures and hedged conclusions.

### Q: Are quantitative results decisive or worthless?

Neither. A quantitative result is decisive for the question it actually answers, which is always a question about the distribution of textual features under specified conditions, and it is silent on every other question until an argument connects them. It is worthless only when severed from interpretation, presented as a chart without the surrounding reasoning that says what was measured, why it matters, and where the measurement stops. The two extreme positions make the same mistake: they treat the number as though it carried a fixed epistemic value independent of its use. In practice, counts serve scholarship in five legitimate ways: they test impressions against the text's actual distribution, they ensure exhaustiveness by inventorying every occurrence, they reveal background patterns that reading misses, they discipline comparisons by supplying explicit baselines, and they teach students to attend to textual surface. Each use respects the boundary between measurement and meaning, which is where the value of the methods lives.

### Q: How do the methods complement interpretation?

The methods complement interpretation through a cycle rather than a division of territory. Close reading generates hypotheses, such as a sense that a vocabulary clusters in a particular stretch or that a character's speech has a distinctive texture. Quantification tests those hypotheses against the complete text, confirming some, complicating others, and occasionally refuting them. The refined hypotheses return to close reading, which examines the individual passages with new attention, noticing placements and ironies the numbers cannot see. Neither partner is dispensable: without the hypothesis the count has no question, and without the count the hypothesis has no check against the text's full extent. This partnership also sets mutual obligations. Quantification owes interpretation honesty about limits, reproducibility of procedures, and restraint in rhetoric. Interpretation owes quantification the seriousness to let a count overturn an impression and the discipline to ask the methods only the questions they can answer.

### Q: How does digital stylometry test authorship questions about the novel?

Digital stylometry tests authorship questions by comparing the frequency profiles of common function words across the passages in doubt, not by detecting any literary quality. The procedure converts each passage into a list of relative frequencies for the most common words, computes a statistical distance between the profiles, and compares that distance against the background variation within the novel itself. If the distance between two passages exceeds the typical distance among passages known to share an origin, the analyst has evidence of a stylistic break at the level of word-choice habit. For Gatsby, the natural internal test compares the narration with each character's dialogue, asking whether the voices separate measurably. The method's limits are strict: short passages yield unstable profiles, genre and era confound the signal, and deliberate imitation defeats it. Results are reported as distances with their conditions, never as certainties about identity.

### Q: What does concordance software reveal about repeated words across the novel?

Concordance software reveals every occurrence of a chosen word displayed with its surrounding context, typically a line of text on either side, which lets the researcher survey all instances without relying on memory. This completeness is its principal value: where a reader recalls the striking occurrences, the concordance shows the ordinary ones too, and the ordinary ones often change the picture. Reading down a concordance of a repeated word exposes its grammatical habits, its typical neighbors, and the range of its uses, from the thematically charged to the merely functional. The display also supports the crucial move from counting to checking, because each line links the abstract frequency back to a concrete passage. What the concordance cannot do is interpret: it shows that a word occurs in certain contexts, but whether those contexts constitute a pattern worth arguing about is a judgment the software leaves entirely to the scholar.

### Q: How is character dialogue separated from narration in a digital text?

Dialogue is separated from narration by identifying the stretches of text enclosed in quotation marks and assigning them to speakers, with the remainder classified as narration. In practice this requires several judgment calls. Quotation marks provide the mechanical basis, but interrupted speech, dialogue tags, interior passages, and free indirect discourse blur the boundary, and automated separation misclassifies exactly the passages where literary interest concentrates. Pronouns inside and outside quotation marks must be resolved to the correct speaker or to the narrator. A researcher who needs a clean dialogue corpus for stylometric comparison must either accept this noise or correct it by hand, chapter by chapter. The choice must be documented, because the separation directly affects every downstream comparison: a frequency profile of a character's speech is only as clean as the boundary that defined it. This is one of the preparation decisions that shapes results before the first count is made.

### Q: What is a corpus, and how does Gatsby fit into larger text collections?

A corpus is a defined collection of texts assembled for analysis, and Gatsby fits into such collections as one item among many, which is what makes comparative measurement possible. A reference corpus of American novels from the same decade provides the baseline against which the novel's distinctive vocabulary is measured: a word is unusually frequent in Gatsby only relative to its rate in comparable books. A corpus of Fitzgerald's other fiction provides a different baseline, marking what is distinctive about this novel within his own output. The choice of corpus is interpretive, because each answers a different question, and the honest study states which question it asks. Building a corpus also requires consistent preparation across all its texts, since a finding computed from mixed editions or mixed cleaning procedures measures the mixture rather than the literature. For the single-novel analyst, the corpus is the instrument that turns a raw frequency into a claim about distinctiveness.

### Q: How do researchers prepare a clean machine-readable Gatsby text?

Researchers prepare a clean machine-readable text in five documented stages. First, they choose a source edition and record its provenance, since editions differ in small but consequential ways. Second, they clean the file, removing scanning artifacts, running heads, and front and back matter, and they record what was removed. Third, they tokenize, applying explicit rules for contractions, hyphenation, and punctuation, and they record the rules. Fourth, they normalize, typically lowercasing and sometimes lemmatizing or removing stop words, and they record how far normalization went. Fifth, they separate narration from dialogue where the research question requires it, and they record the criterion used. Each stage involves choices that shift the numbers, so the preparation is published as part of the method rather than treated as a technical preliminary. A quantitative result presented without this documentation is not a result but a rumor, because no other scholar can evaluate or reproduce it.

### Q: How does network analysis map relationships among the novel's characters?

Network analysis maps relationships by converting the cast into a graph in which each character is a node and each edge records co-occurrence under a defined condition, such as shared presence in a scene or exchanged dialogue. The researcher first defines the cast list, deciding whether minor and unnamed figures count, then defines the edge criterion, then extracts the pairs from the text by hand or by automated name recognition. Software computes structural measures: degree, the number of direct connections; betweenness, how often a figure bridges otherwise separate groups; and closeness, how few steps reach everyone else. The resulting picture shows the novel's social geometry, including the dense core of recurring figures and the sparse periphery of party guests. What it maps, strictly speaking, is co-occurrence, not relationship: the edge records presence, not affection or hostility. The quality of each connection remains a matter for close reading.

### Q: What does social network analysis assume about fictional characters?

Social network analysis assumes that characters can be treated as nodes and their co-presence as edges, which imports several simplifications that the scholar must keep in view. It assumes that appearing together is a meaningful unit of relation, though a shared scene can contain lovers, enemies, or strangers. It assumes that the cast list is well defined, though minor figures and unnamed presences resist clean enumeration. It assumes that the text's record of who appears where is complete, though a first-person narrator filters every scene through limited attention. It assumes that structural position correlates with literary significance, though the novel routinely invests marginal figures with great weight and central figures with little. None of these assumptions is fatal, but each must be stated and managed. The analysis is sound when it treats its graph as a model of arrangement under stated assumptions, and unsound when it forgets the assumptions and reads the graph as the novel's social truth.

### Q: How does topic modeling sort the novel's vocabulary into clusters?

Topic modeling sorts vocabulary by dividing the text into segments and finding groups of words that co-occur within those segments more often than chance predicts, using an iterative algorithm that reassigns words until the arrangement stabilizes. The researcher chooses the number of topics and the segment size, both consequential decisions with no uniquely correct values, and the algorithm outputs word lists of the most strongly associated terms per topic. The researcher then labels each list, for instance as a wealth vocabulary or a vision vocabulary, which is an interpretive act rather than a computational one. The clusters reveal the novel's recurring word neighborhoods, including some the reader had not consciously registered, and they fail to cohere around some themes the reader considers central, which itself informs how those themes achieve their effects. The method is exploratory: it generates hypotheses that must be confirmed by returning to the text.

### Q: What are the dangers of cherry-picking word counts to support a thesis?

The dangers are threefold, and all stem from the fact that a large text offers many countable features, so a researcher who looks long enough will find some number that appears to support any thesis. First, selective reporting: the one striking chart is published while the dozen analyses that showed nothing are forgotten, creating a false impression of the method's reliability. Second, parameter shopping: the researcher tries many configurations and reports only the one that confirms the hypothesis, which is the quantitative equivalent of quoting out of context. Third, post hoc storytelling: a pattern found by exploration is presented as though it had been predicted, reversing the logic of hypothesis and evidence. The defenses are procedural: preregister the question where possible, report the full range of analyses attempted, vary the parameters and report the stability of the finding, and mark exploratory results as exploratory. A count that cannot survive these disciplines is not evidence, however suggestive its chart.

### Q: How do digital editions differ from printed editions for researchers?

Digital editions differ from printed editions in provenance, stability, and affordances, and each difference matters for quantitative work. Provenance: a printed scholarly edition documents its copy-text and emendations, while many circulating digital files are of unknown origin, silently corrected or corrupted, so the researcher must verify the source rather than assume it. Stability: a printed page is fixed, while a digital file can be silently updated, which means results must cite the exact file version used. Affordances: the digital text can be searched, counted, concordanced, and reprocessed, which the printed page cannot, but these operations depend on the cleaning and tokenization decisions discussed in this article. The practical rule is to treat the digital edition with the same textual skepticism scholars apply to print: know which text you have, document how it was prepared, and never let the convenience of the file substitute for the question of what it contains.

### Q: What role does statistical significance play in literary word counts?

Statistical significance plays a smaller and more qualified role in literary word counts than its scientific prestige suggests. Significance testing was designed for experiments with controlled conditions and independent observations, while the words of a single novel are deeply entangled: every occurrence sits in a context shaped by plot, style, and authorial choice. A p-value computed on frequencies within one text therefore does not carry its usual meaning, and reporting it without qualification borrows an authority the number does not possess. The more relevant standard for single-text analysis is robustness: does the observed difference survive alternative tokenization rules, different segmentations, and reasonable parameter changes? A pattern stable across such variations deserves interpretive attention; a pattern that appears under one configuration and vanishes under the next does not. Effect size matters more than thresholds: how large the difference is, stated plainly, tells the scholar more than whether it crosses a conventional line.

### Q: How might a student run a simple frequency study on a single chapter?

A student can run a simple frequency study with a documented text and basic scripting in an afternoon. Choose one chapter and one question, for example whether the chapter's dialogue uses shorter words on average than its narration. Separate the dialogue from the narration by quotation marks, recording the rule used. Tokenize both parts identically, compute the average word length in each, and normalize by the number of words so the two parts are comparable. Then perform the essential checks: does the difference survive if dialogue tags are excluded, does it hold across the chapter's individual scenes, and is each part long enough for the average to be stable? Write up the procedure before the conclusion, stating the text source, the separation rule, and the checks performed. Whatever the outcome, the exercise teaches the method's core discipline: the count begins the argument rather than ending it, and the documentation is part of the result.

### Q: Why can't word counts settle debates about meaning?

Word counts cannot settle debates about meaning because meaning is not a property of word distributions but of words in context, under an intention, before a reader. A count records how often a form occurs; it cannot record whether each occurrence is sincere or ironic, literal or figurative, central or incidental. Two passages with identical frequency profiles can assert opposite things, and the methods have no access to the difference. Debates about meaning turn on exactly these inaccessible qualities: what a sentence implies, what a scene withholds, how a narrator's unreliability colors a claim. Quantitative evidence can constrain such debates, by ruling out claims that the text's distribution does not support, and it can enrich them, by supplying complete inventories and revealing unnoticed patterns. But the step from the pattern to the meaning is always an argument, made by a reader, answerable to the text. No count crosses that step on its own, which is why the methods complement interpretation rather than replacing it.

### Q: How do distant and close reading work together on the same passage?

Distant and close reading work together on the same passage through a cycle of hypothesis, test, and return. Close reading first notices something: a density of a certain vocabulary, a shift in the narration's texture, a minor figure who seems structurally load-bearing. Distant reading then tests the notice against the whole text: is the density real or an artifact of memory, does the shift survive measurement, does the figure's co-occurrence pattern support the impression? The results send the reader back to the passage with sharper questions, attending to placements, ironies, and contexts the numbers cannot see. A concrete routine makes this practical: run the concordance on the noticed word, read every occurrence in context, and let the full inventory revise the initial impression. The passage is the meeting ground where the two approaches check each other, and the scholarship that results is more precise than either approach achieves alone.
