## A small study and a very large conclusion

In 1993 a short study from the University of California, Irvine reported that college students who listened to a Mozart sonata for two pianos performed better, for a matter of minutes, on a test of spatial reasoning than students who sat in silence or listened to relaxation instructions, and that narrow result is where any honest account of Mozart influence on music has to begin, because the distance between what those researchers measured and what the wider culture concluded from it contains the whole argument of this article in miniature: the compositional influence was narrow, technical and specific, while the cultural footprint grew enormous, and confusing the second for the first is the error that keeps repeating.

<!--more-->

![Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart influence on music shown through scores, catalogues and concert life - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-42.webp)

Within five years of that 1993 report, the governor of Georgia, Zell Miller, was proposing public money so that a compact disc of classical music could be given to the parents of newborn children in his state, with Mozart as the name that made the proposal intelligible to voters who had never heard the sonata in question. A laboratory finding about paper folding and cutting, measured in minutes, had become a policy about infant development, measured in press releases. Nobody in that chain had lied about the original experiment. Each step had simply enlarged the claim by one plausible-sounding increment, until a result about a single task performed by adults had turned into a promise about the brains of babies.

The enlargement matters because it was not an aberration. The same pattern, in slower and more respectable forms, runs through two centuries of writing about what Mozart left behind. A composer praises a specific device and the praise becomes a general debt. A catalogue organises the works and the organisation becomes proof of supremacy. A city builds a trade around a birthplace and the trade becomes evidence that the music itself must act on everyone who hears it. At each stage something real happened, and at each stage the record of what happened was quietly replaced by something larger.

So this article separates the two stories and keeps them separate. The first story is about transmission: named composers who had dated access to named works and who took identifiable techniques from them, sometimes transforming what they took and sometimes refusing it in ways that reveal as much as the borrowing does. The second story is about footprint: catalogues, editions, festivals, curricula, films, advertisements and one famous psychological claim, all of which made the name familiar to millions of people who could not identify a single theme. Both stories are true. They are not the same story, and the sections that follow measure each on its own evidence.

## What influence means for a composer who died young

Influence, used with any discipline, means a traceable change in what a later musician did that would not have happened, or would have happened differently, without access to an earlier body of work. It requires three things that can in principle be checked: access, so that the later musician demonstrably encountered the work before writing the passage in question; specificity, so that what passed across is a technique, a formal decision or a texture rather than a mood; and consequence, so that the borrowing shows up in finished music rather than in compliments. Praise alone fails the test, however warm it is, because musicians praise many predecessors whose procedures they never adopt.

Measured by that standard, the shape of this case is unusual from the start. Mozart died in December 1791 at the age of thirty five, leaving a body of work that was large but incompletely published, partly circulating in manuscript copies, partly known only to the Viennese circle that had heard it performed. There was no collected edition, no reliable numbering of the works and no biography based on documents. A later composer could not simply consult the output the way a student in a later century would consult it, bound and indexed. What could be learned had to be learned from what happened to be available, in the places where it happened to survive, and that accident of availability shaped the influence as surely as the quality of the music did. The full chronology of the life that produced those conditions belongs to [the account of Mozart's life and career](/2010/10/21/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-life-and-career/), and it is compressed here to the single point that the downstream story cannot be understood without it.

That compression leaves a puzzle standing in the middle of the subject, and it is worth stating plainly rather than answering it early. If he was so influential, why do so few composers sound like him? The symphonies, concertos and operas are performed constantly, the name is recognised on every continent, and yet the list of major composers whose surface style resembles his is remarkably short, far shorter than the equivalent list for Beethoven or for Bach. The sections on the nineteenth century return to that question and close it there, after the evidence about reverence has been separated from the evidence about technique.

The broader frame also matters. Mozart worked inside a shared European practice that the [history of classical composers](/2010/03/07/history-of-classical-composers/) traces across the whole period, a practice in which forms, gestures and orchestral conventions belonged to everyone and circulated freely between courts and public concerts. Inside such a practice, influence is hardest to prove exactly where it is most pervasive, because a device found in two composers may have reached both from the common stock rather than from one to the other. The discipline this article applies is therefore conservative by design. Where a common source explains a resemblance as well as direct borrowing does, the resemblance is recorded as shared practice, not as influence, and the claim is reserved for cases where the documentary trail and the musical match point the same way.

## The immediate circle in Vienna

The first transmission was personal and small. In the Vienna of the 1780s Mozart taught a handful of pupils, played in the houses of patrons who copied his music for their own use, and worked alongside singers and instrumentalists who carried his procedures into their own playing by imitation rather than by study of scores. Franz Xaver Sussmayr, who had been his pupil and copyist, absorbed the orchestral habits at close range and was close enough to the workshop to be asked, after the composer's death, to complete unfinished material, a task whose results scholars such as Simon Keefe have examined as evidence of how thoroughly an assistant could internalise a master's routine decisions. Keefe, whose work on Mozart's Viennese years concentrates on the piano concertos and on the audience that received them, treats that circle as a working environment rather than a court of admirers, and the distinction is the right one for this section.

Johann Nepomuk Hummel lived in the Mozart household for close to two years as a child pupil, receiving lessons without payment, and carried away a pianism built on clarity of fingerwork, singing tone and restraint in the use of the pedals, qualities he later taught in his own published piano school and demonstrated across a long touring career. Thomas Attwood, an English pupil sent to Vienna at the expense of the Prince of Wales, studied composition with Mozart between 1785 and 1787, and his surviving exercise books, corrected in his teacher's hand, show the instruction moving from strict counterpoint toward free composition, which means Attwood carried back to London not a style but a training sequence. He later taught, among many others, musicians who stood close to Mendelssohn's English circle, so a Viennese lesson book became one thread in a much later English practice.

Around the pupils stood the professionals. Emanuel Schikaneder, the actor manager who commissioned and performed in the Singspiel of 1791, took from the collaboration a model of how serious musical construction could carry popular theatre, and his company kept that repertory before Viennese audiences for years. The singer Benedikt Schack and the other members of that troupe sang music written for their exact voices, and the tailoring itself was a lesson in craft that younger opera composers in Vienna could observe from the pit. Antonio Salieri, so often cast in the popular imagination as an obstructing rival, conducted Mozart's music, taught some of the same pupils and maintained professional relations that the documentary record, as Cliff Eisen has repeatedly emphasised in his work on the Mozart documents, shows to have been correct and at times cooperative. Eisen, the editor of collections of Mozart's letters and documents, is the authority this article follows whenever a claim depends on what a paper record does or does not say.

What the immediate circle transmitted, then, was not a doctrine. It was a set of working habits: how to write for specific hands and voices, how to pace an ensemble so that each entry feels inevitable, how to let a wind instrument carry an argument rather than decorate one. Those habits travelled in people, and people die, move and change employers, which is one reason the first generation of transmission was so much thinner than the fame that eventually attached to the name. The circle preserved the music by performing it, but it did not yet possess the machinery, the catalogue, the edition and the biography, that would later make the music available to strangers.

## Haydn's assessment and what it carried

The single most consequential endorsement Mozart ever received was spoken by Joseph Haydn to the composer's father in Vienna in February 1785, after Haydn had heard the quartets that Mozart would publish that year with a dedication to him. The words survive because Leopold Mozart reported them in a letter to his daughter Maria Anna, written on 16 February 1785, and that provenance matters: the assessment is not a remark Haydn published, and it is not a judgement delivered by a committee of posterity, but a private statement relayed by a proud father within days of the occasion. In Leopold's report, Haydn declared before God and as an honest man that the younger composer was the greatest he knew, by person or by name, possessing taste together with the most profound knowledge of composition. The quotation is kept under fifteen words in any responsible retelling, and the substance can be carried in paraphrase, as here.

The context gives the remark its weight. Haydn was in his early fifties, the most respected instrumental composer in Europe, and he had no professional need to flatter a colleague nearly twenty five years younger who worked in the same city and competed for some of the same patrons. He had also heard the music at close quarters, playing in the quartet evenings at which the new works were tried, alongside the composers Dittersdorf and Vanhal, in gatherings the English tenor Michael Kelly later described in his reminiscences. An assessment formed while holding a viola part, listening to how the inner voices behaved, is a different kind of evidence from a review written from a single hearing, and later generations were right to treat it as testimony from inside the workshop.

What the assessment carried was permission. Through the following decades, whenever Mozart's standing was questioned on grounds of difficulty, lightness or early death, Haydn's reported verdict stood in the record as the judgement of the one contemporary whose authority nobody could discount. Publishers used it, biographers repeated it, and the dedication of the six quartets, in which Mozart described the works to Haydn in print as the fruit of long and laborious endeavour, bound the two names together in every subsequent account of the string quartet. Simon Keefe's reading of the Viennese reception stresses how much of Mozart's early reputation was built inside exactly this network of senior endorsement and domestic performance, before print culture took the music elsewhere.

Yet the assessment also shows the limits of endorsement as influence. Haydn praised the quartets; he did not write like them. His own later quartets continued along a path established well before 1785, enlarging a rhetoric of wit, delay and surprise that was his own property. What passed between the two men ran in both directions and had begun earlier, with the younger composer studying Haydn's quartets of the previous decade and answering them. The verdict of 1785 is therefore best filed as evidence of standing, not as a mechanism of transmission. It told the world how to rank Mozart. Ranking, as the following sections show, is a force of its own, but it is not the same force as a borrowed technique.

## Beethoven and the direct inheritance

### How did Mozart influence Beethoven?

Beethoven encountered Mozart's music in Bonn through the court orchestra and through his teacher Christian Gottlob Neefe, then studied specific works after reaching Vienna in 1792, and the borrowing is documented in works modelled openly on Mozart originals, including a wind quintet, a C minor piano concerto and a string quartet in A major.

The Bonn preparation was concrete. As a teenage musician in the electoral orchestra, Beethoven played viola in performances that included Mozart's operas, and the Bonn court owned scores that a curious player could study between duties. Whether the young Beethoven played for Mozart during a Vienna visit in 1787 is a story repeated for two centuries on evidence too thin to settle, and this article does not build on it, because the documented inheritance begins later and needs no legendary interview to support it. What can be dated is the arrival in Vienna in November 1792, the lessons with Haydn that quickly disappointed both parties, the counterpoint studies with Johann Georg Albrechtsberger and the vocal writing exercises with Salieri, all of which placed Beethoven inside the tradition Mozart had dominated while giving him teachers who were not Mozart.

Count Ferdinand von Waldstein, his Bonn patron, wrote in the young man's autograph album at his departure a farewell predicting that he would receive Mozart's spirit from Haydn's hands, a sentence that tells us exactly how the succession was understood by the people arranging it. Beethoven was sent to Vienna to inherit a position, the leading keyboard composer of the city, and the inheritance was to be mediated by the senior master of the previous generation. The pupil then spent the decade studying the scores of the supposed predecessor more closely than the lessons of the actual teacher, which is why the strongest evidence sits in the music of the 1790s rather than in any document of instruction. The [life of Beethoven](/2011/01/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-life-and-career/) carries the full chronology of that decade; here the concern is only with what crossed from one desk to another.

The borrowings are specific enough to name. The Quintet for Piano and Winds of 1796 follows the scoring, layout and much of the phrase architecture of Mozart's Quintet for the same combination, K. 452, a work Beethoven knew from Viennese performance. The Piano Concerto in C minor, composed around 1800, takes its key, its orchestral rhetoric and its dramatic confrontation between soloist and orchestra from Mozart's Piano Concerto in C minor, K. 491, down to the unusual choice of a minor key for a public concerto and the use of a long orchestral exposition that states material the soloist will later contest. The String Quartet in A major from the Opus 18 set follows Mozart's Quartet in A major, K. 464, movement by movement in its opening design, an act of study so close that scholars treat the pair as a deliberate exercise in matching a model before departing from it. Beethoven also wrote variations on themes drawn from the operas, including sets on material from The Magic Flute for cello and piano, which placed Mozart's melodies under his own fingers in public.

Charles Rosen, in his study of the classical style first published in 1971, characterised this relationship as one in which Beethoven learned the language from Haydn and Mozart together and then used it to say things neither predecessor had attempted, and Rosen's characterisation is the one this article holds throughout: he is the analyst of a shared language and its transformation, not a witness for simple discipleship. Julian Rushton, whose Mozart scholarship centres on the operas and on the composer's standing in the nineteenth century, makes the complementary point that the operatic Beethoven, the composer of Fidelio, worked in a house tradition that Mozart's Singspiel and rescue opera conventions had already reshaped in Vienna.

## What Beethoven took and what he refused

Beethoven's inheritance is specific and technical, and what he refused is as informative as what he took. That sentence states the first rung of this article's argument, and the evidence for both halves can be set out without recourse to general talk about greatness. The taking came first, in the 1790s, and it is most visible in procedures. From Mozart's concertos Beethoven took the idea that a concerto is a drama of opposed forces rather than a display frame, that the orchestra possesses arguments the soloist must answer, and that a slow movement can sustain a singing line over a texture reduced almost to chamber scale. From the quartets he took the treatment of the four voices as conversational equals, each capable of carrying the theme, and from the operas he took the ensemble as a machine for simultaneous, conflicting intentions, a device that reappears whenever his stage and choral works need several truths at once.

He also took working habits that leave no notes to compare. The evidence of the sketchbooks, which Beethoven kept far more systematically than Mozart did, shows a composer testing a borrowed design against his own material over many pages, a method of public self-correction that the model of the effortless Mozart, already forming in the 1790s, made almost a point of honour to contradict. Where the received image presented finished perfection arriving without labour, Beethoven's surviving drafts advertise labour, and the advertisement was itself a position taken against the inheritance: the music would be heard as earned.

The refusals are equally legible. Beethoven declined the galant surface, the polished sociability that lets a Mozart movement smile while its structure does severe work underneath, and replaced it with a rhetoric of struggle in which difficulty is displayed rather than concealed. He declined the opera buffa world almost entirely, writing a single completed opera of rescue and conjugal devotion where Mozart had moved between comedy, Singspiel and opera seria with professional ease. He declined the older patronage economy that Mozart had navigated, insisting on terms with publishers and aristocratic supporters that treated the composer as a contracting party of standing. And in the piano music he declined the older ideal of clarity as the supreme keyboard virtue, expanding sonority, register and violence of attack beyond anything the Viennese instruments of Mozart's decade were built to produce.

None of this makes the debt smaller. It makes it measurable, because a refusal can only be identified where the refused model was present and available, and the presence is documented in the modelled works of the same years. The influence therefore ran in the direction of equipment rather than accent. Beethoven took the concerto's drama, the quartet's equality of voices and the ensemble's conflicting intentions, and then spoke, deliberately, with a different accent from the man who had supplied the equipment. Later sections return to that distinction, equipment against accent, because it explains more about the whole nineteenth century than any ranking of the two composers can.

## The pupils and the Vienna line

Beyond Beethoven, the Viennese transmission ran through pupils whose names now survive mainly in reference books, and their relative obscurity is itself evidence about how influence behaves when it depends on personal contact. Hummel is the exception who proves the scale of the rest. Trained in the Mozart household, launched as a travelling child virtuoso under the older composer's written recommendation, he became one of the most admired pianists in Europe, a Kapellmeister at Weimar and the composer of concertos that younger pianists, including Chopin, studied with care. What Hummel carried from Vienna was a keyboard ideal of evenness, clarity and singing legato, together with a suspicion of mere velocity, and he codified it in his piano school of 1828, which reached students who never knew whose practice stood behind its precepts.

Attwood's line ran through England. His Mozart-corrected exercises remained in his possession, he became organist of St Paul's Cathedral and a founding professor at the Royal Academy of Music in London, and the discipline he taught was the Viennese sequence of strict counterpoint opening into free composition. Through Attwood the Mozartean training entered English institutional life at the moment that life was being organised, a quiet channel that no concert statistic will ever register. Sussmayr's line was shorter and stranger: his name attached permanently to the completion of the Requiem, his own operas vanished from the stage, and he survives as a footnote in another man's biography, which makes him the first clear case of a pattern section thirty three examines at length, the contemporary whose identity was absorbed by the reputation he helped to serve.

Vienna itself kept a performing tradition that functioned as a living edition. The Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde, founded in 1812, programmed Mozart alongside the newer repertory and preserved a local memory of tempo and articulation that visiting musicians remarked on. Publishers in the city, Artaria and later Diabelli among them, kept the sonatas and chamber works in print for a domestic market of amateur players, and that market mattered more to transmission than any single virtuoso, because a work that sits on a thousand piano stands is being taught nightly by the families who own it. Influence at this level is not a chain of famous names. It is a stock of printed paper, a habit of domestic performance and a standard of execution passed from teacher to pupil in rooms that left no record.

The Vienna line also had a limit, and the limit was geography. While the music remained a Viennese possession, sustained by local institutions and local memory, its European standing depended on travellers and on the slow export of editions. The transformation of a local tradition into a universal one required the two developments this article reaches in sections thirteen and fourteen, the catalogue that made the output countable and the complete edition that made it consultable. Until those existed, Mozart was a composer whom the nineteenth century praised in general terms and knew in partial ones, which is precisely the condition in which reverence flourishes and influence starves.

## Rossini, opera and the Italian reception

Italy received Mozart first as an opera composer and judged him by Italian standards, which produced a reception unlike any other country's. The operas written for Naples, Milan and Prague in his youth were known, the great Viennese comedies travelled slowly across the Alps, and Don Giovanni acquired a peculiar Italian career as the Mozart work that Italian houses could neither ignore nor comfortably classify. Into that landscape came Gioachino Rossini, who as a student in Bologna copied and studied scores by Haydn and Mozart so intently that his fellow students nicknamed him the little German. The nickname is the reception in miniature: in Italy the classical instrumental tradition was foreign property, admired and slightly suspect, and the young Rossini made himself its local student.

What Rossini took can be specified. From Mozart's opera buffa he took the act finale as an engineered escalation, in which entrances accumulate, keys shift and the stage fills until confusion resolves in a closing ensemble, a machine Rossini rebuilt at larger scale and higher speed in his own comedies. From the orchestration he took the discipline of the winds as commentators rather than padding, and from the arias he took, and then conspicuously exaggerated, the principle that a character's music should fit the singer's exact capabilities, since Rossini wrote ornamentation out in full precisely to control what Mozart had often left to a trusted performer's invention. The famous reported remark in which Rossini apportioned his week, taking Beethoven twice, Haydn four times and Mozart every day, survives in later recollection and should be weighed as such, but its currency in the nineteenth century shows how Italian musicians framed the debt: Mozart was not an enthusiasm but a diet.

The resistance was as instructive as the debt. Italian critics of the period repeatedly charged Mozart with excess of learning, with an orchestral density that threatened the voice, and with a Germanic preference for instrumental argument over melody, charges that Rossini's generation answered in practice by writing operas whose surfaces were Italianate while their engines, the finale construction, the accompanied recitative and the wind writing, were substantially Viennese. Julian Rushton, in his work on Mozart's operatic reception, treats this Italian negotiation as the clearest case of influence operating under declared hostility: a tradition announces that it wants none of the foreign model and then adopts the model's load-bearing structures while rejecting its accent. The pattern recurs so often in this history that it deserves a name, and this article calls it structural adoption, to be distinguished from the surface imitation that almost never happens.

Rossini also stands at the head of a second Italian story, the preservation story. Retiring early and settling in Paris, he became a living link to a repertory that younger French and Italian musicians encountered through his conversation and his salon performances, and his advocacy carried weight because it came from the most commercially successful opera composer of the age, a man with nothing to gain from praising a predecessor. When such a figure treats a body of work as the permanent standard of the craft, students listen in a way they do not listen to scholars. The Italian reception therefore ends in a paradox that the rest of this article keeps meeting: the country that resisted Mozart's accent most articulately did some of the deepest structural borrowing from him.

## The romantic generation's version of Mozart

Influence and reverence are different relationships, and the nineteenth century mostly offered the second. That claim, the second rung of the ladder this article is climbing, belongs here at the opening of the romantic story because everything the romantics wrote about Mozart tests it. The generation that came of age between 1810 and 1840 inherited a composer already converted into an emblem. E. T. A. Hoffmann, writing on Don Giovanni in 1813, read the opera as a window into a spirit world and made its composer the prophet of a romanticism he never lived to see, a reading of genius in which the music's meaning lay precisely in what it suggested beyond itself. Soren Kierkegaard, in the first volume of Either/Or in 1843, placed Don Giovanni at the summit of all art as the perfect union of idea and medium. Alexander Pushkin, in his short tragedy of 1830, fixed the Salieri legend in literature a century and a half before the stage play and film that spread it worldwide.

Notice what none of these tributes contains: a technique. Hoffmann does not analyse a modulation, Kierkegaard does not discuss a finale, and Pushkin is interested in envy, not in counterpoint. The romantic Mozart was a figure to think with, a standard of spontaneity against which the laboured modern artist could be measured and found wanting, and the music that embodied the standard was increasingly the music of the operas and the G minor Symphony rather than the full range of the output. Reverence selected its objects. It preferred the works that could be read as demonic, melancholic or divinely effortless, and it left the church music, the occasional pieces and most of the chamber music in a shadow from which the complete editions only later retrieved them.

The practical musicians of the generation split along the same line. Chopin revered Mozart with a consistency documented across his letters and his teaching, kept the operas as his measure of singing line and advised pupils toward that clarity of touch, yet his own piano writing, in its harmony, its rubato and its construction from dance and nocturne models, owes its audible debts elsewhere, to Hummel's concertos, to John Field's nocturnes and to the bel canto stage. Schumann wrote about Mozart as a critic with genuine penetration and founded much of his critical campaign on classical standards of form, while his own music pursued a confessional, fragmentary aesthetic that the classical model could bless but could not generate. The pattern is stable enough to state as a rule for this period: the romantics knelt at the altar and then went home and wrote in other languages.

There is a reason the rule held. Romantic composition defined itself through expansion, of harmony toward chromatic saturation, of form toward the single sweeping span, of the orchestra toward mass and colour, and of subject matter toward the confessional and the sublime. Mozart's art offered almost nothing that could be expanded in those directions without ceasing to be itself, because its effects depend on proportion, transparency and the exact placing of limited means. A composer could worship that art or could practise the romantic art. Doing both in the same piece proved, for most of the century, nearly impossible, and the few who managed a genuine synthesis did so late and at the margins rather than at the centre of the romantic project.

## Schubert, Mendelssohn and the formal inheritance

Where the romantic mainstream revered, two composers close to the century's opening absorbed, and their cases show what formal inheritance, as distinct from worship, actually looks like. Franz Schubert heard Mozart's music in Salieri's Vienna as a matter of daily professional life, and his diary for June 1816 preserves a response to a performance of the Clarinet Quintet in which he wrote of the magic of those sounds staying with the listener, a private entry that records an experience rather than a programme. What Schubert took was not a surface. It was a permission and a set of procedures: the permission to let a lyrical theme, complete in itself, carry the weight of a large movement, and the procedures of slipping between major and minor within a theme, of expanding a development by sequence and songlike variation rather than by Beethovenian fragmentation, and of writing chamber music for winds and strings in blended, vocal colours that descend directly from the clarinet works of Mozart's last years.

Schubert's debt is clearest exactly where he is most himself. The great string quintet and the late piano sonatas build spans out of melody in a way that Beethoven's example forbids and Mozart's allows, because in the Mozart concerto and quintet a beautiful theme is permitted to be the argument rather than merely its raw material. Scholars differ on how much of this came through Salieri's classroom teaching of Italianate lyric principles and how much from score study, and the honest position is that both channels ran together in a city saturated with performances. The influence claim survives the dispute because the procedures match and the access is documented, even where the share belonging to each channel cannot be separated.

Felix Mendelssohn inherited Mozart institutionally as much as musically. Raised in a Berlin household that treated the classical repertory as a family religion, trained by Carl Friedrich Zelter in a counterpoint discipline descending through the Bach tradition, and exhibited as a prodigy in terms that invited comparison with the most famous prodigy of all, he grew up inside a Mozart cult administered by his elders. His own practice, conducting and performing the piano concertos, programming the symphonies with the Gewandhaus Orchestra in Leipzig and writing string quartets that argue openly with the classical masters, made him the century's most effective professional advocate among composers. What Mendelssohn took technically was a standard of finish: the featherweight scherzo, learned partly from Shakespeare and partly from the finales of Mozart's operas, the orchestral transparency that keeps winds audible inside a full texture, and an insistence that virtuosity never be allowed to thicken the sound.

Between them, Schubert and Mendelssohn define the two honest modes of romantic-era inheritance. One absorbed procedures into a music that sounds nothing like its source. The other maintained a performing and institutional tradition that kept the source in circulation while writing, increasingly, a music of his own. Neither sounded like Mozart for longer than a phrase. The question left open in section two can now be read in its proper light, with the closing reserved for the next but one section: sounding like a predecessor is the rarest and usually the least valuable form of influence, and its absence is weak evidence about the presence of every other form.

## Tchaikovsky's attachment and what it produced

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky's attachment to Mozart was the most emotionally explicit declared by any major composer of the nineteenth century, and it produced the century's most literal acts of homage. In his diary for 1886 he described encountering Don Giovanni in terms of religious conversion, recording that through that music he had found what he called the highest point of beauty in art, and elsewhere he ranked Mozart with a vocabulary of devotion he applied to no other predecessor, calling him a musical Christ in a diary entry that later editors of the letters discussed with some embarrassment. The vocabulary matters less than the behaviour it licensed. Tchaikovsky, alone among the century's symphonists, repeatedly stopped his own work to arrange, quote and reframe Mozart's.

The principal product is the Orchestral Suite No. 4 of 1887, titled Mozartiana, which orchestrates four Mozart piano pieces, including the Gigue K. 574 and the Ave verum corpus in an arrangement that passes through Liszt's piano transcription, and presents them as a suite for the modern concert hall. The act is usually filed as homage, and it is, but its structure deserves a colder description. A leading Russian symphonist, at the height of his powers, spent orchestral capital making salon and sacred miniatures by a predecessor audible to an audience that would not otherwise have met them, which is an act of transmission disguised as an act of worship. Tchaikovsky in those years was also arguing, in letters to his patron Nadezhda von Meck, that the modern orchestra had lost a clarity he located in the eighteenth century, and Mozartiana is that argument staged in sound.

The attachment also shaped original work in identifiable places. The Variations on a Rococo Theme for cello, from 1876, adopt an eighteenth century theme and a discipline of ornament and proportion that Tchaikovsky explicitly associated with the earlier age, and the opera The Queen of Spades, completed in 1890, builds its ballroom act around a pastoral interlude in period style that quotes a melody by Gretry inside a frame of deliberate Mozartean artifice, using the older manner to signify a lost social world. Here the influence has become semantic: Mozart's procedures no longer merely organise the music, they mean something inside the drama, innocence and artifice and a vanished order, which is a stage of influence the younger romantics never reached because they were too busy revering the man to use the language.

Against this, the limits should be recorded with equal plainness. Tchaikovsky's symphonies, the works on which his standing rests, proceed from Beethoven, Schumann and the French grand tradition, not from Mozart, and his harmony, his orchestral mass and his rhetoric of confession belong to his own century. The attachment was real, productive and specific, and it touched the centre of his output hardly at all. That combination, maximal declared love with bounded technical consequence, makes him the nineteenth century's most instructive case, the one that proves declared devotion and compositional influence are independent variables that must be measured separately.

## The nineteenth century as reverence rather than influence

The evidence can now be drawn together, and the question carried since section two can be closed. If he was so influential, why do so few composers sound like him? Because the nineteenth century's dominant relationship to Mozart was reverence, and reverence, unlike influence, does not require a composer to change a single note of his own practice. Reverence builds monuments, names children, edits works and writes essays. Influence changes procedures. The century did the first with unmatched energy and the second only in pockets, in Schubert's lyric procedures, in Mendelssohn's finish, in Rossini's finale engineering and in Tchaikovsky's acts of transmission, while its central projects, the Beethovenian symphony, the Wagnerian music drama, the Lisztian symphonic poem and the national schools, all defined themselves against the classical balance that Mozart was taken to represent.

Three mechanisms kept reverence and influence apart. The first was the model of Beethoven, whose career offered the century something Mozart's could not, a narrative of heroic self-overcoming that matched its own idea of the artist, so that ambitious composers measured themselves against the later master even when they admired the earlier one more. The second was scale: the growth of orchestras, halls and the publishing market rewarded weight and colour, and the Mozartean virtues of transparency and economy read, to ears retrained by Berlioz and Wagner, as slightness. The third was the image itself. A composer presented as an effortless divine child, pouring out finished perfection, offers nothing a working musician can imitate, because there is no visible method to copy, and the romantic Mozart myth, by concealing the labour documented in the sketches and letters, actively discouraged the close technical study that influence requires.

The following table sets the pattern out by period, naming who engaged with the music, what they took where taking occurred, what they passed over, and whether the relationship is honestly described as influence or as reverence.

| Period | Who was engaging with the music | What they took | What they ignored | Influence or reverence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1791 to 1810 | Beethoven, Hummel, Attwood and the Viennese pupils | Concerto drama, quartet equality of voices, finale construction, training sequence | The galant surface and the patronage economy | Influence |
| 1810 to 1840 | Hoffmann, Kierkegaard, Pushkin and the romantic critics | An emblem of spontaneity and a subject for literature | Technique, church music and most chamber music | Reverence |
| 1820 to 1850 | Schubert, Mendelssohn and Rossini | Lyric procedures, orchestral finish and finale engineering | The classical scale and the rhetoric of balance | Influence |
| 1850 to 1900 | Tchaikovsky, Brahms and the editors | Acts of arrangement, autograph collecting and complete edition scholarship | Any change to their own symphonic practice | Reverence with transmission |
| 1900 to 1950 | Mahler, Strauss and the early recording conductors | Opera conducting tradition and neoclassical models | Romantic orchestral mass in the Mozart works themselves | Influence |

Johannes Brahms illustrates the table's fourth row in a single career. He collected Mozart autographs with a scholar's passion, owned the manuscript of the G minor Symphony, conducted the music with devotion and wrote variations in a classical discipline he acknowledged, yet his symphonies answer Beethoven's challenge and his chamber music pursues a density of motivic work that is his own invention. Gustav Mahler, in the following generation, conducted the operas with a reformer's zeal in Vienna, cutting and reordering in the name of dramatic truth, which is influence exerted on performance rather than on composition. The century's verdict, stated without romance, is that Mozart became the musician's musician, the standard inside the profession, while the profession's public art developed along lines he had not drawn. Standards and sources are different things, and the confusion of the two is the master error this article exists to correct.

## The Kochel catalogue and what cataloguing did

### What is the Kochel catalogue?

The Kochel catalogue is the chronological thematic index of Mozart's works compiled by the Austrian naturalist Ludwig von Kochel and published in 1862, which assigned the K. numbers, established a defensible order of composition and gave scholars, publishers and performers a common language for the output.

Cataloguing and complete editions did more for his standing than any composer's advocacy. That is the third rung of this article's ladder, and it sounds like an exaggeration until the state of the output before 1862 is described. Seventy years after the composer's death, his works circulated under a confusion of publishers' numbers, opus designations borrowed from other catalogues, wrong attributions and undated manuscripts. A symphony might be known by the name of the publisher who issued it, a quartet by a number that another publisher used for a different work, and whole categories, the dances, the church music, the fragments, had no public existence at all. Advocacy under those conditions is a sermon about a country the congregation cannot visit. Kochel, a botanist and mineralogist by training who applied the habits of taxonomy to music, spent years examining autographs, watermarks, copyists' hands and paper types, and produced an index in which every known work had a number, a date argued from evidence, an incipit by which it could be recognised and a record of its sources.

The consequences were structural. A numbered output could be counted, and counting changed the argument: the figure of more than six hundred works, endlessly repeated since, is a Kochel artefact, impossible to state before the catalogue existed. A chronological output could be narrated, so the story of early, middle and late periods, with the last years crowned as a summit, acquired the scaffolding on which biography and criticism have hung ever since. And a catalogued output could be completed, in both senses: editors could see what was missing, hunt for it, and define what a complete edition would have to contain. Cliff Eisen's work on the documents and on the early catalogues shows how much of the modern Mozart, the Mozart of totality, was manufactured by this bibliographic machinery rather than discovered by listeners.

The catalogue was also a living instrument, revised as evidence changed. Alfred Einstein, the German musicologist and a cousin of the physicist, produced the third edition in 1937, redating many works in the light of paper studies and moving numbers wholesale, so that scholars cite both the original K. number and Einstein's revision where they differ. A sixth edition, prepared by Franz Giegling, Alexander Weinmann and Gerd Sievers and published in 1964, carried the revision further. Each edition silently rewrote the chronology on which interpretations depended, which is why the catalogue is best understood not as a list but as an institution, a standing committee on the shape of the life's work, meeting every few decades to rearrange the evidence.

For a researcher assembling material of this kind across hundreds of documents, the practical problem is custody: notes, source references and draft arguments have to survive for years and stay private while they accumulate, which is precisely the use served by the offline encrypted notes app obtained from [the VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/), where a scholar's working papers remain on a local machine rather than on a shared service. The point of mentioning the tool inside a discussion of Kochel is the parallel of method. Kochel's revolution was a filing system. Filing systems, maintained honestly over decades, are what turn admiration into knowledge, and the catalogue's dry columns did more for the standing of the music than every rhapsody the romantics wrote.

## The complete editions and the authoritative text

A catalogue tells the world what exists. An edition tells performers what to play, and the two complete editions of Mozart mark the difference between a reputation and a repertory. The first, published by Breitkopf and Hartel in Leipzig between 1877 and 1883 with supplements following to 1905, was edited by a generation of German scholars including Johannes Brahms among the advisers on parts of the series, and it placed the whole output, as then known, into uniform volumes that libraries and conservatories could buy. Works that had existed in single manuscripts became available in print on two continents. The church music, the serenades, the unfinished fragments and the juvenilia entered the public stock, and the image of the composer widened from the author of a dozen famous pieces to the author of a world.

The edition also carried the assumptions of its age in its texts. Nineteenth century editors corrected what they took to be errors, filled gaps without always marking the join, and presented a smoothed Mozart whose notation had been partly rewritten to match later practice. Performers who learned the music from those volumes were learning, in places, an editorial idea of the music, and the scholarly reaction against that smoothing produced the second complete edition, the Neue Mozart-Ausgabe, published by Barenreiter in Kassel from 1955 onward under the auspices of the International Mozarteum Foundation in Salzburg. Its editors returned to the autographs and earliest sources, printed variant readings in critical reports and left fragments visibly fragmentary, on the principle that a scholarly text shows its evidence. The main series of volumes was substantially complete by the early 1990s, and in 2006 the edition was placed online without charge, an act that moved the authoritative text from the reference library to any desk with a connection.

Nicholas Kenyon, the critic and broadcaster who served as controller of BBC Radio 3 and of the Proms and who wrote a widely used pocket guide to Mozart in 2005, has argued in his criticism that this editorial history is the hidden biography of the reputation: each generation in fact plays a different text while believing it plays the same notes. Kenyon's characterisation is held consistently in this article, as the critic who insists that mediation, editors, broadcasters and institutions, is part of the music's history rather than a transparent window onto it. The point bites hardest on famous cases. The Requiem exists in completions by Sussmayr and by later scholars, the Coronation Mass and the clarinet works survive with textual questions that editions answer differently, and in each case the edition, not the long dead composer, decides what an audience hears on a given night.

The authoritative text also settled arguments that advocacy never could. Dating disputes that had supported whole theories of decline or of final transcendence collapsed when paper studies redated the works concerned. Attributions that had padded the output with attractive strangers were pruned, and a smaller number of doubtful works were quarantined in an appendix of the catalogue where performers could find them without mistaking their status. An influence built on an inflated and misdated output would have been an influence of legend. The editions, by making the output smaller in places and better ordered everywhere, gave later claims about transmission something solid to stand on, which is why this article treats bibliography as a chapter of influence rather than as housekeeping.

## Performance practice and the changing orchestra

How Mozart sounded changed more across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries than what was played, and performance practice is the channel through which most listeners, in every period, actually received the influence. The orchestra of the 1780s in Vienna numbered perhaps thirty five players for a public concert, with winds in pairs, natural trumpets and horns, timpani and a string body balanced so that a fortepiano could converse with it. The nineteenth century enlarged every element of that picture. String sections doubled and doubled again, the piano grew an iron frame and a tone built to fill large halls, conductors adopted slower tempi for the famous symphonies and applied the expressive habits of Wagnerian performance, continuous vibrato, swelling dynamics and rhetorical pauses, to music written for a cleaner rhetoric. By 1900 the G minor Symphony in a great hall under a celebrated conductor was, in weight and colour, a romantic work wearing classical notation.

That transformation had a cost that musicians themselves named. Composers and critics of the early twentieth century who returned to the sources, among them Richard Strauss in his edition and performances and Ferruccio Busoni in his essays, complained that the music had been inflated into false grandeur and, in reaction, began stripping it back. The recording era accelerated both tendencies at once. Early recordings by conductors such as Thomas Beecham presented a genial, full toned Mozart for the gramophone public, while the chamber scale performances associated with the Busch Quartet and with festival ensembles between the wars demonstrated how much contrapuntal life returned when the texture thinned. A listener in 1935 could therefore hold two incompatible Mozarts on shellac, and the choice between them was an aesthetic position, not a detail of taste.

Mahler's Viennese opera performances around 1900 belong in this history as practice rather than composition. As director of the Court Opera he staged the operas as continuous drama, reordering numbers, cutting in the name of pace and seating the orchestra to serve the stage, and his successors inherited a performing tradition in which the operas were living theatre rather than museum ritual. Conductors of the next generation divided over that inheritance, some restoring what he had cut, others keeping his dramatic priorities with cleaner texts, but the argument itself kept the works at the centre of operatic life in a way that silent score study never does. Performance traditions are arguments conducted in sound, and Mozart's music survived the century of its inflation partly because each generation found the argument worth continuing.

The changing orchestra also altered which works could act as influence. A young composer learns from what he hears in the hall, and the hall, for most of the period covered here, offered a Mozart of symphonies and concertos played by forces twice the intended size, in which the wind writing that composers most needed to study was buried inside a string cushion. The recovery of the original balance, described in the next section, is therefore not a footnote about taste. It is the moment when the music's actual procedures, the conversational winds, the transparent bass lines, the chamber scale argument, became audible again to the people best placed to borrow them.

## The twentieth century return to smaller forces

The return began as scholarship and ended as sound. From the 1950s, ensembles devoted to historical performance rebuilt the conditions of eighteenth century music making: Nikolaus Harnoncourt founded the Concentus Musicus Wien in 1953 to play on original instruments, Christopher Hogwood's Academy of Ancient Music, founded in 1973, recorded the complete symphonies with period forces in a cycle begun at the end of the 1970s, and Trevor Pinnock and John Eliot Gardiner carried the approach to the concertos and operas with their own ensembles. Gut strings, natural brass, woodwind built to eighteenth century patterns and fortepianos restored or copied from Viennese makers changed the recorded image of the repertory within a single generation of listeners.

What the smaller forces revealed was mechanism. At period scale, the wind parts in the late symphonies stop behaving like colour and start behaving like argument, answering the strings phrase for phrase. The piano concertos recover their character as chamber music with an orchestra attached, the soloist weaving inside the texture rather than projecting across it. Tempi quicken because the lighter articulation permits it, dance movements recover their metre, and the famous clarity that the romantics praised as a moral quality turns out to be, in large part, an acoustic fact about numbers of players and the instruments they held. Composers and conductors who had grown up on the inflated version described the period performances, in interviews of the period, as hearing the works for the first time, and the phrase, repeated often enough to become a cliche of the movement, records a genuine transfer of knowledge.

Opposition was real and should be recorded in the movement's own terms. Critics charged the period ensembles with thin tone, with an antiquarian refusal of expression and with mistaking evidence about instruments for evidence about feeling, and some mainstream conductors answered by reducing their own forces and sharpening articulation while keeping modern instruments, a compromise that by the 1990s had reset the default sound of the repertory in most major halls. That compromise is the real measure of the return's success: the historical movement did not need to win the argument about pitch and gut in order to change how every orchestra on earth played this music. It changed the baseline, and baselines are influence in its least visible and most durable form.

For the argument of this article the episode carries a warning and a confirmation. The warning is that influence claims must specify a version: the Mozart who acted on a composer in 1890 was a heavily mediated, large scale figure, and the Mozart who acted on a composer in 1990 was a different sounding composer with the same name. The confirmation is that the music survived its mediation, which not all repertory does. When the inflation was stripped away the procedures were still there, exact and teachable, waiting inside the smaller sound.

## Neoclassicism and the use of Mozart as a model

Between the wars, Mozart became the only major predecessor whom avant garde composers could use as a model without being accused of nostalgia, and neoclassicism is the name of the movement that cashed that permission. The logic was polemical. Against the late romantic orchestra and its rhetoric of overwhelming feeling, the younger generation after 1918 proposed economy, clarity, dance rhythms and borrowed eighteenth century forms, and it needed ancestors. Igor Stravinsky built The Rake's Progress, completed in 1951, as an opera in deliberate dialogue with the Mozart stage, with secco recitative accompanied by harpsichord, set arias, ensembles and a moral epilogue addressed to the audience, a structure he and his librettists, W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman, took from the example of Cosi fan tutte and Don Giovanni as filtered through a modern harmonic accent. The borrowing is documented in the work's design and in Stravinsky's own statements about the operas of Mozart as his model for the piece, and it is influence of the purest kind this article recognises, access, specificity and consequence all present.

Sergei Prokofiev's Classical Symphony of 1917 points at Haydn more than at Mozart, and this article says so plainly because the distinction is exactly the kind that influence writing usually blurs: a classical model is not automatically a Mozart model, and honesty about the Prokofiev case strengthens the Stravinsky one. Richard Strauss presents the reverse configuration. His opera Der Rosenkavalier of 1911 builds its Viennese world in homage to the Mozart of The Marriage of Figaro, and Ariadne auf Naxos, in its revised form of 1916, sets a commedia troupe inside a classical frame with an orchestral transparency learned from the older master, while his harmony and his orchestral virtuosity remain unmistakably his own. Max Reger's Variations and Fugue on a Theme by Mozart of 1914 take the theme of the A major Piano Sonata, K. 331, the same theme Tchaikovsky's generation had loved, and subject it to a German contrapuntal apparatus that Mozart never used, a homage that demonstrates its distance in every bar.

What neoclassicism took, across these cases, was not melody and not form in the abstract. It took a licence: the licence to be brief, to be witty in serious genres, to let a small orchestra carry a large argument, and to treat the eighteenth century as usable material rather than as a shrine. That licence had to come from a figure whose standing was beyond dispute, and only Mozart, among the classical masters, combined the standing with the required lightness of reputation. Beethoven could not license wit. Bach could not license the theatre. The model function was therefore Mozart's by elimination, and the movement's existence is evidence that by 1920 his influence had changed category, from a set of procedures borrowed by individuals to a position in the culture that other composers could occupy, cite and argue from.

## Mozart in the conservatory curriculum

### Why do music students study Mozart?

Music students study Mozart because the sonatas, concertos and arias expose faults of rhythm, tone, balance and phrasing that heavier repertory conceals, because the output covers every level from early exercises to professional tests, and because examiners across countries share the repertory as a common standard.

The curriculum is where influence stops being a story about great names and becomes a daily mechanism acting on thousands of musicians who will never be famous. Piano syllabuses across Europe and North America place the easier sonatas, above all the C major Sonata, K. 545, composed in 1788 and described by the composer himself in his own catalogue as a small sonata for beginners, at the intermediate grades where a student first meets a complete classical movement. The choice is not sentimental. That sonata's Alberti figuration lies under the left hand in patterns that expose uneven fingers instantly, its phrases fall into balanced periods that punish rhythmic drift, and there is nowhere in its texture for a weak player to hide, so teachers reach for it the way mathematics teachers reach for a clean proof, as an instrument for making errors visible.

Orchestral training uses the repertory the same way at a higher level. The Clarinet Concerto became the standard audition piece for its instrument across the profession, and the horn concertos serve the same function for horn players, not because committees conspired to enthrone a composer but because the works test intonation, breath, articulation and musicality inside three minutes of exposed writing. Conservatory entrance requirements and orchestral audition lists, documents that can be compared across decades, show the same excerpts recurring in country after country, which makes Mozart the closest thing classical training possesses to an international examination. The [study of music education and the prodigy myth](/2026/07/07/music-education-and-the-prodigy-myth/) examines how institutions build such standards around exemplary figures; the point here is narrower, that the standard, once built, trains the profession's ears toward one composer's procedures as the definition of correctness.

Singers meet the repertory as a first professional language, and section nineteen takes that case in detail. Composers meet it in the theory classroom, the subject of section twenty. What unifies the curriculum is suitability, and the next but one section states the ladder's fourth rung in full: dominance of the teaching repertory is partly a fact about the music's fitness for training purposes, its graded difficulty, its transparent textures and its complete coverage of genres, rather than a simple referendum on quality. A slightly less suitable output of equal quality would occupy less of the syllabus, and a slightly less distinguished output of equal suitability might occupy nearly as much. The curriculum therefore measures something real about the music, but what it measures is teachability braided together with merit, and honest influence accounting keeps the two strands separate.

There is also a feedback loop, and it is the strongest in this article. Students trained on the repertory become the teachers, examiners, orchestral players and conductors who select the repertory, and their judgement of what sounds correct was formed by the very works they now prescribe. Within two generations the standard no longer needs defenders, because it has become the medium of judgement itself. That is institutional influence at full strength, and it explains why the curriculum held its shape through periods, the high romantic decades among them, when the concert hall's relationship to the same music was comparatively distant.

## Why pianists and singers train on this repertoire

His dominance of the teaching curriculum is partly a fact about suitability for training, not only about quality. The fourth rung of the ladder was announced in the previous section; this section carries its evidence, instrument by instrument and voice by voice. Begin with the piano. A student who plays romantic repertory badly can disguise the failure inside pedal resonance and mass of tone. A student who plays a Mozart sonata movement badly has nowhere to put the evidence: the textures are two or three voices deep, the ornaments must land inside the beat, the repeats expose whether the phrasing was a decision or an accident, and the dynamic range of the Viennese writing punishes banging more severely than it rewards brilliance. Teachers across two centuries have described the same experience in their memoirs and method books, the experience of hearing a pupil's real standard within eight bars, and a repertory that functions as an X ray will be prescribed by any profession that has to grade its young.

The voice makes the case even more strongly. Mozart's arias and ensembles demand a legato line carried on steady breath, clean coloratura without aspirated gaps, Italian diction projected at conversational intensity and the ability to act inside an ensemble where the tempo will not wait for the singer. Voice teachers assign Susanna, Zerlina, Donna Elvira, Tamino and Papageno to students in their late teens because the writing sits in the middle of the voice, builds technique without forcing weight, and teaches ensemble listening years before a young singer will be trusted with heavier repertory. Julian Rushton, whose operatic scholarship this article follows for the stage works, notes that the roles function as a graded course in themselves, from the page's simplicity to the Countess's long breathed paragraphs of tone, a syllabus the profession did not design but recognised and adopted because it worked.

Instrumentalists beyond the pianists confirm the pattern from their own studios. Violinists meet the concertos as the test of an unforced, singing bow arm. Flutists and oboists take the concertos and quartets as their standard of classical phrasing. In each case the stated reason in method books and examination reports is identical in substance: the writing is transparent, the faults are audible and the musical rewards arrive early enough to keep a student working. Quality is a precondition, since badly written music cannot reward study at any level, but the selection among good music is made on pedagogical fitness, and any account that reads syllabus dominance as a pure quality ranking mistakes how professions actually choose their tools.

One more consequence follows, and it closes the section. A training repertory shapes the taste of the trained. Singers who learned line from these arias carry that standard into Puccini and into new music. Pianists who learned touch from these sonatas hear thicker repertory with ears calibrated by transparency. The influence therefore travels forward inside bodies and habits, in breath and finger, long after the student has stopped naming the source, which is why the conservatory may be the most powerful single channel in this entire history, ahead of the concert hall and the publishing house.

## Mozart in music theory teaching

Theory teaching preserves a composer differently from performance teaching: it preserves him as grammar. In the harmony and analysis classrooms of Europe and North America, the phrase structures of Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven became the examples from which the rules of the classical sentence and period were drawn, and within that trio Mozart's music supplies a disproportionate share of the textbook illustrations, because his phrases balance with a regularity that lets a teacher show the rule before showing the exceptions. Heinrich Schenker built analyses of the piano sonatas into his theory of structural levels. Charles Rosen's account of the classical style, which this article has followed as its characterisation of the shared language, gave a generation of students the analytical vocabulary, sonata form as dramatic opposition, the concerto as ritualised contest, with which the repertory is still described in seminar rooms.

The sonata form diagram itself, the exposition, development and recapitulation scheme printed in every textbook, was reverse engineered largely from this repertory during the nineteenth century, by theorists such as A. B. Marx who needed models of the form at its most intelligible. Mozart's first movements, in the G minor Symphony, the C major String Quintet and the piano sonatas, became the normative instances against which Beethoven's expansions and Schubert's lyric alternatives are defined as departures. A student therefore learns the rule as Mozart and the licensed exceptions as everybody else, a distribution of roles with obvious consequences for how the history gets told: the rule maker appears classical and finished, the exception makers appear romantic and striving, and a pedagogical convenience hardens into a story about the century.

Counterpoint and orchestration teaching use the music in the same normative way. The string quartets and the Jupiter Symphony's finale serve as the standard demonstration that learned counterpoint can operate inside a public, pleasurable surface, and orchestration treatises excerpt the slow movement of the Clarinet Concerto and the wind serenades to show how two or three instruments can be blended without doubling into thickness. These examples persist across editions of textbooks whose other contents change completely, because each new author inherits the examples that worked for his own teachers. Theory's Mozart is therefore the most conservative of all the versions in this article, altered least by fashion, and in some ways the most influential, since it defines the terms in which students first learn to hear any music at all.

The limits deserve one paragraph. A grammar drawn from one repertory judges other repertories as deviations, and musicians formed in that classroom have historically undervalued music, modal, folk derived or built on ostinato, whose logic the classical period does not describe. The theory curriculum's Mozart is thus both a gift and a filter, and the scholars who revised textbooks in the closing decades of the twentieth century, broadening the examples while keeping the classical core, were negotiating exactly that double character.

## The opera house repertory and its economics

Opera houses are businesses with stages, and the repertory that survives in them survives partly on arithmetic. The three Da Ponte operas, The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni and Cosi fan tutte, together with The Magic Flute and, a step behind, Idomeneo, The Abduction from the Seraglio and La clemenza di Tito, occupy a position in the international repertory that statistics of performances collected by industry bodies in the decades before 2010 consistently placed at or near the top of the composer table, rivalled season by season by Verdi and Puccini. A Mozart title could be cast from a house ensemble without guest stars, rehearsed quickly because every singer arrived knowing the idiom, marketed to subscribers as an obligation of taste and revived for decades in a successful production. For an intendent balancing a season, those properties are not decoration on the music's merits. They are the mechanism by which merit reaches the stage at all.

The economics cut deeper than programming convenience. A house that performs Figaro trains its own company in the process: young singers take the small roles, graduate to the leads, coaches and conductors learn the house style in the pit, and the production becomes an asset that can be lent, revived and exported. Mozart functioned in the twentieth century opera economy as the repertory's training currency, the works through which institutions reproduced their own competence, which is the operatic form of the conservatory argument made two sections back. Houses that neglected the repertory found the loss showing up in their Verdi and their Wagner, in ensemble precision and in the pit's ability to play quietly, and artistic directors said so in interviews with a bluntness that suggests the lesson was learned expensively.

There is a question standing behind this section that the article now opens and will not close until section thirty three, near the end. What did his fame do to the musicians working beside him? An opera economy organised around a fixed canon of dead masters allocates its stages, its budgets and its rehearsal weeks among a small company of names, and every week given to a revival of Figaro is a week not given to a living composer or to a forgotten contemporary whose works once shared the stage with it. The contemporary in question here is not an abstraction: the repertory Mozart displaced belonged to named men whose operas Vienna applauded, and section thirty three names them and counts the cost. Until then the question stays open, as the economics get worse before they get clearer.

The repertory position also protected itself through recording and broadcast. Complete opera recordings from the 1950s onward standardised a Mozart canon for listeners who never entered an opera house, radio relays made festival performances national events, and the arrival of surtitles in the 1980s removed the language barrier that had kept the comedies a connoisseur's property in English speaking countries. Each technology widened the audience for the same small set of titles, deepening the groove the economics had already cut. Influence measured in performances is therefore partly influence measured in infrastructure, and the distinction, by now familiar in this article, applies to the opera house exactly as it applied to the catalogue.

## Salzburg, tourism and the commercial afterlife

Salzburg built the most complete commercial afterlife of any composer in this series, and it did so on the basis of a biographical accident: Mozart was born in the city in 1756, left it as soon as he could, quarrelled with its archbishop, his employer, and settled in Vienna, where the whole of his mature career unfolded. The city he escaped became the city that owns him. The Mozarteum Foundation, established in its international form in 1880, opened the birthplace in the Getreidegasse as a museum in the same period, and the dwelling where the family later lived became a second shrine. The Salzburg Festival, founded in 1920 by Hugo von Hofmannsthal, Max Reinhardt and Richard Strauss, placed Mozart at the centre of a summer economy of performances that grew, across the twentieth century, into one of the most valuable cultural brands in Europe, and the Mozart Week, founded in 1956 around the birthday in January, extended the season into the winter.

The commerce radiated outward from the institutions into the street. The Mozartkugel, a confection of marzipan and nougat created by the Salzburg confectioner Paul Furst in 1890, became an industry in itself, manufactured by rival firms whose trademark disputes ran for decades, exported worldwide and sold to visitors who might never attend a concert. The composer's face, taken from portraits of varying authenticity, appeared on chocolate, liqueurs, candles and hotel stationery. Economists of culture who studied the city in the 1990s treated it as the textbook case of a single name carrying a regional tourist economy, with the festival's ticket scarcity at the luxury end and the chocolate at the popular end drawing on the same stock of recognition.

What Salzburg sold, precisely, was not the music. Concert tickets were the smallest part of the trade by volume. The city sold proximity to a story, the story of the miraculous child and the unappreciated master, in the streets where the first half of the story happened, and the trade therefore depended on the simplified version of the life that the romantic century had manufactured. Scholars attached to the Mozarteum spent their careers correcting that version in the documents while the correction itself became one more attraction, the authentic manuscripts exhibited a floor away from the gift shop. The tension is not hypocrisy. It is the normal condition of a cultural economy, in which rigour and merchandising fund each other and neither could survive alone at that scale.

For the measurement of influence, Salzburg belongs firmly in the footprint column, and the article insists on the classification against a common error. A million visitors to a birthplace tell us that a name can organise travel, employment and civic identity two centuries after a death. They tell us nothing, by themselves, about whether a single bar of music written in that city changed what another composer wrote. Confusing the two measurements is the commercial version of confusing reverence with influence, and it is the version with the largest numbers attached, which is exactly why it persuades so many people.

## Film, advertising and the ambient Mozart

Film gave the twentieth century its Mozart, and the gift came with a plot attached. Peter Shaffer's play Amadeus, first staged in London in 1979, and Milos Forman's film of 1984, which won the Academy Award for best picture, organised the life as a confession by Salieri, the respectable court composer destroyed by the realisation that grace had been given to a giggling child rather than to his own diligent piety. The film's soundtrack carried the music into cinemas and then into record shops in arrangements of excerpts chosen for emotional legibility, the slow movement of the Clarinet Concerto, the Lacrimosa of the Requiem, the G minor Symphony's opening, and for a generation of listeners those excerpts, in that dramatic frame, simply were Mozart. Historians objected, accurately, that the Salieri of the record taught Mozart's pupils, conducted his works and could not be made to carry the murder legend that Pushkin had invented for other purposes, and the objections were absorbed without effect, because a film's version of a life, seen by tens of millions, outweighs a shelf of corrections.

The ambient uses went further and asked less. Bo Widerberg's film Elvira Madigan of 1967 attached the slow movement of the Piano Concerto No. 21 so firmly to its images of doomed summer love that the concerto acquired the film's title as a permanent nickname, used by record companies on reissues for decades. Advertisers discovered that a few bars of Eine kleine Nachtmusik or of the Turkish rondo from the A major Sonata could certify a product as tasteful, clever or luxurious inside two seconds, and the excerpt became a sonic logo, detached from any work, movement or composer intention. Film trailers reached for the Lacrimosa whenever a studio needed instant apocalypse. In each case the music's associations, clarity, order, European prestige, did the work, and the associations were themselves the product of two centuries of the footprint this article has been tracing.

Ambient presence is not influence on composers, and this article files it accordingly, but it has one feedback effect on the serious story that deserves recording. A culture saturated with excerpts produces listeners who meet the complete works already feeling that they know them, and performers and record companies plan seasons for that audience, favouring the excerpted works and starving the unexcerpted remainder. The film and advertising Mozart is therefore a selection mechanism acting on the repertory, narrowing the effective canon to perhaps twenty works while the editions make six hundred available. Nicholas Kenyon's insistence, noted earlier, that mediation is part of the history finds its clearest confirmation here: for most of the world, the mediator with the largest audience was not an editor or a scholar but a film director and an advertising industry that needed a shorthand for quality.

That shorthand had a final consequence the next sections take up. A name that functions as a certificate of quality can be borrowed by causes the music never touched, and the most successful borrower of all was a claim about infant intelligence. When the claim arrived in 1993, it did not need to argue that Mozart mattered. The ambient culture had already established that, in the minds of legislators and parents, and the claim simply redirected an existing reverence toward a new promise.

## The 1993 study and what it actually measured

### Does listening to Mozart make you smarter?

The 1993 study measured performance on one spatial reasoning task by thirty six college students, found a brief improvement after listening to a Mozart sonata, and made no claim about general intelligence, school achievement or the development of children of any age.

The Mozart effect originated in a 1993 study reporting a brief improvement on a spatial reasoning task. Later work did not support the broader claim that listening to Mozart raises general intelligence. That sentence is this article's shareable fact, stated in the section where the evidence begins, and the rest of the section earns it by describing the experiment with the same discipline applied to a disputed attribution earlier in this article. The shareable fact matters because a reader who carries away only one sentence from these pages should carry away one that is true at the size at which it is stated.

The paper appeared in the journal Nature in October 1993, as a short letter by Frances H. Rauscher, Gordon L. Shaw and Katherine N. Ky of the University of California, Irvine. Thirty six undergraduate students completed spatial reasoning items drawn from the Stanford Binet intelligence test, specifically items of the paper folding and cutting type, in which a folded and cut sheet must be imagined unfolded. Each student was tested after three conditions on different occasions: ten minutes of listening to Mozart's Sonata for Two Pianos in D major, K. 448, ten minutes of a relaxation recording, and ten minutes of silence. Scores after the music condition were higher, by an amount the authors expressed as equivalent to eight or nine points on the spatial subscale, and the advantage had faded when students were retested after intervals of roughly ten to fifteen minutes. The authors proposed, cautiously and in the language of neuroscience, that music and spatial reasoning might share neural firing patterns, and they claimed nothing about intelligence in general, nothing about children and nothing about lasting benefit.

Three features of the design deserve emphasis because each became a casualty of the retelling. The subjects were adults, university students, not infants. The outcome was a single subtest of a single kind, spatial temporal reasoning, not a full intelligence measure. And the effect, in the original data, was temporary on a scale of minutes, a fact printed in the paper and absent from almost every popular account that followed. An article in this series handles a scientific claim the way it handles a claim about Haydn's opinion: by returning to the document, stating its provenance, its scope and its limits, and refusing to let the later legend grade the earlier evidence.

A second loop opens here and closes two sections on, after the expansion of the claim has been described. Did listening to Mozart ever make anyone smarter? The replication record answers it, and the answer is narrower than either the enthusiasts or the scoffers of the 1990s wanted, because the honest finding involves arousal, mood and task specificity rather than either miracle or fraud. The researchers, it should be said at once and repeated when the record is examined, behaved properly. Rauscher and her colleagues published a bounded result, proposed a mechanism as a hypothesis and spent years afterwards correcting the inflated version in public, a pattern familiar from the Salieri case, where the person attached to a legend turns out to have been more careful than the legend required.

## How the finding became a parenting claim

The expansion from laboratory letter to parenting doctrine took about five years and can be traced through identifiable carriers, none of whom needed to invent anything. Popular science reporting first converted the spatial subtest into intelligence, a substitution that fits a headline and survives a news cycle. Don Campbell, a music educator, then built the conversion into a book published in 1997 under a trademarked title, The Mozart Effect, which gathered the Irvine result together with a wide range of claims about music, healing and learning, and sold the package to parents as a programme. Trademarking the phrase mattered commercially and epistemically at once: a laboratory finding cannot be owned, but a brand can, and once the finding had become a brand its value depended on the claim staying large.

Politics completed the enlargement. In January 1998 Zell Miller, the governor of Georgia, played a passage of Beethoven to his legislature, proposed a budget line of a little over a hundred thousand dollars and arranged for a classical compact disc to be given to every child born in the state, arguing in public that music in infancy could raise later school performance. Florida in the same period required classical music to be played in state funded childcare settings. Neither policy rested on a study of infants, because no such study existed. Both rested on the Irvine subtest, on adult subjects, enlarged by two steps of retelling, and on the cultural certificate described in the previous section, the ambient assurance that this particular composer's name already meant quality, so that a policy bearing the name felt like a policy bearing evidence.

The market supplied what the policies advertised. Record companies issued compilations aimed at expectant parents, toy manufacturers built classical excerpts into infant products and a small industry of books and recordings promised developmental advantage through listening, an industry whose advertising rarely mentioned spatial reasoning, paper folding or the fifteen minute duration of the original effect. Parents who bought the products were not foolish, and this article does not treat them as such. They were offered a claim by credentialed reporting, endorsed by a state government, wrapped in a name their culture had taught them to trust, and priced at the cost of a compact disc against the chance of helping a child. Under those conditions scepticism is the position that needs explaining, and the explanation lies in the replication record, which arrived more slowly and travelled less far than the claim.

One distinction should be fixed before that record is examined, because the whole episode turns on it. The claim that music education benefits children, through discipline, attention, social cooperation and the direct goods of musical skill, is a separate claim with its own evidence, and nothing in the collapse of the listening claim touches it. What collapsed, as the next section shows, was a specific proposition: that passive listening to a particular composer's music raises general cognitive performance. Keeping those propositions apart is the difference between reporting a scientific episode and joining a culture war, and the series stance charter, which requires contested positions to be given at their strongest, requires it here in both directions.

## The replication record

Did listening to Mozart ever make anyone smarter? The loop opened in section twenty four closes here, on the record of attempts to repeat the experiment. Through the later 1990s, laboratories in several countries ran the Irvine procedure and variants of it, and the pattern of results was consistent enough to summarise without selecting favourites. Some repetitions found small advantages for the music condition on spatial tasks. Many found none. In 1999 Christopher Chabris published a meta analysis in Psychological Science combining the available studies and concluded that any enhancement was small, restricted to tasks closely resembling paper folding and cutting, and insufficient to support a general effect on intelligence or reasoning. In the same year Kenneth Steele and colleagues, who had worked directly on the question, reported failures to reproduce the effect under conditions matching the original design and argued that the literature as a whole did not sustain the original reading.

A second explanation then did the work that a good alternative hypothesis should do. In 2001 William Forde Thompson, E. Glenn Schellenberg and Gabriela Husain reported experiments in which the advantage followed the listener's arousal and mood rather than the composer: music that a subject enjoyed, and even a narrated story that a subject preferred, produced comparable improvements on the spatial task, while the supposedly specific effect of the Mozart sonata shrank to the size of a preference. Schellenberg's subsequent work on music and cognition, conducted with the care that this article has credited to the original Irvine team, consolidated the position that brief listening changes state, alertness and mood among them, and that changed states change test performance briefly and modestly, on some tasks, for some listeners. That is a real finding. It is also a finding about being pleasantly awake, not about a Viennese composer reorganising the brain.

The Mozart effect rested on a short-lived spatial task result and did not survive replication as a general claim about intelligence. That is the fifth rung of this article's ladder, and its wording is chosen to be exactly as strong as the record and no stronger. The narrow result was never shown to be fabricated, and small task specific effects after enjoyable listening remained defensible in the literature. What failed was the enlargement, the chain from subtest to intelligence, from adults to infants and from minutes to development, and it failed at every link under independent testing. Rauscher herself, in later statements and collaborations, continued to defend a bounded version of the spatial claim while distancing her work from the parenting industry built on it, a position the record supports and one that the caricature of the episode, in which careless scientists promised genius, gets entirely wrong.

The episode's residue in the scientific literature was, paradoxically, healthy. The controversy forced precision about what a cognitive effect would have to look like, funded careful work on music and arousal and left the field with better instruments than it had in 1993. A later literature on rats, on premature infants and on seizure activity in epilepsy, where the same sonata was studied as a clinical stimulus, continued on its own evidence and is neither vindicated nor damaged by the fate of the parenting claim. Science is allowed to contain a modest true result, an inflated public version and a productive correction at the same time, and the discipline of holding all three apart is the same discipline this article has applied to Haydn's letter and to Beethoven's borrowings.

## What the episode shows about cultural authority

A result about thirty six students and a paper folding test became state policy for newborns inside five years, and the speed of that journey is a measurement of something other than science. It measures the cultural authority stored in a name. No legislature in 1998 proposed distributing the music of Domenico Cimarosa to infants, although Cimarosa's operas outsold Mozart's in the 1780s and his music shares the period style on which any plausible neural mechanism would have to operate. The claim needed Mozart, because only that name arrived pre-certified by two centuries of the processes this article has documented: the catalogue that made him countable, the editions that made him official, the curriculum that made him the definition of correctness and the ambient culture that made him a two second signal of quality in an advertisement. The Irvine result landed on a runway built by bibliography and tourism, and it travelled at the speed the runway allowed.

Cultural authority of that kind behaves like credit. It lets a claim borrow against a reputation instead of posting its own evidence, and the borrowing is invisible to the borrower, who experiences the claim as obviously respectable rather than as leveraged. Journalists who repeated the enlarged version were not, in the main, deceiving their readers. They were extending to a psychological finding the trust that the name had accumulated in domains where the trust was earned, editing, teaching and performance, and the transfer felt like common sense because the name was the same. The episode therefore belongs in an article about influence precisely because it is not about musical influence at all. It is about what a sufficiently large footprint permits strangers to assert without checking, which is a power no other figure in this series possessed to the same degree in the 1990s.

The corrective institutions worked, but on their own timetable and inside their own channels. Meta analysis, replication and the arousal studies corrected the record in the journals within six years, while the parenting products built on the enlargement continued to sell on the authority the correction could not reach, because a meta analysis has no trademark, no governor and no gift shop. That asymmetry, between the speed of an error carried by a great name and the speed of its correction carried by method, is the episode's durable lesson, and it applies far beyond music. It is the same asymmetry that kept the Salieri legend alive through a century of documentary correction, and it is why this article has insisted throughout on provenance, on where a claim was printed, who repeated it and what each repeater added.

There is a final, quieter point. The scientists at the centre of the episode emerged from it having behaved better than the culture that consumed them, and the composers at the centre of the older legends, Salieri above all, emerge from the documents in the same condition. Reputations in this history are not only assets. They are also liabilities, attracting claims their owners never made and cannot answer, and the scholar's obligation, which Cliff Eisen's documentary work exemplifies in the Mozart case, is to keep returning the claims to the size of their evidence.

## Mozart as shorthand for genius

By the middle of the nineteenth century the name had begun a second career as a common noun. To call a young musician a Mozart was to make a complete claim in one word: that the talent was early, effortless in appearance, comprehensive across genres and probably doomed to brevity, and the compliment carried its shadow inside it from the start. Rossini was hailed in Italy with a version of the label, the Italian Mozart, before his own style made the comparison obsolete. Mendelssohn's childhood was narrated by his family and teachers inside the comparison whether he welcomed it or not. Edvard Grieg, Franz Liszt in his performing youth, Camille Saint-Saens and, in the twentieth century, Erich Wolfgang Korngold and the young Benjamin Britten were all processed through the same word by critics reaching for the readiest certificate of precocious mastery, and in Korngold's case the Viennese press applied it to a child whose father was the city's most powerful critic, which made the shorthand a family trade wind as much as a judgement.

The shorthand does specific work, and examining it exposes what the culture actually values in the story. It is never applied to a slow developer. It is rarely applied to a musician whose gift is narrow. It selects for earliness, fluency and range, the three properties of the Mozart legend that depend least on the mature works and most on the travelling child of the 1760s, which means the shorthand certifies a biography more than a body of music. Parents who named children for him, conservatoires that marketed their prodigies through him and journalists who reached for him in an obituary of any young musician were all drawing on the same account, and the account paid out in intelligibility: a reader who knew nothing of Korngold knew, on being told the word, exactly what kind of phenomenon was being announced.

Shorthand of this power also flattens its object. A name that means genius in general stops meaning the particular composer at all, and the particular composer, the writer of the G minor Quintet and of Figaro's second act finale, disappears behind the certificate. The makers of dictionaries registered the change as they registered the Salzburg trade, without judgement, and by the twentieth century the word could be used ironically, of a failed prodigy or a boardroom strategist, which is the surest sign that a proper name has completed its conversion into currency. Currency circulates. It does not instruct, and a young composer handed the label received a burden of comparison and a marketing asset, but not a single usable technique.

The burden deserves its sentence. Several of the musicians processed through the label spent adult careers escaping it, insisting in interviews on their differences from the model and on the labour the model concealed, and the [examination of how critics shaped reputations](/2026/07/21/how-critics-shaped-reputations/) takes up the general machinery of such labels across the series. Here it is enough to note the direction of the flow: the shorthand is influence flowing backward, the dead composer's name altering the reception, the contracts and the self understanding of the living, while the living composer's music remains entirely his own.

## Where the influence claim is overstated

### Why do few modern composers sound like Mozart?

Few composers after 1900 sound like Mozart because the shared practice that made his procedures a common language had dissolved, because orchestral and harmonic resources had moved elsewhere, and because the classical surface depends on conventions that later composers could quote but could not inhabit.

The overstatement has a geography, and it can be mapped claim by claim. The first overstated territory is the symphony. Histories written for general readers often present the nineteenth century symphony as a river rising in Haydn and Mozart and flowing through Beethoven to Brahms and Mahler, but the documentary record of what symphonists studied, conducted and imitated shows Beethoven occupying the channel almost alone after 1825, with Mozart honoured at the source and bypassed in practice. The second territory is opera, where the claim that Mozart founded the modern ensemble opera ignores the Neapolitan and French traditions he inherited and overstates his direct action on Verdi, whose ensembles descend through Rossini, Bellini and Donizetti and the Parisian grand opera machine. Mozart's operatic influence is real and section eight located it, in finale construction and wind writing transmitted through Rossini, but an influence that arrives through three intermediaries at a century's distance is a different magnitude from the founding role the shorthand assigns.

The third territory is the piano, where the claim of direct descent dissolves on inspection of the instruments. The Viennese action of the 1780s, light, shallow and quick to decay, shaped a technique of clarity that the iron framed concert grand made obsolete as a physical system, and the pianism of Chopin, Liszt and their descendants was built for the new machine out of other materials, Hummel's school and the Parisian makers among them. What passed from Mozart into later piano playing came through pedagogy and through Hummel's codification, not through an unbroken performing style, and accounts that present the modern Mozart pianist as the heir of an intact tradition are describing a reconstruction, much of it admirable, that the twentieth century assembled from scores, treatises and recordings of older players.

The fourth territory is the largest and the vaguest, the claim that his music improves its hearers, in character, in intelligence or in health, for which the previous three sections examined the one version precise enough to test. Behind that examined version stands a penumbra of untestable ones, printed on concert programmes and recording sleeves across the whole period, in which exposure to order in sound is said to order the mind. Such claims cannot be refuted, which is exactly why they should not be repeated as findings. The stance of this article is that a claim which no evidence could embarrass is not a compliment to its subject. It is a use of his name as decoration, and the subject of this article, whose letters show a professional of exact and unsentimental judgements about other musicians, deserves the respect of claims that can be wrong.

Stating the overstatements plainly returns the genuine influence to visibility. Beethoven's modelled works of the 1790s, Rossini's finales, Schubert's lyric procedures, Mendelssohn's finish, the neoclassical licence and the curriculum's quiet machinery survive every audit in this section untouched, because each names its works, its dates and its channel. Influence that survives audit is smaller than influence that does not. It is also the only kind worth having in a reference work.

## Composers who worked against him

Opposition is evidence too, and the composers who defined themselves against the Mozart model tell us where its boundaries lay. Hector Berlioz built his operatic aesthetics on Gluck and on the monumental, and his criticism treated the Viennese classical manner, Mozart included by implication and sometimes by name, as a discipline too narrow for the passions he intended to represent, preferring scale, irregularity and the orchestral sublime. The New German School around Liszt and Wagner then organised the opposition into a philosophy of history: music was progressing from absolute forms toward the drama of the future, and in Wagner's prose the classical symphony after Beethoven became a completed vessel that only the music drama could refill, a scheme in which Mozart figures as a gracious ancestor inside a story whose arrow points away from everything he stood for. Ancestorhood of that kind is a polite form of dismissal, and the nineteenth century's reverence and its dismissal were, on inspection, the same gesture in different dress.

In the twentieth century the opposition changed its grounds. The Canadian pianist Glenn Gould, whose recordings and broadcasts made him one of the most articulate Mozart sceptics on record, argued in interviews that Mozart died too late rather than too early, meaning that the later works seemed to him to settle into mannerism where an earlier death would have left a purer promise, a provocation that scandalised programme note writers and that Gould defended with detailed, if perverse, attention to the scores. Whatever its merits, the remark performed a service: it forced defenders to argue from the music rather than from the certificate, and several of the finest analytical defences of the late works in the later twentieth century were written by scholars answering, directly or not, the suspicion that reverence had replaced listening.

Other oppositions were quieter and structural. Composers of the French tradition who rebuilt their art on Rameau and Couperin, Debussy among them in his critical persona, treated the Germanic classical line as a foreign occupation of French taste. The Soviet and post war avant gardes, for different reasons in different decades, treated classical balance as an ideology to be dismantled. Schoenberg, who revered Mozart and said so, nonetheless constructed his own ancestry through Bach, Beethoven and Brahms, the line of developing variation, leaving Mozart honoured and outside the genealogy that mattered to his school. A genealogy is a claim about influence disguised as a family tree, and the trees drawn by the twentieth century's most influential teacher placed Mozart, repeatedly, in the position of the beloved uncle who leaves nothing to the heirs.

The pattern across two centuries of opposition is consistent enough to summarise. Nobody of consequence opposed Mozart by calling the music incompetent. They opposed the use of the music as a standard, a licence or a genealogy, which is to say they opposed the footprint in the name of their own work, and their opposition confirms the article's central measurement from the negative side: what later composers had to reckon with was rarely a technique they wanted and often a monument they needed to walk around.

## Testing one influence claim properly

Method deserves a section of its own, because the reader who can test an influence claim owns the whole subject. Take the strongest documented case in this article and run it slowly. The claim: Beethoven's String Quartet in A major, Opus 18 No. 5, published in 1801, is modelled on Mozart's String Quartet in A major, K. 464, published in 1785. Access is established first. Beethoven arrived in Vienna in 1792, the quartet had been in print for seven years from Artaria, it was performed in the city and Beethoven's study of the Haydn and Mozart quartets in the 1790s is documented in his surviving sketches and copies. The claim therefore clears the access gate with dated print and a dated residence, no anecdotes required.

Specificity comes next, and it is musical rather than verbal. The two opening movements share a design in which a theme of innocent regularity is subjected, already in the exposition, to imitative entries that distribute it among the four voices, and Beethoven's movement follows the model's disposition of material passage by passage closely enough that analysts from the nineteenth century onward have printed the two in parallel. The minuet and variations movements repeat the correspondence in plan. What Beethoven does with the borrowed plan then supplies the consequence and the individuality at once: the harmonic rhythm quickens, the imitative entries harden into argument and the surface loses the model's ease while keeping its architecture, which is transformation rather than copying, the signature of influence at full strength.

Now run a weak claim through the same gates and watch it fail honestly. The claim that a graceful movement by an 1830s composer shows Mozart's influence usually offers a resemblance of mood, no evidence of access to a specific work and no feature that the shared practice of the period could not supply from a dozen sources. It fails specificity and it fails the exclusion of common sources, and the correct verdict is not that the claim is false but that it is unproven, a distinction the Salieri literature and the Mozart effect literature both illustrate from opposite ends. Charles Rosen's analytical writing remains the model for this discipline in the Mozart literature because Rosen consistently argued from passages that could be placed side by side, and Simon Keefe's reception studies supply the complementary discipline on the documentary side, insisting that a claim about who heard what, where and when, is a claim about paper before it is a claim about genius.

A reader assembling such a comparison for study, pairing movements, aligning dates of publication against dates of composition and tabulating the correspondences, is doing clerical work on which everything else depends, and clerical work is easier to keep straight when it can be laid out visually; a browser based presentation viewer such as [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) serves exactly that humble purpose, letting paired examples and a dated timeline be placed on slides and inspected side by side without specialist software. The tool does not make the argument. It keeps the evidence in view while the argument is made, which is the same service Kochel performed for the whole output in 1862, at a different scale.

## The reputation as a measure applied to others

A reputation large enough becomes a measuring instrument, and instruments distort what they measure in predictable ways. Across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, critics reached for Mozart as the unit in which other musicians were denominated. A new symphonist was praised for Mozartian clarity or blamed for lacking it. A singer's line was certified by comparison with the demands of the arias. A child at a keyboard was priced, by teachers and journalists alike, in fractions of the one historical case in which childhood fluency had coincided with adult greatness, and the pricing was almost always unfair to the child, because the unit itself was a legend assembled from a father's publicity, a sister's memoirs and two centuries of retelling, against which no living ten year old could be expected to stand level.

The measure also operated between the dead. Beethoven's achievement was narrated for generations as the answer to a question Mozart had posed, which diminishes both men in a single move, turning the earlier composer into a preliminary and the later into a solution, when the documents show two professionals solving different problems in overlapping forms. Schubert was measured against both and found, by the same instrument, to be the lyric third term, a classification that hid his harmonic originality for a century. Even Bach's nineteenth century revival was argued partly in Mozart's currency: the revivalists presented Bach as the profound lawgiver and Mozart as the radiant natural, a pairing of temperaments rather than an analysis of either man's counterpoint, and pairings of that kind, repeated in programme notes for a hundred years, did real work in deciding which works were programmed and which were left in the editions.

Institutions applied the measure with money attached. Competitions named for the composer, scholarships restricted to his repertory, festival auditions that required a concerto from the canon all converted the reputation into gatekeeping, so that a young musician's access to a career could depend on proficiency in a style two centuries old. There are defences of that arrangement, and they were made ably in the period by teachers who argued that a common standard, however arbitrary in origin, lets strangers be compared fairly. There are also costs, counted in the careers of musicians whose gifts ran to other repertories and who were measured, early and decisively, in a unit that did not fit them.

The distortion cuts the other way as well, and fairness requires the paragraph. Being the unit flattens the unit's owner. A composer used to measure everyone else ceases to be listened to as an individual with failures, experiments and a development that includes false starts, and becomes a surface of polished generality against which specificity in others is detected. The scholars who restored the particular Mozart, Eisen with the documents, Keefe with the Viennese context, Rushton with the operas and Kenyon with the mediations, were in this sense rescuing their subject from his own usefulness, returning a working musician to the place of the measuring rod.

## What his afterlife cost his contemporaries

The question opened in section twenty one closes here, with names. What did his fame do to the musicians working beside him? It rearranged their afterlives, in several cases past recognition. Begin with the extreme case. Antonio Salieri, imperial Kapellmeister, teacher of Beethoven, Schubert and Liszt, composer of operas performed across Europe for four decades, had his posthumous identity confiscated by a fiction: the rumour of poison that attached to him in the 1820s, Pushkin's tragedy of 1830, Rimsky Korsakov's opera of 1898 and the Shaffer play and film of the following century converted a respected professional into the patron saint of envious mediocrity, and his music vanished from the stage for most of two centuries. The confiscation was not Mozart's doing, and the documents clear Salieri of the legend's charges, but the legend needed a great name to envy, and the greatness that supplied it was the footprint this article has measured. The cost, in performances never given and a body of work never assessed on its merits, is the largest single debit in the ledger.

The quieter cases fill a page of their own. Vicente Martin y Soler, the Spanish composer whose opera Una cosa rara outdrew The Marriage of Figaro in Vienna in 1786 and whose melody Mozart himself quoted in the supper scene of Don Giovanni, is remembered, where he is remembered at all, as that quotation's footnote. Leopold Kozeluch, the most successful pianist composer in Vienna in the 1780s, a man whose works outsold his rival's in the local market, survives as a name in Mozart's dismissive letters. Muzio Clementi, who built a piano manufacturing and publishing empire in London and wrote sonatas that Beethoven admired and prescribed for his nephew, is reduced in the popular story to the opponent in a single contest before the Emperor in 1781. Carl Ditters von Dittersdorf and Johann Baptist Vanhal, the other two players in Haydn's quartet evenings, wrote symphonies and quartets that filled European programmes in their lifetimes and emptied out of them as the canon narrowed around three names. Giovanni Paisiello, the most performed opera composer in Europe in the 1780s, watched his repertory contract across the nineteenth century as the Da Ponte operas expanded into the space his generation had occupied.

The mechanism was not malice and not conspiracy. It was the arithmetic of canon formation under scarcity: limited stage weeks, limited shelf space in the complete editions market, limited pages in the histories and limited hours in the curriculum, allocated by institutions that needed a small number of names to stand for the whole period. Every hour of that allocation given to the expanding Mozart repertory was taken from a commons in which his contemporaries also grazed, and the taking accelerated itself, because familiarity justified programming and programming produced familiarity. Hummel, the pupil, felt the squeeze inside his own lifetime, as the Beethoven narrative and then the romantic piano school displaced the classical clarity he had inherited. Sussmayr felt it in full, surviving only as the completer of another man's fragment.

A fair ledger also records what the rising name returned to its generation. The editions, festivals and scholarship built for Mozart preserved the context in which the contemporaries can be studied at all, and the late twentieth century revival of Salieri, Paisiello and Martin y Soler was financed, intellectually and sometimes in money, by institutions founded for the central figure. The cost was real and the partial repayment was real. Both belong in the account, and neither cancels the other.

## Footprint and influence are not the same thing

His cultural footprint is enormous while his compositional influence is narrow, and conflating the two is the single most common error made about him. That sentence, the sixth and last rung of the ladder, is the claim the whole article has been earning, and it can now be stated with its evidence arranged behind it. The footprint includes a catalogue that made six hundred works countable, editions that made them playable, a festival city that built an economy on a birthplace, a curriculum that trains the world's pianists, singers and clarinettists on the repertory as its definition of correctness, an ambient culture that uses two bars of the music to certify taste in an advertisement, and a psychological legend that a state government once acted on. Measured in recognition, in money, in visitors and in hours of compulsory study, no composer of the eighteenth century casts a longer shadow, and Bach alone among his predecessors casts a comparable one in a different shape.

The influence, measured by the stricter standard of section two, access, specificity and consequence, is a shorter and more interesting list. Beethoven took the concerto's drama, the quartet's equality and the ensemble's conflict, and refused the surface. Rossini's generation took the finale machine and the wind writing under a declaration of independence. Schubert took lyric procedures for large forms, Mendelssohn took finish and institutional custody, Tchaikovsky took the language itself as a dramatic meaning, the neoclassicists took a licence to be brief and clear, and thousands of unnamed teachers took the works as the instrument by which the profession reproduces its standards. Every item on that list names a person, a work and a channel, and the list's shortness relative to the footprint is not a disappointment. It is a finding, of the same kind as the finding that a spatial task result did not generalise to intelligence: the truth about a famous name, measured carefully, is smaller in one dimension and larger in another than the fame suggests.

The distinction travels. Every figure in this series presents the same two quantities in different proportions, and the reader who has learned to separate them here owns a tool that works on reputations far beyond music, wherever a name's recognition is offered as evidence of its effect. The footprint is what a culture does with a figure after the work is done. The influence is what the work does to the next person's work. Mozart's case is the extreme that teaches the rule, because his footprint may be the largest in Western music while his influence, though deep in the channels this article traced, never governed the main road of the century that worshipped him. He became the standard by which music was judged, the emblem by which genius was announced and the name by which quality was certified, while the music of the future was being written, mostly, out of other materials. Holding both halves of that sentence at once, without letting either dissolve the other, is the whole discipline this article has tried to practise, and it is the respect that a professional of Mozart's exactness is owed by anyone who writes about him.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why is Mozart considered so important?

Mozart is considered important for two different reasons that are often merged into one. The first is the range and quality of the output itself, more than six hundred catalogued works across opera, symphony, concerto, chamber music and church music, produced inside a working life of roughly twenty five years. The second is the position the music came to occupy after his death, as the teaching standard in conservatories, the core of the opera house repertory and the example from which textbooks drew the rules of classical form. The first reason concerns what he wrote. The second concerns what institutions did with it, and importance in the full sense is the product of both, which is why this article measures the footprint and the compositional influence separately rather than treating fame as proof of effect.

### Q: What is the Mozart effect?

The Mozart effect is the name given to a claim that listening to Mozart's music improves cognitive performance. It began with a bounded laboratory result published in 1993, in which college students performed better on a spatial reasoning task for a short period after listening to the Sonata for Two Pianos, K. 448. In popular retelling and in a commercial book and product line of the later 1990s, the claim expanded into a general proposition that listening raises intelligence, especially in babies and young children. Scientific usage and popular usage therefore refer to different things, and any discussion has to specify which one it means. The bounded result belongs to the psychology of arousal and task performance. The expanded proposition, the one sold to parents, is the version that the replication record failed to support.

### Q: What did the 1993 study actually find?

The study, published as a short letter in Nature in 1993 by Frances Rauscher, Gordon Shaw and Katherine Ky, tested thirty six undergraduate students on spatial reasoning items of the paper folding and cutting type. Students completed the items after listening for ten minutes to Mozart's Sonata for Two Pianos in D major, after a relaxation recording and after silence. Scores were higher after the music condition, by an amount the authors expressed as eight or nine points on the spatial subscale of the test, and the advantage faded within roughly ten to fifteen minutes. The study used adults, measured one kind of task and reported a temporary effect. It did not test children, did not measure general intelligence and did not claim that listening produces lasting improvement of any kind.

### Q: Has the Mozart effect been replicated?

The narrow result was repeated in some laboratories and not in others, and the enlarged claim was not sustained. A meta analysis by Christopher Chabris in 1999 combined the available studies and found any benefit to be small and confined to tasks resembling paper folding. Kenneth Steele and colleagues reported failures to reproduce the effect under conditions matching the original. Work by Thompson, Schellenberg and Husain in 2001 then showed that enjoyable music of other kinds, and even a preferred spoken story, could produce comparable gains, pointing to arousal and mood as the mechanism rather than anything specific to one composer. The defensible conclusion is that pleasant listening can briefly sharpen performance on some tasks, and that the general claim about intelligence, the version applied to infants, did not survive independent testing.

### Q: How did Mozart influence Beethoven's early work?

The influence is documented in works Beethoven modelled openly on Mozart originals during the 1790s, after his arrival in Vienna in 1792. His Quintet for Piano and Winds follows the scoring and layout of Mozart's Quintet, K. 452. His Piano Concerto in C minor takes its key, its dramatic opposition of soloist and orchestra and its long orchestral exposition from Mozart's Piano Concerto in C minor, K. 491. His String Quartet in A major, Opus 18 No. 5, follows the movement design of Mozart's Quartet in A major, K. 464, closely enough for analysts to print the two in parallel. Beethoven also wrote variations on themes from The Magic Flute. What he took was equipment, concerto drama, equality of quartet voices and ensemble construction, while refusing the polished surface, which he replaced with a rhetoric of struggle.

### Q: What did Haydn say about Mozart?

Haydn's assessment survives in a letter written by Leopold Mozart to his daughter Maria Anna on 16 February 1785, reporting words Haydn had spoken to him in Vienna after hearing the quartets Mozart later dedicated to Haydn. In Leopold's report, Haydn declared before God and as an honest man that the younger composer was the greatest he knew, by person or by name, joining taste to the most profound knowledge of composition. The provenance matters for how the remark is used. It was not published by Haydn and it was relayed by a proud father, yet its weight comes from the source: the most respected instrumental composer in Europe, in his fifties, speaking about a colleague whose quartets he had played from inside, holding a part. Later generations treated it as testimony, and publishers and biographers repeated it whenever standing was questioned.

### Q: How did cataloguing change Mozart's reputation?

Before Ludwig von Kochel published his chronological thematic catalogue in 1862, the works circulated under conflicting publishers' numbers, with wrong attributions, uncertain dates and whole categories effectively invisible. Kochel examined autographs, paper types and copyists' hands and gave every known work a number, an argued date and an identifying opening, producing the K. numbers that scholarship and performance have used since. The effects were structural. A numbered output could be counted, so the figure of more than six hundred works entered the argument. A chronological output could be narrated as early, middle and late periods. And a catalogued output defined what a complete edition had to contain, leading to the Breitkopf edition of 1877 onward and the Neue Mozart-Ausgabe from 1955. Cataloguing converted admiration into an organised body of knowledge, and this article argues it did more for the standing than any composer's praise.

### Q: Why is Mozart central to singing training?

The arias and ensembles function as a graded course that voice teachers recognised and adopted because the writing develops technique without damaging young voices. The line must be carried on steady breath in long phrases, the coloratura must stay clean and unaspirated, the Italian diction has to project at conversational intensity, and the ensembles train a singer to act and listen while a tempo set by others will not wait. Roles such as Susanna, Zerlina, Papageno and Tamino sit in the middle of the voice and suit students in their late teens, while the Countess's sustained paragraphs of tone test an advanced singer's breath and control. Singers trained on this repertory carry its standard of line into heavier repertory for the rest of a career, which makes the opera house and the studio together one of the strongest channels of influence described in this article.

### Q: How did performance practice for Mozart change?

Performance practice changed in scale, in instruments and in rhetoric across two centuries. Viennese performances of the 1780s used orchestras of roughly thirty five players, winds in pairs, natural brass and a fortepiano able to converse with the ensemble. The nineteenth century doubled the string body, adopted the iron framed piano, slowed the famous symphonies and applied romantic habits of vibrato, swelling dynamics and rhetorical pause. The twentieth century then argued its way back. Richard Strauss and others protested against inflation, festival ensembles between the wars thinned the texture, and from the 1950s the period instrument movement, Harnoncourt's Concentus Musicus, Hogwood's Academy of Ancient Music and their successors, restored smaller forces and quicker tempi. Mainstream orchestras absorbed much of that correction while keeping modern instruments, so the default sound of the repertory by the century's end sat between the two extremes.

### Q: How did Salzburg build an industry around him?

Salzburg built the industry on institutions first and merchandise second. The Mozarteum Foundation, established in international form in 1880, opened the birthplace in the Getreidegasse as a museum, and the family's later residence became a second site. The Salzburg Festival, founded in 1920 by Hofmannsthal, Reinhardt and Richard Strauss, placed Mozart at the centre of a summer performance economy, and the Mozart Week of 1956 extended it into January. Around the institutions grew the trade: hotels, guided tours and the Mozartkugel, the confection created by Paul Furst in 1890, manufactured by rival firms and exported worldwide. The city sold proximity to the story of the miraculous child in the streets where that childhood happened, although the mature career belonged to Vienna. The industry measures the footprint at its largest, and this article classifies it there rather than as compositional influence.

### Q: Is Mozart more famous than influential?

On the evidence assembled in this article, yes, and the comparison can be made precise. The fame is measurable in recognition, in festival tourism in Salzburg, in curriculum requirements across continents and in the use of the name as a general certificate of genius. The influence, held to a strict standard of documented access, a specific technique and a visible consequence in later music, is real but bounded: Beethoven's modelled works of the 1790s, Rossini's finale construction, Schubert's lyric procedures, Mendelssohn's finish, Tchaikovsky's acts of transmission and the neoclassical licence of the twentieth century. That list is distinguished and it is short beside the footprint. The gap between the two quantities is not a scandal and it does not diminish the music. It is the normal condition of a very famous name, visible here with unusual clarity because the documentation is unusually good.

### Q: How does his influence compare with Bach's?

The two influences travelled by different vehicles. Bach's influence was carried first by manuscript copying and by teaching, passing through his sons and pupils to Mozart and Beethoven themselves, and then by a nineteenth century revival that installed his counterpoint at the centre of compositional training, so that his procedures entered the bloodstream of later music as grammar. Mozart's influence was carried by performance, by print and by institutions, the opera house, the conservatory and the festival, and it acted most strongly on standards, on what correctness and finish were taken to mean. Bach shaped how composers thought. Mozart shaped how musicians were trained and judged. The comparison also runs in a circle that honours both: Mozart studied Bach in Baron van Swieten's library in the 1780s and arranged Bach fugues, so part of what later composers received from Mozart was Bach, transmitted through Viennese paper.

### Q: Should parents play classical music to babies?

Parents who enjoy classical music have every reason to play it in the home, and babies benefit from the voices, attention and shared pleasure that accompany music making and listening together. What the evidence does not support is the specific promise attached to the Mozart effect in the 1990s, that passive listening to a particular composer raises a child's intelligence. That promise rested on a temporary spatial task result in adults, enlarged through reporting and marketing, and it failed independent testing as a general claim. The separate and better supported proposition is that active musical training, lessons, practice and ensemble playing across childhood, is associated with discipline and with skills that transfer in modest, debated ways. A parent choosing between a listening product marketed on intelligence claims and actual music lessons for an older child is choosing between a failed claim and a real activity, and the record points one way.

### Q: Is studying Mozart necessary for a composer today?

For a composer working in the Western concert tradition, close study of this repertory remains the fastest route to certain specific competences: balancing phrases, writing for winds as conversational partners, constructing an ensemble in which several intentions sound at once and achieving large effects with limited forces. Those competences can be learned from other repertories, and a composer whose work grows from electronic production, from improvisation or from a non European tradition may need them less or in different forms. Necessity is therefore the wrong frame. The practical question is what a given composer wants to be able to do, and for the skills listed above the study remains unmatched as training because the textures are transparent enough to show exactly how each effect is made. Teachers prescribe the works for the reason examiners do: the faults and the methods are both audible, and nothing in the music hides how it works.

### Q: How do I evaluate a claim that music improves cognition?

Apply the same tests this article applied to the 1993 study. First, find the original paper and read what it measured, with which subjects, over what duration, rather than relying on a report of a report. Second, separate the outcome measured from the outcome advertised: a gain on one task is not a gain in intelligence. Third, look for independent replication and for meta analysis combining many studies, since a single small study is a starting point and not a verdict. Fourth, ask what alternative mechanism could explain the result, as arousal and mood explained much of the listening effect. Fifth, check who profits from the enlarged version of the claim, because a trademark, a product line or a policy attached to a finding is a reason to re-read the finding at its original size. A claim that survives all five tests at full size is rare and worth believing.

### Q: How do I trace a specific influence between two composers?

Begin with access and date it. Establish that the later composer could have encountered the earlier work before writing the passage in question, through print, performance, teaching or a documented library, and treat undated admiration as no evidence at all. Next, name the specific feature said to have travelled, a formal design, a scoring decision, a procedure for handling themes, and place the two passages side by side in analysis, as scholars place Beethoven's A major Quartet beside Mozart's K. 464. Then exclude the common source: if the feature belonged to the shared practice of the period, resemblance proves nothing about direct transmission. Finally, look for consequence and transformation, the feature doing new work in the later piece. A claim that clears all four stages is influence. A claim that clears only resemblance is a hypothesis, and honest writing labels it as one.

### Q: Which recordings show the influence most clearly?

Choose recordings that let the borrowed structures be heard side by side. Pair a period instrument performance of Mozart's Quintet for Piano and Winds, K. 452, with Beethoven's Quintet for the same combination, and the modelling of scoring and layout becomes audible within minutes. Pair the two C minor piano concertos, Mozart's K. 491 and Beethoven's Third, in recordings that keep the orchestral winds clear, and the shared rhetoric of confrontation can be followed movement by movement. For the operatic line, set a Rossini comedy finale, recorded with a responsive ensemble cast, against the second act finale of The Marriage of Figaro. For Tchaikovsky's homage, the orchestral performance of Mozartiana explains itself, especially beside recordings of the small Mozart piano pieces it orchestrates. In each pairing, prefer complete works over excerpts, since influence lives in structures that excerpts cut away.

### Q: What should I listen for when comparing Mozart and Beethoven?

Listen first for the handling of the theme. In Mozart a theme tends to arrive complete and balanced, and the movement's drama comes from how it is exchanged, combined and re-lit among the voices. In Beethoven the theme is often a fragment, a motto of a few notes, and the drama comes from what the fragment is forced to become. Listen next for the orchestra in the concertos: in Mozart the winds converse with the soloist as equals in a chamber texture, while in Beethoven the orchestra increasingly confronts the soloist as a massed opposing force. Listen then for surface and effort. Mozart's writing conceals its labour and keeps a social ease even in minor keys. Beethoven's displays its labour and treats ease with suspicion. The comparison, heard this way, is not a contest. It is the clearest available demonstration of influence and refusal operating in the same works.

### Q: Which composers deliberately avoided Mozart's model?

Avoidance took several forms. Berlioz built his operatic ideals on Gluck and on the monumental, and treated the Viennese classical discipline as too narrow for the passions he wanted to represent. The New German School around Liszt and Wagner organised avoidance into a philosophy of history, presenting the classical forms after Beethoven as complete and superseded by the music drama of the future, which left Mozart honoured as an ancestor and bypassed as a resource. Schoenberg, who expressed reverence for Mozart, constructed his own teaching genealogy through Bach, Beethoven and Brahms, the line of developing variation, and left the classical balance outside the inheritance his school claimed. In the twentieth century Glenn Gould argued on record that Mozart died too late rather than too early, a deliberate provocation against the reverence. In each case the target was less the music than its use as a standard.

### Q: What happened to composers overshadowed by Mozart?

Their repertory standing contracted as the canon narrowed around a small number of names, a process driven by the arithmetic of stage weeks, publishing and curriculum hours rather than by any single judgement of quality. Salieri suffered the extreme case, his identity confiscated by the poison legend that Pushkin dramatised in 1830 and later works spread, while his operas left the stage for most of two centuries. Martin y Soler, whose Una cosa rara outdrew Figaro in Vienna in 1786, survives chiefly as a footnote to a quotation in Don Giovanni. Kozeluch, Clementi, Dittersdorf, Vanhal and Paisiello all held European reputations in the 1780s and all saw their works empty out of programmes as the editions, festivals and histories organised the period around three Viennese names. A partial correction came late in the twentieth century, when institutions built for the central figure financed recordings and editions that returned several of these composers to hearing.
