# The Friar's Duplicated Lines: A Famous Crux of Revision

The most instructive four lines in *Romeo and Juliet* are the four lines the play prints twice. At the end of the balcony scene, the second quarto of 1599 gives Romeo a quatrain about the breaking dawn: the grey-eyed morn smiling on the frowning night, the eastern clouds chequered with streaks of light, darkness reeling drunk from the path of day. Romeo leaves. The Friar enters alone with a basket, and the same quatrain begins again, in a second and slightly different form, as the opening of the Friar's soliloquy. One dawn, two drafts, standing eight printed lines apart in the book that every modern edition of the play descends from. No audience ever heard both. No performance could speak both. The duplication survives because the manuscript behind the second quarto preserved an act of revision that its author never finished tidying: Shakespeare wrote the dawn for Romeo, thought better of the passage while composing the Friar's entrance, rewrote it in improved form, and left the cancelled first version standing close enough to the second that the printing house set both.

That is the verdict this article defends, and it is a verdict with a fight inside it. The duplicated lines, often called simply the dawn doublet, are the single most concrete piece of evidence about how the text of this play was made, and precisely because the stakes are high, every link in the argument has been contested. Bibliographers built the theory of the author's foul papers partly on survivals of this kind, and Paul Werstine and other skeptics have spent decades warning that the foul papers are a story editors tell themselves, a category with no surviving specimen against which its supposed symptoms can be tested. Editors, meanwhile, have had to decide what a reader should actually see, and they have not agreed: the consensus deletes Romeo's quatrain and prints the Friar's, Brian Gibbons broke ranks to give the lines a home in Romeo's closing speech, and René Weis, following the Second Folio of 1632, gives the quatrain to Romeo rather than the Friar, a decision Emma Smith faulted for arriving with no bibliographic theory attached. The crux is small. Four lines, twice told. What it opens onto is the whole question of what kind of object a Shakespeare text is: a finished artefact, or a working document caught in the act of becoming one.

## The Duplicated Lines Themselves: Two Dawn Quatrains, Word by Word

Precision comes first, because the entire argument rests on small differences that paraphrase destroys. The second quarto, printed by Thomas Creede for the publisher Cuthbert Burby in 1599, ends Romeo's part in the orchard scene like this, in the quarto's own spelling. Romeo has taken his leave of Juliet, who has withdrawn above. He speaks two lines of wish, "Would I were sleep and peace, so sweet to rest," and then, in Q2, four more lines follow before his vow to visit the Friar: "The grey eyde morne smiles on the frowning night, / Checkring the Easterne Clouds with streaks of light, / And darknesse fleckted like a drunkard reeles, / From forth daies pathway, made by Tytans wheeles." Only then comes the couplet every reader knows, "Hence will I to my ghostly Friers close cell, / His helpe to crave, and my deare hap to tell," and the direction Exit. The stage direction that follows is one of the most quoted in the play's textual history: "Enter Frier alone with a basket." The Friar speaks, and the dawn breaks a second time: "The grey-eyed morne smiles on the frowning night, / Checking the Easterne clowdes with streaks of light: / And fleckled darknesse like a drunkard reeles, / From forth daies path, and Titans burning wheeles." From there the soliloquy runs on without disturbance into territory no one disputes: "Now ere the sun advance his burning eie, / The day to cheere, and nights danke dew to drie, / I must upfill this osier cage of ours / With baleful weeds and precious-juiced flowers" (2.3.5-8).

Set the two quatrains side by side and the pattern of agreement and difference is exact. The first line is identical in substance: grey-eyed morn, smiling, frowning night. The second line carries the same words in both versions, chequering the eastern clouds with streaks of light, with only the spelling wandering between "Clouds" and "clowdes," the ordinary instability of a period that had not fixed its orthography. The third line holds the first real variant. Romeo's version runs "darknesse fleckted," noun before participle, and the participle itself is a misprint or a mangled form, "fleckted," where sense wants "flecked" or "fleckled"; a collation printed in *Shakespeare Survey* 8 (1955) already flagged the point, noting misprints in both Q2 versions of the passage, "fleckted" among them. The Friar's version reverses the order, "fleckled darknesse," adjective before noun, and gets the participle into its smoother shape. The fourth line holds the variant that matters most. Romeo's dawn travels "from forth daies pathway, made by Tytans wheeles": day has a pathway, and the pathway was made by Titan's wheels, a small explanatory clause bolted onto the image. The Friar's dawn travels "from forth daies path, and Titans burning wheeles": the pathway contracts to a path, the explanatory clause vanishes, and in its place the wheels themselves catch fire, "burning," so that the line ends not with a piece of mythology explained but with an image alight.

Four lines, then, of which two are effectively one text twice spelled, and two carry the fingerprints of a mind reworking its own sentence: a transposition that tidies the grammar of the third line and a substitution in the fourth that trades explanation for energy. One further detail fixes the character of the reworking. The rhyme skeleton survives the revision untouched: both versions rhyme "night" with "light" and "reeles" with "wheeles," couplet answering couplet in the same order. The second version was therefore not composed fresh in forgetfulness of the first, as the design theory would require and as a compositor's accident could not produce. It was written with the first version in view or in ear, its rhymes kept as the frame and its interiors rebuilt, the participle transposed, the fourth line's tail cut away and replaced with fire. Revision of that kind, frame retained and filling renewed, is the signature of a writer reworking a page he could see, and it narrows the scene of the event: the Friar's quatrain was drafted in the presence of Romeo's, whether on the same leaf, a facing page, or a memory fresh from the writing of it. The InsightCrunch duplication crux, the table below, records the pair line by line, because the doublet is one of those rare textual facts that a reader can verify entire from a single page, and because every theory of the passage is, in the end, a theory of these eight lines and nothing else.

Two features of the exhibit deserve emphasis before any theory is built on it. The first is that the variants are substantive. A spelling can drift between one setting of type and another without any mind intervening; compositors spelled by ear and by habit, and the same word could leave the same shop in three forms on one page. Word order and substituted words are another matter. "Darknesse fleckted" becoming "fleckled darknesse" is an act of composition, and "made by Tytans wheeles" becoming "Titans burning wheeles" is an act of imagination: the second version does not copy the first, it answers it. The second feature is the direction of quality, which any reader can test without bibliography. Romeo's fourth line explains the picture it has just painted, as though the writer did not yet trust it. The Friar's fourth line trusts the picture and sets it burning. One version reads like a draft that knows what it wants to say; the other reads like the same draft after its author found the saying. Whatever else is disputed in the scholarship of this crux, that gradient, from the looser to the tighter, from the explained to the enacted, is there on the page, and every serious account of the doublet has to begin by explaining it.

## On the Printed Page: What the Early Books Actually Show

The doublet has one virtue no other major crux in the play can match: it can be inspected whole, in the original documents, by any reader willing to climb a short evidentiary ladder. At the ladder's top stand the modern editions, where the crux is invisible, resolved before the reader arrives. One rung down stand the transcriptions, the second quarto's text letter for letter, in which the two quatrains appear in their quarto spellings with the junction between them marked by nothing more than Romeo's Exit and the entrance direction for the Friar. At the ladder's foot stands the facsimile, the page as Creede's shop left it in [the second quarto of 1599, the "good" quarto](/2026/04/03/second-quarto-1599-good-quarto/), where the farewell speech runs down the page under its wavering prefix, the dawn quatrain sits inside it, the exit direction follows, and within a page the same dawn opens again under the Friar's name. The argument of this article is, at bottom, a report on that page. Every theory of the passage is an attempt to explain how the page came to look as it does, and the page disciplines the theories: a theory that requires the two quatrains to be identical, or far apart, or in different books, dies on contact with the facsimile.

The textbook tradition understood the page's power early and exploited it honestly. The dawn passage became the standard first exhibit in the teaching of Shakespeare's text precisely because it could be reproduced in facsimile and read by a beginner entire: here is the best early text of the play, the exhibit runs, and here, in its best passage of dawn poetry, it prints the same lines twice, in two states, eight lines apart. An 1874 reprint of Q2 put the quarto's text into scholars' hands spelling by spelling, and the collations that followed, the *Shakespeare Survey* note of 1955 flagging "fleckted" among the misprints shared across the versions, built the modern dossier. The digital transcriptions of the quarto have since lowered the ladder's foot to the floor: the two states of the quatrain can now be called up, compared, and searched by any student with a browser, and the crux has become a rare thing in bibliography, a famous argument whose entire evidence base fits on two screens. A reader who wants to test the argument personally, rather than inherit it, can lay the two states side by side in a research workspace such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) and watch the variants align: the spellings wandering, the participle transposing, the wheels catching fire. Collation at this scale is not a specialist mystery. It is the article's whole method, miniaturized for one quatrain.

The first quarto's page at the same junction makes the complementary exhibit. Q1's balcony scene closes without ceremony: Romeo speaks his vow to visit his ghostly father's cell and is gone, and the Friar enters to a dawn that has not been spent already. The page is shorter, poorer, and at this joint cleaner than Q2's, a combination that the play's textual scholarship has learned to find eloquent. Q1's cleanliness here is not the cleanliness of a superior manuscript; it is the cleanliness of resolution, of a text in which the choice between the two dawns has already been made, by whomever's hand, in the Friar's favour. Set the two books' junctions side by side and the whole crux stands in a single visual field: one book with the choice made, one book with the choice preserved unmade, and the history of the text flowing from the second book because the second book, for all its disorder, carried the fuller and more authorial account of everything else.

A methodological pearl hides in the ladder, worth extracting before the theories resume. The two quatrains differ wildly in spelling and trivially in substance between their first two lines, and differ in substance exactly where the sense concentrates, in the last two. That distribution is itself the beginner's guide to the distinction on which Greg built his rationale of copy-text: accidentals, the spelling and punctuation that a printing house supplies and varies at will, against substantives, the words and their order, which descend from the copy. Between the two settings of the first two lines, every difference is accidental, the noise of two passages through the same shop. Between the two settings of the last two lines, the differences are substantive, the signal of a mind. The doublet thus teaches its own method on its own face: trust the substantives, discount the accidentals, and the passage sorts itself into a draft and its revision without any theory being imported at all.

## The Afterlife of a Half-Line: Burning, Fiery, Fleckled, Flecked

The received text of the Friar's dawn, the version carried in the memory of every reader who has met the play at school, is a small editorial mosaic, and the doublet explains how it was assembled. Consider the fourth line in the form the received text gives it: "From forth day's path and Titan's fiery wheels." The adjective "fiery" stands in neither version the second quarto prints. Q2's Friar gives the wheels "burning"; Q2's Romeo gives them no adjective at all, only the explanatory "made by." The "fiery" of the received line is a gift of the later tradition, naturalized so completely that popular texts print it without a note, and its presence in the line is a standing reminder that the text of this play, at its most familiar points, is an inheritance of judgments rather than a transcript of any single document. Editors chose, century by century, and the choices hardened into what readers mistake for the thing itself.

The third line tells the same story with the documents reversed. Q2's Friar reads "fleckled darknesse," and "fleckled" is a defensible period form, yet a long line of editors, recorded in the New Variorum tradition of 1871, preferred the first quarto's participle and printed "flecked," so that the received line "And flecked darkness like a drunkard reels" carries a word healed from the shorter text at the very place where Q2's own two versions wrestle with the participle between them: "fleckted" in Romeo's setting, "fleckled" in the Friar's. Three early forms of one word, and the received text's fourth. None of this is scandal, and none of it is arbitrary: each choice can be defended, and the Variorum's collations exist so that the defences can be audited. But the mosaic character of the received quatrain is the doublet's lesson at the smallest scale. If four of the most famous lines in the play reach readers as a construction assembled from rival states of the text, then the clean page of a modern edition is everywhere a verdict, and the dawn doublet is simply the place where the verdict's working is easiest to see, because there the suppressed alternative is not a word but a whole speech position, a different speaker standing in a different scene.

The accidentals make the same point from below. Between the two Q2 settings, the second line's key word appears as "Clouds" in one and "clowdes" in the other, and the verb as "Checkring" against "Checking," variations no one ascribes to the author because spelling in 1599 belonged to the shop, the compositor's ear and habit deciding what the copy's letters became. Readers untrained in the distinction sometimes ask why, if the spellings can differ innocently between the settings, the substantive differences cannot be innocent too. The doublet itself supplies the answer in its distribution: the innocent differences gather where nothing is at stake, in orthography, and stop at the border of sense, while the guilty differences, the transposed participle, the recomposed half-line, sit exactly on the sense. A printing house produces the first kind by existing. Only a writing desk produces the second. The mosaic and the doublet are one phenomenon at two magnifications, and learning to see the small one is the price of admission to the large.

## Why the Doublet Sits Exactly on the Scene Break

The position of the duplication is the second fact, and it is not a coincidence. The doublet does not lurk in the middle of a continuous stretch of dialogue. It straddles the junction between the orchard scene and the Friar's cell, the moment where the play's long night ends and its fatal day begins. In the second quarto there is no scene heading to mark the joint: Q2 prints the play continuously, without the numbered architecture of acts and scenes that modern editions supply, and the transition from orchard to cell is carried by two stage directions, Romeo's Exit and the entrance of the Friar alone with his basket. The scene division that readers cite, 2.2 closing and 2.3 opening, is an inheritance from the Folio tradition and its eighteenth-century regularizers, laid over a quarto that simply runs on. The doublet therefore sits precisely where a dramatist's manuscript changes its unit of work. A scene ends. A new scene begins, with a new speaker, a new place, and a new set piece to compose.

That placement matters because it identifies the moment of revision. The standard account, and the one this article's verdict adopts, runs as a sequence of ordinary writing decisions. Shakespeare composed the balcony scene and, reaching its close near dawn, gave the departing Romeo a quatrain to mark the hour and motivate the exit to the Friar's cell: the lines did structural work, closing the night in the imagery the whole play has taught its audience to read, light against dark, before "Hence will I to my ghostly Friers close cell" carried Romeo off the stage. Then the next scene had to be written, the Friar's first appearance, a soliloquy that needed its own dawn, since the Friar is discovered at daybreak with his basket, gathering the herbs his speech is about. Facing the second dawn, the writer reached back for the first, or found it rising unbidden from the same imaginative pressure, and wrote the quatrain again, better. The Friar's version is not a fresh start on an unrelated subject. It is the Romeo quatrain rewritten at the moment of its reuse, improved in the two places where the first attempt was slack, and then carried forward into a speech that builds on it: "Now ere the sun advance his burning eie" continues the same sky the quatrain opened. In the manuscript, the first version was superseded. In a carefully prepared fair copy it would have been cancelled or simply not transcribed. In the papers that reached Creede's printing house, it stood, and the house printed what stood.

### Could any audience ever have heard the passage twice?

No audience could have heard the dawn quatrain twice, because performance is merciless toward duplication in a way print is not: an actor who has just described the grey-eyed morn cannot watch the next entrance describe it again without the house hearing an echo that no emphasis can justify.

Every playhouse text and tradition that descends from actual staging gives the passage once. The doublet is a page phenomenon, a survival from the document behind the book, and its natural home is the study of manuscripts rather than the history of the stage.

The first quarto supports the staging logic from its own side. Q1, printed in 1597, two years before Q2, contains no repetition at this junction at all: as Emma Smith puts the point in her *Review of English Studies* assessment of Weis's edition, "In Q1 there is no repetition: only the Friar has the speech." Romeo's exit in Q1 is managed by the plainer vow, "Now will I to my Ghostly fathers Cell," and the dawn waits for the Friar. Whatever account a scholar gives of Q1's origins, and the debate over those origins is a large one, pursued head-on in [Q1 vs Q2: what the differences reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/), the fact for this crux is narrow and usable: the only other early text of the play knows a single dawn, spoken by the Friar, and knows nothing of a Romeo version. The shorter text thus behaves exactly as a performable text must behave at this junction, and exactly as Q2, at this junction, does not. Two documents stand behind the play's early print history, and only one of them carries the marks of a desk rather than a stage. The doublet is the most legible of those marks, which is why it has been quoted in textbooks ever since the foul papers theory gave scholars a name for the kind of document Q2 was printed from.

A stage detail sharpens the same conclusion. In performance the Friar's entrance must motivate itself: a man alone at dawn with a basket of herbs is a picture that explains its own hour, and the soliloquy that follows is anchored in the gathering it describes, the osier cage to be filled before the sun dries the dew. The quatrain belongs to that picture organically. In Romeo's mouth, by contrast, the quatrain floats free of any action: Romeo is not gathering, watching, or waiting; he is leaving, and the lines suspend his exit while the sky is described at a length his errand cannot afford. The passage fits its second home and fits its first home only loosely, like a paragraph drafted for one page and pasted, improved, into another. Dramatic fitness is not proof, and this article will weigh the skeptics' warning against building too much on such judgments. But fitness is evidence of a kind, and it points the same way as the word order and the burning wheels: the quatrain was conceived in the orchard and came of age in the cell.

## Could a Printer Have Done This by Accident?

Before the doublet is allowed to testify about a manuscript, it has to survive cross-examination as a possible accident of the printing house, because printing houses generate accidents in industrial quantities. The second quarto was set in type by workmen in Thomas Creede's shop, reading manuscript copy and building pages letter by letter. The classic compositorial accident for a duplicated passage is eye-skip: the setter's eye, returning to the copy after setting a line, lights on the wrong occurrence of a repeated word or phrase and sets a stretch of text twice. Eye-skip is real, common, and well documented across early modern printing. If the dawn doublet were an eye-skip, it would tell scholars nothing about Shakespeare at all, only something about a tired workman in 1599.

Gabriel Egan, in his lecture "Shakespeare's Typos" and in his broader defense of the traditional textual scholarship, states the objection that kills the eye-skip theory for this passage, and it deserves to be given at full strength. If the repetition were identical, Egan argues, an eye-skip would be the natural supposition: the setter set the same lines twice because his eye travelled to the same place twice. But the two dawn quatrains are not identical. They differ in word order, in a repaired participle, and in a whole rewritten half-line, "pathway, made by Tytans wheeles" against "path, and Titans burning wheeles." A wandering eye reproduces; it does not revise. No compositor, skipping within his copy, could set "fleckled darknesse" the second time if the words before his eyes both times read "darknesse fleckted," because the second reading exists nowhere for his eye to land on. The variants are the signature of a composing mind working the sentence twice, and compositors, whatever their other sins, did not recompose their authors' sentences into better ones. The doublet's differences, small as they are, are therefore the crux inside the crux: they transfer the passage's origin from the printing house to the writing desk, and any theory of the doublet that ignores them explains a different, imaginary duplication, an exact repeat that happens not to be the one Q2 contains.

What a printing house could do, and probably did, is fail to choose. Suppose the copy before Creede's men contained both versions with the first cancelled, marked for deletion by a pen stroke, a marginal sign, or the lighter cancellation that a busy writer makes when a replacement stands close by. Cancellation in a working draft is an instruction to a reader, and like all instructions it can be missed, misread, or quietly set aside by a workman paid by the page and reluctant to guess. Suppose, alternatively, that the copy contained both versions with nothing cancelled at all: a first version in the flow of Romeo's speech, a second version opening the new scene, and no signal which was to stand. Then the compositors did the most defensible thing available to them and set everything, letting the buyer of the book inherit the author's indecision entire. Either supposition makes the printing house a passive agent, a conduit rather than a cause: it explains how both versions reached print, while the versions themselves, their order, their differences, and their gradient of quality were fixed before any compositor touched them. That division of labour, house transmits and desk originates, is the platform on which the foul papers argument is built.

One more printing-house possibility deserves its hearing, because it is the strongest alternative the desk theory faces within the trade itself. A scribe might have stood between the author's papers and Creede's shop: a copyist preparing a fair transcript for the printer, who, meeting a passage his exemplar treated ambiguously, transcribed both versions rather than decide. The possibility has a pedigree in this play's scholarship. The Cambridge editors W. G. Clark and W. A. Wright, in the nineteenth century, already judged that Q2's copy was a careless transcript rather than the author's own manuscript, and a transcript hypothesis can absorb almost any feature of Q2 by relocating it one step from the author. But absorption is not explanation. A scribe who transcribes a cancelled passage and its replacement has still found both in his exemplar, and the exemplar, on this theory, contains an authorial first thought and an authorial second thought in unresolved proximity. The scribe changes who failed to cancel, not what was written. The doublet remains a draft phenomenon, one remove further from the desk but a draft phenomenon still, and the question of what kind of document held both versions survives every attempt to blame the trade.

The mechanics of the shop floor complete the printing-house account. Play quartos were not proofread against an author's intention; correction, where it happened at all, caught literals, turned letters and dropped words, the accidents a reader of proofs could see without knowing the play. A duplicated passage defeats that regime completely, because on the page each setting of the quatrain is, locally, perfect: correct words, correct metre, correct speaker, nothing for a corrector's pen to catch. Detection required collation, the comparison of the passage against a standard, and the only standard in the shop was the copy, which contained both. Nor did the economics of the book argue for intervention: eight lines of type cost Creede's house a few minutes and no paper worth counting, while a decision to cut authorial-looking verse exposed the shop to the risk of mutilating the commodity it had bought. Printing houses conserve. The doublet survived the shop for the same reason it survived the desk, because at every handover the safest action was to transmit everything, and the sum of those safe actions is the book.

## From Desk to Stage: The Prompt Book Would Have Had to Choose

Between the author's papers and the printed book stands a third document, the one the playhouse actually used, and the doublet's fate in that document is a thought experiment with a certain answer. A prompt book, the book-keeper's prepared copy from which performances were run and actors' parts extracted, cannot contain the dawn twice. The actor playing Romeo cannot be handed a part that describes the dawn and then, a page later in the book, hear the Friar describe it again; the book-keeper preparing such a copy must strike one version, and the version struck, in any preparation made after the Friar's scene existed, would be Romeo's, since the Friar's speech cannot open without its first movement while Romeo's farewell demonstrably can. Whoever regularized the play for performance therefore performed the cancellation the desk had omitted, silently and completely, which is why the doublet leaves no trace in any performance tradition and why the first quarto's disposition at this junction, one dawn, the Friar's, is exactly the disposition a prompt tradition produces, whatever route Q1 took to print.

The consequence for the play's first audiences deserves one sentence of its own, because it is easy to lose among the documents. The crowds who heard *Romeo and Juliet* in the later 1590s heard one dawn at this junction, the Friar's, not because the author's papers held one, but because performance performs the cancellation that paper postpones. The doublet is thus a purely bibliographical event with a theatrical shadow: it marks the exact point where the play that was written and the play that was played come apart, and where the printed book, uniquely, preserves the written one. The annotated copy behind the First Folio represents a later pass of the same kind, a reader with quarto and playhouse knowledge marking a printed text toward usability, and the Folio tradition's eventual assignment of the lines in F2 is that pass made visible. Textual history at this junction is a series of forced choices, and Q2 is precious because it is the one witness that refused to choose.

## The Foul Papers Case: W. W. Greg and the Working Manuscript

The framework that made the doublet famous is the theory of foul papers, and its architect is W. W. Greg. In *The Editorial Problem in Shakespeare* (1942) and across a career that included *The Shakespeare First Folio* (1955), Greg argued that the manuscripts from which Shakespeare's plays were printed could be sorted by their character: at one end, the author's own working draft, the foul papers, bearing the untidiness of composition; at the other, the fair copy or the prompt book, bearing the tidiness of the theatre. R. B. McKerrow's *Prolegomena for the Oxford Shakespeare* (1939) had already systematized the diagnostics, and A. W. Pollard's *Shakespeare Folios and Quartos* (1909) had supplied the taxonomy of good and bad quartos inside which the discussion moved. Foul papers, on the Greg and McKerrow account, announce themselves by a syndrome rather than a single symptom: speech headings that waver as the writer's attention wavers, stage directions that describe what the author imagined rather than instruct what the stage must do, loose ends in the action, and, above all, revision left standing, the first thought and the second thought cohabiting a page that no fair copyist has cleaned.

The second quarto of this play became the theory's parade case, and the dawn doublet its parade exhibit. Around the doublet, the symptoms cluster with unusual density. The speech headings of Q2 waver exactly as the foul papers model predicts, introducing the hero as Romeo, Rom., and Ro. within the same scene, as though the writer's hand shortened its own labels while the pen kept moving. The stage directions carry an author's descriptive looseness rather than a book-keeper's precision. At one stretch of Act 1, the book abandons its main copy altogether: for the passage modern editions number 1.2.55 to 1.3.37, Q2 simply reprints Q1, a patch that suggests the printer's copy was defective or incomplete at that point and had to be made good from the earlier edition, the behaviour of a shop working from a bundle of papers with a damaged or missing leaf rather than from a clean, continuous transcript. Elsewhere the text preserves the texture of drafting in miniature. Juliet's oxymoron speech against the banished Romeo contains, in Q2, the strange unmetrical line "Rauenous douefeatherd rauen, woluishrauening lamb" (3.2), in which first and second thoughts appear fused inside a single line: "ravenous" and its replacement were both set, and modern editors print the line as "Dove-feathered raven! wolvish-ravening lamb!" only by deleting the superseded word. The dawn doublet is that same accident at paragraph scale, and its scale is what makes it precious: a whole quatrain, superseded and standing, with its replacement four pages of reading away in the same opening.

### What other traces of a working manuscript stand in the second quarto?

Beside the doublet stand the wavering speech prefixes that shorten and lengthen the hero's name as the copy hurries, the Act 1 stretch reprinted from the first quarto where the printer's copy evidently failed, and stage directions that imagine a scene rather than instruct a stage.

The unresolved puzzle of the name Susan Grindstone in the Nurse's family history, and single lines in which a superseded word stands fused with its replacement, complete the syndrome: a set of small untidinesses that no one symptom could establish alone and that together describe a manuscript caught before its final cleaning.

J. Dover Wilson, editing the play for the New Shakespeare in 1936, put the doublet's kind of evidence at the centre of his account of Q2's copy, and the description that stuck to such survivals in the editorial tradition is "false starts": passages the author began, replaced, and failed, in the press of composition, to cancel beyond recall. The phrase matters because it assigns the doublet a biography. A false start is not an error in transmission. It is a moment in composition, preserved by the failure of its own deletion, and to call the dawn quatrain a false start is to claim that a reader of Q2 can watch the play being written at the exact point where its two most famous movements, the balcony and the Friar, are joined. Greg's larger claim for Q2, that its copy was Shakespeare's autograph draft, the foul papers themselves, gives that biography its fullest form: the book Creede printed is, on this account, the closest surviving approach to the author's desk, nearer to the act of writing than any fair copy, prompt book, or reported text could be, and its blemishes are the price and the proof of the proximity.

The doublet contributes something to that claim which the other symptoms cannot: it is datable within the act of composition and directional. Wavering prefixes show carelessness but not sequence. The Act 1 patch shows defective copy but not authorship. The doublet shows a specific writer, at a specific joint in the play, writing a passage, then writing it again differently and better, in that order, since the second version presupposes the first as the thing improved. Sequence is the rarest commodity in textual scholarship, where most evidence is static, and the doublet supplies it in eight lines. That is why, when the foul papers of Q2 are introduced to students, the dawn passage is the exhibit carried into the room: it does not merely suggest that the copy was a draft; it shows the draft drafting.

## The Skeptics' Reply: What the Doublet Cannot Prove

The foul papers case, as just stated, is powerful, and the history of Shakespeare scholarship since about 1990 is in part the history of its cross-examination. The cross-examiner in chief is Paul Werstine, whose essay "Narratives About Printed Shakespeare Texts: 'Foul Papers' and 'Bad Quartos'" (*Shakespeare Quarterly*, 1990) argues that the discipline's master categories are narratives imposed on evidence rather than conclusions drawn from it. The charge, applied to the doublet, runs as follows. No manuscript of a Shakespeare play in the author's hand survives against which the profile of foul papers could be checked. The category was instead assembled by reasoning backward: editors decided which features of printed texts looked draft-like, gathered those features into a syndrome, named the syndrome foul papers, and then used the name to certify the features that had defined it. A duplicated passage fits the syndrome beautifully. But its fit proves only that the printed text contains a duplication, a fact nobody denies. To move from the duplication to the author's own uncanceled draft requires the whole narrative machinery, authorial papers, authorial cancellation, authorial oversight, and machinery is not evidence. Laurie E. Maguire's *Shakespearean Suspect Texts* (1996) pressed a parallel audit on the features used to diagnose memorial reconstruction in the short quartos and found diagnostic after diagnostic failing against control texts, which deepened the methodological suspicion: the features editors treat as symptoms have a way of appearing wherever they are looked for.

The skeptics' discipline is valuable, and this article's verdict accepts part of it. It is right to insist that the doublet, strictly handled, proves a short list and no more: that the copy behind Q2 contained both versions of the dawn quatrain; that the versions differ as draft and revision differ, not as copy and miscopy differ; and that nobody between the writing and the printing resolved the duplication. Claims beyond that list, about whose hand held the pen, about the physical state of the manuscript, about the precise psychology of the oversight, are inference, and should be labelled inference. Where the skeptics overreach is in treating the short list as though it were a long defeat. The short list is not small. A printer's copy that contains a passage twice, in two authorially distinct states, with the improved state second and structurally integrated into the scene that follows, is a printer's copy standing unusually close to composition, whether the hand that failed to cancel was the author's, a scribe's, or a book-keeper's. Werstine's critique dissolves a label, foul papers, into its evidentiary parts; it does not dissolve the parts. The doublet survives the dissolution, diminished in what it certifies but intact in what it shows: revision, preserved.

A second skeptical pressure deserves separate statement, because it comes from inside the play's own textual situation. Eric Sams spent a career arguing that the profession had the two early texts backwards, that Q1 was Shakespeare's first version of the play and Q2, whose title page announces itself "newly corrected, augmented, and amended," was his revision of it, the title page meaning what it says. Sams's reading is a minority one and this article does not adopt it, but it performs a service for the doublet: it reminds the argument that revision is not a desperate hypothesis wheeled in to rescue an anomaly. On every account of the play's making, including the orthodox one, this is a text its author reworked, between performances, between versions, and, the doublet shows, within the very manuscript that founded the printed tradition. The disagreement between Sams and the orthodoxy is about where the revision happened, in the study across years or at the desk across a page. The doublet is the one piece of evidence that shows revision at the smallest scale, and both camps must build their larger stories around it or explain it away.

There is also a quieter skepticism, aimed not at the inference but at the romance that gathers around it. The doublet is often presented as a window onto genius in motion, and the presentation can smuggle in more than the evidence holds: the inspired second thought, the discarded draft, the blot that was never blotted. The evidence, handled coldly, shows something both less and more interesting than the romance. Less, because a duplicated quatrain is, mechanically, a common accident of draft transmission, the kind of survival any busy professional writer's papers might produce. More, because the accident happens to have occurred at the most scrutinized joint of the most famous love tragedy in the language, and to have preserved, in the two states of four lines, a complete specimen of the author's revising hand at work on his imagery, compressing, transposing, and setting fire to a pair of wheels. The romance is optional. The specimen is not.

One further body of evidence disciplines both sides of the foul papers quarrel, and it is the nearest thing the period offers to a control specimen. The manuscript of *Sir Thomas More* preserves three pages widely attributed to Shakespeare's own hand, the attribution argued in the 1923 volume *Shakespeare's Hand in the Play of Sir Thomas More*, which A. W. Pollard himself edited. Those pages show a working dramatist's revision in the physical act: lines interlineated above the text they improve, second thoughts crowding into the space the first thoughts left, the page visibly reworked as the sense sharpened. The More pages cannot prove that Q2's copy was autograph, and the skeptics are entitled to the distinction. What they prove is the prior probability: the one specimen of this author's drafting that survives shows revision carried forward in exactly the untidy, accumulative manner the doublet implies, first thoughts standing while second thoughts gather around and after them. When the choice lies between reading the dawn doublet as the survival of a known habit and reading it as an otherwise unattested accident, the More manuscript votes, quietly, for the habit. Greg's narrative may exceed its evidence in the large. At this junction, the evidence and the narrative fit each other like a key cut for its lock.

## Which Version Came First, and What the Rewrite Shows

Every part of the argument so far assumes an order: Romeo's quatrain first, the Friar's second, the second an improvement on the first. The order is not printed in Q2 in any explicit way; both versions simply stand. It has to be established, and establishing it is a useful exercise because the tests involved are the same tests an editor applies to any pair of rival readings, magnified to paragraph scale.

The first test is integration. The Friar's version is load-bearing. It opens a scene and a speech that continues from its materials: the dawn it describes is the dawn the Friar is working in, basket in hand, and the lines that follow, "Now ere the sun advance his burning eie, / The day to cheere, and nights danke dew to drie" (2.3.5-6), extend the quatrain's sky into the errand of the scene, filling the osier cage before the dew dries. Remove the quatrain and the soliloquy loses its first movement but the speech's dependence on it is visible in every line that presupposes the hour. Romeo's version bears no load at all. It interrupts a farewell whose shape is complete without it: the wish for sleep and peace, the vow to visit the Friar, the exit. Remove it, and nothing else in the scene notices. Revision moves, characteristically, from the detachable to the structural, because a writer reusing material in a new place builds the new place around it. The quatrain was an ornament in the orchard. In the cell it is a foundation.

The second test is the texture of the variants themselves. First thoughts characteristically explain; second thoughts characteristically trust. Romeo's fourth line, "From forth daies pathway, made by Tytans wheeles," carries its gloss inside itself, spelling out the mythology for a reader who might not supply Titan and his chariot unaided. The Friar's line drops the gloss and lets the wheels burn. Likewise the third line: "darknesse fleckted" tangles its participle, in a form so awkward that the *Shakespeare Survey* collators singled it out as a misprint, while "fleckled darknesse" runs clean. A copyist degenerates texts in transmission, making the smooth rough; the doublet runs the other way, rough to smooth, which is the direction of an author revising rather than a scribe corrupting. Put together, integration and texture make the order as certain as such things get: the Romeo quatrain is the draft, the Friar quatrain the revision, and Q2 preserves them in the order of their making.

### How should a reader picture the manuscript page behind the doublet?

The reader should picture a working page, not a fair one: Romeo's farewell written out in full with the dawn quatrain standing inside it, the scene ending, and then, as the Friar's entrance was composed, the same dawn written again at the head of the new scene in its improved form.

The first version stands uncancelled, or cancelled so lightly that the mark failed to govern the printing house: a page on which an author mid-composition changed his mind about where his best quatrain belonged, and trusted a later tidying that never happened.

Peter Ackroyd, in his biography of Shakespeare, puts the event in a single sentence: the speech beginning "The grey eyed morne smiles on the frowning night" is "transferred from Romeo to Friar Lawrence in a significant change of emphasis." Transfer is the right word for what the doublet records, and Ackroyd's surrounding examples show how ordinary the event was in this author's workshop. In *Love's Labour's Lost*, Berowne's great speech in 4.3 survives in two versions, one more lyrical than the other, the second presumably added in a margin or on a separate sheet without the first being cancelled clearly enough to stop the printer setting both: the same accident, at the same scale, in a comedy printed a year before Q2. And inside *Romeo and Juliet* itself, the fused line in Juliet's oxymoron speech, "Rauenous douefeatherd rauen, woluishrauening lamb" (3.2), shows the same two thoughts colliding within a single line of Q2, the first adjective never deleted when its replacement arrived. A writer whose pages could produce a conflated line and a doubled speech was a writer whose drafts habitually carried their own history. The dawn doublet is the grandest survival of the habit, not an anomaly requiring a special psychology.

Against the whole picture stands the most famous testimony about Shakespeare's hand, and the confrontation is instructive. Ben Jonson reported, in his *Discoveries*, that the players made it a point of honour that Shakespeare, in his writing, "never blotted out line," and Jonson's retort has become as famous as the claim: "would he had blotted a thousand." Jonson meant the retort as criticism of facility, of a fluency too proud to revise. The doublet gives the exchange an irony neither party intended. The players were wrong about the blotting, if the doublet is read straight: here is a passage blotted in effect, superseded by its own improvement. But the blotting was never executed, the cancellation never made visible enough to bind the men who printed the book, so the facility the players praised and the revising the doublet proves are the same fact seen from two sides. Shakespeare revised in the flow of composition, carrying the second thought forward into the next scene rather than back over the page with a pen, and his drafts therefore travelled to the printing house with their first thoughts still legible inside them. The doublet is what that method looks like when it survives. Heminges and Condell, introducing the First Folio, boasted that they had "scarce received from him a blot in his papers," and meant it as a guarantee of clean copy. The second quarto, printed a generation earlier from far messier papers, lets a reader check the boast: the papers had blots, second thoughts, and unexecuted cancellations, and the play is more interesting, and more honestly come by, for every one of them.

## Revision, Error, or Design: Trying the Rival Explanations

The brief for this crux poses the complication exactly: is the duplication revision, error, or both? The question is well put, because the honest answer is that the doublet is a compound event, and each rival explanation seizes one part of the compound and mistakes it for the whole. Four candidates stand before the tribunal.

The first candidate is deliberate design: the echo as meaning. On this reading, Shakespeare intended the dawn to sound twice, Romeo's version closing the night of love and the Friar's answering it across the scene break, a rhyme between the lover's world and the confessor's that binds the play's two dawns into one design. The reading flatters the passage, and it fails three tests. It fails the test of Q1, the shorter early text, which has the passage once: a designed structural rhyme between the play's two central movements is not the kind of feature that disappears entire from the other substantive text. It fails the test of the variants: a designed echo varies its repetition for sense, throwing the repeated words into new light, whereas the doublet's differences are the static of drafting, a transposed participle, a repaired misprint, a half-line recomposed, differences that point backward to a writing desk rather than outward to an audience. And it fails the test of performance already administered: no playing text can speak the same quatrain twice within a page. Design is the explanation readers reach for when they cannot bear waste in a canonical text. The doublet is waste, glorious and informative waste, the scaffolding a builder forgot to strike.

The second candidate is compositorial error, and it has already been tried and convicted of insufficiency in the printing-house section: an eye-skip reproduces a text, and this text was not reproduced but revised. The third candidate is scribal duplication, the careful transcript that set both versions because its exemplar held both. It survives, but as a stage in the story rather than an alternative to it: a scribe can duplicate only what a draft supplied. The fourth candidate is authorial revision, uncancelled. It alone explains the full exhibit: the gradient of quality between the versions, their order, their placement across a scene break, the continuation of the Friar's speech out of the revised version's materials, and the company the doublet keeps among Q2's other draft symptoms. The tribunal's verdict can therefore be stated as a distribution of roles. The duplication is revision in composition: a passage written, then rewritten in a better place. It is error in transmission: a cancellation unmade, unread, or unenforced somewhere between the desk and the printed page. Revision explains why there are two versions. Error explains why both are in the book. Any simpler answer, pure revision or pure error, explains half the fact and calls it the whole.

### What makes the Friar's wording the stronger of the two drafts?

The Friar's wording is stronger because every change spends less to get more: "path" sheds the explanatory padding of "pathway," the mythology lecture of "made by" vanishes, and the picture gains the one thing the draft lacked, wheels that burn, so the line ends in light rather than in commentary.

The transposed "fleckled darknesse" untangles a participle the draft had mangled, and the revised quatrain as a whole stops describing the dawn as information and starts performing it, which is the difference between a note toward a speech and the speech itself.

One refinement remains, and it concerns the word "cancelled." To say the first version was left uncancelled is to picture a pen stroke that failed. The manuscript reality may have been subtler. A dramatist drafting in sequence, scene by scene, might never return to the earlier page at all: the revision happened forward, in the new scene, and the earlier version was not so much cancelled and missed as never revisited, superseded in intention and untouched in fact. On that picture, nobody erred at the desk; the error, if error is the word, belongs entirely to the chain of custody that carried a sequential draft to a printing house as though it were a finished text. The refinement matters because it aligns the doublet with the Jonson testimony rather than against it. A writer who revises forward, scene over scene, blots nothing, since blotting is a backward operation, and yet revises constantly. His papers reach the printer full of first thoughts precisely because his method generates second thoughts downstream of them. The dawn quatrain's two states are the fossil of that method, and the method, once seen in the fossil, becomes visible across the play: in the conflated line of 3.2, in the wavering prefixes, in a text that everywhere bears the grain of composition rather than the smoothness of transcription.

## The Editors' Four Centuries: Deleting, Restoring, Reassigning

What editors have done with the doublet is a history of changing answers to a single question: when the documents preserve two authorial versions, which one is the text? The transmission first. Q3, printed in 1609, is a reprint of Q2, and Q4 followed from Q3; the First Folio text of 1623 was set from a copy of Q3 that had been annotated by a hand with access to other authority, the Folger's introduction to the play summarizes, so the doublet travelled down the main line of descent with every opportunity to persist. The Second Folio of 1632 made the tradition's boldest move: it prints the quatrain as Romeo's and not as the Friar's opening, resolving the duplication by giving the lines to the first speaker and striking them from the second. Whoever corrected copy for F2 preferred a clean page to a documentary one and chose, between the two versions, the draft over the revision, an astonishing preference that lay dormant in the tradition until an editor of the twenty-first century revived it.

The eighteenth century edited the play from the late folio line, Rowe's edition of 1709 resting on the Fourth Folio, and Alexander Pope's edition of 1725 began the long return to the quartos, preferring quarto readings and quarrying Q1 for stage directions the later texts lacked. The Cambridge edition of the 1860s, Clark and Wright's, consolidated the scholarly text on Q2 collated against the tradition, judging Q2's copy, as noted earlier, a careless transcript rather than the autograph itself, and the New Variorum of 1871 gathered the passage's early forms for inspection, spelling by spelling. By the time the modern scholarly editions arrive, a settlement has formed, and its terms are the ones the standard textbook statement reports: almost all modern editors delete the lines from Romeo's speech and retain the Friar's lines. T. J. B. Spencer's Penguin text follows the settlement; G. Blakemore Evans's Cambridge edition of 1984 follows it; Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000 follows it; the Folger Shakespeare, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, prints Romeo's exit as the clean couplet pair, "Sleep dwell upon thine eyes, peace in thy breast! / Would I were sleep and peace, so sweet to rest! / Hence will I to my ghostly father's cell, / His help to crave, and my dear hap to tell" (2.2.186-9), and opens the Friar's scene with the quatrain in its revised form. The settlement's rationale is the argument of this article's middle sections: the Friar's version is the revision, the revision is the author's last word on the passage, and an edition's text should print the author's last word while its notes preserve the first. There is a pleasing irony in Werstine's presence on the Folger title page: the scholar who taught the profession to distrust the foul papers narrative co-edits the edition that quietly enacts the narrative's editorial consequence, deleting a false start. The irony is only apparent. Editing and skepticism answer different questions, what a reader should be given and what a scholar may claim to know, and the doublet is big enough to require both answers at once.

Two further witnesses belong in the record before the dissenters are heard, one editorial and one theatrical. The editorial witness is the apparatus itself. J. Dover Wilson's New Shakespeare edition of 1936 carried the foul papers account of Q2, doublet included, to the wide readership that series commanded, and after Wilson the passage lived the double life that defines its modern existence: absent from the reading text, present in the notes, a deletion every scholarly edition performs and immediately confesses. Readers who want the settlements side by side will find them mapped in [the modern editions compared: Arden, Oxford, Folger](/2026/06/12/modern-editions-compared-arden-oxford/), but the deeper point is structural. An editorial tradition that deletes a passage and prints the deletion in its apparatus has not suppressed the evidence; it has filed it, and the doublet's file, swollen with collations from the Variorum onward, is among the fullest any four lines in the play possess.

The theatrical witness is unanimous in the opposite direction. In preparing her Oxford edition, Jill Levenson assembled records of some 170 promptbooks for the play, spanning the seventeenth to the twentieth century, and the promptbook record functions as a four-century referendum on the doublet: no production can speak the dawn twice, and none does. Promptbooks cut, transpose, and reassign lines with a freedom no editor would permit, Garrick's among them, but the freedom stops at this junction, because the constraint is not taste but physics, two bodies, one dawn, a page apart. Performance history therefore agrees with the first quarto and with the deleting editors, not because actors and editors share a theory, but because the stage enforces the resolution that the documents left open. The played play has one dawn. Only the printed play, descending from unresolved copy, ever had two, and the doublet's whole significance lives in that asymmetry: it is evidence about the text precisely because it could never be evidence about the performance.

### How does the doublet sit against the second quarto's title page boast?

Awkwardly, and therefore honestly: the second quarto advertises itself as newly corrected, augmented, and amended, yet its most famous survival is a passage no corrector corrected, an augmentation no reader was meant to receive twice, and the boast and the doublet end up refereeing each other.

The boast claims a finished and improved book; the doublet proves the copy behind the improvement was a working draft whose improvements had not all been carried through, which is exactly what a text newly augmented from foul papers would be expected to look like.

Against the settlement, two dissenters stand, and they dissent in opposite directions. Brian Gibbons, in his Arden second series edition of 1980, prints the quatrain inside Romeo's closing speech, where a generation of readers used only to the settlement met it with genuine shock: a teacher writing to the SHAKSPER list in 1998 reported the discovery with the bewilderment of a man who had found a familiar room rearranged, the first four lines of 2.3 sitting in the middle of Romeo's last speech. Gibbons's defence, as the report summarizes it, is literary: the lines suit Romeo's style, the play's appointed describer of light, the poet of "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2), and they answer to the lovers' dawn parting in 3.5, where the imagery of reluctant daybreak again belongs to the pair in the orchard world rather than to the cell. The defence has force as criticism: Romeo's version is not rubbish, it is a good quatrain in the wrong place, and Gibbons's text performs the experiment of leaving it where Q2 put it. René Weis, succeeding Gibbons in the Arden third series in 2012, goes further and stranger. Weis follows F2: he gives the quatrain to Romeo rather than the Friar, so that the Friar's soliloquy in his text begins downstream of the dawn, and his stated ground, quoted by his reviewer, is that "the lines hardly fit with the Friar's meditation on medicinal herbalism." Emma Smith's review in the *Review of English Studies* is the necessary corrective, and its complaints are procedural rather than preferential. Weis offers no substantive discussion of the bibliographic question, nothing on authorial second thoughts, no reason for passing over Q1's simple clarification, and he compounds the liberty by emending the exit line with a word from Arthur Brooke's source poem, printing "Hence will I to my ghostly sire's close cell" where both quartos read otherwise, "sire" imported from Brooke's "He is my gostly syre." An editor may reach any conclusion the documents allow; what the office forbids is reaching it without showing the documents.

The adjudication, then, distributes praise and blame unevenly, as adjudications should. The settlement is right about the text: the Friar's quatrain is the revision and the foundation of its scene, and a reading text that printed Romeo's version instead, or both in sequence, would misrepresent the play its author finished. Gibbons is right about the record: Romeo's version is authorial, accomplished, and evidence, and an edition that deletes it into a one-line note undersells the most instructive survival in the book; the honest formats are the full textual note and the parallel presentation, of which Jay L. Halio's parallel-text edition, printing Q1 and Q2 on facing pages, is the standing model, letting a reader watch Q2's doubled dawn face Q1's single one. Weis's reassignment is the one outcome the evidence will not carry: it deletes the author's revision and enthrones his draft, on a ground, the quatrain's supposed unfitness for the herb speech, that collapses on inspection, since the soliloquy's whole movement, dawn, dew, gathering, the virtues of plants, grows out of the quatrain's sky. The Friar's meditation on medicinal herbalism does not merely tolerate the dawn lines. It is unthinkable without them, and an editorial rationale that severs the speech from its own first movement has mistaken the play for its paraphrase.

The most recent editorial movement has changed the question itself. Where Wilson's generation asked which version to print and the settlement answered, the Friar's, a generation of scholars formed by the skepticism of the 1990s began asking why a version must be chosen at all. Leah S. Marcus's *UnEditing the Renaissance* (1996) gave the movement its slogan, "unediting": the proposal that editions might present the plurality of early texts rather than silently resolving them into a single reconstructed one, letting readers meet the play in its documented states. Randall McLeod's essays on the transformations of the text pressed a kindred case from the facsimile side, treating the early printed page, accidents and all, as the object of study rather than as a corrupt copy of a lost ideal. The dawn doublet is the movement's ideal exhibit, and the digital facsimile and parallel-text resources that now put Q1 and Q2 on every student's screen are its institutional victory: the passage can be exhibited unresolved, both dawns standing, with the argument about them conducted in the open instead of being settled in an apparatus. The versioning answer does not refute the settlement; a reading text still has to choose, and for readers the Friar's quatrain remains the right choice. What versioning changes is the status of the loser. Romeo's version, in the versioning dispensation, is not an error to be deleted but a version to be read, and its promotion from apparatus to exhibit is the doublet's latest editorial fate, the one under which its fame has grown fastest.

## The Stakes of Attribution: What Changes If Romeo Keeps the Dawn

The editorial argument has been conducted so far in the language of documents, but Gibbons's dissent was a literary argument, and it deserves a literary trial, because the attribution of four lines moves real weight in the play. Suppose Romeo keeps the quatrain. The first consequence falls on his portrait. Romeo is the play's appointed poet of light, the speaker of "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3), and a dawn speech in his mouth at the parting would crown that portrait: the lover who made his mistress the sun now reads the actual sunrise with a poet's fluency. Yet the coronation has a crack in it, and the SHAKSPER teacher who stumbled on Gibbons's text put a finger on the crack in 1998: the quatrain celebrates the day and calls the night frowning, and Romeo nowhere else in the play privileges day over night. His whole erotics run the other way, toward the "love-performing night" and against the daylight that ends it. The Friar's dawn, by contrast, is spoken by the one character for whom dawn is a working hour and light a professional instrument, the herbalist whose philosophy of the earth begins in the gathering light. Attribution is characterization at the level of the document, and the document's second thoughts gave the play's dawn to the character whose world it belongs to.

The second consequence falls on the Friar's entrance, and it is structural. Stripped of the quatrain, the soliloquy begins in mid-errand, "Now ere the sun advance his burning eie," with its sky assumed rather than painted, and the herb speech that follows loses its first movement: the passage from dawn, to dew, to gathering, to the virtues and poisons of plants is a crescendo whose opening chord is the quatrain. Weis's rationale, that the lines hardly fit the meditation on medicinal herbalism, inverts the actual architecture, in which the meditation is the quatrain unfolded, the dawn's own doctrine of a world where light and dark, cure and poison, share one earth. The attribution also touches the long critical quarrel over the Friar's authority, the standing debate over [Friar Laurence as healer, schemer, or bungler](/2022/10/14/friar-laurence-character-analysis/): a Friar who enters inside a great set piece of natural philosophy enters with the play's intellectual credentials in his hands, and an edition that starts his scene four lines later quietly demotes him from philosopher to plot mechanic before he has spoken a word about herbs.

The third consequence is a pattern, and it may be the deepest thing the revision accomplished. The play has three great dawns. The dawn of 2.3 belongs to work and counsel, a man gathering the materials of rescue in the first light. The dawn of 3.5 belongs to the lovers, and it is fought over in the aubade's terms, "It was the lark, the herald of the morn" (3.5.6) against the nightingale, closing in Romeo's surrender to the light: "Night's candles are burnt out, and jocund day / Stands tiptoe on the misty mountain tops" (3.5.9-10). Between those poles, the draft quatrain in Romeo's farewell would have planted a third dawn, a lover's dawn, decorative and unowned, blurring the distribution the revision achieved. The transfer to the Friar clarified the play's economy of daybreak: dawns in this play belong to whoever must act in them, the Friar to his gathering, the lovers to their parting, and the quatrain's migration from the orchard to the cell is the moment the economy was struck. Gibbons's stylistic case proves that Romeo's version is good enough to keep. It cannot prove that keeping it serves the play the revision was building, and on that question the documents and the design vote together, which is as close to certainty as editing gets.

## Citing a Passage That Exists Twice: Numbers, Notes and Appendices

A practical consequence of the doublet, rarely stated, is that half of it has no address. The Friar's quatrain lives at a fixed coordinate, 2.3.1-4, cited in every commentary and classroom. Romeo's version has no line numbers of its own in any standard edition, because line numbers belong to the edited text and the edited text has deleted it. Scholars referring to it must resort to description, "the first version of 2.3.1-4, printed in Q2 at the end of 2.2," or to the textual note at the passage, in which editions record the deletion in the compressed grammar of the apparatus: after "rest," Q2 prints a first form of the following scene's opening quatrain, here omitted. The asymmetry is a quiet lesson in what editions are. A text gives its contents addresses; an apparatus gives the suppressed contents a file; and the doublet's Romeo half, authorial, accomplished, and four centuries famous, exists for most readers only as a file, retrievable by those who know the system and invisible to those who take the text as the play.

Editions have handled the file in three ways, and the choice among them is a small referendum on editorial philosophy. The confessing note, the settlement's usual instrument, records the deletion at the point of loss and gives the quarto's wording or a reference to it; a reader alerted at 2.2's close can reconstruct the whole fact without leaving the page. The appendix, favoured by editions with documentary ambitions, prints the suppressed version entire among additional passages, restoring it to readability while keeping it outside the text's numbering. The parallel text, Halio's solution, abolishes the asymmetry by printing Q2 as it stands and letting the doublet occupy its documentary position beside Q1's single dawn. Each instrument concedes the same thing from a different angle: the reading text alone cannot carry this passage's truth, because the truth is that there are two passages. Students citing the crux in essays should cite both addresses, the Friar's by number, 2.3.1-4, and Romeo's by document and position, Q2 at the close of 2.2, a double citation that is not pedantry but accuracy, the bibliography of the passage written into the reference itself.

The actors' documents tell the same story from below. Elizabethan players learned their roles from parts, rolls containing a single actor's lines with short cues, extracted by a scribe from the company's full copy. A part is a resolution machine even more ruthless than a prompt book: the scribe copying Romeo's roll had to write one dawn speech or none, since a part containing both versions would have been unlearnable nonsense, two answers to one cue. Whatever state the full manuscript was in, the parts system forced the choice at the moment of extraction, and the choice, once embodied in the Friar's part and omitted from Romeo's, became the play the company owned. The doublet could therefore exist in only one class of document, the author's full papers and papers copied faithfully from them. It is a fossil with a precise habitat, and its survival in Q2 tells us which habitat Q2's copy came from more certainly than any argument about handwriting could: the full, unresolved manuscript, the one document in the play's economy that nobody had yet been forced to tidy.

Fame has ratified the asymmetry the parts system created. Dictionaries of quotations carry the dawn quatrain under the Friar's name and the 2.3 address; no anthology prints Romeo's version as a freestanding poem, and the draft survives in the culture only as a story told about the revision, never as a text quoted for its own sake. The draft is not negligible verse. "From forth daies pathway, made by Tytans wheeles" has its own crepuscular music, and in a counterfactual history where the Friar's scene was never written, Romeo's quatrain would be anthologized as the balcony scene's dawn chorus and schoolchildren would learn it by heart. The accident of supersession, not any failure of quality, exiled it to the apparatus. That exile is the doublet's final lesson about how canons form around a text: the version that enters the play enters memory, and the version that stays in the manuscript enters only the notes, however fine it is, however much it reveals. Four lines of Shakespeare, good enough for any anthology, live permanently in a footnote to themselves.

## The Doublet in Company: Standing Revision Across the Early Texts

The dawn doublet is the most instructive survival of its kind, but it is not the only one, and placing it in company sharpens what it proves. The early printed texts of the plays preserve revision at every scale, from the single word to the whole alternative version, and the R&J quatrain sits at the scale where the process is easiest to read: large enough to show direction, small enough to hold entire in the mind.

At the smallest scale stand the conflated lines, of which this play's own "Rauenous douefeatherd rauen, woluishrauening lamb" (3.2) is the type specimen: two states of a phrase occupying one line because the replacement arrived before the replaced had been struck. Such lines are the doublet's atoms. They demonstrate that the copy behind the affected books habitually carried superseded matter in live positions, and they answer, by their frequency, the suggestion that the dawn passage requires some special catastrophe to explain it. No catastrophe is required. A drafting culture in which first thoughts survived inside single lines will, given four thousand lines of verse, eventually preserve a whole quatrain the same way.

At the middle scale stands *Love's Labour's Lost*, where Berowne's speech in 4.3 survives in two full versions, as the tribunal section noted: a whole paragraph of composition preserved twice, the second attempt standing after the first in the printed book. The LLL case matters to the doublet beyond mere analogy, because its book was printed in 1598, a year before Q2, from copy that scholars likewise take to stand close to the author. Two books, two printing houses' worth of transmission, the same phenomenon: the accident is not Creede's, and it is not Burby's. It belongs to the papers, and to a period in which a dramatist's manuscript could reach a printer with its alternatives still embodied, because the culture of the playhouse valued the play as a going concern rather than as a monument requiring a fair copy.

At the largest scale stand the two-text plays, where revision separates into whole alternative documents. *King Lear* survives in the 1608 quarto and the 1623 Folio in versions differing by hundreds of lines and a thousand variants, a divergence that Steven Urkowitz's *Shakespeare's Revision of King Lear* (1980) taught the profession to read as authorial revision rather than corruption, and that the Oxford Shakespeare of 1986 honoured by printing two Lears. *Othello* presents the same architecture at a smaller span, and E. A. J. Honigmann's *The Texts of Othello and Shakespearian Revision* argued its variants into a history of the author returning to his play. The dawn doublet belongs recognizably to this family and instructively differs from it. In Lear and Othello the revision produced two documents, and the argument runs between books. In the dawn passage the revision produced two states inside one document, and the argument runs within a single opening of the second quarto, where no theory of transmission can intervene between the evidence and the eye. That is the doublet's unique evidentiary gift. The large cases require the reader to trust a reconstruction across editions; the small case shows the same process in vitro, draft and revision under one glass.

The census also disciplines the doublet's claims in the other direction. If standing revision is a general feature of the period's play texts, then its presence in Q2 cannot, by itself, certify the romantic version of the foul papers story, the lone author and his untouched autograph, which is Werstine's caution returning with reinforcements. What the census certifies is a culture: plays were written forward, revised in motion, and printed from papers at every degree of tidiness, and the texts that survive are snapshots taken at arbitrary points in that motion. The second quarto of this play happens to be a snapshot taken very early in the tidying, early enough to catch a famous passage twice. Other snapshots catch other plays at other moments. The doublet's fame is the fame of the clearest photograph, not of a unique event, and its evidence is strongest exactly where it is most ordinary.

## What the Crux Reveals About the Play's Origins: A Verdict

The verdict can now be drawn together. The duplicated dawn lines of the second quarto are a preserved act of authorial revision: a quatrain composed for Romeo at the close of the balcony scene, then recomposed, tightened, transposed, and set burning, as the opening of the Friar's soliloquy when the next scene was written. The revision survived into print because the copy behind Q2 stood close enough to the act of composition to contain both states of the passage, and because nobody in the chain from desk to printing house executed the cancellation that would have left the book clean. Whether that copy is called, with Greg, the author's foul papers, or described more cautiously, with the skeptics, as copy bearing unresolved authorial revision, the doublet's testimony is the same in substance: the text of *Romeo and Juliet* that founded the tradition was not a finished artefact handed down entire, but a working document whose second thoughts had not all been reconciled with its first ones, and the most famous love tragedy in the language reaches its readers through an editorial rescue operation that has been running, with changing rules, since 1632.

Three consequences follow, one for each constituency the crux touches. For the reader, the doublet is an instruction in how to hold the play: the clean text on the page of a modern edition is a reconstruction, excellent and necessary, and the notes beneath it are not pedantry but the exposed wiring of the reconstruction, nowhere more worth reading than at 2.3.1-4, where four familiar lines conceal four suppressed ones. For the editor, the doublet is the limiting case that tests every theory of copy-text: Greg's rationale, which follows the copy for accidentals and judges substantives on their merits, finds here a substantive that is two substantives, and the judgment it demands, revision over draft, is as much literary as bibliographical, which is why Gibbons could dissent in good faith and why Weis's dissent fails only in its arguing, not in its existence. For the student of the play's making, the doublet is the antidote to the two sentimentalities that between them govern the play's popular image: the sentimentality of the flawless classic, a text that descended perfect, and the sentimentality of the blotted genius, a writer whose fluency never looked back. The papers blotted. The pen moved forward anyway. In the space between those two facts, eight lines long, the whole real history of how this play got written is briefly, uniquely visible, and the cliche of the clean text dissolves. Restoring the crux shows the author revising on the page, and a play that can survive that sight, and gain by it, needs no protection from its own origins.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch duplication crux, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch duplication crux is the side-by-side reading of the two dawn quatrains the second quarto prints at the junction of the balcony scene and the Friar's cell, Romeo's version and the Friar's, set line against line so that every agreement and difference can be inspected at once. The pattern it exposes is the pattern of a draft revising itself: two lines identical in substance, one line transposed into better order, and one line rewritten from explanation into image, "pathway, made by Tytans wheeles" becoming "path, and Titans burning wheeles." Read whole, the table shows composition happening in a fixed direction, from the looser to the tighter, and that direction is the evidence on which the revision account of the doublet stands.

### Q: How many lines does the second quarto print twice where the balcony scene hands over to the Friar's cell?

The second quarto prints four lines twice, a complete quatrain, once inside Romeo's farewell at the close of the balcony scene and once as the opening of the Friar's soliloquy. The four lines are the dawn description beginning "The grey eyde morne smiles on the frowning night" and ending with Titan's wheels. Because the two settings stand in different speeches, the duplication is easy to miss in a reading text that has silently resolved it, and most readers of modern editions never learn that Romeo once spoke a version of the Friar's dawn. Counting both settings, the passage occupies eight printed lines in Q2 for four lines of verse, which is why editors call the phenomenon a doublet: one composition, two impressions.

### Q: Which words differ between Romeo's dawn quatrain and the Friar's, and which lines are word for word the same?

The first two lines are word for word the same in substance, differing only in spelling, "Clouds" against "clowdes." The real variants crowd into the last two lines. In the third line Romeo's version reads "darknesse fleckted," with the noun before a mangled participle, while the Friar's reads "fleckled darknesse," participle before noun and the participle repaired. In the fourth line Romeo's dawn moves "from forth daies pathway, made by Tytans wheeles," while the Friar's moves "from forth daies path, and Titans burning wheeles": pathway contracts to path, the explanatory "made by" disappears, and the wheels acquire the adjective "burning." The differences are few, exact, and all of one kind, the tidying and intensifying of a sentence by its own author.

### Q: Which dawn version did Shakespeare draft first, and what evidence fixes the order?

Romeo's version was drafted first, and two kinds of evidence fix the order. The first is integration: the Friar's quatrain is load-bearing, opening a soliloquy that continues out of its materials into the sun, the dew, and the herb gathering, while Romeo's version is detachable, interrupting a farewell that is complete without it, and revision characteristically moves material from a detachable position into a structural one. The second is texture: the variants run from rough to smooth, from the tangled "darknesse fleckted" to the clean "fleckled darknesse," and from an explanatory fourth line to an imagistic one, and transmission corrupts in the opposite direction, smooth to rough. A copyist degrades; only an author improves in this direction, which is why the order of the two versions is as secure as any inference in the play's textual history.

### Q: Why do the small differences between the two versions rule out a compositor's eye-skip?

The differences rule out eye-skip because eye-skip is a mechanical accident that reproduces the words before the compositor's eye, and the second dawn quatrain does not reproduce the first. Gabriel Egan states the logic cleanly: had the two settings been identical, a wandering eye landing twice on the same passage would explain them, but a setter whose eye skipped could not set "fleckled darknesse" from copy reading "darknesse fleckted," nor invent "Titans burning wheeles" where his copy read "made by Tytans wheeles." The variants exist nowhere for an eye to find; they had to be composed. Eye-skip therefore explains a duplication Q2 does not contain, an exact repeat, and fails for the duplication it does contain, a revised one.

### Q: What does the standing doublet imply about the manuscript handed to Thomas Creede's printing house in 1599?

The standing doublet implies that the manuscript handed to Creede's house contained both states of the dawn passage with the earlier state unresolved, neither cleanly cancelled nor reconciled, so that the copy stood at most one remove from the act of composition. A fair copy prepared for the press, or a prompt book prepared for the stage, would have forced a choice between the versions, since a transcript must transcribe something and a performance must speak something. Only a working document, the author's draft or a transcript faithful to a draft's disorder, could carry both versions forward indifferently. The doublet does not by itself prove whose hand wrote the copy, a point the skeptics have established, but it fixes the copy's character: unreconciled, compositional, and closer to the desk than to the stage.

### Q: Where does the doublet rank among the signs that the second quarto was set from the author's working papers?

The doublet ranks first among those signs, because it is the only one that shows sequence. The wavering speech prefixes, Romeo shrinking to Rom. and Ro., show haste but not authorship. The stretch of Act 1 that Q2 reprints from Q1, at 1.2.55 to 1.3.37, shows defective copy but not whose copy was defective. The fused line in Juliet's oxymoron speech, "Rauenous douefeatherd rauen," shows two thoughts colliding but inside a single line, where a compositor's dittography might still be pleaded. The dawn doublet alone presents a complete unit of verse in two authorially distinct states, in an order that can be established, at the joint between two scenes. W. W. Greg's foul papers case for Q2 rests on the syndrome, but the doublet is the exhibit that converts the syndrome from a description into a narrative: a passage written, then rewritten, then printed twice.

### Q: Why does Paul Werstine deny that the doublet can certify foul papers?

Paul Werstine denies it because, in his argument, the category of foul papers was built from the very features it is then used to explain, and no authorial manuscript of a Shakespeare play survives to test the profile independently. A duplication in Q2 is a fact; calling the copy foul papers is a narrative laid over the fact, complete with an author who failed to cancel and a manuscript nobody has seen. Werstine's essay "Narratives About Printed Shakespeare Texts" (1990) insists that the discipline distinguish the evidence from the story assembled around it. The denial is methodological, not factual: Werstine does not claim the passage was printed once, or that its variants are compositorial. He claims that the doublet certifies a duplication in the printer's copy, and that everything beyond that certification, including the attractive word "foul," is inference wearing the clothes of proof.

### Q: Why does the first quarto print the dawn quatrain once only, and what does its silence about a Romeo version prove and not prove?

The first quarto prints the quatrain once only because the text it transmits, whatever its route from stage or memory to print, is a performable disposition of the play, and no performable disposition can speak the same dawn twice within a page. Its silence about a Romeo version proves that the play as Q1 knows it had already resolved the duplication in the Friar's favour, which agrees with the revision account: the second thoughts were the ones that entered the play's working life. The silence does not prove that Romeo's version was never written, since Q1 is shorter than Q2 throughout and omits much that is certainly authorial, nor does it date the revision. Emma Smith's summary remains the cleanest statement of the fact itself: in Q1 there is no repetition, and only the Friar has the speech.

### Q: What did the Second Folio of 1632 do with the duplicated quatrain?

The Second Folio resolved the duplication by assigning the quatrain to Romeo and withholding it from the Friar, so that the dawn lines stand in Romeo's farewell and the Friar's soliloquy opens without them. The choice is the reverse of the modern settlement, which keeps the Friar's version and deletes Romeo's, and it shows an early corrector of the text treating the doublet as an attribution problem rather than a draft survival: one speech had to own the lines, and F2 awarded them to the first claimant. The decision then slept inside the folio tradition until René Weis, editing the play for the Arden third series in 2012, followed F2 and gave the quatrain to Romeo rather than the Friar, reviving a seventeenth-century judgement that the intervening centuries of quarto-based editing had set aside.

### Q: Why did Brian Gibbons restore the dawn quatrain to Romeo's closing speech in his Arden edition?

Brian Gibbons restored the quatrain to Romeo's speech on literary grounds: the dawn description suits the voice of the play's great poet of light, the Romeo of "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2), and it answers to the lovers' other dawn parting, in 3.5, where daybreak imagery again gathers around the pair in the orchard world. His 1980 Arden text prints the lines where Q2 places them, inside the farewell, and the surprise the placement caused, recorded by a teacher writing to the SHAKSPER list in 1998 after meeting the lines in the middle of Romeo's speech for the first time, measures how completely the deleting consensus had trained readers to expect the quatrain only at the Friar's entrance. Gibbons's edition remains the settlement's most respectable breach.

### Q: Why does René Weis give the quatrain to Romeo rather than the Friar, and what did Emma Smith find missing from his defense?

René Weis gives the quatrain to Romeo, following the Second Folio, on the stated ground that "the lines hardly fit with the Friar's meditation on medicinal herbalism," so his Arden third series text of 2012 opens the Friar's soliloquy downstream of the dawn. Emma Smith, reviewing the edition in the *Review of English Studies*, found the bibliography missing: no substantive discussion of why the speech appears twice in Q2, nothing on the idea of authorial second thoughts, and no reason given for ignoring the first quarto's simple clarification, in which only the Friar speaks the lines. Her objection is procedural and decisive as far as it goes. An editor may assign the quatrain where judgment leads, but a crux of this fame has to be argued from the documents, and Weis's defense argues from a preference about herbalism instead.

### Q: Which dawn quatrain do the Oxford, Cambridge and Folger texts print, and where can Romeo's version still be read in them?

The Oxford, Cambridge and Folger texts all print the Friar's quatrain, in its revised form, as the opening of 2.3, and none of them prints Romeo's version in the text of 2.2, where Romeo's farewell closes with the sleep-and-peace wish and the vow to visit the Friar. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000, G. Blakemore Evans's Cambridge edition of 1984, and the Folger Shakespeare edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine are at one on the point, which is the modern settlement in its standard form. Romeo's version survives in these editions in the textual notes and commentary, where the deletion is recorded and the quarto spellings can be inspected, and it survives entire in parallel-text presentations such as Jay L. Halio's edition of the two quartos, which prints Q2 as it stands.

### Q: Does the second quarto itself mark a scene break between Romeo's exit and the Friar's entrance?

The second quarto does not mark the break with any scene heading, because Q2 prints the play continuously, without the numbered acts and scenes that modern editions supply and readers mistake for part of the original book. The junction is carried entirely by stage directions: Romeo's Exit after the farewell couplet, and then "Enter Frier alone with a basket." The familiar division, 2.2 closing in the orchard and 2.3 opening at the cell, descends from the Folio tradition and its eighteenth-century regularizers, who converted entrances and exits into an architecture of numbered scenes. The fact matters for the crux, because the doublet straddles exactly that unmarked joint: the passage was revised when the writing crossed from one scene unit to the next, and the quarto's continuous page preserves the crossing without comment.

### Q: How does Juliet's conflated "ravenous dove-feathered raven" line show the same drafting habit inside a single line?

Juliet's line shows the habit in miniature because the second quarto prints it as "Rauenous douefeatherd rauen, woluishrauening lamb" (3.2), a single verse line containing both the first thought, "ravenous," and the thought that replaced it, the dove-feathered raven set against the wolvish-ravening lamb. Editors print "Dove-feathered raven! wolvish-ravening lamb!" only by deleting the superseded word, performing in one line the same operation the dawn doublet requires at paragraph scale: recognize two authorial states fused in the copy, keep the later, and record the earlier. Peter Ackroyd cites the line for exactly this purpose, as the process of the author's thought made visible inside a line. The parallel matters because it answers the objection that the dawn doublet is too strange to be a draft survival. The same book preserves the same phenomenon at two scales, and a habit explains both better than two unrelated accidents.

### Q: What does the doubled speech in Love's Labour's Lost add to the case that the dawn doublet is authorial revision?

The doubled speech in *Love's Labour's Lost* adds an independent specimen of the same accident in another play from the same years: Berowne's great speech in 4.3 survives in two versions, one markedly more lyrical than the other, both printed, as though a second attempt, added in a margin or on a separate sheet, reached the printing house without the first being cancelled clearly enough to stop it. Ackroyd pairs the cases, and the pairing does real work. A single duplication might be a freak of one shop or one manuscript; the recurrence of the pattern, a superseded version and its improvement standing together in print, in two plays printed within a year of each other, describes a workshop habit rather than an accident. Shakespeare's drafts, on the evidence of both books, carried their second thoughts forward without reliably striking their first ones, and printers printed what the drafts carried.

### Q: Could Shakespeare have intended the dawn quatrain as a deliberate echo between the lovers' night and the Friar's morning?

The deliberate echo is the one explanation the evidence excludes with something approaching completeness. A designed echo must survive into performance, and no performance can speak the same quatrain from two mouths inside a page without the repetition reading as error; correspondingly, the first quarto, the play's other early text, knows nothing of a repetition and gives the dawn to the Friar alone. A designed echo also varies its terms for meaning, throwing repeated language into new light, whereas the doublet's variants are the static of composition, a transposed participle, a repaired misprint, a half-line recomposed for energy. The changes point back to a desk, not out to an audience. Readers reach for the echo reading because waste in a canonical text feels like a scandal; the doublet is better honoured as what it is, the record of a passage the author improved and moved, whose first state the documents happened to keep.

### Q: What did Ben Jonson say the players claimed about Shakespeare's blotting, and how does the doublet complicate the claim?

Ben Jonson reported in his *Discoveries* that the players made it a point of honour that Shakespeare, in his writing, "never blotted out line," and Jonson's own retort was "would he had blotted a thousand," a complaint that fluency had become carelessness. The doublet complicates both halves of the exchange. Against the players, it preserves a passage that was blotted in effect, superseded by its own improvement four lines of scene later, so the author did revise, visibly and well. For the players, it shows why the myth was believable: the revision was never executed as a blot, since the second thought was written forward into the next scene and the first was never struck through, so the papers could reach the printer carrying both, and a company handling such papers might honestly remember a writer whose pages showed no cancellations. Jonson wished for a thousand blots. The second quarto preserves the one that matters most, unmade.

### Q: How can an essay use the InsightCrunch duplication crux to argue that the play's text preserves a draft in motion?

An essay can use the duplication crux as its central exhibit by building from the table outward: begin with the two quatrains set side by side, establish that their differences are revisions rather than copyists' errors, since an eye-skip cannot transpose a participle or set a pair of wheels burning, and then argue that a printed text preserving a passage in two authorial states is a text whose copy stood inside the process of composition. The supporting exhibits arrange themselves in widening rings, the conflated line in 3.2 showing the same habit at line scale, the wavering speech prefixes and the Act 1 reprint patch showing the copy's general untidiness, and the first quarto's single dawn showing the resolved state the play reached in performance. The essay's claim should be stated at the strength the evidence bears: not that every word of Q2 is autograph, but that at this junction the book preserves a draft in the act of revising itself.

### Q: Why is the dawn doublet famous out of proportion to its four lines?

The doublet is famous out of proportion to its size because it is the most legible specimen of something otherwise unrecoverable: Shakespeare in the act of revising, caught at the joint of the play's two most famous scenes. No autograph manuscript of the play survives, and the general run of textual evidence is static, variant readings that show difference without direction. The doublet shows direction, a first thought and its improvement in establishable order, with the improvement still warm from the scene it was written to open. It also sits at the exact point where the play's popular image, the flawless classic, meets its documentary reality, a working text imperfectly tidied, so that four lines have become the standard classroom proof that the plays readers inherit are rescues as well as creations. Fame, in this case, is proportionate after all: not to the lines, but to the visibility they give a process that everywhere else has to be taken on trust.
