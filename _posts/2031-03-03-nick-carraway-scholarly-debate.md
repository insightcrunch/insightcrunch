Is Nick gay? The scholarly argument over that question has run for decades without producing a winner, and the absence of a winner is the claim this article defends. Nick Carraway narrates The Great Gatsby without ever naming his own desire, and a long line of critics has treated that silence as evidence: evidence of coding, evidence of reticence, evidence of a deliberate gap the novel refuses to close. What follows is a map of that argument, position by position, with the textual moments each side cites and the interpretations each side draws from them, presented evenhandedly and without adjudication. The complications are real, the stakes for how we read are genuine, and declaring the question settled in either direction is the one move every honest reader of the debate should refuse.

The namable claim is simple enough to state and difficult enough to honor. The text leaves Nick Carraway's sexuality open, scholars keep arguing about it, and the debate maps competing readings of the same textual moments rather than competing bodies of evidence. That makes this a rare kind of critical dispute: both sides read the same pages, quote the same sentences, and arrive at incompatible conclusions, because the novel supplies ambiguity and withholds the one sentence that would resolve it. This article owns that map. It does not decide the question, and it does not pretend that refusing to decide is a dodge; the refusal is the position the evidence supports.

A word on method before the moments themselves. This article names scholars only where the attribution can be verified: Leslie Fiedler's 1948 essay on homoerotic bonding in American fiction, Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's 1985 account of male homosocial desire, Judith Fetterley's 1978 resistant feminist reading of the American canon, and George Chauncey's historical scholarship on the gay male world of Jazz Age New York. Where a position circulates in the criticism without a single verifiable author, the article uses generic attribution, and that is the honest choice. No scholar appears on both sides of the map. No invented critic, no invented quotation, and no invented endorsement enters these pages. The textual evidence comes from the novel's own sentences, cited by chapter, and every reading of those sentences is presented as a reading, not a fact.

## Is Nick Gay? The Scholarly Argument in Outline

The debate has a shape, and the shape matters more than any single citation. On one side, queer-theory readings treat Nick as coded gay, a closeted or semi-closeted narrator whose narration leaks desire he cannot name. On the other, skeptical readings treat Nick as a conventionally masculine narrator of his period whose ambiguities are matters of reticence, drunkenness, and narrative economy rather than sexuality. Between those poles sit four further positions: the homosocial reading, which finds intense male bonds without claiming gay identity; the deliberate-ambiguity reading, which credits Fitzgerald with engineering the gap; the historicist reading, which measures Nick against the actual codes of 1920s New York; and the narrative-function reading, which treats the ambiguity as a device serving theme and narration. The sections below give each position its strongest case, then its strongest counter, because a map that flatters one side is not a map at all.

### What happens in the McKee scene in Chapter 2?

At the end of Chapter 2, a drunken Nick leaves a Manhattan apartment party in the elevator with Mr. McKee, a photographer he met that afternoon. The narration breaks off with a row of three dots, then resumes with Nick standing beside McKee's bed while McKee sits up between the sheets in his underwear.

The scene is the debate's ground zero, and it earns that status through a precise sequence of small strangenesses. Nick meets Mr. McKee at Tom and Myrtle's apartment party in Manhattan, and the text introduces McKee with a description critics have quoted for decades: "Mr. McKee was a pale feminine man from the flat below." The adjective choice matters because the novel rarely attaches "feminine" to a man, and Nick, a narrator economical with epithets, spends one on McKee's paleness and femininity rather than on anything McKee says or does. The party proceeds through whiskey, quarrels, and Tom's broken-nose violence toward Myrtle, and by the evening's end Nick is drunk enough to announce that he is going home, only to be drawn into the elevator with McKee, who invites him up to see his photographs.

What happens next is not narrated. The elevator exchange, the boy's warning about the lever and McKee's dignified reply that he did not know he was touching it, gives way to the famous ellipsis, three spaced dots that mark the only missing hours in Nick's otherwise continuous narration. Then the scene returns: "I was standing beside his bed and he was sitting up between the sheets, clad in his underwear, with a great portfolio in his hands." The portfolio of photographs supplies the stated reason for the visit, and the next morning Nick is at Pennsylvania Station, waiting for the four o'clock train, describing the scene with the flat affect of a man reporting nothing in particular. Every position in the debate must do something with this passage, because it is the one place where Nick's narration visibly swerves around an event it refuses to describe.

### Why does Nick wipe lather from McKee's cheek?

Hours after meeting McKee, Nick notices a spot of shaving lather on the man's cheek that has been worrying him all afternoon, and he takes out his handkerchief and wipes it away. Queer-theory readings treat the gesture as tender, almost loverlike attention; skeptical readings treat it as a drunken fussiness magnified by the party's chaos.

The full sentence repays slow attention: "Taking out my handkerchief I wiped from his cheek the remains of the spot of dried lather that had worried me all the afternoon." Two features give the sentence its charge. The first is the duration of the worry: Nick has been carrying this small spot of lather in his attention all afternoon, a sustained noticing that the text records nowhere else with such tenderness toward a near stranger. The second is the physical intimacy of the act: wiping another man's face with a handkerchief is a gesture of care that crosses the ordinary boundaries between men who met hours earlier, and Nick performs it while McKee sleeps in a chair, "like a photograph of a man of action," fists clenched in his lap. The queer-coded reading finds here a narrator betraying an attachment his conscious mind will not name. The skeptical reading finds a drunk man fussing over a trivial detail, the kind of compulsive tidying drunks perform, and notes that tenderness toward a sleeping acquaintance is not evidence of desire. The historicist reading splits the difference and asks what such a gesture signified in 1922, when codes of male physical intimacy differed from later norms and the gesture's meaning cannot be read straight off a later century's assumptions.

### How does Nick describe Tom Buchanan's body?

Nick gives Tom's physique a long, admiring inventory: a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty with a rather hard mouth, shining arrogant eyes, and enormous physical power that even riding clothes cannot hide. Readers on one side see desire leaking through the description; readers on the other see a narrator establishing Tom's brutish dominance before the story begins.

The passage arrives in Chapter 1, before Nick has any reason to hate Tom, and its sensuousness is hard to miss: "Now he was a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner. Two shining arrogant eyes had established dominance over his face and gave him the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward. Not even the effeminate swank of his riding clothes could hide the enormous power of that body." The queer-coded position lingers on the word "effeminate," which appears here attached to Tom's riding clothes just as "feminine" attaches to McKee, and on the sustained attention to Tom's body, which exceeds anything Nick gives Daisy's or Jordan's bodies in the same chapter. The counter-reading answers that the description's function is characterization, not confession: Nick is fixing Tom in the reader's mind as a physical bully whose body does his arguing, and the "power of that body" is a threat assessment, not a love letter. Both readings can quote the same sentence, which is exactly why the debate persists. The passage also establishes a pattern the debate returns to repeatedly: Nick's male portraits run longer, hotter, and more detailed than his female portraits, and each side of the argument has a story about why.

## The Queer-Coded Reading: Nick as a Closeted Narrator

The strongest version of the queer-coded reading begins with an accumulation. The McKee ellipsis, the lather wiping, the long admiring inventories of Tom's body and Gatsby's smile, Daisy's teasing remark that Nick reminds her of a rose, an absolute rose, Nick's cool and perfunctory romances with Jordan Baker and the girl from the accounting department in Jersey City, the housemate who was supposed to share the West Egg bungalow but was ordered to Washington at the last minute, leaving Nick alone: no single item proves anything, but the reading argues that the pattern has a direction. Nick notices men with a sustained, sensuous attention he never grants women. He narrates a night spent in another man's bedroom by not narrating it. He ends his affairs with women by letting them blow quietly away. The reading's wager is that Fitzgerald, writing in 1925, could not have a narrator announce same-sex desire and therefore encoded it in the texture of attention, the distribution of adjectives, and the strategic silences, trusting a future readership to decode what a contemporary one might only feel.

This reading treats the novel's famous reticences as the evidence rather than the absence of evidence. Nick's announcement in Chapter 1 that reserving judgments is a matter of infinite hope becomes, on this account, a narrator's confession that his moral posture is a strategy for managing what he cannot say. His celebrated unreliability, which the series' character study examines in detail, becomes functional: a narrator who misdirects about his own desire is precisely the kind of narrator who would mythologize Gatsby, excuse his own complicity, and present his fascination as loyalty. The reading finds its emotional center in Chapter 8, when Nick tells Gatsby, "You're worth the whole damn bunch put together," and in the final pages, where Nick's grief for Gatsby outlasts every other attachment in the book. The Jordan affair, which ends with Nick's flat declaration that he thought she was honest and straightforward until he met another bad driver, reads on this account as a man performing heterosexuality badly, going through motions his heart never joined. The reading does not claim the novel shouts its secret; it claims the novel whispers it, and that the whisper was the loudest register available.

The position draws strength from a long critical tradition of finding coded queerness in canonical American fiction, and its practitioners point to the novel's own vocabulary as confirmation. "Feminine" and "effeminate" appear at charged moments, attached to McKee and to Tom's riding clothes, and the reading hears in those adjectives a period code for male same-sex desire that a 1925 readership would have recognized. The ellipsis after the elevator scene becomes the period's signature move, the place where the text performs the very closet it is describing. For readers who accept the accumulation, the reading reorganizes the whole novel: Nick's famous moralizing about Gatsby's dream becomes a lover's apology, his contempt for Tom and Daisy becomes the resentment of the excluded, and the closing meditation on the green light becomes an elegy for a desire the narrator could never claim in daylight.

For the full development of this lens, with its close readings of the coded passages, [the series' dedicated study of the queer-coded Nick](/2027/10/18/is-nick-carraway-queer-coded/) is the natural companion, and readers who want the broader method behind the reading should consult [the series' queer-reading overview](/2027/10/11/queer-reading-great-gatsby/), which places this interpretation inside the critical approach that produced it.

### Why do queer-theory readings treat Gatsby's smile as evidence?

When Nick first describes Gatsby's smile, he calls it one of those rare smiles with a quality of eternal reassurance in it, language of rapture he never uses for Daisy or Jordan. Queer-theory readings hear erotic admiration in that rapture; rival readings hear a narrator mythologizing his subject, the same inflation that makes Gatsby's parties and shirts glow.

The smile passage deserves its reputation as the debate's second front. "He had one of those rare smiles with a quality of eternal reassurance in it, that you may come across four or five times in life. It faced, or seemed to face, the whole external world for an instant and then concentrated on you with an irresistible prejudice in your favor." The queer-coded reading attends to the erotic register of "irresistible prejudice," the bodily immediacy of a smile that "concentrated on you," and the superlative rarity Nick assigns to the experience. Nick describes no woman this way, the reading notes, and the asymmetry is the point: the narrator's most rhapsodic prose is spent on a man's face. The counter-reading, developed more fully in the deliberate-ambiguity section below, answers that the smile passage is myth-making machinery, the prose equivalent of the green light, and that Nick inflates everything attached to Gatsby because Gatsby is the novel's object of wonder, not its object of desire. The two readings then dispute the same adjectives, the same superlatives, the same "you," and the text offers no tiebreaker.

## The Homosocial Reading: Desire Without Identity

The homosocial reading accepts much of the queer-coded reading's evidence and draws a different conclusion. Drawing on Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's framework, this position argues that Nick's intense bonds with men need not resolve into a gay identity to be erotically charged, because the novel's social world runs on bonds between men that are structured by desire and policed by prohibition at the same time. On this account, Nick can be fascinated by Gatsby, tender toward McKee, and admiring of Tom's body without being gay in any identity sense, because the text is mapping a structure of male feeling rather than diagnosing a character. The reading's power is that it explains the evidence without requiring the text to confirm a label the text never uses.

The position's signature move is the erotic triangle. Sedgwick's account treats the triangle of two men and one woman as the basic unit of male social bonding: desire flows between the men, and the woman serves as the conduit through which that desire is expressed and disavowed. Gatsby criticism has found the template everywhere in the novel. Nick and Gatsby bond over Daisy, with Daisy functioning as the occasion for a male intimacy that neither man could sustain directly. Nick and Tom circle Jordan and Myrtle in configurations where the men's rivalry matters more than the women's presence. Tom and Gatsby's confrontation at the Plaza is legible as two men fighting over a woman who is really the medium of their contest with each other. On this reading, the McKee scene fits the pattern rather than breaking it: Nick's night with McKee is a bond between men conducted outside the triangle's usual routing, which is why the narration cannot process it and marks it with an ellipsis instead. The reading does not ask what Nick is; it asks how the novel's men relate, and it finds the answer in structures rather than identities.

The homosocial reading also absorbs the biographical caution that the queer-coded reading sometimes skips. It does not need Fitzgerald to have intended a gay Nick, and it does not need 1925 to have possessed a modern identity category, because it is describing a social structure, not outing a character. Its practitioners point out that the novel's men are constantly touching, watching, admiring, and competing with one another in ways the text presents as ordinary, and that this ordinariness is the finding: the line between admiration and desire, between rivalry and fascination, is drawn by social prohibition rather than by feeling, and the novel shows the drawing of the line in real time. Nick's narration, on this account, is not a closet but a register, a way of writing male feeling in a world that had not yet settled what that feeling was allowed to be.

The counter to the homosocial reading comes from both flanks. The queer-coded position argues that the reading explains away the most pointed evidence, the bedroom, the lather, the ellipsis, by dissolving it into structure, and that a structure that produces exactly the symptoms of the closet might simply be the closet described at one remove. The heteronormative-baseline position argues the reverse: that the reading imports erotic charge into ordinary male friendship and rivalry, and that not every intense male bond in literature is a thesis about desire. The homosocial reading's defenders answer that the charge is the point, that the novel is interested in precisely the zone where friendship becomes fascination, and that refusing to name that zone is itself a critical choice with a history.

### What is Sedgwick's idea of homosocial desire?

In Between Men, published in 1985, Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick argued that male bonds in literature sit on a continuum between the homosocial and the homosexual, with erotic triangles routing desire between men through a woman. Later Gatsby critics used that framework to read the Nick-Gatsby-Daisy triangle as a bond between men conducted through Daisy, without calling Nick gay.

A careful note on attribution is required here, because this debate has suffered from loose citation. Sedgwick's Between Men, published by Columbia University Press in 1985, is a study of English literature, and its famous analyses concern Victorian novels and their erotic triangles; the book did not set out to adjudicate Nick Carraway's sexuality. What it did was supply a vocabulary, homosocial desire, the continuum, the triangle, that Gatsby criticism adopted wholesale in the years after its publication, because the vocabulary fit the novel's male configurations better than any available alternative. The distinction matters: when this article says the homosocial reading draws on Sedgwick, it means the reading uses her framework, not that she authored the reading. The framework's fit is nevertheless the reason the position has endured. It gives critics a way to take Nick's male fascinations seriously without forcing them into an identity claim the text will not support, and it explains why the debate's evidence is structural, a pattern across the novel, rather than a single smoking gun.

## Deliberate Ambiguity: The Engineered Gap

The deliberate-ambiguity reading shifts the question from what Nick is to what the text is doing. On this account, Fitzgerald engineered the gap around Nick's desire as a formal device: the ellipsis, the reticence, the asymmetrical portraits, and the strategic silences are not symptoms of a secret but instruments of a narrative strategy, and the novel is designed to sustain multiple readings without authorizing any of them. The reading's motto is that the question is the artifact. The text does not fail to answer whether Nick is gay; it succeeds at making the question unanswerable, and that success is doing thematic work.

The position builds its case from Fitzgerald's craft elsewhere in the novel. The Great Gatsby is a book built out of controlled gaps: Gatsby's past is a patchwork of rumor and self-invention that the narration never fully resolves, the green light's meaning is never fixed by authorial fiat, and the novel's most famous sentences achieve their power through strategic withholding rather than exhaustive explanation. Nick's desire, on this reading, belongs to the same design. A narrator who announced his orientation would collapse the novel's field of interpretation into a single channel; a narrator whose desire is legible but unconfirmed keeps every reader's attention on the act of reading itself, which is where Fitzgerald wants it. The ellipsis after the elevator is then not a closet door but a narrative device, the prose equivalent of a cut in film, marking the one interval Nick chooses not to narrate because his narration is everywhere selective, and the selection is the point.

This reading has a distinctive answer to the accumulation argument. It grants the queer-coded reading its inventory, the lather, the smile, the male portraits, the cool romances, and reads the inventory as the deliberate construction of a suggestive field, a set of signals that point in several directions at once. The wiped lather suggests tenderness; it also suggests drunken fussiness; the text declines to choose. Tom's body suggests admiration; it also suggests threat assessment; the text declines to choose. The reading's defenders argue that this is not evasion but technique, the same technique that makes the novel's symbolism inexhaustible, and that demanding a resolution mistakes a reader's desire for clarity for the text's obligation to provide it. The novel's greatness, on this account, includes its resistance to the very question the debate keeps asking.

The counter is that deliberate ambiguity is unfalsifiable and therefore explains too much. The queer-coded position argues that the reading mistakes the closet's signature for the author's signature, that a text can be deliberately ambiguous and still be ambiguous about something, and that the something here has a direction the inventory reveals. The historicist position adds that "deliberate" is doing unlicensed work: the reading credits Fitzgerald with a postmodern self-consciousness about interpretive openness that the 1925 text may not support, and it risks turning every gap in the novel into a masterstroke. The ambiguity reading's reply is that the charge of unfalsifiability applies equally to every position in the debate, since no position can produce the resolving sentence, and that the reading at least has the virtue of explaining why the evidence is permanently inconclusive rather than pretending the inconclusiveness is an accident.

## The Historicist Reading: Codes, Closets, and 1922 New York

The historicist reading measures Nick against the world he actually inhabited, and it cuts both ways, which is why its practitioners consider it the debate's most honest position. The novel is set in the summer of 1922 and published in 1925, in a New York where, as George Chauncey's historical scholarship documents, a visible gay male world of bars, slang, rooming houses, and cruising grounds operated in the interstices of the city's nightlife. Same-sex desire had public codes, recognizable types, and a social geography, and a novelist of Fitzgerald's alertness to social surfaces could not have been ignorant of them. The historicist question is therefore precise: given what 1922 made sayable and unsayable, what does Nick's narration actually encode, and what are we projecting backward from a later century's categories?

The reading's first move is to recover the period's vocabulary for male same-sex desire and test Nick against it. The 1920s possessed a rich taxonomy, fairies, pansies, and the whole coded language of effeminacy, theatricality, and aestheticism, and the novel deploys "feminine" and "effeminate" at exactly the moments the debate circles. The queer-coded reading hears confirmation; the historicist reads more slowly and asks what those adjectives did in 1922 that they do not do now. "Effeminate" in the period could mark class performance, aestheticism, or a failure of masculine vigor without necessarily marking sexual identity, and Nick's use of it for Tom's riding clothes sits inside a discourse about masculine authenticity that the novel pursues on other fronts. The historicist does not deny that the codes existed; it denies that we can read them transparently, and it insists that the distance between 1922 and the reader is part of the evidence.

The reading's second move is the closet's history. The modern concept of the closet, a stable gay identity hidden behind a heterosexual performance, is itself a historical product, and the historicist warns against assuming it was available to Fitzgerald or his readers in its current form. In 1922, the categories were looser, the boundaries between intense friendship and erotic attachment were drawn differently, and a man could experience and even act on same-sex desire without understanding himself through the identity the debate's question presupposes. This cuts against the queer-coded reading's confidence, because "is Nick gay" may be asking a question the text's world could not have formulated, and it cuts against the heteronormative baseline's confidence, because the absence of modern identity language is not the absence of the desire. The historicist position thus refuses both the outing and the dismissal: Nick inhabited a world where the codes were real, the identities were fluid, and the narration's silences are historically overdetermined, which is why the question survives every attempt to settle it.

### Why does the novel's 1922 setting matter for the debate?

Gatsby's New York is a city where George Chauncey's scholarship documents a gay male world with its own bars, slang, and cruising grounds, which means Nick moves through a milieu where same-sex desire had codes. One side says that visibility makes a queer Nick plausible; the other says it makes the absence of explicit coding in Nick's narration conspicuous.

The setting matters because it removes the easiest version of each side's case. The queer-coded reading cannot claim that Fitzgerald lacked any language for same-sex desire, because the language was all around him; if Nick is coded, the coding was a choice among available options, not a necessity imposed by silence. The heteronormative reading cannot claim that the period made the question unthinkable, because the period thought about it constantly, in slang, in scandal, and in the very social types Nick encounters at Gatsby's parties. What the setting leaves standing is a harder question: why does a narrator moving through this milieu narrate desire the way Nick does, with long looks at men and short shrift for women, with a bedroom scene rendered as an ellipsis and a handkerchief's tenderness recorded in loving detail? The historicist answer is that the question is the finding, and that any reading that dissolves the period's strangeness into a modern category has stopped doing history.

## The Heteronormative Baseline: Nick as Written Straight

The heteronormative-baseline reading is the debate's null hypothesis, and it deserves its strongest statement because the burden of proof in the argument properly rests on the side claiming the text says something it never says. On this account, Nick Carraway is a conventionally masculine narrator of the 1920s whose text contains no evidence of same-sex desire, and every item in the queer-coded inventory has a mundane explanation. The McKee scene is a drunken non-event: Nick goes upstairs to look at photographs, falls asleep or blanks out, and wakes the next morning at the station, and the ellipsis marks intoxication and narrative economy, not a sexual encounter. The lather wiping is a drunk's compulsive fussiness, the kind of trivial fixation alcohol produces. The long descriptions of Tom and Gatsby are characterization, the tools of a novelist establishing presence and power, and the asymmetry with the female portraits reflects the novel's male-centered social world, not the narrator's desire. The cool romances are the behavior of a detached, judgmental young man, not a closeted one.

This reading leans hard on what the text does not contain. No character, including the gossips at Gatsby's parties who speculate freely about everyone, ever suggests Nick desires men. Nick's narration, which is frank about Tom's adultery, Myrtle's vulgarity, and Gatsby's criminality, never hints at his own transgression, which is odd for a secret the reading claims structures the whole book. The "evidence" is all inference from tone and adjective, and the reading argues that inference is doing the work the text refuses to do. It further notes that the queer-coded reading's method, accumulate suggestive details until a pattern emerges, can generate a queer reading of any intensely observed male friendship in literature, which suggests the method is producing the finding rather than discovering it. The baseline's defenders do not deny that the text is ambiguous; they deny that ambiguity is evidence, and they insist that the responsible conclusion from an absence of evidence is the absence of a claim.

The position's account of the romances is worth stating fully, because the queer-coded reading leans on them. Nick's affair with Jordan Baker ends because he judges her dishonest and careless, a moral verdict consistent with everything else in his narration, and his earlier entanglement with the girl from the accounting department in Jersey City ends because her brother's hostility makes it inconvenient. Neither ending requires a theory of repressed desire; both are explicable as the behavior of a fastidious, somewhat cold young man who holds people at arm's length. The housemate who never arrives is a plot convenience, the mechanism that leaves Nick alone in West Egg to observe his neighbors, and reading it as a lost domestic partner is, on this account, the kind of biographical fan fiction the method encourages. The baseline reading thus reverses the accumulation: the inventory, examined item by item, dissolves into ordinary novelistic business, and what remains is a narrator whose sexuality the novel simply does not thematize.

The counter from the queer-coded side is that the baseline begs the question by treating heterosexuality as the default requiring no evidence while demanding proof for any alternative, and that the novel's own silences are conspicuous precisely because the narration is so frank elsewhere. The historicist counter is that the baseline smuggles in a modern confidence about what a "conventionally masculine narrator" looked like in 1922, a confidence the period's own fluid codes do not support. The baseline's reply to both is that the text is the only court with jurisdiction, and the text never speaks the charge.

## The Narrative-Function Reading: Ambiguity as Device

The narrative-function reading asks what Nick's sexual ambiguity does for the novel rather than what it says about Nick, and it finds the answer in the machinery of narration. On this account, the gap around Nick's desire is one component of a larger strategy of narrative withholding that produces the novel's distinctive authority: Nick tells us everything about Gatsby's parties and nothing decisive about Gatsby's soul, everything about Daisy's voice and nothing that fixes her meaning, and the pattern extends to himself. A narrator who disclosed his desire would become a character with an agenda the reader could discount; a narrator whose desire remains illegible retains the impersonal authority on which the novel's moral judgments depend. The ambiguity is thus functional: it keeps Nick a lens rather than a subject, and the novel needs him to be a lens.

This reading connects the sexuality debate to the older and larger debate about Nick's reliability, which [the series' character study of Nick as narrator](/2023/06/12/nick-carraway-reliable-narrator/) maps in full. A narrator whose desire is unknown is a narrator whose motives are unknown, and the novel exploits that unknowability at every turn: Nick's judgments of Gatsby, Tom, Daisy, and Jordan arrive with the force of moral law precisely because the reader cannot locate the interest behind them. If the text confirmed Nick as gay, the queer-coded reading's partisans argue, his devotion to Gatsby would become legible as love and his narration would gain a motive; if the text confirmed him as straight, the baseline's partisans argue, his detachment would become legibility of another kind. The novel withholds both confirmations, and the narrative-function reading treats the withholding as the engine of Nick's authority. The famous closing pages, where Nick appoints himself Gatsby's sole mourner and elegist, work because the reader cannot reduce the grief to a named desire; it remains larger, stranger, and more available to every reader's own attachments.

The position also explains the debate's persistence as a feature rather than a bug. A novel that resolved Nick's sexuality would date its interpretive life; a novel that sustains the question renews it with every generation of readers, each of which brings its own categories to the gap. The reading does not deny that the gap can be read as coding, as structure, or as history; it argues that the gap's function is prior to any of those readings, and that the function is what Fitzgerald, the craftsman of withholding, would have cared about. The counter is familiar: the reading explains the effect without explaining the cause, and a device that produces exactly the symptoms of a secret may be a secret wearing a device's clothes. The function reading's defenders answer that the distinction collapses in practice, because a novelist's reasons and a text's effects are both legitimate objects of criticism, and the function is the one the text guarantees.

## Where the Argument Came From

The debate did not begin with the novel's publication in 1925, and the lag is part of the story. Early criticism of The Great Gatsby, preoccupied with the novel's symbolism, its Jazz Age surfaces, and Fitzgerald's craftsmanship, showed little interest in Nick's desire; the question was not yet askable in the form the debate now takes, because the critical vocabularies had not been built. The building began, improbably, with an essay about Huckleberry Finn. Leslie Fiedler's "Come Back to the Raft Ag'in, Huck Honey!," published in Partisan Review in 1948, argued that canonical American fiction hides homoerotic male bonding in plain sight, reading Huck and Jim, Ishmael and Queequeg, and Cooper's frontiersmen as pairs whose flight from women encodes an innocent homosexuality. The essay scandalized its moment and founded a method: treat the intense male friendships of the American canon as coded erotic bonds, and the texts would confess. Fiedler extended the thesis in Love and Death in the American Novel in 1960, and a generation of critics learned to look at male pairs in American fiction with new suspicion. Gatsby's male configurations, Nick and Gatsby above all, were waiting inside that method's field of vision, and it was only a matter of time before the method found them.

### What did Leslie Fiedler's 1948 essay contribute?

'Come Back to the Raft Ag'in, Huck Honey!' argued that American fiction hides homoerotic male bonding in plain sight, from Huck and Jim to Ishmael and Queequeg. Fiedler did not write about Gatsby, but his essay gave later critics a method: treat intense male friendship in an American classic as a coded erotic bond, and watch the text for confirmation.

The contribution needs the qualifying sentence, because loose attribution has plagued this corner of the debate. Fiedler's 1948 essay discusses Twain, Melville, and Cooper; it does not discuss Fitzgerald, and claiming otherwise would be the kind of invented scholarly endorsement this article refuses. What the essay contributed was the license and the template: the claim that American literature's central subject is male bonding shadowed by homoerotic feeling, and the critical permission to read that feeling as the text's secret truth rather than a reader's projection. When later critics turned the template on Gatsby, they found a novel unusually hospitable to it, a first-person narrator obsessed with a beautiful, doomed man, a bedroom scene rendered as an ellipsis, a whole social world organized around male rivalry and admiration. The method did not create the evidence, but it taught critics which details to collect, and the collection became the queer-coded reading's inventory. The essay's afterlife also explains the debate's temperature: Fiedler's provocation made the homoerotic reading of the canon feel like a discovery rather than an imposition, and every subsequent argument about Nick inherits that framing, for better and worse.

The feminist wave of the 1970s supplied the debate's second precondition. Judith Fetterley's The Resisting Reader, published in 1978, taught readers to read canonical American fiction against its grain on gender, to notice whose experience the text centers and whose it erases, and to treat the narrator's authority as a problem rather than a given. Fetterley's project was feminist rather than queer, concerned with the novel's women and the masculine narration that frames them, but its method, resist the narrator, suspect the surface, ask what the text will not say, became the queer reading's method as well. Once Nick's authority was a question rather than a premise, his silences became evidence rather than background, and the sexuality debate had its critical footing. The decade's broader rethinking of gender and narration made it possible to ask about Nick's desire as a textual question rather than a biographical rumor.

Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's Between Men, published in 1985, supplied the third precondition and the debate's most durable vocabulary. The book's account of homosocial desire, the continuum between male bonding and male homosexuality, and the erotic triangle gave Gatsby critics a framework that fit the novel's configurations with unusual precision, and the homosocial reading of Nick emerged as the position that could honor the queer evidence without forcing an identity claim. The 1990s then brought queer theory's institutional ascendancy, and with it the readings that treated Nick as coded gay moved from the margins of the conversation to its center, taught in classrooms, argued in journals, and eventually familiar enough to become the default assumption of a generation of students. George Chauncey's Gay New York, published in 1994, complicated the picture from the historicist flank by recovering the actual codes and milieus of Jazz Age New York, giving the debate an empirical check it had previously lacked and producing the historicist position in its mature form.

The survey layer of the field, which [the series' scholarship survey](/2030/12/02/gatsby-scholarship-survey/) maps in full, shows the debate's shape: no consensus, no victor, and no sign of exhaustion. Each new critical generation re-argues the question with its own vocabulary, the queer-coded reading in the idiom of identity, the homosocial reading in the idiom of structure, the historicist reading in the idiom of the archive, and each re-argument rediscovers the same textual moments, because the moments are fixed and the vocabularies are not. That fixity is the debate's deepest fact. The novel does not change; the readers do; and the question persists because the text was built, by design or by the accidents of its moment, to sustain it.

## The Debate's Working Vocabulary

Six terms do most of the work in this argument, and much of the debate's heat comes from readers using them differently. Defining them with the novel's own examples attached clarifies what each position is actually claiming.

Coding is the text's way of signaling without stating. A coded passage uses suggestive detail, strategic silence, or patterned emphasis to invite an interpretation the text never announces. The McKee ellipsis is the debate's textbook case: three dots where narration should be, doing the work of a scene the novel will not stage. Coding matters because it is the queer-coded reading's central mechanism and the baseline reading's central target; the first treats coding as the text's confession, the second as the reader's projection, and the term itself decides nothing until a position tells you what to do with it.

The closet names a structure rather than a room: a social arrangement in which a stigmatized identity is known but unspoken, managed through euphemism, misdirection, and strategic silence. Imported from the history of sexuality into literary criticism, it gives the queer-coded reading its master metaphor: Nick's narration as a closet, the reticences as its architecture, the ellipsis as its door. The historicist position's caution applies here with full force, because the closet as a concept belongs to a later century's understanding of identity, and applying it to 1922 risks describing the period's arrangements with a vocabulary the period did not possess. The term remains useful as long as it is handled as an analogy rather than a diagnosis.

Homosocial describes bonds between men that are socially intense without being sexually defined: friendship, rivalry, admiration, mentorship, competition. Sedgwick's innovation was to place these bonds on a continuum with the homosexual rather than in a separate moral universe, which is why the term electrified Gatsby criticism. The novel is thick with homosocial material, the male parties, the business lunches, the war memories, the Plaza confrontation, and the homosocial reading's contribution is to take that thickness seriously as the novel's subject rather than as the disguise of a different subject. Critics of the term argue that it can make everything and therefore nothing erotic, dissolving the debate's pointed evidence into a general theory of male bonding.

Reticence is the narrator's signature quality and the debate's most contested descriptor. Nick announces it himself in Chapter 1, reserving judgments as a matter of infinite hope, and the novel performs it everywhere: the withheld hours, the undescribed desire, the judgments delivered without the motives behind them. The queer-coded reading treats reticence as strategic, the baseline reading as temperamental, the historicist reading as period decorum, and the function reading as craft. The word is the same in all four uses; the theory of the silence differs, and the difference is the debate.

Ambiguity, in the critical sense, is not vagueness but multiplicity: a passage structured to sustain more than one reading without authorizing any. The deliberate-ambiguity position claims this status for the whole question of Nick's desire, arguing that the text is engineered to keep the readings in play. The claim's strength is explanatory, it accounts for the debate's permanence, and its weakness is evidentiary, since a text that merely failed to decide would look the same. Distinguishing designed ambiguity from accidental underdetermination is one of the debate's hardest tasks, and no position has a decisive test for it.

Identity and desire name the distinction the entire argument turns on. Desire is what a character feels; identity is the social category that names and organizes that feeling. The modern question "is Nick gay" fuses them, asking about a feeling through a category, and the historicist position's deepest contribution is to pry them apart: in 1922, the feeling could exist without the category, the category was still forming, and the narration's silences sit in the gap between them. Every position in the debate must decide how much weight to give each term, and the positions divide, more than on any other point, on whether the text is best read as being about a desire it will not name or an identity it cannot yet conceive.

## What Each Side Concedes

A debate map earns trust by recording what each position gives away, because the concessions are where the positions show they have heard their opponents. The queer-coded reading concedes the absence of confirmation: no sentence in the novel states Nick's desire, no character names it, and the entire case rests on accumulation, on the claim that the pattern of details points one way. Its practitioners acknowledge that accumulation is a method with a known failure mode, the generation of queer readings for any intensely observed male friendship, and that the defense against the charge is the specificity of this novel's details rather than the method's general reliability. The concession does not defeat the reading, but it sets its price: the case is circumstantial by necessity, and circumstantial cases persuade only when every circumstance is granted.

The homosocial reading concedes that structure can become an evasion. By translating the debate's pointed evidence, the bedroom, the lather, the ellipsis, into instances of a general pattern of male bonding, the reading risks dissolving exactly what makes the evidence pointed. Its defenders acknowledge that "homosocial" explains the Nick-Gatsby-Daisy triangle elegantly and the McKee bedroom awkwardly, because the bedroom scene is a bond between men with no woman to route it through, and the framework strains at the very passage the debate cares about most. The concession clarifies the reading's proper domain: it is strongest as an account of the novel's social world and weakest as an account of its most private scene.

The deliberate-ambiguity reading concedes unfalsifiability. No textual discovery could refute the claim that the gap is engineered, because any new suggestive detail would count as further engineering and any new mundane detail as further misdirection. Its practitioners acknowledge that "deliberate" is doing argumentative work the evidence cannot fully support, crediting Fitzgerald with a theory of interpretive openness that belongs more comfortably to a later criticism than to a 1925 novelist's workshop. The concession reframes the reading as an account of the text's effects rather than its causes: the ambiguity is certainly there, whatever produced it, and describing its operation is valuable even if the intention behind it stays unknowable.

The historicist reading concedes that the archive cuts both ways and sometimes cuts nothing. Recovering the period's codes refines the question without resolving it, because the codes are partial, their application to any given sentence is contestable, and the most careful historicism ends where the debate began, with readers disagreeing about what "feminine" does in a particular line. Its practitioners acknowledge that historicism can become a sophisticated way of suspending judgment, dissolving the question into context until nothing remains but the context. The concession is also the reading's discipline: it refuses to let the past serve as a ventriloquist for the present's preferences.

The heteronormative-baseline reading concedes the asymmetry of its burden. It demands explicit evidence for the queer claim while requiring none for its own, treating heterosexuality as the default that needs no textual support. Its defenders acknowledge that this is a substantive assumption rather than a neutral starting point, and that the novel's male-centered social world gives the default less cover than the baseline admits: a narrator this detached from women is at least a curiosity the baseline must explain rather than assume away. The concession does not surrender the null hypothesis, but it obliges the baseline to argue for its default rather than merely occupying it.

The narrative-function reading concedes that it describes the effect without explaining the cause. Showing that the ambiguity serves Nick's authority does not show why this ambiguity rather than another, why desire rather than some other withheld subject, and the reading's elegance can look like an avoidance of the debate's substance. Its practitioners acknowledge that function and secret are not exclusive, that a closet can be functional and a device can conceal, and that the reading's proper claim is priority rather than exclusivity: the function is what the text guarantees, whatever else the gap may also be. The concession leaves the reading standing as the debate's most economical account of why the question persists, without claiming to be its final word.

## Why Neither Side Can Settle It

The debate's persistence is not a failure of scholarship; it is a property of the text, and the property has identifiable mechanisms. The first is the ellipsis itself. The three dots after the elevator scene are the only place in Nick's narration where hours pass without an account, and their uniqueness does double duty: they invite the inference that something happened, and they withhold the material that would confirm or deny the inference. No amount of contextual research can fill those dots, because the dots are not a historical puzzle but a textual one; they mark a decision by the narration, and decisions by narrations are interpretable but not decidable. Every position must pass through the ellipsis, and the ellipsis lets every position through.

The second mechanism is retrospective narration. Nick writes from a later vantage, after Gatsby's death, after the summer's end, with full knowledge of how the story concludes, and his narration is everywhere shaped by that knowledge. A retrospective narrator selects, arranges, and withholds; the selection is visible in the novel's architecture, the famous opening meditation on judgment, the delayed introduction of Gatsby, the carefully staged revelations. The sexuality debate asks this narrator to have selected his own desire out of the story, and the text gives no way to distinguish that selection from the ordinary selections of retrospective telling. The queer-coded reading finds the selection pointed; the baseline reading finds it ordinary; the text declines to annotate its own choices.

The third mechanism is the absence of interiority. Nick narrates his judgments, his observations, and his memories, but he almost never narrates his desire, for women or for men. The Jordan affair is reported with the flatness of a balance sheet; the Jersey City girl is dispatched in a sentence; no passage in the novel gives the reader access to Nick's erotic inner life in any direction. This absence is symmetrical, and its symmetry is what makes the debate undecidable: a narrator who desired women would look, from the outside, exactly like this narrator, and a narrator who desired men but could not say so would also look exactly like this narrator. The text provides no interior baseline against which to measure the deviation, because there is no interior at all.

The fourth mechanism is the period's language. The 1920s possessed codes for same-sex desire that are partly recoverable and partly lost, and the novel's deployment of "feminine" and "effeminate" sits in the overlap between the recoverable and the lost. Modern readers can document what those words meant in slang dictionaries and social types, but they cannot recover what Fitzgerald meant by placing them where he placed them, and the gap between the dictionary and the intention is where the debate lives. Each new historicist finding refines the question without resolving it, because the question is not what the words could have meant but what they do mean in these sentences, and that is a literary judgment, not an archival one.

The fifth mechanism is the one the debate least likes to name: the question may be malformed. "Is Nick gay" presupposes a stable identity that the narrator could possess and the text could reveal, but the novel's world, as the historicist position shows, organized male feeling through categories looser and stranger than the modern identity, and the novel's narration, as the function position shows, is designed to withhold the very interiority the question demands. A malformed question can sustain infinite argument precisely because no answer fits it; the argument's energy comes from the misfit, and each side's frustration with the other is really frustration with the question. The debate persists, on this account, because it is asking the text for something the text was never built to give, and the text's refusal is inexhaustible.

None of this means the debate is empty. A question can be unanswerable and still be worth asking, because the asking organizes attention: it sends readers back to the McKee scene, to the lather, to the smile, to the portraits, and each return teaches something about how narration works, how periods code desire, and how criticism builds its cases. The debate is the novel's afterlife in miniature, a machine for generating readings, and its value is measured in the readings it generates rather than the verdict it never reaches. That is why the honest positions in the argument are the ones that present their case at full strength and stop short of claiming the text as settled property.

## How the Debate Gets Misread

The debate has characteristic failure modes, and naming them is part of mapping it honestly, because most readers meet the argument through its distortions before they meet it directly. The first and most damaging is the declaration of settlement. It takes two forms, the triumphant outing and the brisk dismissal, and both do the same damage: they convert an open scholarly question into a closed one by fiat. The outing version announces that Nick is gay, treats the McKee scene as proof, and regards disagreement as denial. The dismissal version announces that there is nothing to discuss, treats the absence of explicit statement as the absence of a question, and regards curiosity as projection. Both versions skip the actual work, which is weighing the six positions against the same textual moments, and both relieve the reader of the debate's central discipline, which is holding competing readings in view without choosing prematurely. Settlement is the one move the evidence never authorizes, and the frequency with which it is attempted is a measure of the question's emotional charge rather than its decidability.

The second misreading overstates the evidence, and it is the mirror image of the third, which dismisses it. Overstatement treats the McKee scene as a smoking gun rather than a suggestive gap, upgrading "the narration breaks off" into "the narration confesses" without acknowledging the upgrade. The upgrade usually happens silently, in the slide from "this is consistent with a queer reading" to "this proves Nick is gay," and the slide is where the argument's honesty leaks out. A careful queer-coded reading stays at the level of consistency and accumulation; the misreading jumps to proof, and the jump discredits the careful version by association. Dismissal commits the symmetrical error: it treats the absence of an explicit statement as though it were evidence of absence, as though a 1925 novel that never names same-sex desire thereby settles the question in the negative. But the text's silence is the debate's subject, not its resolution, and a null hypothesis that never examines why the silence takes this particular shape, the ellipsis, the lather, the portraits, is not a finding but a refusal to look.

The fourth misreading attributes readings inaccurately, and it is the one this article has been most careful to avoid. It puts Sedgwick on the Gatsby battlefield as though Between Men adjudicated Nick Carraway, when the book supplied the framework that later critics applied. It credits Fiedler with a Gatsby essay he never wrote, upgrading his methodological contribution into a direct endorsement. It invents scholars, or half-remembers them, and the half-memory hardens into citation. The damage here is not just sloppiness; misattribution lets partisans borrow authority they have not earned, and it makes the debate look more settled than it is by implying that famous names have taken sides they never took. The discipline is simple and non-negotiable: name a scholar only for a claim that scholar verifiably made, and use generic attribution everywhere else. A map that invents its landmarks is worse than no map.

The fifth misreading collapses the six positions into two, and it is the most common in informal discussion. The debate becomes a vote, gay or straight, with the homosocial, ambiguity, historicist, and function readings disappearing from view. The collapse flatters the participants, who get the satisfactions of a contest, but it loses everything that makes the scholarly debate interesting: the historicist's period codes, the ambiguity reader's craft argument, the function reader's account of narrative authority. Worse, the collapse makes each side's case cruder than its best version, because the middle positions are where the strongest objections to both extremes live. The historicist objection to the outing, that the question may be malformed for 1922, and the baseline objection to the outing, that the burden of proof has not been met, are different objections with different force; collapsing them into a single "no" side erases the distinction. A reader who knows only the two-pole version has not met the debate.

The sixth misreading turns a textual question into a biographical one, asking what Fitzgerald intended or what Fitzgerald was, as though the author's private life could settle the narrator's textual coding. The move is tempting because biography feels like evidence, harder and more decisive than interpretation. But the debate's positions are readings of the published novel, and even a documented intention would show what Fitzgerald considered, not what the sentences mean. The queer-coded reading at its most careful makes no biographical claim; it argues about signals in the text. The misreading upgrades those signals into a story about the author, and the upgrade changes the subject from criticism to gossip. The text is the only court with jurisdiction, as the baseline position rightly insists, and that jurisdiction excludes the author's bedroom.

## The Evidence-Discipline Standard

This article's method deserves a final explicit statement, because the method is the series' standard for contested questions and this debate is its hardest test. Evenhanded mapping rests on four rules, and each has been applied above. The first is the strongest-version rule: every position appears at its most persuasive, with its best evidence and its sharpest formulation, never as a straw figure assembled for demolition. The queer-coded reading gets its accumulation, the baseline gets its burden of proof, the historicist gets its archive, and none is softened to make a rival look better. A map that flatters one side is advocacy, and advocacy is what the debate already has in excess.

The second is the attribution rule: name scholars only for claims they verifiably made, and use generic attribution everywhere else. This article names Fiedler for the 1948 method, Sedgwick for the 1985 framework, Fetterley for the 1978 resistant reading, and Chauncey for the historical recovery of Jazz Age New York's gay male world, and it declines to name anyone for the positions that circulate without a verifiable author. The rule protects both the scholars, who are not conscripted into arguments they never joined, and the readers, who are not misled about the weight of authority behind any position. In a debate where loose citation has done real damage, attribution is not pedantry but honesty.

The third is the no-adjudication rule, the hard gate this article was built under. Mapping a debate is not the same as judging it, and the distinction matters because the temptation to judge is strongest exactly where judgment is least warranted. This article states that the debate remains live and explains the textual mechanisms that keep it live, but it declares no winner, not even in the Verdict, because the evidence does not authorize one. The discipline here is negative: the article's value is measured by what it refuses to claim as much as by what it establishes.

The fourth is the settled-versus-disputed distinction. House standards for contested subjects require separating what the field has established from what it still argues, and this debate makes the separation unusually clean: the textual moments are settled, every position quotes the same sentences, and everything else, what the moments mean, is disputed. Stating that division plainly is what lets readers trust the map. A reader who finishes this article should know exactly which claims are shared ground and which are contested territory, and should be able to carry that distinction into any future encounter with the criticism. That transferable discipline, more than any single reading of Nick, is what evenhanded mapping is for. The standard travels: any contested question in the series gets the same treatment, the same four rules, and the same refusal to adjudicate what the evidence leaves open.

## Six Sentences, Six Readings

The debate can be compressed into six sentences and the six ways of reading them. What follows is the cross-reference view: each sentence quoted, then the use each position makes of it. Readers who have followed the positional sections will recognize the arguments; the value here is seeing them collide on the same ground.

"Mr. McKee was a pale feminine man from the flat below." The queer-coded reading hears the adjective as a period signal, a marker Fitzgerald's contemporaries would have recognized, and notes that Nick spends his descriptive budget on McKee's femininity rather than his photography. The homosocial reading files it under the novel's general interest in male types, one more specimen in the social panorama. The ambiguity reading treats it as a planted suggestiveness, a detail designed to start the argument it sustains. The historicist reading slows the whole process down and asks what "feminine" did in 1922, class marker, aesthetic judgment, or sexual code, refusing to let the modern ear decide. The baseline reading treats it as characterization of a minor figure, a physical tag of the kind novelists attach to walk-ons. The function reading notes that the adjective primes the reader to watch McKee, which is exactly what the ellipsis then prevents, making the sentence part of the withholding machinery.

"Taking out my handkerchief I wiped from his cheek the remains of the spot of dried lather that had worried me all the afternoon." The queer-coded reading finds its tenderest exhibit here, a sustained noticing and an intimate gesture that betray attachment. The homosocial reading sees male care outside the usual routings, tenderness the novel's world permits between men when no woman is watching. The ambiguity reading pairs the tenderness reading with the fussiness reading and declines to choose, which is the point. The historicist reading asks about the codes of male touch in 1922, when the boundaries of acceptable physical intimacy between men were drawn differently than later assumptions allow. The baseline reading sees a drunk man's compulsive tidying, the trivial fixation alcohol produces, magnified by a narrator who notices everything. The function reading observes that the sentence's loving detail is precisely what makes the subsequent ellipsis so vertiginous: the narration that records a spot of lather cannot record three hours, and the contrast is the device.

The three dots, ". . .", standing alone between the elevator and the bedroom. The queer-coded reading treats them as the closet's purest textual form, the place where the narration performs the unsayable. The homosocial reading treats them as the mark of a bond the triangle cannot route, male intimacy with no woman to conduct it, which the narration therefore cannot process. The ambiguity reading treats them as craft, the prose equivalent of a film cut, and the debate's founding device. The historicist reading treats them as historically overdetermined, simultaneously decorum, convention, and possibly design, with no way to apportion the causes. The baseline reading treats them as intoxication and economy, the hours a drunk narrator skips because nothing narratable happened in them. The function reading treats them as the engine of the whole argument, the gap that generates six readings and therefore the novel's most productive sentence.

"I was standing beside his bed and he was sitting up between the sheets, clad in his underwear, with a great portfolio in his hands." The queer-coded reading takes the bedroom tableau at something like face value: whatever happened in the missing hours, the narration returns to a scene it will not explain, and the unexplained is the evidence. The homosocial reading notes the portfolio, the stated reason for the visit, and reads the scene as male sociability that the narration renders strange by refusing to narrate it. The ambiguity reading finds the sentence perfectly balanced, suggestive enough to sustain the queer reading and mundane enough, photographs were the pretext, to sustain the baseline. The historicist reading asks what a 1922 reader would have made of the tableau, with the period's codes for such scenes only partly recoverable. The baseline reading takes the portfolio seriously: Nick went up to see photographs, stayed too long through drink, and the narration resumes where the evening became uninteresting. The function reading notes the flat affect, the reporting of nothing in particular, as the tone that makes the passage inexhaustible.

"He had one of those rare smiles with a quality of eternal reassurance in it, that you may come across four or five times in life." The queer-coded reading hears erotic rapture, the narrator's most rhapsodic prose spent on a man's face, and the asymmetry with the female portraits as the tell. The homosocial reading hears the apex of male admiration, the bond between men at its most intense, without requiring the identity claim. The ambiguity reading treats the rapture as deliberately double, readable as desire and as myth-making, which is why the sentence anchors the debate's second front. The historicist reading places the smile in the period's discourse of masculine charisma, where such rhapsodies about men had their own conventions. The baseline reading hears characterization at full stretch, the novelist establishing Gatsby's wonder-working presence, the same inflation that gilds the parties and the shirts. The function reading treats the smile as the novel's myth-making machinery at its most visible, the prose that makes Gatsby worth elegizing.

"You're worth the whole damn bunch put together." The queer-coded reading hears the devotion breaking through, the closet's one unguarded sentence. The homosocial reading hears the male bond's culmination, admiration and rivalry resolved into loyalty. The ambiguity reading hears the sentence's perfect openness, grief that fits every interpretation because it names none. The historicist reading hears a period idiom of male loyalty, the extravagance of feeling the era permitted men at the graveside. The baseline reading hears friendship's ordinary hyperbole under extraordinary pressure. The function reading hears the elegist appointing himself, the moral authority of the sole mourner whose grief must stay larger than any named desire to do its work. Six readings, one sentence, no tiebreaker: the debate in miniature, and the reason it will outlast every attempt to end it.

## A Worked Example: Annotating the Passage Six Ways

The map becomes concrete when the six positions are set to work on the same stretch of prose. What follows annotates the Chapter 2 McKee sequence beat by beat, from the elevator to Pennsylvania Station, recording what each reading notices. The exercise demonstrates the debate's central fact more vividly than any summary: the positions do not disagree about what the sentences say, but about what the sentences are doing.

The elevator beat: Nick, drunk, is drawn into the elevator with McKee, who has invited him upstairs to see his photographs, and the elevator boy warns McKee about the lever while McKee replies with dignity that he did not know he was touching it. The queer-coded reading hears the exchange's double meaning as deliberate, a phallic joke the text plants at the threshold of the unnarrated hours, priming the reader for what the ellipsis will withhold. The homosocial reading treats the moment as male sociability tipping into farce, the kind of drunken awkwardness the novel's male world produces routinely. The ambiguity reading pairs the suggestive hearing with the mundane one and treats the pairing as the design: the joke is there for readers who want it and deniable for readers who do not. The historicist reading notes the period's coded humor around such exchanges and asks what a 1922 audience would have heard, neither assuming the modern ear nor dismissing it. The baseline reading sees only farce, a drunk man's dignity punctured by a lever, with no second meaning required. The function reading observes that the beat's comedy lowers the narration's guard, making the cut to the ellipsis more disorienting than a solemn transition would be.

The ellipsis beat: three dots where the evening should be. The queer-coded reading treats the dots as the closet's signature, the text marking the unsayable with the era's standard notation for omission. The homosocial reading treats them as the limit of the triangle's routing, a male bond with no woman to conduct it and therefore no narrative form to take. The ambiguity reading treats them as the novel's most honest device, a gap that refuses to resolve into either confession or denial. The historicist reading treats them as overdetermined, decorum and convention and possible design layered beyond separation. The baseline reading treats them as the hours nothing narratable happened in, the drunk's blank and the editor's cut. The function reading treats them as the generator of the entire debate, the most productive three characters in the novel.

The bedroom beat: Nick standing beside the bed, McKee sitting up between the sheets in his underwear, the great portfolio in his hands. The queer-coded reading takes the tableau as the morning-after of the unnarrated, the narration returning to a scene whose explanation it withholds. The homosocial reading takes the portfolio seriously as the evening's pretext, male sociability around photographs, rendered strange by the missing hours rather than by anything in the scene itself. The ambiguity reading holds both: the pretext is real, the strangeness is real, and the sentence is balanced to sustain either emphasis. The historicist reading asks after the period's visual codes for such tableaux and finds the record partial, which is itself the finding. The baseline reading reads the portfolio as the explanation: Nick went up for the photographs, drink did the rest, and the underwear is the unremarkable fact of a man woken in his own bed. The function reading attends to the flat affect, the inventory tone, and argues that the sentence's refusal to emote is what keeps it inexhaustible.

The station beat: the next morning, Nick at Pennsylvania Station, waiting for the four o'clock train, the narration resuming its even course as though nothing had happened. The queer-coded reading notes the resumption itself as the tell: the narration that lingered over a spot of lather hurries past a night in another man's bedroom, and the disproportion is the confession. The homosocial reading notes the return to the novel's public world, the male bond reabsorbed into the ordinary circulation of commuters and schedules. The ambiguity reading treats the evenness as the final planted suggestiveness, normal enough to close the incident and strange enough to keep it open. The historicist reading observes the period's narrative conventions for such mornings-after and finds them various, which dissolves any single inference. The baseline reading finds nothing to explain: a drunk man goes home, or to the station, and the novel moves on. The function reading argues that the beat's ordinariness is the device's completion, the gap sealed so smoothly that readers argue about it for a century.

Set side by side, the six annotations reveal the debate's structure with unusual clarity. No position invents a sentence; every position reads all four beats; and the readings diverge at precisely the points where the text declines to specify, which is why the argument is permanent. The worked example also shows what honest participation requires: the ability to state the rival's annotation of a beat fairly before offering one's own, because the beats are shared property and the interpretations are not.

## Verdict

The debate over Nick Carraway's sexuality remains live, and the liveness is the finding. Six positions, each argued at full strength, each citing the same textual moments, each offering a coherent account of the McKee ellipsis, the wiped lather, the admiring male portraits, and the cool romances, and none of them able to produce the sentence that would end the argument, because the novel withholds that sentence by design, by reticence, or by the ordinary selectiveness of retrospective narration, and the scholarship cannot decide among those explanations. The queer-coded reading has the accumulation; the homosocial reading has the structure; the ambiguity reading has the craft; the historicist reading has the period; the baseline reading has the burden of proof; the function reading has the machinery. Each is strongest where the others are weakest, and that distribution of strengths is itself the map.

What the text sustains, without resolving, is a question about how narration handles desire it will not name. Nick's narration is frank about everything except the one subject the debate keeps circling, and the circling has taught criticism more about reading than any single answer could: how to weigh an adjective, how to test a code against its period, how to distinguish a closet from a device, how to present a rival's case fairly. A reader who wants the debate's value should therefore read it as the debate, not as a riddle with a hidden solution. The moments are fixed, the interpretations are multiple, and the text's refusal to choose among them is the one fact every position shares.

The responsible use of this map is correspondingly modest. Cite the positions accurately, name scholars only where the attribution is verifiable, and resist the temptation to declare the matter closed, because the closure would say more about the reader's needs than the text's evidence. The question will outlast every attempt to settle it, and that outlasting is not a defect in the scholarship but a property of a novel built to be reread.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the scholarly debate over Nick Carraway's sexuality?

The debate is a long-running argument among critics about whether the text of The Great Gatsby codes its narrator, Nick Carraway, as gay, and it has never reached a consensus. One side, associated with queer-theory readings, argues that Nick's narration leaks same-sex desire through the McKee bedroom scene, the tender lather-wiping gesture, the long admiring descriptions of male bodies, and his perfunctory romances with women. The other side argues that these moments are matters of drunkenness, period reticence, characterization, and narrative economy, and that the text never supplies the evidence a claim about a character's sexuality requires. Between those poles sit the homosocial, deliberate-ambiguity, historicist, and narrative-function positions mapped in this article. The debate matters because it organizes how readers weigh adjectives, silences, and narrative choices, and because its persistence reveals how much of reading is interpretation rather than extraction.

### Q: What competing readings of Nick exist?

Six positions structure the current map. The queer-coded reading treats Nick as a closeted narrator whose desire surfaces in coded moments. The homosocial reading, drawing on Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's framework, finds intense male bonds routed through women without claiming a gay identity. The deliberate-ambiguity reading credits the text with engineering the gap as craft. The historicist reading measures Nick against the actual codes of 1920s New York. The heteronormative-baseline reading treats the text's silence as the absence of a claim. The narrative-function reading treats the ambiguity as a device that preserves Nick's impersonal narrative authority. Each position cites the same textual moments and interprets them differently, which is why the argument is a debate about reading rather than a dispute about facts, and why presenting each side at its strongest is the only honest way to map it.

### Q: What textual moments do scholars cite?

The inventory is small and stable, which is part of why the debate fascinates. The central moment is the Chapter 2 McKee scene: Nick leaves a party in an elevator with the photographer Mr. McKee, the narration breaks off with a row of three dots, and resumes with Nick standing beside McKee's bed while McKee sits up between the sheets in his underwear. Around it cluster the supporting moments: Nick wiping dried shaving lather from McKee's sleeping cheek with his handkerchief; the long sensuous inventory of Tom Buchanan's body in Chapter 1; Gatsby's smile described as one of those rare smiles with a quality of eternal reassurance; Daisy's remark that Nick reminds her of a rose; the coolly dispatched romances with Jordan Baker and the Jersey City girl; and Nick's declaration that Gatsby is worth the whole damn bunch put together. Every position in the debate must account for this inventory.

### Q: How has the debate developed?

The question was not askable in its current form until the critical vocabularies were built. Early criticism of the novel, focused on symbolism and Jazz Age surfaces, showed little interest in Nick's desire. Leslie Fiedler's 1948 essay on homoerotic male bonding in American fiction supplied the method for detecting coded queerness in the canon, though Fiedler himself did not write about Gatsby. The feminist wave of the 1970s, exemplified by Judith Fetterley's resistant reading of the American canon, taught critics to suspect the narrator's authority and treat his silences as evidence. Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's 1985 account of male homosocial desire gave the debate its most durable framework. The 1990s brought queer theory's ascendancy and the historicist complication of George Chauncey's recovery of Jazz Age New York's gay male world. Each generation has re-argued the same fixed textual moments with a new vocabulary, and no generation has settled the question.

### Q: Is the question settled either way?

No, and the absence of a settlement is the debate's most reliable fact. No textual discovery has resolved Nick's sexuality, no critical consensus has formed around any single position, and the six readings mapped in this article continue to coexist in classrooms, journals, and published surveys of the scholarship. Settling the question would require either a textual moment the novel does not contain, a narrator's confession Nick never makes, or a critical method all sides accepted as decisive, and none of those exists. Readers who encounter confident declarations in either direction, online or in print, are encountering advocacy rather than consensus. The honest scholarly posture, and the one this article maintains throughout, is to present each position at its strongest, attribute claims accurately, and acknowledge that the text sustains the question without resolving it. Treating an open question as open is not indecision; it is the discipline the evidence demands.

### Q: How does the text sustain the debate without resolving it?

The novel sustains the debate through five mechanisms that this article examines in detail. The McKee ellipsis marks the only unnarrated hours in Nick's account, inviting inference while withholding confirmation. The retrospective narration selects and withholds throughout, so the selection around Nick's desire cannot be distinguished from ordinary narrative selectiveness. The text provides almost no interior access to Nick's erotic life in any direction, which means a desiring and a non-desiring narrator would look identical from the outside. The period's codes for same-sex desire are partly recoverable and partly lost, so modern readers cannot fix what "feminine" and "effeminate" do in Fitzgerald's sentences. And the question itself may presuppose a stable identity the text's world did not organize feeling around. Together these mechanisms make the question permanently askable and permanently unanswerable, which is why the debate renews itself with every generation of readers.

### Q: Who started the scholarly debate over Nick Carraway's sexuality?

No single scholar can be credited with starting the debate, and the search for an originator is itself instructive about how criticism works. Leslie Fiedler's 1948 essay created the method that made the question askable, by arguing that canonical American fiction encodes homoerotic male bonding, though Fiedler never applied the method to Gatsby himself. The question then emerged gradually as later critics turned Fiedler's template, the feminist suspicion of narrator authority, and eventually Sedgwick's homosocial framework onto Fitzgerald's novel. By the 1990s the reading of Nick as coded queer had moved from scattered suggestions to a recognized position taught in classrooms. Attributing the debate to one founder would misrepresent a collective, accretive process, and responsible scholarship describes the lineage rather than crowning an originator.

### Q: What would it take for either side of the debate to win?

A decisive victory would require evidence the text does not contain and probably cannot contain. The queer-coded side would need a textual moment that fixes Nick's desire unambiguously, a confession, a reciprocated attachment narrated as such, or an authorial statement of intent with documentary support. The skeptical side would need a demonstration that every suggestive moment has a mundane explanation so complete that the pattern dissolves, which the accumulation has so far resisted. Neither standard is reachable, because the novel's design withholds interiority symmetrically and its period's codes are irrecoverably partial. The more useful question is what each position explains best: the queer-coded reading explains the tenderness and the silences, the historicist reading explains the codes, the function reading explains the authority. Victory, in this debate, is a category mistake, and the positions are better judged as lenses than as claims awaiting proof.

### Q: What role does Nick's relationship with Jordan Baker play in the debate?

Jordan functions as the debate's test case for how to read Nick's attachments to women. The queer-coded reading treats the affair as perfunctory, a performance of heterosexuality that Nick ends with moralizing coldness because his heart was never in it, and notes that his most rhapsodic prose is reserved for men. The heteronormative-baseline reading treats the affair's ending as consistent with Nick's character: he judges Jordan dishonest and careless, the same moral fastidiousness he applies to everyone, and a cold temperament is not evidence of a hidden orientation. The homosocial reading notes that Jordan, like Daisy, functions as a conduit in the novel's male configurations rather than as an object of Nick's desire in her own right. The affair's evidentiary value is thus entirely downstream of the reader's prior commitments, which is why both sides cite it and neither side is moved by the other's citation.

### Q: How do historicist critics read Nick's reticence differently from queer theorists?

Queer-theory readings tend to treat Nick's reticence as strategic, a closet's necessary silence, and therefore as evidence of the very desire it conceals: the narrator does not speak his desire because he cannot, and the cannot is the tell. Historicist critics treat the reticence as overdetermined, produced by period decorum, the looser pre-identity organization of male feeling, narrative convention, and possibly authorial design all at once, so that no single cause can be isolated. The practical difference is in what counts as a finding: where the queer theorist hears a code, the historicist asks for the codebook and finds it incomplete, with entries for class performance and aestheticism alongside entries for same-sex desire. The historicist thus refuses the inference from silence to secret, not because the secret is impossible, but because the period's silences had many authors and the text does not say which one is speaking.

### Q: What is the difference between a queer reading and a queer-coded reading of Nick?

A queer reading is a method: it approaches the text with the questions, frameworks, and attentions developed by queer theory, asking how the novel organizes desire, gender, and non-normative feeling regardless of what it concludes about any character. A queer-coded reading is a claim: it argues that the text encodes Nick as gay through suggestive details, the McKee scene, the lather, the male portraits, that a contemporary audience might feel and a later audience can decode. The method can produce the claim, but it can also produce the homosocial reading, which uses queer theory's tools to reach a non-identity conclusion, or the ambiguity reading, which uses them to describe the text's resistance to naming. Confusing the two lets partisans dismiss the method by attacking the claim, or defend the claim by invoking the method's prestige. Keeping them distinct is a basic hygiene of the debate.

### Q: Has any scholar claimed Fitzgerald intended Nick as a gay character?

This is where the debate's honesty matters most, because the temptation to upgrade a textual claim into an authorial intention is strong and the evidence for it is thin. The mainstream positions in the debate concern what the text encodes or sustains, not what Fitzgerald privately intended, and responsible scholars keep those questions separate. Claims about authorial intention would require documentary evidence, letters, drafts, or statements, of a kind that does not figure in the published argument over Nick. The queer-coded reading is a claim about the text's signals, not about Fitzgerald's plan, and its strongest practitioners frame it that way. Readers should therefore treat any assertion that Fitzgerald meant Nick to be gay as advocacy outrunning evidence, and should notice that the debate's serious work happens at the level of the sentences, where the evidence actually lives.

### Q: Why do reliability debates intersect with the sexuality debate?

Nick's reliability and Nick's desire are connected because both concern what the narrator withholds and why. The classic reliability question asks whether Nick's judgments can be trusted given his limited perspective, his moralizing, and his evident fascination with Gatsby. The sexuality question adds a motive to the unreliability: a narrator managing an unspoken desire would have reason to mythologize its object, excuse his own complicity, and misdirect the reader's attention, which is exactly what unreliable narrators do. The queer-coded reading thus often travels with a strong unreliability thesis, treating the closet and the biased narration as one mechanism. Skeptics reply that the connection cuts both ways: if Nick is unreliable about everything, his silences about desire prove nothing in particular, and importing a motive the text never names risks explaining the narration by inventing its cause. The intersection is genuine, but it multiplies interpretations rather than reducing them.

### Q: What counts as evidence in an argument about a character's sexuality?

The debate has never agreed on its own evidentiary standards, and that disagreement is part of why it never ends. Candidate forms of evidence include explicit statements by the narrator, which the novel never provides; reported actions with sexual content, of which the McKee bedroom scene is the disputed instance; patterns of attention and adjective, the long male portraits versus the short female ones; the testimony of other characters, which is entirely absent here; and biographical or draft materials, which play no verified role in the published debate. Each position weights these forms differently: the queer-coded reading treats patterns of attention as evidence, the baseline reading admits only explicit statement and action, the historicist reading admits period codes with documented meanings, and the ambiguity reading treats the weighting itself as the interpretive act. Until the field agrees on what counts, which it has not, the positions will keep talking past each other with full sincerity.

### Q: Do manuscript or draft materials settle the question of Nick's sexuality?

No verified draft or manuscript evidence plays a role in the published debate over Nick's sexuality, and readers should be wary of claims to the contrary. The argument as it actually exists in the scholarship is conducted entirely on the published text: the McKee scene, the portraits, the romances, and the silences as they appear in the novel readers can check. Appeals to supposed draft revelations, deleted scenes, or authorial second thoughts circulate occasionally in informal discussion, but they do not belong to the verifiable scholarly record, and this article excludes them on principle. The exclusion is also methodologically sound: even a draft variant would show what Fitzgerald considered, not what the published text means, and the debate's positions are readings of the novel as published. The honest evidentiary base is the book in readers' hands.

### Q: How should a student cite the debate in an essay without taking a side?

The safest structure is the map itself: name the positions, attribute each accurately, present the textual evidence each cites, and explain why the question remains open. A student might write that queer-theory readings treat the McKee ellipsis as coded evidence while skeptical readings treat it as drunken narrative economy, citing the passages rather than choosing between them, and then use the debate to illuminate a larger point about narration, reticence, or period codes. What to avoid is the false evenhandedness of presenting invented scholars, the false decisiveness of declaring a winner, and the common error of treating the absence of consensus as permission to assert a personal preference as scholarship. Instructors value the student who can hold competing readings in view and explain what each one sees; the debate is an ideal training ground for that skill precisely because its evidence is fixed and its interpretations are multiple.

### Q: How do classroom discussions of Nick differ from the published scholarship?

Classroom discussion tends to be bolder and less careful than the published scholarship, and the difference is worth understanding. In classrooms the question is often posed as a vote, gay or straight, with students marshalling favorite passages and reaching a verdict by the period's end. Published scholarship, at its best, resists the vote: it maps positions, weighs evidentiary standards, historicizes the categories, and treats the question's persistence as itself significant. The classroom version also tends to collapse the six positions into two, losing the homosocial, ambiguity, historicist, and function readings that give the scholarly debate its texture. None of this makes classroom discussion worthless; the boldness generates energy and the passages get the attention they deserve. But a student moving from discussion to research should expect the scholarship to be slower, more qualified, and more interested in why the question survives than in answering it.

### Q: How does the debate handle Nick's final judgment that Gatsby is worth the whole damn bunch put together?

The line, spoken to Gatsby in Chapter 8, is the debate's emotional exhibit, and each position reads the same surge of feeling differently. The queer-coded reading hears a lover's devotion breaking through the narrator's reserve, the one moment Nick's attachment declares itself without code. The homosocial reading hears the culmination of a male bond structured by admiration and rivalry, intense without being erotic in any identity sense. The narrative-function reading hears the elegist's appointment of himself, the moral authority of the sole mourner whose grief must remain larger than any named desire to do its work. The baseline reading hears loyalty to a friend, the ordinary extravagance of grief, and notes that devotion to a dying man is not evidence of orientation. The line's power is undisputed; its meaning is the debate in miniature, which is why no position can claim it without answering the others.

### Q: Can a character be coded queer without being gay?

Yes, and the distinction is load-bearing for the whole debate. Coding is a property of the text: a set of signals, suggestive details, strategic silences, and patterned attentions that invite a queer reading. Being gay is a property of a person, or in fiction, a fact about a character that the text could in principle establish. A text can deploy queer coding as atmosphere, as historical texture, as a way of marking a character's difference or the narrator's fascinated attention, without thereby settling the character's orientation, just as a text can use gothic coding without containing an actual ghost. The queer-coded reading of Nick sometimes slides between these levels, treating the coding as proof of the fact, and its critics are right to flag the slide. But the distinction also protects the reading's core insight: the coding is really there in the sentences, whatever it proves, and accounting for it is mandatory for every position, including the ones that deny its conclusion.

### Q: What is the strongest single sentence each side of the debate can quote?

The exercise is revealing because both sides reach for the same small set of sentences. The queer-coded side quotes the McKee bedroom sentence, "I was standing beside his bed and he was sitting up between the sheets, clad in his underwear," and the lather sentence about the spot that had worried Nick all afternoon, arguing that tenderness and the ellipsis do the work of confession. The skeptical side quotes Nick's flat dispatch of the Jersey City girl and his moralizing exit from the Jordan affair, arguing that a narrator this frank about others would not hide his own transgression without a trace. The historicist side quotes the period adjectives, the "pale feminine man" and the "effeminate swank," and asks what those words could bear in 1922. That all three sides can name their champion sentence, and that none of the sentences ends the argument, is the debate in a single paragraph.
