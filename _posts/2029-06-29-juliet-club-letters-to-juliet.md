## The Letters to Juliet: A Correspondence with a Fictional Confidante

Every week, envelopes arrive in Verona addressed to a girl who never lived. The addresses on them are often no more than a name and a city: Juliet, Verona. The Italian postal service delivers them anyway, and in a small office in the historic centre a team of volunteers opens them, sorts them by language, reads them, and answers every one by hand, signing the reply with the name of the addressee. The institution that performs this work is the Juliet Club, the Club di Giulietta, a nonprofit cultural association founded in Verona in 1972. Its members are known as Juliet's secretaries, and the mail they answer has been arriving, in one form or another, since the 1930s. The letters ask for what letters to a confidante have always asked for: counsel in a love affair that parents oppose, comfort after a betrayal, a verdict on whether to forgive, to wait, to leave, to confess. The writers address the one figure in world literature who kept a love secret under the worst possible pressure and never betrayed it, because the price of her silence was her life.

![Juliet Club volunteers answering letters at a table in Verona - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict of this article can be stated at the outset and defended across the whole record. The letters to Juliet are not a tourist attraction that happens to use the postal service. They are a reading practice, carried on by correspondents who have read the play closely enough to know exactly which of its characters can be trusted with a secret. The club that answers the letters is an institution built around a practice it did not invent and could not fabricate, because the raw material of the practice, the mail itself, arrives uninvited from every continent, in dozens of languages, in handwriting that ranges from a schoolgirl's to a dying woman's. Verona's tourist industry owns the balcony and profits from the courtyard; the mail belongs to the writers. What the Juliet Club does, and what this article examines in detail, is administer that mail with a seriousness that the play's own Verona never managed. In the tragedy, the decisive letter is the one that never arrives. In the modern city, a small office exists for the single purpose of ensuring that letters addressed to the heroine are answered, each and every one, without exception. The reversal is not a coincidence of tourism. It is the meaning of the phenomenon.

The evidence falls into four parts. First, the origin: the letters began at Juliet's tomb in the 1930s, decades before any club existed, and the first person to answer them was the tomb's custodian acting on his own conscience. Second, the institution: Giulio Tamassia and a circle of Veronese artists and scholars formalized the answering in 1972, and his daughter Giovanna leads the work in the documented accounts of the following decades. Third, the practice itself: the sorting, the languages, the signing, the archive, the ethics the secretaries describe for the letters that arrive from people in genuine despair. Fourth, the interpretation: cultural historians and tourism scholars disagree about whether the whole phenomenon is devotion or staged authenticity, and the documentary record settles that disagreement more decisively than either side usually admits. Around those four parts sit the club's public calendar, its Dear Juliet prize for the finest letters of the year, its literary award, and the measurable surge in the mail after the film of 2010 carried the secretaries' existence to a worldwide audience. Each of those belongs to the film's own article in this series for its full treatment; here the film is weighed only for what it did to the postbag.

One caution governs everything that follows. The Juliet Club is famous, and fame generates numbers. The volume of the mail has been reported at figures that differ by an order of magnitude, from roughly four thousand letters a year to fifty thousand, and the counts of secretaries differ almost as widely. A responsible account does not average those figures into a single confident claim. This article attributes every number to the source that published it and the year that source describes, and treats the divergence itself as evidence: evidence that the mail's scale is real, that its counting has never been audited, and that the difference between the low and high figures is largely a difference in what is being counted, since a posted letter, a note left in a mailbox at the heroine's house, and an email are three different objects that lazy reporting merges into one. The profile table later in this article, named the InsightCrunch Juliet Club profile, keeps the figures separated in exactly that way.

## Where the Letters Began: The Tomb at San Francesco al Corso

The correspondence began at a tomb. On the edge of Verona's historic centre stands the former Franciscan monastery of San Francesco al Corso, and in its crypt a red marble sarcophagus, empty and lidless, has been shown for centuries as the tomb of Juliet. The identification is a tradition, not a documented fact, and the tomb's own article in the architecture of this series belongs to Verona's shrines rather than to the club. What matters here is the documented behaviour of visitors. From the 1930s onward, and in some accounts from still earlier in the history of literary pilgrimage to the city, visitors who stood before the sarcophagus began leaving letters there: notes addressed to the heroine, set down at the grave of a girl who, in the fiction, died for love at fourteen. The letters were not requests for a reply in any organized sense. They were deposits, closer to votive offerings than to correspondence, confessions left at the one address where the confessor was guaranteed to understand.

That guarantee is worth pausing over, because it explains the destination. A tomb is where the living speak to the dead without expecting an answer, and a fictional tomb doubles the safety. The writers of the 1930s were not deceived about the heroine's existence; nobody who writes to a character in a play believes the character keeps a house in Verona. They were choosing the most private mailbox in Europe. A letter left at the tomb could be read by no parent, no husband, no priest, no rival. Its addressee was beyond blackmail and beyond betrayal, sealed in a story whose whole plot is the keeping of a secret. The play itself supplies the curriculum for that trust. Juliet keeps the secret of her marriage from her mother, her father, and the county her parents intend for her, and when every adult around her fails, she fails with none of them. The Nurse, who carried her messages and knew everything, counsels her in Act 3, Scene 5 to commit bigamy with Paris, and Juliet's verdict on that counsel is the harshest line she speaks to any intimate: "Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend!" (3.5). Lady Capulet, in the same scene, washes her hands of her daughter in words that end the conversation forever: "Talk not to me, for I'll not speak a word. Do as thou wilt, for I have done with thee" (3.5). A girl whose mother and nurse both abandon her in the hour of her worst secret does not abandon anyone in hers. The tomb letters were addressed, with a reader's precision, to the only confidante in the story who never talked.

The tomb's setting reinforced the choice. The monastery church was a working religious site, the crypt a quiet room apart from the city's commercial centre, and the practice grew in the shadow of confession even though no sacrament was involved. Visitors came down the stairs, stood at the sarcophagus, and left paper behind. The volume was small enough to be gathered by hand. It needed, however, a gatherer, and the gathering changed everything, because a letter collected is a letter read, and a letter read by a conscientious man becomes a letter that demands an answer. The history of the Juliet Club starts at the moment when the deposits at the tomb stopped being litter and started being mail.

## Before the Secretary: A Century of Pilgrims at the Tomb

The letters of the 1930s did not begin the tomb's traffic; they changed its medium. Through the nineteenth century the sarcophagus at San Francesco al Corso was already a station of literary pilgrimage, visited by travellers who came to Verona because a play had made the city sacred to them. Byron visited the tomb during his Italian years and recorded the visit in his correspondence, one of the early documents of the cult. Charles Dickens, passing through Verona for Pictures from Italy in the 1840s, sought the tomb out and described finding it in neglected surroundings, a plain sarcophagus in an orchard-like enclosure, its romantic charge supplied entirely by the visitor's reading. The sequence those two visits establish became the tomb's permanent pattern: the place offers almost nothing, and the pilgrim brings everything. What the nineteenth-century pilgrims brought was reverence, and what they left, in the main, was prose about the visit published at home.

Some accounts of the letters trace the depositing of written messages at the tomb to that same nineteenth-century pilgrim culture, and the claim is plausible in kind even where it is thin in documentation: a visitor who has travelled across Europe to stand at a fictional grave is a visitor already composing sentences, and the step from composing them to leaving them is short. The firm documentary floor, however, is the 1930s, the decade in which the deposits had become numerous enough to constitute a practice and to impose a duty on the man employed to keep the place. The distinction matters to this article's argument, because a practice with a nineteenth-century penumbra and a twentieth-century documentary floor is a practice that grew from reading, slowly and without promotion, in the decades when Verona's Juliet industry was still small. The city bought the heroine's house only in 1905, and the famous courtyard across town was still, in the early decades of the century, a modest affair. The tomb letters embarrass any account in which the correspondence is a creature of the modern heritage business: the business grew up around the letters, not the letters around the business.

The guidebook trade standardized the devotion. Through the nineteenth century the great handbook series that organized British and American travel in Italy listed the tomb among Verona's sights, and a sight listed in a handbook acquires a liturgy: the traveller goes, stands, feels what the form requires, and records the feeling. What the tomb's liturgy increasingly required, as the century turned, was a written deposit. The shift from published travel prose to private deposited letters tracks the democratization of the pilgrimage itself: the early pilgrims were authors by profession, Byron and Dickens writing for print, while the later ones were readers pure and simple, whose only publication was a sheet of paper left in a crypt. The letters at the tomb are, in that sense, the moment the cult of Juliet passed from literature's producers to its consumers, and the whole subsequent history of the club is the history of that transfer being administered with respect.

There is also a geography to the prehistory. The tomb stands apart from the centre, in the Franciscan complex beyond the Adige's bend, and a visit there was never a casual detour between shops. The pilgrims who left letters in the 1930s had walked to a deconsecrated monastery to address a stone coffin, which is to say they arrived in a devotional frame of mind and with time to write. The letters they left were correspondingly unhurried compositions, not the scraps and wall-scribbles that later accumulated at the house in the commercial centre. When the club, decades afterward, inherited the correspondence, it inherited from the tomb a standard of seriousness that the office's rules still carry: letters are read whole, answered whole, and kept.

## Ettore Solimani, the First Secretary of Juliet

The gatherer was Ettore Solimani, the custodian of Juliet's tomb in the 1930s. The documented accounts agree on the outline of his role, and the agreement across independent sources, the Friedmans' book of 2006, the club's own published history, and decades of Verona journalism, makes the outline solid. Solimani collected the letters visitors left at the sarcophagus. He read them. And then, moved by what they contained, he began writing back, answering strangers' accounts of their love troubles in the heroine's name and on her behalf. In doing so he became, in the phrase every account of the phenomenon repeats, the first secretary of Juliet.

The importance of Solimani's decision is easy to miss because it looks so small. A custodian who sweeps a crypt is not obliged to conduct a correspondence, and no institution instructed him to begin one. Verona's municipality had not yet built the apparatus of Juliet tourism that later decades produced; the balcony across the city was itself a recent addition to a house the city had bought in 1905, and the tomb sat outside the commercial itinerary. What Solimani did was private and, in the strictest sense, literary: he treated the letters as genuine correspondence addressed to a person whose office he happened to keep. The letters asked a question each, in effect: is there anyone who understands? His replies answered that someone did. Every later development in this history, the club, the prizes, the archive, the film, is the scaling of that single act of conscientious reading.

His example also fixed the practice's defining rule, the rule that distinguishes the Juliet mail from every other fan correspondence in the world. All of the letters are answered. Not the charming ones, not the literate ones, not the ones from countries whose stamps collectors prize: all of them, as a matter of the office's founding obligation. The rule descends directly from the tomb. A custodian who answered only some of the letters left at a grave would be performing a cruelty the fiction itself condemns, since the play's catastrophe is precisely a failure of delivery. Friar Laurence's letter to Mantua, the letter "full of charge, of dear import," as Laurence calls it in Act 5, Scene 2, never reaches Romeo, and Laurence's cry when he learns of the failure is the play's own verdict on neglected mail: "Unhappy fortune!" The neglecting of a letter "may do much danger" (5.2). Solimani, whether or not he framed it to himself in those terms, built the opposite institution: a place where no letter to Verona's heroine would ever be neglected. The club that inherited his work inherited the rule with it.

Solimani's secretaryship also established the second permanent feature of the practice, its impersonation. He did not answer as himself, a municipal employee offering sympathy. He answered as Juliet's secretary, from her office, in her name's authority. The distinction matters to the writers, whose letters almost never ask for a Veronese pen friend. They ask for Juliet, and the office's fiction, openly maintained and never disguised as anything but a fiction maintained in earnest, is that Juliet's household receives and replies. The secretaries who followed Solimani kept the impersonation and refined it. Replies are written by hand, on the club's paper, and signed by Juliet herself, according to the accounts of visitors who have watched the work and of writers who have received the letters. The hand that writes is a volunteer's. The signature belongs to the heroine. Between those two facts stands the whole phenomenon, and neither half of it can be removed without destroying the thing entire.

## Giulio Tamassia and the Founding of the Juliet Club in 1972

Between Solimani's secretaryship and the modern club stands a gap of decades, and then a founder. In 1972, Giulio Tamassia, a Veronese man of letters surrounded by a circle of friends whom the accounts variously describe as artists, scholars, and local intellectuals, established the Club di Giulietta as a cultural association devoted to the legend of the two lovers and to the city that keeps it. The club was not founded as a mail office. It was founded as one of Verona's many cultural circles, staging events, concerts, and exhibitions around the Shakespearean inheritance. But Tamassia knew the letters, as every Veronese custodian of the legend knew them. The mail addressed to the heroine had continued to arrive through the postwar decades, at the tomb, at the heroine's house, and at the municipality itself, and the city authorities, in the Friedmans' account, struggled to answer it. Tamassia's club took the correspondence into its charge, recruited multilingual secretaries to handle it, and gave Solimani's private practice an institution, an address, and a succession.

One popular magazine account of the club gives a different founding year, 1993, and describes the founder as a baker. The divergence deserves a sentence because it illustrates how carelessly the phenomenon is often reported. Against that single account stand the club's own published history, the Friedmans' researched book of 2006, and the consistent testimony of Verona journalism across several decades, all of which give 1972 and a founder embedded in the city's cultural life. The 1972 date is the one this article uses, and the discrepancy is recorded here so that a reader who meets the other figure elsewhere knows it exists and knows its weight: one unsourced magazine profile against the institution's own record and the standard book on the subject.

Under Tamassia the club settled into the form the later accounts describe. It is a nonprofit association, sanctioned by the city of Verona, whose best-known work is the correspondence but whose charter is broader: the promotion of the lovers' legend and of Verona's culture around it. The club keeps an office in the historic centre, in a courtyard building near the church of Sant'Anastasia, at the address it publishes for the mail, Vicolo Santa Cecilia 9. From that office the secretaries conduct the answering, maintain the archive, and organize the club's calendar of prizes and celebrations. The succession from founder to daughter is part of the documented record: Giovanna Tamassia, who began the work after language studies at the University of Verona and who is described in the profiles as the club's president and leading secretary, took up her father's endeavour and leads the team in the accounts of the 2010s and after. The wallet she carries in one magazine profile bears the words "love is blind," a detail the profile's writer plainly included because it condenses the office's atmosphere: professional about the mail, unembarrassed about the sentiment.

The founding matters for the interpretation argued later in this article, so its sequence should be fixed precisely. Letters first, in the 1930s. A private answerer second, in the same decade. An institution third, forty years later, taking over an obligation that already existed. At no point in that sequence does a marketing department appear. Verona's tourism industry was fully capable of inventing attractions in those decades, and did invent them, as the history of the heroine's [house and its assembled balcony in the Casa di Giulietta article](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/) demonstrates. The mail is not one of its inventions. It is a folk practice that an institution was eventually created to serve, and the club's whole legitimacy rests on that order of events.

## Giovanna Tamassia and the Office in the New Century

The club's modern face is the founder's daughter. Giovanna Tamassia came to the work, in the profiles' account, through languages: studies at the University of Verona, with English and German translation among her competences, in a family whose cultural association needed exactly those skills for its growing international mail. She began the work roughly two decades before the profiles of the early 2020s described her, and after her father Giulio's death she took the club's presidency, becoming its public voice in interview after interview. The descriptions converge on an administrator of a peculiar kind: guardian of an archive, manager of a multilingual volunteer roster, spokeswoman for a heroine, and working secretary among secretaries, reading and answering letters at the same desks as the volunteers she leads.

Her quoted observations supply much of what the world knows about the mail's contents. It is Giovanna who itemizes the languages, Italian, German, English, Spanish, Japanese, Russian, Arabic, Finnish, letters even in braille, and Giovanna who summarizes the mail's matter in a sentence the profiles repeat: the main theme is love issues, or the search for love. It is also Giovanna who gives the ten-thousand figure for the annual mail, a number this article reports as hers, in her role, against the lower and higher figures other sources give. The presidency's style matters to the phenomenon's interpretation. The club is not fronted by a tourism official or a marketing appointee but by the founder's daughter, a translator who does the work herself, and the continuity is dynastic in the way of family firms and religious orders alike: the office passes by inheritance and vocation, not by appointment. Verona's other Juliet institutions are administered by the municipality and its appointees; the correspondence, alone among them, is kept by a family that adopted the duty in 1972 and has not put it down.

Under her leadership the office absorbed the two shocks of its modern history, the email era and the film. Email changed the mail's texture: typed confessions arriving instantly, from correspondents who might never have bought a stamp, answered by the same rule as the handwritten letters and increasingly forwarded to remote secretaries for reply. The film of 2010 changed the mail's scale and the office's visibility, sending visitors to the small rooms near Sant'Anastasia as well as letters to its boxes. A documentary portrait of the secretaries' work circulates under the title The Secretaries of Juliet, one more sign that the office itself, and not only its heroine, had become an object of the world's curiosity. Through both shocks the office's method survived unchanged, which is the strongest evidence that the method, and not any medium, is the institution: handwriting, the signature, the archive, and the rule that all of the letters are answered.

## Three Addresses of One Correspondence: Tomb, House, Office

The correspondence has had three homes, and the sequence of homes is the institutional history in miniature. The first home was the tomb, where the letters were deposits rather than deliveries and the custodian was their unintended reader. The second is the heroine's house on Via Cappello, the Casa di Giulietta, where the red mailboxes receive the letters of visitors who prefer to deliver their confidences in person at the shrine itself; the house's own history, its assembled balcony, and the rules governing the notes left in its entrance passage belong to the series' article on the house, and are treated here only as the mail's middle address. The third home is the club's office, to which the house's mailbox contents, the world's posted letters, and the email all converge. Each move in the sequence regularized the practice further, from votive deposit, to visitor's letter, to administered correspondence, and each move preserved the obligation the previous stage had created. The tomb made the letters sacred, the house made them numerous, and the office made them answered.

The hinge between the second address and the third is a red box. The mailboxes at the house collect what the courtyard inspires, and their contents travel the short distance to the office, where a note composed in a crowd becomes a letter on a desk. The journey is the phenomenon in miniature: emotion produced at a shrine, converted by an institution into correspondence, and answered as correspondence, in writing, by hand, months later if need be. Nothing else in Verona's romantic economy makes that conversion. The statue is touched and left; the balcony is photographed and left; the letter is taken away, read, and replied to, which is why the correspondence, alone among the courtyard's rituals, has a history with documents.

The office's location completes the geography. It sits in the historic centre, a short walk from the house and within the same few streets as the city's other Shakespearean stations, in a courtyard building near Sant'Anastasia whose modesty every visiting profile remarks on: a small suite of rooms, famously short even of amenities, filled with the apparatus of paper. The contrast between the address's fame and the rooms' plainness is itself part of the documentary record, and part of the phenomenon's defence. Verona possesses grander rooms in abundance. The correspondence lives in a small office because the correspondence was never the city's showpiece. It is the city's chore and its honour, performed at desks.

## The Office as Destination: Visiting the Secretaries

The office itself has become a minor destination, and the accounts of those who have visited it form a distinct shelf of the evidence. Journalists, travel writers, film researchers, and the curious among Verona's visitors have climbed to the small rooms near Sant'Anastasia for decades, and their descriptions agree to a degree that suggests the rooms discipline the describing: an ordinary workspace, piles of envelopes sorted by language, the club's paper and envelopes, boxes of the archive, secretaries at their desks writing by hand. A visiting writer in 2017 described the branded stationery and the wooden box of waiting letters. A dance company's delegation, received before a Verona performance, described the sorting explained to them by three secretaries at work. A magazine's correspondent described the archive as a papery labyrinth. None of the accounts describes a showroom. The office receives visitors the way a working chapel receives them, without rearranging itself.

The visits matter beyond local colour, because they are the phenomenon's verification system. A correspondence conducted by an imaginary office would dissolve under inspection; correspondents cannot check, but visitors can, and the stream of profiles across forty years constitutes a rolling audit of the club's claims. The visitors found volunteers where the club said volunteers would be, handwriting where the club said handwriting happened, and an archive where the club said the answered letters went. In an afterlife crowded with manufactured sites, the office's verifiability is a rare property, and the club's willingness to be watched at work, by journalists, scholars, and casual pilgrims alike, is continuous with its founding act: Solimani's practice was never hidden either, performed as it was in a public crypt by a public employee. The secretaries keep the heroine's secrets. They keep none of their own.

## How the Juliet Club Works: Sorting, Answering, Archiving

The daily work of the club is clerical, and its clerical character is the point. Letters reach Verona through three channels. They arrive by post from abroad, addressed with a completeness that ranges from a full street address to the famous minimal form, "Juliet, Verona," which senders in dozens of countries have trusted to the postal systems of the world. They arrive by hand, left in the red mailboxes the club maintains at the heroine's house and at its own office by visitors who prefer delivery to postage. And they arrive electronically, since the club publishes an email address for the correspondence alongside the postal one. All three streams converge on the same office and the same obligation: to be read, answered, and kept.

### How does an envelope addressed only to "Juliet, Verona" reach the Club?

It reaches the club because Verona's postal sorting treats the heroine as a known addressee. Letters bearing nothing more than her name and the city's are recognized in the sorting offices, directed to Verona's historic centre, and delivered to the Juliet Club's office, the destination the world's senders intend.

The Friedmans' account of the phenomenon states the outcome flatly: the letters, however briefly addressed, reach their destination, and all of them receive an answer. No return address is guaranteed an answer's arrival unless the writer supplies one legibly, which is why the club's guidance to correspondents stresses the return address as the single practical requirement of the whole ritual.

Once inside the office, the letters pass through a routine that several visiting journalists have described in consistent terms. The mail is opened and sorted, first by language, then read by a secretary competent in that language, answered by hand on the club's paper, and filed. The answered letters are not discarded. They are archived, sorted by criteria that include language and year, building the paper labyrinth that visitors to the office describe: boxes, shelves, and files of correspondence stretching back through the decades of the club's systematic keeping. The Friedmans' account dates that systematic archive from 1989, a detail worth a heading of its own.

### Why does the Club's systematic archive begin in 1989 rather than in the 1930s?

Because the early decades of the practice were personal rather than institutional. Solimani's answering in the 1930s was one man's correspondence conducted from a custodian's post, and the letters of that era were handled as encounters, not records; no archive was designed to receive them.

The club founded in 1972 gave the practice an office, but the Friedmans' account dates the loving, systematic preservation of the correspondence from 1989, when the keeping of the letters as a collection, sorted and retained as the documentary memory of the phenomenon, became part of the office's method. The distinction explains why the club can speak with authority about the modern mail and only with piety about its origins.

The routine's final step is the reply's despatch. Visitors' accounts describe a waiting period on the order of two to three months between a letter's arrival and its answer's return journey, a delay the club does not apologize for, since handwriting thousands of individual replies is slow by definition. The delay belongs to the ritual's meaning. A reply from Juliet that arrived by automated response in an afternoon would announce itself as a service. A letter that arrives months later, handwritten, on paper headed with the heroine's image, announces itself as correspondence. The writers, judging by the letters of thanks that form a documented subclass of the mail, understand the difference and value it.

### What training does a new secretary of Juliet receive?

A new secretary is trained by the secretaries already doing the work. The club requires no formal qualification, and its members come from every walk of life, students, writers, translators, retired diplomats among them; what the office supplies is apprenticeship.

A volunteer begins under the supervision of experienced secretaries, learning the tone the office keeps, kindness balanced with practicality, the discipline of answering in the writer's own language where the team allows, and the signature convention by which every reply goes out over the heroine's name. One volunteer's published account of joining the club describes the training as rigorous and the supervision as constant, which matches the office's responsibility: its letters go to strangers at vulnerable moments, and a careless sentence in Juliet's name would be a betrayal of the office's founding trust.

## Who Are Juliet's Secretaries?

The secretaries are volunteers. That single fact, confirmed by every account of the club, does more interpretive work than any other in this article, because it removes the correspondence from the economy of services. Nobody is paid to answer the letters, nobody is paid by the letter, and the office sells nothing to its correspondents. The team is international, as it must be to read the mail: accounts describe secretaries drawn from Italy, Britain, the Philippines, Poland, the United States, and beyond, including volunteers who travel to Verona for a season of answering and others who continue to receive forwarded letters, including email, in their home countries after returning. A New Zealand secretary described in one account spent a month's holiday answering letters daily in the office and still receives mail to answer from home. The office is, in effect, a small republic of readers, recruited by a fiction.

The size of that republic is one of the figures the sources refuse to agree on, and the disagreement is recorded in full in the counting section below. A magazine profile from 2001 described the club as seven women and a cat named Romeo, answering some four thousand letters a year. Later accounts describe forty-five secretaries, or fifteen permanent secretaries supported by an army of volunteers, or about fifty volunteers in total. The three descriptions are not necessarily contradictory, since a core team, a wider roster, and a worldwide circle of occasional helpers are three different sets wearing one name, but no source performs the reconciliation, and this article does not invent it. What all accounts agree on is the composition: mostly women, of many ages and professions, united by languages and by willingness. The mail they answer is itself about seventy percent from women, in the club's reported estimate, a proportion that makes the correspondence one of the largest sustained exchanges of women's writing about love in the modern world, conducted almost entirely outside literature's institutions.

Recruitment into the office follows a pattern the profiles repeat: witness first, service after. Visitors who watch the work, journalists among them, describe the same conversion, the wish to join the desk rather than merely report it. One magazine's narrator, an American writer who came to Verona on other business, watched the archive and the answering and recorded the outcome without embarrassment: seeing the answered letters and imagining the writers who had reached out was enough, and the visitor became a volunteer, trained and supervised like any other. The pattern matters because it shows the office reproducing itself by the force of its example rather than by advertisement. Nobody recruits for the club in the way attractions recruit staff. The mail itself, witnessed at close quarters, does the recruiting, which is one more way the institution grows from the practice outward instead of from a plan downward.

A secretary's day, in the journalists' descriptions, is unglamorous: piles of envelopes, the sorting by language, the reading, the writing, the filing. The glamour attaches only from outside, from the film image of the office as a sunlit room of beautiful counsellors. The profiles that linger on the work itself stress its emotional weight instead. The secretaries read, every week, a cross-section of the world's unhappiness in love, together with its happiness, its gratitude, and its jokes, and they answer in the voice of a fourteen-year-old fictional girl whose authority derives from having loved once, wholly, and died of it. Holding that voice steady across thousands of letters, neither slipping into parody nor into the tones of a clinic, is the office's real skill, and the training exists to protect it.

## The Secretary's Craft: Voice, Signature, and the Ethics of the Reply

Every reply the club sends performs a small, sustained act of impersonation, and the impersonation's rules are the office's real constitution. The letter is written by hand, because a printed reply would confess the machinery. It is written on the club's paper and sent in the club's envelopes, which visitors' accounts describe as printed with the heroine's image, because the correspondence must arrive looking like what it is, a letter from Juliet's household. And it is signed by Juliet, in the form visitors report as "Love, Juliet," because the correspondent did not write to a volunteer in Verona and must not be answered by one. The secretary disappears into the office exactly as an actor disappears into a part, and for the same reason: the person addressed exists only while the performance holds.

The impersonation is not a deception, and the office does not treat it as one. The club's existence is public, its volunteers are profiled in the press by name, and the Friedmans' book explains the whole arrangement to any reader who cares to know. The writers who receive the replies are, in the secretaries' testimony, generally aware that a volunteer wrote them, and value them anyway, which shows the contract's true terms: the reply is a token of being heard, written in the voice the writer requested. What the signature certifies is not that a fictional girl took up a pen, but that the writer's letter was received into the heroine's household, read there, and answered from there. The distinction is the same one theatre makes every night, and the club is, among its other natures, a theatre of one spectator per performance, running for ninety years.

Translation is part of the craft, and the least visible part. A reply in the writer's own language is not the office's courtesy but its method, since a confession answered in a foreign tongue arrives diminished, its intimacy taxed by the crossing. The club was multinational from its refounding for this reason: Giulio Tamassia recruited multilingual secretaries at the start, his daughter came to the work from language studies and translation at the University of Verona, and the roster's spread across Britain, the Philippines, Poland, the United States, and beyond is a map of the mail's own origins. The remote network extends the method past the office's walls, with letters forwarded to secretaries in their home countries, so that a confession written in Manila or Warsaw can be answered by a reader formed in its own language and posted back under the heroine's name. Impersonation in one language is theatre; impersonation in a dozen, sustained by volunteers who must each find the voice's equivalent in their own tongue, is a literary institution.

The craft has an ethics, and the secretaries articulate it in their interviews with unusual consistency. The reply is kind without being saccharine and practical without being clinical. It does not mock the premise, does not break character to wink at the arrangement, and does not pretend to powers the office lacks: Juliet's secretary cannot reunite lovers, soften parents, or foresee outcomes, and the replies, in the accounts, offer counsel and company rather than miracles. Where a letter's trouble exceeds counsel, the reply's duty contracts to the duty Solimani discovered at the tomb, which is acknowledgment under the heroine's name. The office also guards the correspondence's privacy as the heroine guarded hers: letters are archived, not exhibited, and the selection printed in the Friedmans' book appeared under the club's guardianship for the practice's honour. The whole craft can be summarized in the office's single rule, from which the others descend: every letter is answered, because in this story, an unanswered letter is how people die.

## Reading the Mail: Four Kinds of Letters and What They Ask

The secretaries' descriptions of the mail sort it into kinds, and the kinds reward a slower reading than the summary above allows, because each kind uses the heroine's office differently. The first and largest kind is the letter of the forbidden or obstructed love, the correspondence that mirrors the play most exactly. Its writers love where a family, a faith, a caste, a border, or a previous marriage forbids, and they write to the one figure whose story is the precedent for their own. What they ask is rarely permission. Juliet cannot grant permission, and the writers know it; what they ask is witness and judgement, whether the love is worth the price the world will charge, asked of the only correspondent who has paid the full price and can be presumed to have an opinion about its value. The secretary answering such a letter writes inside the play's shadow and knows it, and the office's discipline of kindness with practicality was built for exactly this mail.

The second kind is the letter of bereavement and betrayal, written after the fact, when nothing can be decided because everything has already happened. These writers do not ask what to do. They ask to be heard by someone who understands loss from the inside, and they choose the heroine for the same reason the tomb's first depositors chose the tomb: the confessor is safely beyond the social world in which the writer must go on living. A widow writing to Juliet risks nothing among her neighbours. A betrayed husband writing to Juliet risks nothing among his friends. The fiction's dead girl is the perfect analyst precisely because she cannot repeat, invoice, or pity in person, and the secretaries who answer this mail describe their task as acknowledgment, the confirmation that the story arrived and was read by a sympathetic mind. The reply matters less for its counsel than for its existence.

The third kind is the letter of report and thanks, the mail that closes a loop. Writers who confided a dilemma return, sometimes after years, to say what they did and what followed: the marriage made or refused, the lover kept or left, the family reconciled or lost. The club's archive, which keeps the correspondence in order, makes these returns legible as continuations of earlier letters, and the secretaries value them out of proportion to their number because they convert the office's work from a monologue of advice into a correspondence in the full sense, with consequences. A fourth kind comes from the young: school classes set to write to the heroine as an exercise, teenagers writing in earnest beneath the cover of the exercise, children whose letters mix the play's plot with their own first attachments. The office answers these with the same rule as the rest, and the teachers who set the exercise have made the red mailboxes at the house and the posted letter to Verona a small institution of literacy education in several countries, whether or not they intended the letters' confessional depth.

Two features unite the four kinds and separate the club's mail from ordinary fan correspondence. The first is duration: writers address Juliet across whole lifetimes, the interval between a schoolgirl's exercise and a widow's report sometimes spanning decades of the archive. The second is candour about the fiction. The writers, in the secretaries' consistent testimony, do not mistake the heroine for a living girl in Verona; they name the pretence and use it, because the pretence is what makes the candour possible. A correspondence whose terms everyone understands, and whose central personage everyone knows to be a character in a play, sustains confidences that no real institution could be trusted with. That is not a failure of the writers' reason. It is the most sophisticated thing about them, and any account of the phenomenon that treats the letters as evidence of gullibility has simply not read the secretaries' descriptions of the mail.

## What the World Writes to Juliet

The letters themselves are the primary documents of the phenomenon, and the club's members have described their contents in interviews for decades, in terms consistent enough to map. The largest category asks for advice in love: whether to pursue a person, whether to forgive an infidelity, whether to marry against a family's opposition, whether to wait for someone separated by distance, prison, war, or another marriage. A second category narrates rather than asks: writers who tell the heroine their story because the telling itself is the need, and who expect from the reply only the acknowledgment that the story was heard. A third gives thanks, sometimes years after an earlier letter, reporting that the advice was taken or defied and what came of it. A fourth is written by the young, schoolchildren set the exercise by teachers and teenagers writing in earnest, and a fifth, the smallest and gravest, comes from writers in real despair, for whom the letter to Juliet is a message cast toward the only listener they believe cannot judge them.

Running through the mail, according to the secretaries' oft-repeated observation, is a formula. Letter after letter, in language after language, contains some version of the sentence: Juliet, I can only tell you; only you can help me. The formula is the phenomenon's thesis stated by its practitioners. The writers are not confused about the address. They are precise about it. Every living confidant is disqualified by some interest: parents by their plans, friends by their loyalties, clergy by their doctrine, therapists by their fees and their hour. The fictional girl is qualified by her story. She loved against her family's command, kept the secret, held her nerve through the Nurse's betrayal and her mother's dismissal, and died rather than live the substitute life arranged for her. A correspondent whose own family forbids a marriage is not writing to a symbol. She is writing to the one authority whose credentials on the exact question are unimpeachable, and the formula records that she knows it.

The languages of the mail map the play's own world empire. Italian and English predominate in the accounts, with German, Spanish, French, Portuguese, Japanese, Russian, Arabic, and Finnish all named by Giovanna Tamassia in interview, and letters in braille recorded among the arrivals. The team answers in the writer's language wherever its roster allows, which is one reason the roster is international by necessity rather than by decoration. The subjects repeat across the languages with a monotony that the secretaries describe without condescension: the same dilemmas, posed by a student in Manila, a bride in Ohio, a widower in Finland, a schoolgirl in Brazil. Read in bulk, the mail is the largest running survey of romantic trouble ever assembled by accident, and the club's archive, not its office, is where its full scholarly value sleeps.

### What does a secretary do when a letter describes a writer in real despair?

The secretary answers it, as the office answers every letter, and answers it with the care the training exists to supply. Despairing letters are not set aside, not passed to an agency, and not answered with a formula; the club's members have described their method in interview as a balance of kindness and practicality, applied where the need is greatest.

The reply does not play at being a clinician's letter, does not offer what a fictional heroine's office cannot deliver, and does not leave the writer unacknowledged. It writes back as Juliet, warmly, seriously, and without false promises, because the office's whole discipline rests on the recognition that some correspondents have no other correspondent. The ethical weight of that recognition is the heaviest part of the secretary's work, and it is borne by volunteers, which is why the office supervises its beginners and why the gravest letters are not left to the newest hands.

The content of the mail also vindicates the writers against the easiest sneer. These are not, in the main, requests for lottery luck or for a spell to bind a lover, though the courtyard rituals of the heroine's house attract that register of hope and the distinction between the house's customs and the club's mail is kept throughout this article. The letters the club describes are correspondence: reasoned, anguished, funny, self-aware, often explicitly aware of the fiction they are using. Many writers say outright that they know Juliet is a character and write anyway, which is the single most important sentence in the whole archive for the argument of this article. The practice does not rest on a mistake about what is real. It rests on a judgement about where a true hearing can be had, and the judgement is a reader's judgement, formed from the play.

## The Office's Limits: What the Secretaries Will Not Do

The office's authority rests as much on its refusals as on its answers, and the refusals deserve a section of their own, because they mark the boundary between the real practice and the fantasy the film built on it. The secretaries do not intervene. A letter begging Juliet to reconcile a family, to write to a lover, to appear in a dream, receives a letter back, counsel and company in the heroine's voice, and nothing else; the office conducts no negotiations, forwards no messages between correspondents, and brokers no meetings. The secretaries do not prophesy. Questions about whether a love will last are answered with the office's honesty about its own limits, since the heroine's authority derives from her story, not from omniscience, and her story is precisely a warning against confident prediction. The secretaries do not publish. Beyond the guarded selection in the Friedmans' book, the correspondence stays in the archive, and a writer's name, town, and trouble remain where the writer sent them, in the office's keeping.

The sharpest limit is temporal, and the film of 2010 is built from breaking it. The film's plot requires a letter answered fifty years after it was written, its reply still able to change a life. The real office's discipline is the opposite: letters are answered in their season, within months, because the office knows that counsel has a shelf life and that the play itself is the standing monument to advice delivered late. Friar Laurence's plan was sound, his letter accurate, his intentions perfect; the catastrophe is in the timing, and the club's whole routine, the sorting, the desks, the two-to-three-month queue lamented by no one who understands it, is engineered against that single failure mode. An office that answered letters fifty years late would be a museum. The Verona office is a post office of the heart with a deadline, and the deadline is the difference between the institution and its legend.

There is a final refusal, quieter than the others. The secretaries will not break the impersonation to claim the correspondence as their own achievement. The replies go out over the heroine's name; the profiles name the volunteers, but the desk's discipline returns the credit to Juliet, whose office it remains. In a culture of personal brands, an institution staffed by dozens of skilled, multilingual writers, none of whom signs her own name to her best-read work, is an anomaly worth recording. The anomaly is not humility as decoration. It is the mechanism of the whole practice, since a reply signed by a stranger in Verona would answer a different letter than the one that was sent.

## Counting the Mail: The Club's Diverging Numbers

How many letters? The honest answer is a shelf of attributed figures, and the dishonest one is any single round number. A profile in People magazine in 2001 counted about four thousand letters a year, answered by seven women. A visiting account from 2008 put the flow at five thousand or more letters a year. A Guideposts account spoke of more than six thousand a year. Giovanna Tamassia, quoted in a magazine profile in the travel press, said the club receives more than ten thousand a year. Against those, a family of later accounts, including student journalism and travel features drawing on the club's own publicity, gives fifty thousand letters a year and forty-five secretaries. One Italian travel account from 2019 described about fifty volunteers answering some three hundred letters a month, which multiplies out to a figure lower than all the others. The figures cannot all describe the same quantity, and probably do not.

The resolution is not available in print, but its shape is visible. The low figures plausibly count the posted correspondence, the letters that arrive through the mail and receive a handwritten reply. The high figures plausibly fold in everything the heroine receives in every medium: the notes left in the red mailboxes at the house, the messages deposited in the courtyard, the emails, perhaps the votive scraps gathered from the tomb in earlier decades of the count. Between a posted letter and a note stuck to a wall there is a real difference of kind, and a club describing its whole harvest to a journalist will reach for the larger harvest, while a profile describing the answering desk will reach for the smaller. Both can be honest. Neither can be merged. What the spread of figures does establish beyond dispute is the order of the phenomenon: thousands of correspondences a year at the floor of the estimates, sustained across decades, which means the archive's plausible total runs to the hundreds of thousands of letters the club's own visitors' accounts report having been answered and kept since the practice began.

The secretaries' numbers wobble for the same reason. Seven women in 2001, fifteen permanent secretaries with an army of volunteers in one profile, forty-five secretaries in several accounts, about fifty volunteers in another: the sets overlap, a permanent desk team, a roster of regulars, and a penumbra of seasonal and remote helpers, and each source counts the set its visit happened to show. An almanac of the phenomenon would print the three tiers separately. This article's profile table does exactly that, and the reader who wants one number for the club should take the tiers as the answer: a small permanent team, a roster in the dozens, and a worldwide circle beyond easy counting.

There is a lesson in the mess, and it cuts in the club's favour. Institutions that manufacture an attraction audit it, because the numbers are the product. The Juliet mail's numbers have never been audited because the mail is not the product; the answers are the work, and the work is counted by the people doing it in the units that matter to them, the letters on the desk. The fifty-thousand figure may be the courtyard's arithmetic, the four-thousand figure the desk's, and the truth about the practice needs both: a shrine that draws paper by the ton, and an office that answers what is addressed to it, one letter at a time.

The email era widened the counting problem without changing the practice. A typed message costs its sender nothing, arrives instantly, and can be answered by a secretary on another continent to whom the office forwards it; whether such a message is a letter, for the purposes of the annual figure, is a question each source answers silently and differently. The club's own channels treat the streams as one correspondence, and the profiles that quote the largest totals generally describe the years after email and the film had both enlarged the flow. The prudent reader will therefore date every figure before using it: a count from 2001 describes a smaller, paper-only practice, a count from the following decade a larger, mixed one, and neither describes the other's world.

## Who Pays for the Answers? The Club's Modest Economics

The office's economics are the reverse of an attraction's. Its labour is donated, its premises are modest rooms in a courtyard building, its product is given away to anyone who writes, and its running costs, paper, envelopes, and the postage on thousands of international replies, fall on a nonprofit cultural association. The club is sanctioned by the city of Verona, in the Friedmans' formulation, and its broader cultural work, the prizes, the celebrations, the festival presence, belongs to the city's calendar of honour rather than to its commerce. No profile of the club publishes a budget, and this article invents none; the documentary record supports the structure, volunteer labour under municipal recognition, and leaves the figures unrecorded. The structure is itself the evidence that matters. An enterprise designed to extract money from the lovelorn would charge for replies, sell the letters, or license the office's image; the club does none of these things, and its poverty of apparatus, the small rooms visitors remark on, is the material form of its innocence.

Around the office, of course, money moves in quantity: the hotels, restaurants, weddings, and tours of Verona's romantic economy, examined in the series' separate study of that economy, draw real revenue from the lovers' fame, and the club's fame feeds that economy whether the club wishes it or not. The distinction the record sustains is between profiting from the phenomenon and the phenomenon being a profit device. Verona profits from the letters' fame in the way a city profits from any beloved institution it hosts. The institution itself runs on donated time and answered obligation. In the long adjudication between devotion and marketing that this article conducts below, the club's balance sheet, such as the record reveals it, is the devotion side's most concrete exhibit.

## The Dear Juliet Prize and the Club's Year

The club's calendar turns the private correspondence, once a year, into a public ceremony of itself. On Valentine's Day, within Verona's festival of love, the club awards the Cara Giulietta prize, the Dear Juliet prize, to the authors of the most compelling letters received during the previous year. The ceremony is held in Verona, associated in the accounts with the Piccolo Teatro di Giulietta, and the winning letters are honoured as what the office insists they are: literature written by amateurs to a heroine, judged on the force of what they confide. In 2013 the prize went to an Irish scholar, Jessica Lynch, a winner whose profession closes the circle neatly, since the mail's best letter that year came from a woman who studies writing for a living and still chose the fictional girl as her confessor. The prize performs a quiet argument. By honouring the finest letters, the club declares that the correspondence is a body of writing, that its writers are authors, and that the office's archive is a literary institution's archive, not a tourist board's filing cabinet.

The second prize runs in the opposite direction. Scrivere per Amore, Writing for Love, is the club's international literary award for published novels that tell of love, judged by a jury of standing: the twenty-second edition, announced in 2017, was chaired by the philosopher Umberto Galimberti and awarded at the Teatro Nuovo in Verona. Where the Cara Giulietta prize honours the letters coming in, Scrivere per Amore honours the professional literature going out, and between the two the club positions itself as a house of love-writing in both directions, amateur and professional, received and published. The pairing is shrewd, but it is also sincere in the office's own terms, since the secretaries' daily work is precisely the reading of love-writing, and the step from judging correspondents to judging novelists is shorter than it looks.

### What makes one letter stand out for the Cara Giulietta prize?

A prize letter is chosen from the year's ordinary mail, not commissioned for the occasion. The accounts describe the award as honouring the most beautiful or most compelling letters received during the previous year, and the quality it rewards is candour: a true story of love told completely to the one correspondent who could be told everything.

No literary polish is required, since the secretaries read schoolchildren and the elderly with the same attention, and the prize's history, which includes a professional scholar among its winners alongside correspondents of every kind, shows the jury honouring force of feeling and honesty of telling rather than craft. The prize turns no writer into an author for hire; it certifies, once a year, that the year's mountain of private writing contained pages worthy of the literature the office serves.

The year's other fixed point is September, when the club celebrates Juliet's birthday. The date is Verona's own tradition, and it sits in instructive tension with the play, where the Nurse fixes the heroine's birthday with famous precision: "On Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3), Lammas Eve being the last day of July. The city that keeps the heroine celebrates her birth in September anyway, following a local tradition older than the tourist industry, and the club keeps the city's date. The discrepancy is a small, perfect emblem of the whole phenomenon: the Verona Juliet is not quite Shakespeare's Juliet, but a civic personage descended from her, with her own calendar, her own address, and her own mail. The letters are not addressed to a text. They are addressed to the person the text became in the city that adopted her.

Around those fixed points the club plays its part in the city's romantic season, the Verona in Love festival in the week of Valentine's Day, when the correspondence's public face is busiest and the office's small rooms receive the journalists whose profiles supply much of this article's evidence. Researchers assembling the documentary record of the phenomenon, from the club's own site to the profiles and the standard book, can gather the sources most efficiently with a digital workspace such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), which keeps notes, clippings, and drafts in one ordered place while a project's evidence accumulates across years of publication. The club itself keeps the older technology: paper, sorted by hand.

## The Film of 2010 and the Surge in the Mail

In 2010 the phenomenon acquired a worldwide advertisement. The film Letters to Juliet, directed by Gary Winick and inspired by the Friedmans' book, dramatized the secretaries' office through a fictional plot about a letter answered fifty years late. The film's own claims on the tradition, its liberties and its debts, belong to the series' separate article on [the Letters to Juliet film and the tradition it dramatized](/2029/07/06/letters-to-juliet-film-tradition/), and are not re-adjudicated here. What belongs to this article is the film's documented effect on the club's postbag: a noticeable influx of letters followed the film's release, observed by the club and recorded in the profiles of the years after. Writers who had never heard of the office learned from a cinema screen that a letter addressed to Juliet, Verona would be answered, and wrote.

The surge matters to the interpretation of the phenomenon because it is sometimes used as evidence that the mail is a media effect, a fad manufactured by a film. The chronology refutes that use. The mail predates the film by eight decades, the club by nearly four, and the standard book by four years; the film dramatized the office because the office existed, and the influx it caused was an increase in a flow, not the creation of one. A fad's curve rises from zero and returns to it. The Verona curve rose from thousands and settled, by every account, higher than before, which is the signature of publicity acting on a practice with its own roots. The film's real gift to the club was not the letters but the witnesses: millions of cinemagoers who know that the secretaries exist, and whose knowledge protects the practice's fame from the forgetfulness that kills customs.

## Letters to the Fictional Elsewhere: Holmes, God, and the Verona Difference

Verona's correspondence is not the world's only mail to an addressee who cannot receive it, and the comparisons sharpen what is distinctive about it. Letters addressed to Sherlock Holmes at 221B Baker Street arrived for decades at the building society that occupied the site, which famously employed a secretary to answer them; the Holmes mail is largely a request for the detective's services or a salute to his existence, a correspondence with a celebrity. Letters addressed to God arrive in Jerusalem, where the postal service gathers them and, in the documented practice, places them in the Western Wall; that correspondence is prayer by post, its addressee omnipotent and its expected reply silence. Letters to Santa Claus fill the sacks of every postal service on earth each December, answered by volunteers and charities in many countries; that correspondence is a rite of childhood, written on behalf of a fiction the writers are expected to outgrow.

The Verona mail differs from all three in the one property that matters: the relation it assumes. Holmes is written to as a professional, God as a power, Santa as a benefactor. Juliet is written to as a confidante, an equal in misfortune, and the letters ask for no service but understanding and no gift but judgement. The Holmes writer wants a case solved; the Verona writer wants a heart read. Nor is the Verona fiction outgrown: the archive holds the same writers at fifteen and at fifty, which no Santa correspondence does. The distinction explains the office's longevity. Services can be discontinued when fashions change, and benefactors are abandoned when belief lapses, but the need for a perfectly discreet witness to a forbidden or broken love renews itself in every generation that reads the play, and the play is read in every school system on earth. The club's true endowment is the curriculum.

One further difference completes the comparison. None of the other offices treats its mail as literature. The Holmes letters are answered and forgotten; the letters to God are gathered into a wall; the Santa letters are a season's kindness. The Verona office archives its correspondence as a collection, prints a guarded selection in a book, and awards an annual prize to the finest letters of the year, judging strangers' confidences by the standards of the art the heroine's story belongs to. The distinction is the club's self-understanding in a single gesture: the mail is not a curiosity attached to a literary monument, it is a continuation of literature by other means, written by the play's readers in the play's own city, and the office keeps it the way libraries keep manuscripts, because that is what, on the evidence of ninety years, the letters are.

## The Letters as Literature: The Friedmans' Book and the Archive

In 2006 the phenomenon received its edition. Letters to Juliet, by Lise Friedman and Ceil Friedman, published by Stewart, Tabori and Chang, gathered the history of the correspondence, the story of the club, and a selection of the letters themselves, more than seventy-five of them printed with the club's cooperation. The book changed the phenomenon's documentary status. Before it, the mail was known through journalism, vivid but episodic; after it, any reader could inspect a corpus, test the secretaries' descriptions of the letters' kinds against printed examples, and follow the institutional history from the tomb through Solimani to the Tamassias in one researched narrative. The Friedmans wrote as advocates, and their advocacy should be registered; Ceil Friedman is a Verona resident and translator embedded in the world the book describes. But advocacy backed by a printed corpus is a different evidentiary object from advocacy alone, because the letters in the book can be read against the claims made for them. They sustain the claims. The printed correspondence shows the range the secretaries describe, supplication, narration, gratitude, despair, in voices that are unmistakably individual and unmistakably aware of the fiction they are using.

The book's second life confirms its standing as the standard account. It gave the 2010 film its premise and its producers their approach to the club, and it remains the source to which serious journalism on the phenomenon returns. It also set a precedent for the archive's use: the letters may be published, in selection, under the club's guardianship, for the purpose of witnessing the practice. The precedent is carefully bounded. The archive is not open to casual inspection; the correspondents wrote to Juliet, not to posterity, and the club's willingness to print a selection for a book that honours the practice does not convert private letters into public documents. Scholars of the phenomenon therefore work, as this article works, from the published selection, the club's own accounts, and the profiles of the office, and the boundary itself is evidence of the office's ethics: the archive is kept the way the heroine kept secrets, available to testimony about its existence, closed about its contents except where the club judges publication to serve the writers' own institution.

Read as literature, the corpus supports a judgement that journalism rarely pauses to make. The letters are a genre. They have conventions, the direct address, the formula of exclusive confidence, the compressed narration of a love's whole history in a few pages, the request for judgement; they have a tradition, transmitted not by instruction but by the play itself, which teaches every reader what a confidence to Juliet contains; and they have, in the secretaries' replies, a answering genre, the letter from the heroine's household, kind, practical, and brief. Genres are how cultures store practices. The Verona correspondence is a stored practice of confession, maintained for ninety years by its participants, and the Friedmans' book is its first anthology.

## Devotion or Staged Authenticity? The Critics Disagree

Three kinds of authority have written about the letters, and they do not agree. The first is the keeper of the record: Ceil Friedman, the Verona-based translator who, with Lise Friedman, wrote the standard account of the phenomenon in 2006 with the club's cooperation, printing dozens of the letters themselves. In the Friedmans' presentation the correspondence is what it appears to be, a sincere confessional practice, and the secretaries are its guardians. The second authority is the theory of tourism. Dean MacCannell, whose 1976 study The Tourist gave the field its master concept of staged authenticity, taught readers to suspect that the experiences destinations offer are back regions arranged for display, authenticity manufactured as a product. Applied to Verona, that suspicion has an easy target in the heroine's house, where a balcony was assembled in the 1930s to give visitors the picture they came for. The third authority is the scholarship of literary tourism proper, represented by Nicola J. Watson, whose study The Literary Tourist examined how readers visit the places of books: in Watson's account, the literary place is neither found nor simply fabricated, but produced by the acts of reading visitors perform there, so that the written word and the visited ground continually remake each other.

The disagreement between the second and third authorities is the live one, and the Juliet mail adjudicates it. MacCannell's framework predicts that a phenomenon like the letters would be organized from above: an attraction scripted by its custodians, performed for an audience, and valued for the revenue it draws. The documentary record shows the opposite order at every point. The letters originated with the visitors themselves, at a tomb outside the commercial itinerary, before any office existed to profit from them. The first answerer was a custodian acting on conscience, without instruction or pay. The institution arrived forty years late, and it is a nonprofit staffed by unpaid volunteers whose product, the reply, is given away. The revenue the mail generates flows to Verona's hotels and restaurants, the economy examined in the series' account of [Verona's economy built on two fictional lovers](/2029/07/27/verona-economy-fictional-lovers/), not to the office that does the work. A staged authenticity is staged by someone, for someone; the Juliet correspondence was staged by no one, and its stage manager, when one finally appeared, was a cultural circle that took on the mail as a burden of honour.

Watson's framework survives the evidence better, and the Friedmans' record supplies the proof her theory needs. In Watson's terms, the tomb, the house, and the office are places where reading is performed, and the letters are the purest form of that performance: the reader writes back into the book. The correspondence even repairs the play. Shakespeare's plot turns on a letter that fails, Friar Laurence's explanation entrusted to Friar John, stopped by plague and suspicion in Act 5, Scene 2, while the letter that does arrive, Romeo's farewell to his father, is produced in Act 5, Scene 3 as evidence over two bodies. Verona's modern mail is the systematic negation of that failure: a city that lost the lovers to an undelivered letter built an office whose sole law is that every letter gets through and every letter is answered. The secretaries are, without any of them needing to say so, the anti-Friar Johns of the story's afterlife, and the thousands of writers who address them each year are performing, in Watson's sense, the most literal act of readerly devotion literature knows: they write to the character, and the character's household writes back.

The club's own public works supply the suspicion's hardest test, and should be faced before the verdict. A literary prize chaired by a philosopher of Umberto Galimberti's standing, a Valentine's ceremony honouring the year's finest letters, a birthday celebration for the heroine each September: a skeptic can read the whole calendar as brand management, the office polishing the city's romantic trademark. The reading fails on the calendar's economics and direction. The prizes cost the club effort and return it no revenue; the Cara Giulietta ceremony honours amateur writers who will never buy anything from the office; the birthday is kept for a personage who cannot attend. Brand management spends where the spending shows. The club's calendar spends where the practice points, inward toward the correspondence and outward toward literature, and an institution managing a brand would long ago have monetized the one asset it guards without exception, the archive, which remains unexhibited and unsold. The calendar is not the exception to the devotion. It is the devotion, wearing its public clothes.

The verdict follows. Against the suspicion that the letters are staged, the record shows a practice invented by its participants, maintained by volunteers, and older than its institution. Against any sentimental claim that the practice is untouched by commerce, the record shows a city that profits handsomely around it and a film that multiplied its flow. Both corrections leave the centre standing: the mail is real, its motive is confessional, and its addressee was chosen by readers who knew the play well enough to know exactly who in it could be trusted. The club is the servant of that choice, not its author.

## The Girl Who Had No One to Write To

The correspondence's final irony is biographical, inside the fiction. Juliet is the great confider of the play who herself has no confidant she can keep. Her confidences are made in person, at night, to the boy in the garden, and they are the boldest speeches in the play: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2). After the marriage, the circle of those who know closes to two, the Nurse and the Friar. The Nurse defects in Act 3. The Friar, faithful but fallible, answers her desperation in Act 4 with a plan that depends on a letter, and the letter is the one that fails. In her last soliloquy, alone before the potion, Juliet confides in objects, the vial, the dagger she lays beside her in case the mixture does not work: "What if this mixture do not work at all? / Shall I be married then tomorrow morning?" (4.3). The speech is a letter with no address, a confession delivered to the air of her chamber because every living ear in Verona has been closed to her by betrayal, dismissal, or distance.

The modern writers reverse her position exactly. They are the ones with the unspeakable secret, and she is the ear that cannot close. Where Juliet's world gave her a nurse who talked, a mother who withdrew, and a friar whose post failed, the writers' world gives them Juliet, who in the whole afterlife of the play has never repeated a confidence, never withdrawn from a correspondent, and never, under the club's administration, left a letter unanswered. The correspondence is the play's confessional machinery rebuilt with the defects engineered out, by readers who diagnosed the defects from the text. That diagnosis is literary criticism of a high order, performed not in essays but in envelopes, and it is the deepest reason the phenomenon belongs in a series on the play's afterlife rather than in a guidebook's sidebar.

## The Play's Failed Letter and Verona's Answered Mail

Stand back from the institution and the phenomenon resolves into a single literary fact. Romeo and Juliet is a tragedy about messages: carried by nurses, entrusted to friars, miscarried by plague, and arriving, when they arrive, too late or at the cost of the messenger. Its heroine conducts her whole love by courier, through the Nurse in Act 2, through the Friar's plan in Act 4, and she dies of a message that did not move. The afterlife of the play in Verona has answered that structure point for point. The city receives messages by the thousand, addressed to the heroine herself. It moves them without failure through a postal system that recognizes her name. It answers them all, in her name, by hand. And it keeps them, in an archive that grows by the year, as if to prove that the age of the lost letter is over. The heritage industry built the balcony and profits from the courtyard, and the tourist geography that strings the heroine's house to [Romeo's house across Verona](/2029/07/20/romeos-house-verona-tourism/) is a modern construction, as this series' neighbouring articles show. The mail obeys a different logic, the logic of the text itself, and obeys it in reverse.

That reversal is the deepest reason the writers choose Juliet. Romeo in the play writes one letter, his farewell, and it is read aloud over his corpse. Juliet writes none, because Juliet's medium was never paper; it was confidence, spoken in a garden at night to a boy below the window, kept thereafter under interrogation by her mother, her nurse, and her father's rage. The correspondents of the real Verona are not writing to an author or a letter-writer. They are writing to the keeper of the play's great secret, the person who knows from inside what it costs to love where love is forbidden, and who can be told anything because she told almost nothing, to almost no one, and died with her counsel kept. The cliche treats Juliet as fiction, a name in a schoolbook. The letters restore her as a living confidante, and they do so not by pretending she lived, but by using, with open eyes, the exact properties the fiction gave her. No other character in literature holds the office. Verona's club is simply the office's address.

The play ends with a building programme. Over the bodies, Montague vows a monument: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold, / That while Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3). The city of the afterlife executed the vow in its own materials and its own order. It raised the statue, in bronze, in the courtyard of the house it assigned her. It kept the tomb where the first letters gathered. And when the letters outgrew every private hand, it sanctioned an office to answer them, which is the one part of the vow no one in the play thought to promise: not a figure of the heroine at all, but her household, staffed and working, keeping her confidences for a world that goes on needing to make them. The golden statue would have been the fathers' kind of memorial, splendid and silent. The club is the better one, because it does for strangers what the fathers failed to do for their children: it listens, it keeps the secret, and it answers in time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Juliet Club, and what is it for?

The Juliet Club, the Club di Giulietta, is a nonprofit cultural association based in Verona whose best-known work is answering the letters that the world addresses to Juliet. Founded in 1972, the club exists to keep the Veronese tradition of the two lovers alive, and its instruments are the correspondence, an archive of the answered letters, two prizes, and a calendar of celebrations around the heroine. Its members, Juliet's secretaries, are unpaid volunteers. The club is sanctioned by the city of Verona, and its office stands in the historic centre near the church of Sant'Anastasia. The correspondence is the heart of the institution: everything else the club does, the prizes, the festival presence, the literary award, radiates from the desk where the mail is opened.

### Q: When was the Juliet Club founded, and by whom?

The club was founded in 1972 by Giulio Tamassia, a Veronese man of letters acting with a circle of friends described in the accounts as artists, scholars, and local intellectuals. The founding formalized a practice that already existed: letters to the heroine had been arriving since the 1930s, and the city authorities had struggled to answer the growing mail. Tamassia's association took the correspondence into its charge, recruited multilingual secretaries, and gave the answering an office and a succession. One popular magazine account gives 1993 as the founding year, but the club's own published history, the standard book on the phenomenon, and Verona journalism agree on 1972. Giulio's daughter Giovanna leads the club in the modern accounts.

### Q: Who was Ettore Solimani, and why is he called the first secretary of Juliet?

Ettore Solimani was the custodian of Juliet's tomb, the sarcophagus kept in the former monastery of San Francesco al Corso, during the 1930s. Visitors had begun leaving letters for the heroine at the tomb, and Solimani collected them. Reading what strangers had confided to a fictional girl's grave, he felt obliged to respond, and he began writing back, answering accounts of love troubles in the heroine's name and on her behalf. No institution instructed him and nobody paid him; the answering was a private act of conscience performed from a custodian's post. Every later feature of the club's practice descends from his example: the impersonation of the heroine's household, the personal reply, and above all the rule that all of the letters are answered, without selection or exception.

### Q: Where did visitors leave the earliest letters to Juliet?

The earliest documented letters were left at Juliet's tomb, the red marble sarcophagus in the crypt of the former Franciscan monastery of San Francesco al Corso in Verona. From the 1930s, visitors who came to stand at the tomb began depositing written messages addressed to the heroine there, confessions and requests set down at the grave rather than sent through any post. Some accounts trace the depositing habit to the nineteenth-century pilgrim culture that had already made the tomb a station for literary travellers such as Byron and Dickens, but the firm documentary floor is the 1930s, when the letters had grown numerous enough for the tomb's custodian, Ettore Solimani, to gather them, read them, and begin the answering that created the secretary's office.

### Q: How many letters reach the Club in a year?

No audited figure exists, and the published counts diverge by an order of magnitude, which is why honest accounts attribute each figure to its source. A profile from 2001 counted about four thousand letters a year. An account from 2008 gave five thousand or more. Giovanna Tamassia, the club's president, has been quoted giving more than ten thousand a year. Later accounts drawing on the club's publicity give fifty thousand a year, a figure that plausibly counts every item in every medium, posted letters, notes left in the house mailboxes, messages deposited at the shrine, and email, while the lower figures plausibly count the posted correspondence alone. What the spread establishes is a floor of thousands of answered letters annually, sustained across decades.

### Q: Who are Juliet's secretaries, and are they paid?

Juliet's secretaries are the volunteers of the Juliet Club, and they are not paid. The team is international by necessity, since the mail arrives in many languages: profiles describe a small permanent team in the Verona office, a roster counted in different accounts at forty-five to fifty members, and a wider circle of helpers, including volunteers who travel to Verona for a season of answering and others who receive forwarded letters, including email, in their home countries. The secretaries are mostly women, drawn from every walk of life, students, writers, translators, and retired diplomats among them. No formal qualification is required. New members are trained by experienced secretaries under supervision, learning the office's tone and its single governing rule, that every letter is answered.

### Q: In which languages do the secretaries reply?

The secretaries answer in the writer's own language wherever the team's roster allows, and the roster is built for that purpose. Giovanna Tamassia, in interview, has itemized the mail's languages as including Italian, English, German, Spanish, French, Portuguese, Japanese, Russian, Arabic, and Finnish, and letters in braille are recorded among the arrivals. Incoming letters are sorted by language before anything else is done with them, and each is read by a secretary competent in that tongue. The multilingual character of the office is one of its oldest features: the club's founder recruited multilingual secretaries when the association took over the mail in the 1970s, and the international volunteer network, which forwards letters to members in their home countries, keeps the coverage wide.

### Q: How is one of the Club's replies signed?

A reply from the club is signed by Juliet. The answer is written by hand, by a volunteer secretary, on the club's paper, in envelopes that visitors' accounts describe as printed with the heroine's image, and it goes out over the heroine's name, in the form recipients report as "Love, Juliet." The convention is the office's founding impersonation: correspondents write to Juliet, and Juliet's household answers. Nobody at the club disguises the arrangement, which is described openly in the press and in the standard book on the phenomenon, and recipients generally understand that a volunteer held the pen. What the signature certifies is that the letter was received, read, and answered from the heroine's office, which is precisely what the writer asked for.

### Q: What kinds of letters do people write to Juliet?

The secretaries describe a mail of remarkable consistency across countries and decades. The largest kind asks counsel in love: a forbidden or obstructed attachment, a family's opposition, a choice between waiting and leaving, a betrayal to forgive or not. A second kind narrates rather than asks, writers telling the heroine their story for the sake of being heard. A third reports back, sometimes years later, with thanks and outcomes. A fourth comes from the young, school classes and teenagers. A fifth, the gravest, comes from writers in real despair. Running through the mail, in the secretaries' oft-repeated observation, is a formula found in letter after letter: Juliet, I can only tell you; only you can help me. Giovanna Tamassia summarizes the whole harvest in a sentence: the main theme is love issues, or the search for love.

### Q: What happens to a letter once the Club has answered it?

The letter is kept. Answered letters are filed in the club's archive, sorted by criteria that include language and year, forming the paper collection that visitors to the office describe as a labyrinth of boxes and shelves. The Friedmans' account of the phenomenon dates the systematic keeping of the correspondence from 1989; the earlier decades of the practice were personal rather than institutional, and no archive was designed to receive the tomb-era letters. The modern archive's plausible total runs to the hundreds of thousands of letters that accounts of the club report having been answered and retained. The archive is guarded rather than exhibited: the club has allowed a selection, more than seventy-five letters, to be printed in the Friedmans' book under its guardianship, and otherwise treats the correspondence as private, as the heroine treated secrets.

### Q: What is the Cara Giulietta prize, and when is it awarded?

The Cara Giulietta prize, the Dear Juliet prize, is the club's annual award for the finest letters addressed to the heroine during the previous year. It is awarded on Valentine's Day, within Verona's festival of love, at a ceremony associated in the accounts with the Piccolo Teatro di Giulietta. The winning letters are honoured as writing: the prize declares that the year's mountain of private correspondence contained pages of real force, and that their authors, amateurs writing to a fictional confidante, produced literature. The jury rewards candour and the force of a true story told completely, rather than polish. In 2013 the prize went to Jessica Lynch, an Irish scholar, a winner whose profession pleased the office, since the year's best letter to the heroine came from a woman who studies writing for a living.

### Q: What is the Scrivere per Amore prize that the Club also runs?

Scrivere per Amore, Writing for Love, is the Juliet Club's international literary award for published novels that tell of love. Where the Cara Giulietta prize honours the amateur letters coming into the office, Scrivere per Amore honours professional literature going out into the world, and between the two the club acts as a house of love-writing in both directions. The award's standing is vouched by its juries: the twenty-second edition, announced in 2017, was chaired by the philosopher Umberto Galimberti and awarded at the Teatro Nuovo in Verona. The prize embeds the club in Italian literary life rather than in tourism alone, and it follows naturally from the secretaries' daily work, which is the close reading of more love-writing, year in and year out, than almost any jury in Europe performs.

### Q: How should a letter be addressed so that it reaches Juliet in Verona?

A letter needs no more than the heroine's name and her city: envelopes addressed simply to "Juliet, Verona" are recognized in the Italian postal sorting and delivered to the Juliet Club, the destination every sender intends. The club's full published address is Juliet Club, Vicolo Santa Cecilia 9, 37121 Verona, Italy, and the club also receives letters through the red mailboxes at the heroine's house and at its own office, and by email at the address it publishes for the correspondence. The one practical requirement is a legible return address, since a reply cannot travel to a writer who cannot be found. Senders who include one join a queue measured in months, and receive, in time, a handwritten answer signed by Juliet.

### Q: How long does a writer usually wait for a reply?

Writers usually wait on the order of two to three months, according to the guidance and visitor accounts that describe the office's pace. The delay is structural, not neglectful: every letter is read, answered by hand in the writer's language, and filed, by a team of volunteers handling thousands of letters a year, and handwriting at that scale is slow by definition. The club does not apologize for the interval, and the interval belongs to the ritual's meaning. An answer that returned instantly and automatically would announce itself as a service transaction. A letter that arrives months later, handwritten, on the club's paper, announces itself as correspondence, one more exchange in a practice that has run since the 1930s. Writers' letters of thanks, a documented subclass of the mail, suggest the recipients understand and value the difference.

### Q: Do men write to Juliet, or only women?

Men write as well as women, though the mail is majority female. In the club's reported estimate, about seventy percent of the letters come from women, which leaves roughly three in ten from men, a substantial minority of the whole correspondence. The secretaries' descriptions give the men's letters the same range as the women's: counsel sought in obstructed or broken loves, stories narrated for the hearing, thanks reported years later. The proportion fits the phenomenon's logic. The heroine is the confidante of anyone whose love is forbidden, endangered, or lost, and those conditions are not distributed by sex. The answering team mirrors the mail imperfectly, being mostly women, but the office's rule admits no distinction at the desk: every letter is opened, read, answered, and archived on the same terms.

### Q: What changed in the Club's mail after the film Letters to Juliet appeared in 2010?

The mail grew, noticeably and at once. The 2010 film, directed by Gary Winick and inspired by the Friedmans' 2006 book, dramatized the secretaries' office for a worldwide audience, most of whom had never known the office existed. Profiles of the club in the years after the release record a marked influx of letters from writers who had learned from the screen that an envelope addressed to Juliet, Verona would be read and answered by hand. The film also sent visitors to the office itself. The surge is sometimes misread as the phenomenon's origin, which the chronology forbids: the mail predates the film by eight decades, the club by nearly four. Publicity enlarged a rooted practice; it did not plant one. The film's full treatment belongs to this series' article on the film and its tradition.

### Q: Why do writers confide in Juliet rather than in Romeo?

Because the play assigns the two lovers different relations to secrecy. Romeo's one letter in the tragedy is his farewell to his father, produced after his death as evidence; Romeo confides aloud, impulsively, to friends and friars. Juliet is the play's keeper of secrets: she conceals her marriage from her parents, withstands her mother's dismissal and her nurse's treacherous counsel in Act 3, Scene 5, and dies with her counsel kept. A correspondent with an unspeakable love needs the second of those figures, the one who knows the price of a forbidden attachment from inside and has never repeated a confidence. Writers say as much in the formula the secretaries hear in letter after letter: Juliet, I can only tell you. The address is a reader's judgement, formed from the text, about which character can be trusted.

### Q: When does the Club celebrate Juliet's birthday, and what happens then?

The club celebrates Juliet's birthday in September, following Verona's own tradition for its adopted daughter. The date sits in open tension with the play, where the Nurse fixes the heroine's fourteenth birthday to Lammas Eve, the last day of July, in Act 1, Scene 3. The city keeps its September date regardless, one of several respects in which the Verona personage has grown a civic biography of her own alongside the Shakespearean character, complete with a birthday, an address, and a correspondence. The celebration is one of the fixed points of the club's year, alongside the Cara Giulietta prize on Valentine's Day and the club's part in the Verona in Love festival in February. The September observance reinforces the city's title as a capital of love and gives the office a feast of its own between the winter prize season and the summer's height of visiting.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Juliet Club profile to argue that the letters are a reading practice rather than a tourist attraction?

An essay should begin from the profile's chronology, which is the argument in tabular form. The letters precede the institution by four decades, the first answerer was an unpaid custodian acting on conscience, the club that eventually organized the answering is a nonprofit staffed by volunteers whose replies are given away, and the mail's writers repeatedly declare that they know the addressee is a character and write because of what the character's story proves she can be trusted with. Against that record, the tourist-attraction reading explains the balcony and the courtyard but not the postbag. The essay can then use the profile's attributed figures to concede the commercial penumbra, the city's profits and the film's surge, while holding the centre: a practice invented by readers, administered as an obligation, and renewed by every generation the schools send to the play.
