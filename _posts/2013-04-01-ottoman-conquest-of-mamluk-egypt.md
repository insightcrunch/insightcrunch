In the space of roughly six months, a state that had governed Egypt and Syria for two hundred and sixty-seven years, that had halted the Mongols and expelled the Crusaders, and that commanded the most admired heavy cavalry in the world, ceased to exist. Two battles decided it. The first was fought in northern Syria in the late summer of 1516 and the second outside Cairo in January 1517, and between them lay a march across Sinai that most observers thought impossible for an army of the size that made it.

The claim defended here is what we can call the gunpowder-decided-it thesis: the conquest of Egypt was above all the victory of the gun over the horse, and any account that explains it primarily by numbers, treachery, or Mamluk decadence is reaching for secondary factors while the primary one is standing in plain view. The Ottoman army brought field artillery and disciplined firearm infantry fighting behind prepared obstacles, and against that combination the Mamluk method had no opening move. Everything else that went wrong for the defenders, and a great deal did, operated on top of a tactical problem they could not have solved on the day.

That thesis has to survive an obvious objection, and it is worth naming immediately. The Ottoman army was larger, better supplied, and commanded by a state with an expanding revenue base, and defection within the Mamluk command played a real part in the outcome. All of that is true. What none of it explains is why a cavalry force of acknowledged excellence, fighting on ground of its own choosing outside its own capital, with a fortified position prepared in advance, was destroyed in a matter of hours. Numbers do not produce that result against troops of this quality. A weapons system that removes the enemy's ability to fight the only way he knows how does.

## What Was at Stake

Understanding why this conquest mattered requires seeing what Egypt was in 1516 rather than what it had become by the time anyone was writing about it.

Egypt was the wealthiest, most populous, and most strategically placed Arab province in the world. It commanded the corridor between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean, it held the granary of the eastern Mediterranean, its capital was among the largest cities on earth, and its rulers were the recognized protectors of Mecca and Medina, which conferred a standing across the Muslim world that no amount of territory could buy. The state that took Egypt would take all of it.

The Ottoman state in the early sixteenth century was expanding on several fronts and had lately settled a confrontation with the Safavid power to its east. Egypt and Syria sat between Ottoman Anatolia and the holy cities, and the Mamluk sultanate had positioned itself, in the years before the confrontation, in ways that Istanbul read as hostile. The wider question of Ottoman motives, and the personality and reign of the sultan who launched the campaign, belong to the companion account of [how the Ottomans took Egypt in 1517](/2013/06/01/ottoman-conquest-of-egypt-1517/), which owns that ground; this article is about the military event and its consequences.

The Mamluk state entering the confrontation was formidable in reputation and hollow in several places that mattered. Its agricultural revenue had been eroded by a century and a half of recurring plague, a process traced in the account of [the Black Death in Mamluk Egypt](/2012/12/01/black-death-in-mamluk-egypt/). Its commercial revenue had just been undercut by the Portuguese sea route to India, examined in the account of [how Mamluk Egypt controlled world trade](/2012/11/01/mamluk-egypt-world-trade/). Its army remained excellent and was thinner and more expensive to replace than it had been. And it had spent heavily on a failed naval effort in the Indian Ocean immediately before the crisis arrived.

### What condition was the Mamluk state in?

Militarily its cavalry was still first class and its establishment was thinner than a century earlier. Fiscally it was in serious difficulty, with agricultural revenue eroded by repeated plague and commercial revenue undercut by the Portuguese route, having just funded an expensive and unsuccessful fleet against them in the Indian Ocean.

Two features of the table carry the argument. The first is how quickly the decisive stages ran: from the Syrian battle to the execution of the last sultan is roughly eight months, which is extraordinarily fast for the conquest of a major state and tells us the resistance failed at a structural level rather than being ground down.

The second is the final row. The conquest ended the sultanate and did not end the Mamluks, and holding those two facts together is what separates an accurate account of 1517 from the version in which an era simply stops.

## The Two Military Systems

The confrontation is only intelligible once the two armies are described as systems rather than as forces of a given size, because the mismatch was structural.

The Mamluk army was a cavalry force of exceptional individual quality, organized as a federation of households, in which each senior officer maintained, trained, and commanded his own purchased troopers. Its method was consistent: prolonged missile exchange from the saddle to disorder an enemy formation, followed by successive controlled lance charges against the weakened point, with vigorous pursuit after a break. Its equipment was heavy, its training took a decade per man, and its firearms, where they existed at all, sat in siege trains, fortress garrisons, and low-prestige infantry units rather than in the cavalry. The full anatomy of that system, including the four reasons it never integrated gunpowder into the field arm, is set out in the analysis of [the Mamluk war machine](/2013/02/01/mamluk-war-machine-explained/).

The Ottoman army was a combined-arms force. Its core was an infantry corps armed with firearms, fighting in disciplined formation, trained to volley and to hold position under pressure. Around that core it fielded field artillery capable of being moved, emplaced, and fired in an open engagement rather than only against walls. On the wings it deployed cavalry of its own, both the provincial horsemen supported by revenue assignments and lighter irregular troops for screening and raiding. Behind all of it stood a supply system, an ordnance establishment that manufactured guns and powder, and a treasury drawing on an expanding territorial base.

The decisive Ottoman practice was the field fortification. Guns and infantry were placed behind a barrier of chained wagons, earthworks, stakes, or ditches, producing a position that cavalry could not enter and that shot outward continuously. This was not an Ottoman invention and the Ottomans had refined it into a reliable battlefield method.

### Why could Mamluk tactics not answer that?

Because the Mamluk sequence begins by loosening an enemy formation with arrows, and an entrenched infantry line behind obstacles does not loosen. Removing the first step left only the charge, delivered against an unbroken position into massed firearms and artillery, which is the most costly action heavy cavalry can attempt.

That is the tactical core of the whole subject and it deserves restating in the simplest terms available. A Mamluk army fought by earning the right to charge. An Ottoman army denied it that right. Everything else, the numbers, the supplies, the politics, and the treachery, shaped how badly the encounter went and did not determine that it would go badly.

## Syria, August 1516

The campaign opened in northern Syria, where the Mamluk field army under the sultan met the advancing Ottoman force, and the engagement destroyed the Mamluk position in a single afternoon.

The pattern that would repeat at Cairo appeared here first. Ottoman guns and firearm infantry held the centre, the Mamluk cavalry attacked with the courage that every source on both sides records, and the attack broke against firepower it could not suppress. The sultan died during or immediately after the battle, in circumstances the sources describe variously, and his death removed the one figure whose authority could hold a coalition of households together.

Defection compounded the disaster. The governor of Aleppo, one of the most senior Mamluk officers in Syria, went over to the Ottoman side, and his defection is a recurring feature of the accounts. It matters and it should not be made to carry more weight than it can bear: the battle was already going badly when the defection occurred, and the tactical situation was not created by it.

The consequences were immediate and cascading. Syria, which had been the Mamluk sultanate's other half for two and a half centuries, passed into Ottoman hands within weeks, with the great cities surrendering rather than resisting. The Mamluk field army had ceased to exist as a formation. And Egypt, which had always defended itself in Syria, now had to defend itself in Egypt.

The detailed treatment of that Syrian battle, its course, and the Ottoman decisions that shaped it belongs to the companion account of the 1517 conquest, which owns that engagement; what matters here is the situation it produced.

## The Winter in Cairo

Between the loss of Syria and the defence of Egypt lay a few months in which the Mamluk state had to reconstitute itself, and what it managed is more impressive than the outcome suggests.

A new sultan was needed and the men in Cairo chose Tuman Bay, a senior officer of the dead sultan's household, who by every account was a capable and determined commander. He inherited a situation that would have defeated anyone: a field army destroyed, its best troops dead or scattered in Syria, a treasury emptied by the campaign and by the naval effort that had preceded it, and an elite that had just lost the man who held its coalition together.

He did what could be done. Surviving mamluks were reassembled and new recruits were hastily armed. Money was raised by every available means, including exactions on merchants and the population of Cairo that the chroniclers record with feeling. Fortifications were prepared on the approach to the capital.

Critically, he also acquired guns. The Mamluk state had cannon, had used them in sieges and fortress defence, and had received Ottoman technical assistance for its Red Sea fleet a few years earlier, and Tuman Bay assembled artillery and emplaced it in a prepared position outside Cairo. That decision is the single most important qualification to any simple version of the gunpowder thesis and it is examined in detail below.

What he could not manufacture was time, cohesion, or an infantry corps. Firearm-armed foot soldiers cannot be produced in a season, the tactical doctrine for combining them with cavalry did not exist in the Mamluk system, and the households whose cooperation he needed were divided by the succession he had just won.

## The Sinai Crossing

The march from Syria to Egypt is the least discussed part of the campaign and one of its most consequential achievements.

The land route between Palestine and the Egyptian Delta runs along the northern edge of Sinai, and it is a desert crossing with limited water at fixed points, historically the constraining problem for any army moving in either direction. Mamluk armies had used it for centuries with prepared water stations and a relay system built for the purpose, and the assumption on the Egyptian side appears to have been that a large Ottoman force with artillery and a substantial supply train could not make the crossing in strength.

It did, in winter, when water and temperature were most favourable, bringing its guns with it. The organizational capacity that made this possible is a genuine measure of the difference between the two states: the Ottoman army was an institution with a logistics establishment, a supply corps, and an ordnance train, and it could project that machinery across a desert. The crossing arrived faster than expected and it arrived intact.

The strategic consequence was that the defenders lost the option that had saved Egypt repeatedly in the past. Egypt's traditional defence was depth and delay, letting the desert and the Nile exhaust an invader before meeting him. An Ottoman army that crossed quickly with its artillery denied that, and Tuman Bay was forced to fight a set-piece battle at the gates of his own capital on a timetable he did not set.

## Ridaniya, January 1517

The battle that ended organized Mamluk resistance was fought on the plain north-east of Cairo in late January 1517, and its shape is instructive because the Mamluk commander had understood the tactical problem and had tried to solve it.

Tuman Bay did not repeat the Syrian error of charging guns in the open. He prepared a fortified position across the expected line of approach, with entrenchments, and he emplaced artillery within it. The intention was to receive the Ottoman advance behind cover, use his own guns to blunt it, and commit his cavalry against a disordered attacker. That is a coherent plan and it is the plan a competent commander who had learned from Syria would make.

Two problems defeated it, and both are structural rather than personal.

The first is that his artillery was not field artillery in the Ottoman sense. Mamluk guns of the period were oriented toward siege and fortress use: heavy, difficult to move once emplaced, slow to serve, and laid to fire along fixed lines. A gun that cannot be shifted is a gun that covers only what it was pointed at when it was placed.

The second follows directly. The Ottoman force did not oblige by attacking the fortified front. It manoeuvred around the position, and the Mamluk guns, fixed in place and facing the wrong way, contributed nothing to the battle that actually occurred. The Mamluk cavalry, obliged to fight in the open after all, attacked with the courage the sources uniformly record and was destroyed by the firepower it could not close with.

### Why did the prepared position fail?

Because the guns in it could not be moved. Mamluk artillery was configured for siege and fortress work, fixed on emplacement and slow to serve, so when the Ottoman army manoeuvred around the entrenchments rather than assaulting them frontally, the defenders' firepower was pointing at empty ground and the cavalry had to fight in the open regardless.

The engagement was short. Organized resistance on the plain collapsed within hours, the road to Cairo was open, and the Mamluk survivors withdrew into the city and into Upper Egypt. Casualties among the Mamluk elite were heavy, which mattered disproportionately in a system where each trooper represented a decade of investment.

## The Fall of Cairo

The capture of the capital was not a formal surrender and the population experienced it as something considerably worse than a change of government.

Mamluk survivors fought in the streets. Cairo's dense fabric of narrow lanes, walled quarters, and monumental buildings favoured a defender willing to contest it, and Ottoman troops had to take the city block by block against opposition that continued for days. The chroniclers, several of them writing from inside the events, describe fighting in named quarters, the use of buildings as strongpoints, and the resulting destruction.

The consequences for civilians were severe. Sacking, killing, and the settling of scores accompanied the fighting, and the sources record the numbers of dead in the language of enormity that medieval chroniclers reserve for catastrophe. Public executions followed, aimed at breaking further resistance, and the display of severed heads at the city gates is recorded specifically.

The city that suffered this was the one whose extraordinary architectural legacy is examined in the account of [Mamluk architecture and Cairo's golden age](/2013/01/01/mamluk-architecture-of-cairo/), and the physical damage of 1517, while serious, was less than the human cost. Cairo survived as a great city and it did so as a provincial capital rather than an imperial one, which is a different thing entirely.

Order was imposed within weeks and the practical machinery of administration was restarted quickly, because the Ottomans wanted a functioning revenue province rather than a ruin. That is worth noting because it establishes the character of what followed: this was a conquest aimed at absorption, not at destruction.

## The Army That Came

A closer description of the Ottoman force clarifies what the Mamluks were actually facing, because the phrase combined arms conceals a great deal of organizational detail.

The infantry core was a standing corps of professional soldiers, recruited and trained by the state, paid in cash, quartered together, and armed with firearms. Its distinguishing quality was not the weapon but the discipline: men who would stand in formation, load and fire in sequence, and hold position while cavalry came at them. Infantry that breaks when horsemen charge is worthless regardless of what it carries, and the whole tactical system rested on the fact that this infantry did not break.

The artillery was an establishment rather than a collection of guns. It had foundries producing cannon of standardized types, powder manufacture, trained crews, transport arrangements for moving pieces with an army, and a command structure for emplacing and serving them. Field pieces that could be moved, laid, and re-laid during an engagement are a different weapon from siege guns, and possessing the manufacturing and training base to field them is what separated the Ottoman position from the Mamluk one.

The cavalry was substantial and is often forgotten. Provincial horsemen supported by revenue assignments made up a large mounted force, and lighter irregular troops screened, raided, and pursued. The Ottomans were not an infantry power that happened to defeat cavalry; they were a state that fielded all three arms and combined them.

Behind the fighting formations stood the logistics: a supply corps, magazines, transport, engineers who prepared roads and crossings, and an administrative apparatus that could plan a desert march. This is the least visible and most decisive element, and it is what made the Sinai crossing possible.

The lesson for the comparison is that the Mamluks did not lose to a weapon. They lost to a state that could manufacture, train, supply, and combine, funded by a revenue base that was growing while their own contracted.

## The Households That Fought

On the other side, the composition of the Mamluk force at Ridaniya is worth reconstructing because it explains both the quality and the fragility of the defence.

The core was what remained of the royal corps and of the households that had survived Syria, reassembled under a new sultan whose own household was the largest single bloc but far from a majority. These were the trained troopers the reputation rested on, and there were fewer of them than there should have been.

Around that core stood hastily armed recruits, men brought into service without the decade of training the system normally required, along with the remnants of the halqa, the corps of free cavalry that had declined across the preceding centuries into a formation of limited military value.

Auxiliary contingents included Bedouin horsemen whose cooperation had been purchased and whose reliability varied, and firearm-equipped infantry drawn from groups outside the military elite, whose existence proves the Mamluks understood the value of the weapon and whose marginal status proves they had not integrated it.

The command structure was the weak point. A coalition assembled three months earlier, after a contested succession, with senior figures who had been rivals and some of whom had reason to doubt the outcome, is not a unified army. Tuman Bay's authority rested on agreement rather than obedience, and agreement is brittle when the position is desperate.

Set against a professional standing force with a single chain of command, the asymmetry is stark, and it was not a matter of individual quality. Man for man the Mamluk troopers were as good as anything on the field. The organization behind them was a federation improvised under pressure.

## Terror as Policy

The executions that followed the conquest were deliberate instruments and they deserve to be treated as such rather than as incidental brutality.

Public execution of the last sultan at a city gate, the display of the heads of captured commanders, and the killing of prisoners in numbers were choices made by a command that had just fought a harder campaign than it expected and faced three months of continued resistance afterward. The message was aimed at anyone considering further opposition, and at a Cairo population that had watched street fighting and might have considered rising.

The policy worked in the narrow sense. Organized resistance ended, further risings did not materialize, and the province was pacified quickly enough to begin producing revenue.

It also imposed costs that the new administration recognized. A population terrorized into compliance is not a population that cooperates, and the swift move to restore order, restart the courts, confirm religious endowments, and retain familiar administrative arrangements reflects an understanding that terror secures a conquest and cannot govern a province.

The Egyptian sources record the violence with the particularity of eyewitnesses and it colours their assessment of the conquest permanently. That is worth holding in view against the argument, made earlier and correctly, that the conquest was a transfer rather than a rupture. Structurally that is right. For the people in Cairo in the winter and spring of 1517 it was not a structural event, and an account that reaches the correct analytical conclusion without registering that has told only half of it.

## The First Settlement

The arrangements made immediately after the conquest set the pattern for the following centuries, and their outline belongs here even though the working of the province is a separate subject.

An Ottoman governor was installed, holding senior rank in the imperial hierarchy and answerable to the capital. A garrison of imperial troops was established, including infantry and artillery, quartered in the citadel and the major towns. Mamluk households were retained with defined roles and permitted to continue recruiting. Revenue arrangements were reorganized so that the province met its local costs and remitted a surplus to the centre.

The religious and legal establishment was confirmed rather than replaced, with the endowments that funded mosques, colleges, and hospitals left in place. That decision was both practical and legitimating, since a conqueror who protects pious foundations is a conqueror the scholarly class can accept.

The guilds, the markets, the courts, and the mechanisms of land assessment continued with adjustments. The men who had run them mostly went on running them, which is the ordinary outcome when an incoming power wants revenue rather than transformation.

An early revolt by an Ottoman governor within a few years of the conquest showed that the arrangement had its own instabilities, and its suppression led to a more formal codification of how the province was to be administered. The pattern that emerged, of a governor sent from the centre, imperial garrison units, and a powerful local Mamluk element, contained a tension that would define Egyptian politics for the next three centuries.

## The Memory of 1517

How an event is remembered shapes how it is understood, and 1517 has carried several different meanings.

For the Egyptian scholarly class of the sixteenth century it was primarily a loss of standing. Cairo had been an imperial capital and the seat of the caliph, and it became a provincial city, with the consequences for careers, patronage, and self-conception that follow. That sense of diminishment runs through the writing of the generation that experienced it.

For Ottoman tradition it was one of the great achievements of the empire's expansion, bringing the holy cities under imperial protection and adding the wealthiest Arab province, and it was commemorated accordingly.

In later Egyptian national historiography the conquest acquired a different weight, as the beginning of a long period of foreign rule and as a marker separating an era of Egyptian centrality from centuries of subordination. That framing does real work politically and it has the analytical weakness that the Mamluk regime being displaced was itself a foreign military elite that excluded Egyptians from power, so the change was of which outsiders governed rather than whether outsiders governed.

The most defensible memory is the one this article has argued for. In 1517 Egypt stopped being the centre of its own political world and became part of somebody else's, which is a genuine turning point with consequences that lasted centuries. The society, the economy, the administration, and even the military elite continued, which is why the turning point is best measured in where decisions were made rather than in how ordinary life was lived.

## Tuman Bay's Last Months

The final phase belongs to the last sultan, and it is the part of the story that most complicates the picture of a state that simply collapsed.

After Ridaniya and the fall of the capital, Tuman Bay did not surrender. He withdrew, gathered what forces remained, sought support among the Bedouin of the Delta and Upper Egypt, and continued to resist for roughly three months. There were further engagements, and the resistance was serious enough that the Ottoman command treated its suppression as a priority rather than mopping-up.

He was eventually betrayed and taken, brought back to Cairo, and executed by hanging at one of the city gates in April 1517. The manner of it, a public execution at a gate of the capital he had defended, was chosen for its message, and the sources record it in detail because the significance was understood by everyone watching.

The Egyptian chroniclers treat him sympathetically and there is no obvious reason to discount them on this point. He inherited an impossible position, understood the tactical problem well enough to try to solve it, fought when surrender would have been available, and continued after the cause was lost. Whatever one concludes about the system that produced him, the last Mamluk sultan is not a figure who can be used to illustrate decadence.

His resistance also had a practical effect on the settlement that followed. A conquest that met three months of continued armed opposition, drawing on Bedouin support in both the Delta and the south, taught the new rulers something about how difficult Egypt would be to govern without local partners, and that lesson shaped the arrangements they made.

## Was It Really the Guns?

The thesis needs testing against the alternatives, and there are three serious ones.

The numbers argument holds that the Ottoman army was simply much larger. It probably was, though the figures in the sources are unreliable in the usual way. Numbers are a genuine factor and they do not explain the specific character of the defeats: elite cavalry outnumbered by conventional cavalry loses gradually and expensively, and what happened at both battles was rapid destruction against a position the attackers could not enter. Numbers change the odds; they do not remove an army's ability to fight the way it fights.

The treachery argument points to the defection in Syria and to divisions within the Mamluk command. Both were real. Neither created the tactical situation, and the Syrian battle was already lost when the defection occurred. Treachery is also, in a system organized around personal loyalty to individual patrons, exactly the failure mode one would predict when the patron at the top has just been killed, which makes it a symptom of the political structure examined in the discussion of [how a slave army ruled Egypt](/2013/03/01/how-a-slave-army-ruled-egypt/) rather than an independent cause.

The decadence argument holds that the Mamluks had declined and were no longer the force of their reputation. There is something in this, since standards had genuinely slipped, the establishment had thinned, and contemporaries complained about both. It cannot carry the main weight, because the men who fought in 1516 and 1517 are described by their enemies as formidable, and because the defeats were not the kind that degraded troops suffer. Degraded troops break early and run. These charged repeatedly into artillery.

What survives all three tests is the weapons argument, in a precise form. The Ottomans possessed a battlefield system, combining firearm infantry, movable field artillery, and prepared positions, that neutralized the Mamluk method at its first step. The Mamluks possessed excellent cavalry and no answer to that system. Everything else determined how quickly and how completely they lost.

## The Qualification the Thesis Needs

An honest version of the gunpowder thesis has to accommodate a fact that undermines the popular version of it, and stating that fact clearly makes the argument stronger rather than weaker.

The Mamluks were not innocent of gunpowder. They had cannon. They used them in sieges and in fortress defence. They had raised firearm-equipped infantry from groups outside the military elite. They had bought guns and technical assistance from the Ottomans themselves for the Red Sea fleet a few years earlier. And at Ridaniya, Tuman Bay emplaced artillery in a prepared position specifically to meet the Ottoman advance.

So the story is not that one side had guns and the other did not. It is that one side had integrated gunpowder into a battlefield system and the other had acquired gunpowder as equipment.

That distinction is the whole of it. Integration means movable field pieces, trained crews who can displace and re-lay them, infantry who can hold a line in front of them, a doctrine for combining the three, and commanders who have practised the combination. Acquisition means owning guns. The Mamluks had owned guns for decades and had never built the system around them, for reasons of status, technology, organization, and money that are set out at length in the war machine article.

Ridaniya is the demonstration. A commander who had learned the lesson of Syria, who prepared a fortified position and emplaced artillery in it, was defeated because his guns could not move when the enemy did not attack where they pointed. That is what the absence of a system looks like on a battlefield, and it is a far more interesting finding than a simple contrast between guns and horses.

## Comparing 1517 With Egypt's Earlier Invasions

Egypt had been invaded many times before 1517, and setting the successful and unsuccessful attempts side by side sharpens what was distinctive about the Ottoman campaign.

The Arab conquest of the seventh century succeeded, and it succeeded against a Byzantine administration that was unpopular, distracted by a recent war with Persia, and unable to reinforce a province at the end of a long line. The invaders were not technologically superior; they were better motivated, better led locally, and facing a defender with weak local support.

The Crusader invasions of the Delta in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries failed repeatedly, and the reasons were the ones Egypt had always relied on. Invading forces became bogged down in a landscape of canals and flood basins, the defenders traded space for time, the flood was used as a weapon, and supply from overseas proved insufficient for a campaign that stretched into a second season. Egypt's geography defeated them more than its armies did.

The Mongol threat of the thirteenth century never reached Egypt, because it was stopped in Palestine by a Mamluk army fighting at the far edge of its own supply while the invaders operated at the end of theirs.

The Ottoman campaign of 1516 and 1517 defeated every one of the defensive advantages that had worked before. Syria was lost first, removing the depth in which Egypt had always fought its wars. The desert crossing was made quickly and in winter, denying the defenders time. The arrival in the Delta came when the ground was dry, neutralizing the flood. And the decisive engagement was fought at the gates of the capital, where trading space for time was no longer an option.

The pattern is that Egypt had always been defended by depth, delay, water, and distance, and that the Ottoman campaign was designed, deliberately or by competence, to strip all four away before the fighting started. Add a battlefield system the defenders could not answer, and the outcome follows.

## The Guns Themselves

A sentence about field artillery conceals a great deal of technical difference, and the distinction between the two sides' guns is worth spelling out because it is where the battle was decided.

A siege gun is optimized for throwing a heavy projectile at a fixed target, usually a wall, from a position selected in advance and occupied for days or weeks. It is heavy, it is mounted in a way that makes movement slow and re-laying laborious, and its rate of fire is low because nothing about the target is going anywhere.

A field gun is optimized for a different problem. It must travel with an army at the army's pace, be brought into action within a reasonable time, be laid onto a target that moves, and be re-laid or displaced as the engagement develops. That requires lighter pieces, a carriage designed for travel, teams and drivers trained to move them, and crews who can serve them quickly under pressure.

The Ottomans possessed the second kind and the establishment behind it: standardized manufacture, powder supply, transport, and trained personnel. The Mamluks possessed the first kind, which they had used competently in sieges and fortress defence for decades.

This is why Ridaniya turned on a technical fact rather than on courage or numbers. Guns that cannot be moved defend the ground they were pointed at when placed. An enemy who declines to attack that ground reduces them to spectators. The Mamluk position was defeated not because the defenders had failed to think of artillery but because the artillery they had was the wrong tool for the tactical problem, and acquiring the right tool would have required an establishment they had never built.

## The Defeated

What became of the Mamluk elite in the months after the conquest is a mixed picture and it sets up everything that followed.

A large number died, in Syria, at Ridaniya, in the street fighting in Cairo, and in the executions afterward. Casualties among a corps where each man represented a decade of training and a substantial purchase price were disproportionately damaging, and the establishment that survived was a fraction of what had entered the war.

Senior figures who had defected or come to terms early were confirmed in positions under the new order, which is the ordinary reward for early cooperation and which the sources record without approval.

Households that had fought and survived were disarmed, assessed, and then, over the following years, partially reconstituted within the provincial arrangement, since the new administration had decided it needed them. Property was confiscated selectively rather than universally, and the endowments that supported religious foundations were largely left alone, which protected a substantial part of the elite's wealth exactly as the founders had intended when they created them.

Some withdrew to Upper Egypt or beyond and continued as irregulars for a period. Others left Egypt entirely.

The outcome within a generation was a Mamluk element embedded in an Ottoman province, recruiting again by purchase, training again in the old way, and holding defined military and administrative roles. That is a remarkable recovery for a defeated elite, and it happened because the system that produced them depended on a purchase route, a training method, and a household bond rather than on a throne, and none of those three was destroyed in 1517.

## Timing, Season, and the Nile

The calendar shaped this campaign more than most, and reading the dates carefully explains several decisions that look arbitrary otherwise.

The Syrian battle was fought in late summer 1516, at the end of a campaigning season, which gave the victors the autumn to consolidate Syria and the winter to prepare the next stage. That interval is what allowed Cairo to elect a new sultan and organize a defence, and it is also what allowed the invaders to move guns and supplies forward without haste.

The Egyptian phase was fought in winter, and the choice was deliberate. The Sinai crossing is survivable in the cool months and punishing in the hot ones, water is more manageable, and animals endure the march. An army that waited for spring would have faced a harder crossing and a defender with more time to rebuild.

The Nile's own calendar mattered too. The annual flood rises through late summer and recedes across the autumn, leaving the countryside saturated and then progressively passable. A winter campaign in the Delta meets ground that is workable for an army with wheeled artillery, whereas a late summer campaign meets a landscape in which movement off the levees is close to impossible. Egypt's traditional advantage against invaders arriving from the north had often been the flood, and a winter arrival neutralized it.

The combination is a reminder that the Ottoman campaign was planned by people who had studied the problem. Crossing Sinai in winter with artillery, arriving in the Delta when the ground had dried, and forcing a decision before the defender could rebuild is not opportunism. It is a campaign designed against the specific obstacles Egypt presents, and the professional competence behind it is part of why the outcome was so quick.

## Syria: The Other Half

Egypt dominates the account and Syria was half the sultanate, and what happened there in 1516 is a necessary part of the story.

Syria had been governed from Cairo for two and a half centuries, with governors in Damascus and Aleppo, garrisons in the citadels, and a frontier facing north and east that had absorbed most of the sultanate's military attention. It was where Mamluk armies fought, where the threats came from, and where the state's strategic depth lay.

After the battle in the north it fell within weeks, and the speed is significant. The great Syrian cities did not mount serious resistance, garrisons surrendered or dispersed, and administrations transferred with limited disruption. Several explanations combine: the field army had been destroyed and there was nothing to relieve a besieged city; Syrian populations had no particular attachment to a regime that taxed them and had been unable to prevent a devastating invasion at the end of the fourteenth century; and the Ottomans offered terms that made surrender preferable to resistance.

The consequence for Egypt was decisive and it is easy to underrate. Every previous threat to Egypt from the north and east had been met in Syria, at a distance, with the desert and the Sinai crossing behind the Mamluk army as insurance. Losing Syria in a single season meant losing that insurance, and it meant the next battle would be fought within sight of Cairo with nothing behind it.

The Syrian collapse also removed a large share of the sultanate's revenue and manpower at exactly the moment both were needed, and it delivered to the invaders a supply base, fortified cities, and a friendly staging ground for the march south.

## Upper Egypt and the Rest of the Country

Accounts of the conquest concentrate on Cairo, and the rest of Egypt experienced it differently.

Alexandria and the Delta towns changed hands with limited fighting once the capital had fallen, because a port city without a field army to relieve it has little to gain from resistance and everything to lose. Commercial life was disrupted and resumed.

Upper Egypt was another matter. The valley south of Cairo was where Mamluk survivors regrouped, where Bedouin support could be sought, and where the reach of any government in Cairo had always been weakest. Tuman Bay's continued resistance drew on it, and pacifying the south occupied the new administration well beyond the formal end of the war.

The rural population across the country experienced the conquest as most rural populations experience a change of ruler: as armies passing through, requisitioning food and animals, and as an interruption in the collection of taxes followed by its resumption under different names. The sources record disorder in the countryside during the interregnum, since a state that has lost control of its capital cannot police its districts, and the resumption of order was welcomed for practical reasons whatever anyone thought of the new rulers.

The one group with a specific stake was the merchant community, which had suffered heavy exactions from Tuman Bay's desperate fundraising and then suffered again from the sack. Commercial confidence takes longer to restore than public order, and the disruption to Egyptian trade in the immediate aftermath was real even though the corridor itself survived.

## What Was Taken

Conquest transfers wealth as well as sovereignty, and the immediate extraction from Egypt in 1517 was substantial.

The treasury and the accumulated portable wealth of the defeated elite were seized, which is standard practice and was expected by everyone. Confiscation of the property of amirs who had resisted followed, along with levies on the population of Cairo and on the merchant community that had already been squeezed by the outgoing regime.

Skilled craftsmen were transferred to the imperial capital, which was also standard practice for a conquering power and which had a real effect on Egyptian production in certain trades. Cairo had extraordinary craft traditions in stone, marble, wood, metal, and textiles, and the removal of masters is one of the reasons the character of Egyptian luxury production shifted after the conquest.

Objects and materials of symbolic value were removed, including items associated with the guardianship of the holy places, and the significance of that transfer belongs to the discussion of what the conquest meant for the caliphate and for Ottoman claims to leadership of the Muslim world, which the companion 1517 article owns.

Beyond the one-time seizures lay the permanent change: an annual remittance of surplus revenue to the imperial centre. That is the difference between being conquered and being incorporated, and it is the mechanism by which a wealthy province funds commitments elsewhere. The scale and effects of that transfer over the following centuries are examined in the account of the province's economy, and its immediate effect was simply that money which had been spent in Egypt began leaving it.

## Why It Was So Fast

The speed of the conquest deserves an explanation of its own, because eight months from the first battle to the execution of the last sultan is remarkable for a state of this size and wealth.

Four factors combined. The first is that the Mamluk state concentrated its military capacity in one field army, so a single defeat removed most of it. There were no provincial armies to raise, no feudal levies to summon, and no reserve formation of comparable quality.

The second is that its political structure amplified defeat. A coalition of households held together by loyalty to a sultan loses cohesion when the sultan dies, and the death in Syria produced exactly the defections and hesitations that structure invites.

The third is fiscal. A treasury emptied by a failed naval campaign and eroded by decades of declining revenue could not fund the emergency measures a defence required, which is why Tuman Bay's fundraising fell on merchants and the Cairo population with such severity and still produced too little.

The fourth is that Egypt has no interior. A country whose population and wealth are concentrated in a single river corridor, with the capital at the junction of the valley and the Delta, offers a conqueror one target. Take Cairo and the country follows, because there is nowhere else that matters and no defensible alternative centre.

Set together, these explain why a state that had absorbed a Mongol invasion and a century of Crusader pressure fell in a single winter. It was not that the Mamluks fought badly. It was that their system had exactly one place to lose.

## What Ended in 1517

The conquest closed several things definitively and it is worth being precise about which, because the list is shorter than the rhetoric of the period suggests.

The Mamluk sultanate ended as a state. There was no longer a sultan in Cairo commanding Egypt and Syria as an independent power, no longer an Egyptian foreign policy, and no longer an army answering to a ruler in the citadel.

Egypt's status as the seat of an empire ended, and this is the change with the longest consequences. For centuries Egypt had been the centre of its own political world, with tributaries, client relationships, and a voice in the affairs of the region. After 1517 it was a province, wealthy and important and administered from elsewhere, and decisions about its revenue, its garrison, and its governor were made in a distant capital. The transfer of that centre of gravity is the deepest change the conquest produced.

The guardianship of Mecca and Medina passed to the Ottoman sultan, which mattered enormously for prestige across the Muslim world and removed the most valuable non-material asset the Egyptian state had held.

Cairo's position as an imperial capital ended, with consequences for the city's economy, its elite, and its building programme. Great monumental patronage of the kind that had filled the city depended on rulers who governed from it and needed to make a permanent mark on it.

The revenue that had been spent in Egypt began to be spent elsewhere. A province remits a surplus to the centre, and the Egyptian surplus, which had previously funded an army, a court, and a building programme in Cairo, now partly funded imperial commitments far away.

## What Did Not End

The list of continuities is longer and it is what makes 1517 a turning point rather than a rupture.

The Mamluks themselves continued. Ottoman policy retained Mamluk households as part of the provincial military and administrative order, permitted continued recruitment by purchase from the Caucasus, and used Mamluk cavalry as a component of Egyptian forces. Over the following centuries those households grew back into the dominant power in the province, a trajectory followed in the account of [the Mamluk beys of Ottoman Egypt](/2013/09/01/mamluk-beys-of-ottoman-egypt/).

The administrative machinery continued. Land assessment, tax collection, the courts, the endowment system, and the guilds carried on with adjustments rather than replacement, because a new ruler who wants revenue does not begin by dismantling the apparatus that collects it.

The economy continued. The trade corridor still functioned, and the Red Sea traffic in eastern goods recovered substantial volume under Ottoman administration despite the Portuguese presence, a recovery examined in the account of [trade and decline in Ottoman Egypt](/2013/08/01/trade-decline-ottoman-egypt/). Agriculture, sugar, textiles, and the Cairo market went on.

Religious and intellectual life continued, and the great teaching institutions of the capital retained their standing in the Muslim world.

The population continued to be governed by an armed elite it had no part in choosing, which had been true before 1517 and remained true after it. For most Egyptians the identity of the men at the top changed and the structure of their lives did not.

### Was 1517 a rupture or a transfer?

A transfer of sovereignty rather than a social revolution. The sultanate ended, Egypt became a province governed from a distant capital, and the Mamluk households, the administration, the economy, and the religious institutions all survived and continued under new supervision.

## How Egyptians Experienced It

The chroniclers who recorded these events lived through them, and their accounts give an unusually direct sense of how the conquest was received.

The immediate experience was violence. The street fighting in Cairo, the sack that accompanied it, the executions, and the exactions imposed by both sides during the crisis appear in the sources as recent and vivid, and there is no version of these months in which the population of the capital came off well.

The subsequent reaction was more complicated. Egyptian writers were not partisans of the Mamluk regime, which had taxed them heavily, excluded them from power, and allowed its troops to behave badly toward them. Some of the commentary on the conquest carries a note of judgment on the fallen regime rather than mourning for it.

At the same time the loss of status was felt keenly by the articulate classes. Cairo had been the centre of a world and became a provincial seat, the sultan's court that had employed scholars and patronized institutions was gone, and the careers that depended on an imperial capital narrowed. Those consequences fell hardest on precisely the people who wrote the sources, which is worth remembering when reading their assessments.

The pattern that emerges is a population that experienced the conquest as a catastrophe in the short term, adjusted to it within a generation, and retained a sense that something had been lost that was not merely a dynasty. The details of what provincial government actually meant in practice are taken up in the account of [how Egypt was governed by the Ottomans](/2013/07/01/egypt-under-ottoman-rule/).

## The Pilgrimage and the Holy Cities

One consequence of the conquest ran far beyond Egypt and deserves separate treatment, because it changed the standing of both states in the Muslim world.

The Mamluk sultans had held the guardianship of Mecca and Medina for two and a half centuries. That responsibility meant protecting the pilgrimage caravan, maintaining the sanctuaries and the water supply along the routes, subsidizing the sharifs who governed the holy cities, and providing the security that made the annual journey possible for pilgrims arriving from across the Muslim world. It was expensive and it was the single most valuable non-material asset the Egyptian state possessed, because it conferred a standing that no amount of territory could purchase.

With Egypt and Syria in Ottoman hands, the guardianship passed to the Ottoman sultan, and the transfer was accepted quickly by the authorities in the Hijaz, which had no realistic alternative once the power that paid the subsidies had been destroyed.

The practical arrangements changed less than the prestige. The pilgrimage caravan continued to depart from Cairo, escorted by Egyptian forces including Mamluk cavalry, funded from Egyptian revenue and from endowments dedicated to the purpose. The convoy that had been an expression of Egyptian sovereignty became an obligation of an Egyptian province, performed by much the same people in much the same way.

That continuity is why the caravan escort remained one of the most important functions the retained Mamluk households performed, and why their military value to the new administration was real rather than sentimental. A province that must move thousands of pilgrims across desert routes each year needs mounted troops who know the ground.

The symbolic dimension of the transfer, including what it meant for Ottoman claims to leadership of the Muslim community and what happened to the caliph maintained in Cairo, belongs to the companion account of the 1517 conquest, which owns that question. What belongs here is the practical point: the duty stayed in Egypt, the credit moved to Istanbul, and the men who did the work were the ones who had just been defeated.

## The Cost to the Winners

Conquests are usually assessed from the loser's side, and the Ottoman side of the ledger is worth a moment because it explains the shape of the settlement.

The campaign was expensive. Moving a large combined-arms army from Anatolia through Syria and across Sinai in winter, with artillery, siege equipment, and supplies for a desert crossing, consumed resources on a scale that only a state with a substantial revenue base could contemplate. It also consumed a campaigning season and a half at a moment when the empire had commitments in several directions.

Casualties were real, particularly in the street fighting in Cairo, where a disciplined army loses its advantages in narrow lanes against defenders who know the ground. Taking a great city block by block is the most expensive form of urban warfare and the sources on both sides record it as hard fighting rather than a mopping-up.

The three months of continued resistance after the capital fell imposed a further cost, both in troops committed to pacification and in the delay before the province could begin producing revenue.

Then came the standing commitment. A province of Egypt's size and wealth, with a long Red Sea coast, a difficult southern frontier, desert margins requiring negotiation, and responsibility for the pilgrimage, is not cheap to hold. The garrison had to be paid, the routes secured, the caravan escorted, and the administration staffed.

That accounting explains the retention of Mamluk households more clearly than any argument about respect for a defeated enemy. A conqueror who has just discovered how expensive Egypt is to take will think carefully about how expensive it will be to hold, and a local elite that already knows the country, already has the cavalry, and can be paid from Egyptian revenue is the cheapest available answer. The settlement of 1517 was a fiscal decision as much as a political one.

## Why the Conquerors Kept the Conquered

The decision to retain Mamluk households rather than eliminate them is the most consequential single choice in the settlement, and it was not sentimental.

Three practical reasons drove it. The first is that Egypt is difficult to garrison from a distance. It is large, its population is concentrated along a river corridor with desert margins that need policing, and the routes to the Red Sea and to Upper Egypt run through territory where tribal cooperation is essential. Holding it with imported troops alone would have required a permanent field army, which is expensive.

The second is that Mamluk cavalry was genuinely useful. The Ottomans had just defeated it and had no illusions about what it could and could not do, and heavy cavalry of that quality remained valuable for exactly the tasks a province required: escorting the pilgrimage caravan, policing the desert routes, suppressing disorder, and providing a mobile force for Upper Egypt and the Red Sea coast.

The third is administrative. The men who knew how Egyptian revenue actually worked, which districts yielded what, how the assignment system operated, and who had to be paid to keep the routes open, were the men who had been running it. Removing them would have meant rebuilding that knowledge from nothing while the revenue stopped flowing.

The settlement therefore combined an Ottoman governor, an Ottoman garrison of imperial troops, and a retained Mamluk element with defined roles and continued recruitment. It was an arrangement designed to extract revenue reliably at the lowest cost in imperial attention, and by that standard it worked for a long time.

The unintended consequence appeared over generations. A retained elite that continues recruiting, training, and organizing itself in the way that made it powerful will, given a distant capital and periodic imperial distraction, gradually recover the substance of power while leaving the forms intact. That is what happened, and it is the strongest evidence that the Mamluk system was robust rather than fragile.

## Counting the Forces Honestly

Statements about the size of the armies in 1516 and 1517 circulate widely and almost all of them are firmer than the evidence permits.

The problems are the usual ones. Chronicle figures for medieval and early modern armies are inflated, sometimes formulaic, and frequently intended to convey enormity rather than to report a count. Ottoman administrative records are better than most and describe entitlements and payrolls rather than the number of men present on a particular field. Mamluk records suffer from the entitlement problem in an acute form, since an amir was supposed to maintain a given number of mamluks and inspection complaints show many maintained fewer.

What can be said comparatively is firm enough for the argument. The Ottoman force was substantially larger. It was drawn from a state with a bigger population, a wider territorial base, and an expanding revenue, and it fielded three arms where the Mamluks fielded essentially one. The Mamluk force after the Syrian defeat was a fraction of what the sultanate could have put in the field a generation earlier, because the best troops had been lost in Syria and the treasury could not replace them.

The precise multiple does not matter for the thesis and it matters a great deal for the counterfactual. If the Ottoman advantage was overwhelming in numbers as well as in system, then no Mamluk tactical choice could have changed the outcome. If it was moderate, then the failure of the fortified position at Ridaniya carries more weight. The honest answer is that the sources do not settle it, and any account that assigns a specific ratio is reconstructing.

## The Fleet That Was Not There

A question worth asking about a conquest that involved a march along a coast is what the Mamluk navy was doing, and the answer connects the events of 1517 to a longer story.

The sultanate had no standing navy worth the name and the reasons were structural. Egypt lacked shipbuilding timber and imported it, at expense and subject to the willingness of foreign suppliers. There was no large maritime population to crew a fleet. And the prestige system of the military elite ran entirely through horsemanship, so naval command offered nothing to an ambitious officer.

The sultanate had built ships when it had to. In the years immediately before the Ottoman confrontation it constructed a Red Sea fleet, with imported timber, imported guns, and Ottoman technical assistance, to contest the Portuguese presence in the Indian Ocean. That fleet achieved an initial success and was then destroyed at Diu in 1509.

The relevance to 1517 is twofold. The naval effort had consumed money the treasury could not spare in the years immediately before it needed to fund a land campaign, which is a direct fiscal contribution to the weakness of the defence. And the absence of any Mediterranean naval capacity meant the defenders could not contest the coast, could not threaten Ottoman communications, and could not use the sea in any way at all.

The irony is worth stating plainly. The Ottomans had supplied the guns and the expertise for a Mamluk fleet a few years before they used their own guns to destroy the Mamluk state, and both facts follow from the same underlying reality: the sultanate could purchase military technology and could not produce it.

## The Bedouin Factor

Tribal populations of the Delta, Upper Egypt, and the desert margins play a larger part in this campaign than most accounts allow, and their behaviour illuminates the condition of the state that fell.

Mamluk authority in these areas had always rested on a combination of subsidy, negotiation, and credible force, and all three had weakened as revenue declined across the fifteenth century. The sources record deteriorating control of the desert margins well before the conquest, with disrupted routes and districts effectively outside effective administration.

During the campaign that weakness told in both directions. Tuman Bay sought Bedouin support for his continued resistance after Ridaniya and received some of it, which is why the final phase lasted three months rather than three days. At the same time, tribal groups took the opportunity that a collapsing state presented, and the disorder of the interregnum included raiding and score-settling that had nothing to do with either side's war aims.

For the incoming Ottoman administration this was an early and instructive lesson. A province where the desert margins are held by negotiation rather than by garrison requires local partners with the standing to negotiate, which reinforced the case for retaining the Mamluk households rather than replacing them with imperial troops who knew nobody.

The longer pattern is that Egypt has repeatedly been governed by an arrangement between a central authority in Cairo and the tribal populations of its margins, with the terms shifting according to how much the centre could afford. The Ottoman conquest changed who sat in Cairo and did not change the arrangement.

## Three Myths About 1517

Popular accounts of the conquest carry three claims that the evidence does not support, and correcting them is a good test of whether the argument has been understood.

The first is that the Mamluks had never seen a gun and refused to use one. They had cannon, used them in sieges and fortress defence, maintained artillery establishments, raised firearm-equipped infantry from outside the elite, bought guns and expertise from the Ottomans for their Red Sea fleet, and emplaced artillery at Ridaniya. What they lacked was a system: movable field pieces, crews trained to displace them, infantry doctrine, and a practised combination of the three. Correcting this myth makes the story more interesting rather than less, because it turns a contrast between ignorance and knowledge into a contrast between owning a tool and rebuilding around it.

The second is that the Mamluk army was decadent and broke without a fight. Ottoman accounts of the campaign, which had no reason to flatter the defeated, describe formidable opponents, and the character of both defeats is wrong for degraded troops. Degraded troops break early and run. These charged repeatedly into artillery and firearms, fought street by street in Cairo, and continued an organized resistance for three months after the capital had fallen. Standards had genuinely slipped over the preceding century, and that is a different claim from cowardice or collapse.

The third is that 1517 ended Mamluk Egypt in every sense. It ended the sultanate. It did not end the Mamluks, who were retained by the conquerors, continued recruiting from the Caucasus, and grew back into the dominant power in the province over the following centuries. Nor did it end the administration, the economy, the religious institutions, or the arrangement under which Egyptians were governed by an armed elite they had no part in choosing.

The common error underneath all three is treating a political event as a total one. A sovereignty changed hands in 1517. A society did not stop.

## The Citadel Changes Hands

The physical transfer of the seat of government is a small detail with a large amount of meaning packed into it, and it makes an appropriate closing image for the event itself.

The citadel above Cairo had been the seat of Egyptian government since the Ayyubid period, the residence of sultans, the site of the royal barracks where the sultan's own mamluks were trained, the location of the treasury, and the place from which the country was administered. It was designed to dominate the city visually and to be defensible against the city if necessary, which tells you something about the relationship between the regime and its capital.

After the conquest it became the residence of an Ottoman governor, the quarters of an imperial garrison, and the administrative centre of a province. The building was the same and its meaning inverted: from the seat of a sovereign to the office of an appointee, from the centre of a state to the local node of an empire.

The royal barracks illustrate the change most sharply. They had existed to train the boys who would become the ruling class of an independent country, and after 1517 the training continued while the destination changed, since the men produced would now be a component of a provincial military establishment rather than candidates for a throne.

That single reversal captures the conquest better than any account of the battles. The institution survived, the training survived, the buildings survived, the elite survived. What ended was the answer to the question of where decisions about Egypt were made, and for a country that had been the centre of its own world for most of recorded history, that was the change that mattered.

## What the Sources Disagree About

A well-documented event still leaves genuine disputes, and naming them is more useful than papering over them.

The death of the sultan in Syria is described differently across the accounts, with versions ranging from death in the fighting to collapse during or immediately after the rout, and the discrepancy matters because the manner of it shaped how contemporaries assigned blame for the defeat.

The extent and timing of the Syrian defection are contested. Every account agrees it happened; they differ on whether it was arranged in advance, on how many men went over, and on whether it preceded the collapse or followed it. Accounts written to explain a defeat have obvious reasons to emphasize betrayal, and accounts written by the victors have reasons to advertise it.

The dispositions at Ridaniya are described with enough variation that the precise geometry of the Ottoman flanking movement cannot be reconstructed with confidence. What is consistent across the traditions is that entrenchments existed, that artillery was emplaced in them, and that the decisive action did not occur where the defenders had prepared for it.

The scale of the killing in Cairo is reported in numbers that cannot be taken at face value, in the usual medieval register of enormity. That the fighting was severe and the sack extensive is beyond dispute; how many died is not recoverable.

The reasoning behind retaining the Mamluk households is nowhere set out by anyone who made the decision, so the explanation offered in this article, that it was practical and fiscal, is an inference from the arrangement's shape and from what the new administration evidently needed. It is a strong inference and it is an inference.

Naming these gaps does not weaken the central argument, which rests on the firmest parts of the record: the sequence of events, the tactical character of both defeats, the existence and failure of the Mamluk artillery at Ridaniya, and the terms of the settlement that followed.

## Could the Mamluks Have Won?

Counterfactual questions are only worth asking when they clarify the causes, and this one does, provided the answers are kept honest.

The tactical counterfactual is the narrowest. Could Tuman Bay have won at Ridaniya with better dispositions? Probably not, and the reason is instructive: his plan was sound and his instrument was wrong. Guns that cannot be moved cannot answer an enemy who manoeuvres, and no arrangement of immovable artillery defeats a force that declines to attack it frontally. A different position might have delayed the outcome. It would not have supplied field artillery.

The operational counterfactual is more interesting. Could Egypt have been defended by depth rather than by battle, withdrawing up the Nile, denying supply, and letting the desert and the flood work on an invader far from home? That had worked against Crusader invasions of the Delta in earlier centuries, and the flood in particular had been used as a weapon. Against a winter campaign by a well-supplied army that reached Cairo quickly, the option had largely closed, and a Mamluk elite whose entire identity was built on the decisive cavalry engagement was unlikely to choose a strategy of retreat in any case.

The strategic counterfactual is where the real answer sits. Could the sultanate, over the fifty years before 1516, have built the combined-arms force that would have made the confrontation survivable? In principle yes, and the obstacles were the four described in the war machine analysis: the status system that made firearms unacceptable to the elite, the genuine technical limitations of early firearms for cavalry, the household organization that resisted central reorganization, and above all the money, since building a second army requires funding both during the transition, on a revenue base that was contracting.

The honest verdict is that the tactical outcome was fixed by 1516, the operational options had narrowed to almost nothing, and the strategic window had been open for decades and had closed for reasons that were structural rather than merely stubborn. That is a different claim from saying the fall was inevitable from the start, and it is the one the evidence supports.

## The Wider Pattern

The Ottoman conquest of Egypt belongs to a wider transformation of the sixteenth-century world, and placing it in that frame makes the gunpowder thesis less parochial.

Across Eurasia in this period, states that combined firearm infantry, mobile artillery, and a fiscal system capable of paying for both were expanding at the expense of states that did not. The Ottomans were the most successful practitioners in their region and they were not alone. Similar combinations were reshaping conflicts in Iran, in northern India, and in Europe, and the states that made the transition first gained an advantage that lasted until their neighbours copied them.

What distinguished the losers in these confrontations was rarely courage and frequently structure. A military elite whose status rested on a skill the new weapons devalued had strong reasons to resist, and a state whose revenue was fixed or falling could not fund the transition even when its leadership understood the need. Both conditions applied in Egypt.

The comparison also explains why the Ottomans were so quick to keep what they had defeated. They were not making an argument that cavalry was obsolete, because their own army used cavalry extensively. Their argument was about combination, and a province that needed mounted police, caravan escorts, and a mobile reserve had good use for exactly the men they had just beaten.

The Egyptian case is therefore an unusually clean example of a general pattern: not guns replacing horses, but integrated systems replacing single-arm forces, and the fiscal capacity to fund integration determining who managed the change.

## How We Know

The evidence for the conquest is unusually good for an event of this period, and knowing its shape helps in weighing the competing accounts.

Egyptian chronicles are the richest source and several were written by men who lived through the events in Cairo. They are detailed on the succession of Tuman Bay, the preparations, the street fighting, the executions, and the exactions, and they carry the perspective of the scholarly and administrative class that had lived under Mamluk rule and now lived under Ottoman rule.

Ottoman accounts supply the other side, describing the campaign, the logistics, and the engagements from the perspective of the victors, and they have the usual incentive to present the defeated enemy as formidable, which is worth remembering when they praise Mamluk courage.

Administrative material anchors the aftermath. Ottoman provincial records document how Egypt was organized, what revenue was expected, and what forces were maintained, and this is the firmest evidence for the settlement, as distinct from the fighting.

European observers, principally Venetian, add a third perspective, since the commercial community had every reason to track a change of regime in the market they depended on and reported it home in detail.

### Where does the evidence run thin?

On numbers, on the precise course of the fighting hour by hour, and on the experience of Egyptians outside Cairo. Chronicle figures are unreliable, battle narratives are shaped after the fact by both sides, and the provincial population appears mainly through the taxes it paid and the disorder it suffered.

## The Verdict

The gunpowder-decided-it thesis holds, in the form the evidence supports rather than in the form it is usually stated.

Two battles destroyed the Mamluk sultanate, and in both the decisive factor was an Ottoman battlefield system that combined firearm infantry, movable artillery, and prepared positions in a way that denied the Mamluk method its opening move. Mamluk cavalry could not loosen an entrenched infantry line with arrows, and without that first step it was reduced to charging unbroken firepower, which is the most expensive action heavy cavalry can attempt and the one it is least likely to survive.

The qualification that makes the thesis defensible rather than glib is that the Mamluks had gunpowder and had not built a system around it. Tuman Bay emplaced artillery at Ridaniya and it contributed nothing, because immovable siege pieces cannot answer a manoeuvring enemy. The lesson is about integration rather than about possession, and it is a better lesson.

The secondary factors were real and secondary. The Ottoman army was larger. Defection in Syria accelerated a defeat already under way. The Mamluk state was fiscally exhausted after a century and a half of plague, the loss of its commercial monopoly, and an expensive failed naval campaign, and that exhaustion is why its army was thinner and its treasury empty when the crisis came. The full political architecture that made a coalition of households vulnerable to defection at the moment its leader died is set out in the guide to [the Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt](/2012/07/01/mamluk-sultanate-of-egypt/).

The consequence was a genuine turning point and not a rupture. Egypt ceased to be the seat of an empire and became a province, which changed where its revenue went, where its decisions were made, and what its capital was for. What it did not change was the Mamluk households, the administration, the economy, or the position of the population, all of which continued under new supervision, and the shape of the province that emerged is set out in the guide to [Ottoman Egypt](/2013/05/01/ottoman-egypt-complete-guide/).

## The Historiography of a Defeat

How this conquest has been written about is itself informative, since the framing has shifted several times and readers will meet all of the versions.

The oldest tradition, drawing on the Egyptian chroniclers' own language, treats 1517 as the end of an age and reads the preceding century as decline leading inevitably to it. That reading takes contemporaries at their word about decadence, and contemporaries had reasons of their own for describing the regime that way.

A later framing emphasized technology, casting the conquest as the clean triumph of gunpowder over medieval cavalry. It has the merit of identifying the decisive factor and the defect of oversimplifying it, since it tends to imply that the Mamluks had no guns, which is false, and to skip the question of why they had guns and could not use them.

Institutional and fiscal work shifted the emphasis again, showing that the Mamluk state entering the confrontation was fiscally exhausted for reasons rooted in demographic collapse and the loss of a commercial monopoly, and that a capital-intensive military transition was not affordable on a contracting revenue base. That framing is the one this article has followed, and it makes the conservatism explicable rather than merely regrettable.

More recent attention to what continued after 1517 has questioned the rupture narrative altogether, pointing to the survival of Mamluk households, the continuity of administration, and the recovery of Red Sea trade under Ottoman rule. On that reading the conquest was a transfer of sovereignty whose social effects took generations to appear, and the language of an era ending belongs to political history rather than to the lived experience of Egyptians.

Genuine disagreement remains on how much weight to give the fiscal exhaustion relative to the tactical mismatch, on whether a Mamluk gunpowder transition was ever politically achievable, and on how sharply Egyptian life actually changed in the decades after the conquest.

## What This Episode Teaches

The case has value beyond Egyptian history because it isolates a distinction that gets blurred whenever military change is discussed.

The distinction is between acquiring a technology and building a system around it. The Mamluks had cannon for decades. They bought guns, employed gunners, used artillery in sieges, and fielded firearm infantry drawn from outside the elite. On the day that mattered, a competent commander emplaced artillery in a prepared position specifically to meet the threat, and it made no difference, because the guns could not move, the crews could not displace them, no infantry doctrine existed to hold ground in front of them, and no commander had practised the combination.

A system is the weapon plus the organization, the training, the doctrine, the logistics, and the money to sustain all four. Acquiring the weapon is the cheap part and it is the part that looks like progress. The expensive part is everything else, and it requires reorganizing an institution whose members have careers and status invested in the old arrangement.

That is why the transition defeats institutions rather than individuals. Nobody in the Mamluk elite failed to notice what firearms did; they had seen it in Syria three months before Ridaniya. What they could not do in three months, or in fifty years, was rebuild a military system around a weapon that devalued the skill their entire hierarchy rested on, using money they did not have.

The diagnostic question worth carrying away is this: when this organization adopts a new capability, does it acquire the tool or does it rebuild around it? For Mamluk Egypt the answer was the first, consistently, for as long as the question could be asked, and the second answer was never affordable once it became necessary.

## What a Reader Should Be Able to Argue

Five propositions carry this article and separating them from the detail makes the whole event easy to hold.

The first is chronological. The Mamluk field army was destroyed in northern Syria in the late summer of 1516, Syria fell within weeks, Tuman Bay was elected in Cairo, the Ottoman army crossed Sinai in winter, the Battle of Ridaniya outside Cairo in January 1517 ended organized resistance, and the last sultan was captured and executed in April 1517.

The second is tactical. The Mamluk method required loosening an enemy formation with mounted archery before charging, and an entrenched infantry line behind obstacles with artillery in support could not be loosened, so the cavalry was reduced to charging unbroken firepower.

The third is the qualification that makes the argument serious. The Mamluks had gunpowder and lacked a gunpowder system. Tuman Bay emplaced artillery at Ridaniya and it contributed nothing, because siege pieces cannot answer an enemy who manoeuvres around them.

The fourth is fiscal. The state that fell had been eroded by a century and a half of recurring plague and had just lost its commercial monopoly to the Portuguese route, so its army was thinner, its treasury was empty, and a capital-intensive military transition had been unaffordable for decades.

The fifth is the consequence, stated with its limits. Egypt ceased to be the seat of an empire and became a province, which changed where revenue went and where decisions were made. The Mamluk households, the administration, the economy, and the position of the population all continued, so 1517 was a transfer of sovereignty rather than a social rupture.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did the Ottomans conquer Egypt?

In two decisive battles across roughly six months. The Ottoman army destroyed the Mamluk field force in northern Syria in the late summer of 1516, killing the sultan and taking Syria within weeks. Cairo elected a new sultan, Tuman Bay, who reassembled what remained of the army, raised money by heavy exactions, and prepared a fortified position outside the capital. The Ottoman force then crossed Sinai in winter with its artillery, a logistical feat the defenders had not expected, and met the Mamluks at Ridaniya in January 1517. Rather than assault the entrenchments frontally, it manoeuvred around them, leaving the defenders' immovable guns facing empty ground, and destroyed the cavalry in the open. Cairo was taken after days of street fighting, and Tuman Bay was captured and executed in April 1517.

### Q: What year did the Ottomans take Egypt?

The decisive events span 1516 and 1517, and the conventional date for the conquest is 1517. The Syrian battle that destroyed the Mamluk field army was fought in the late summer of 1516, and Syria passed to Ottoman control over the following weeks. The Egyptian phase belongs to the winter that followed: the crossing of Sinai in late 1516, the Battle of Ridaniya outside Cairo in January 1517, the capture of the capital immediately afterward, and the execution of the last Mamluk sultan in April 1517, which formally ended the sultanate. Egypt was then organized as an Ottoman province. Giving the single year 1517 is correct for the end of the sultanate; giving 1516 to 1517 is more accurate for the campaign as a whole, since half of it was fought in Syria.

### Q: What was the Battle of Ridaniya?

The engagement fought on the plain north-east of Cairo in January 1517 that ended organized Mamluk resistance. Tuman Bay had learned from the Syrian defeat and did not charge guns in the open. He built a fortified position across the expected approach, dug entrenchments, and emplaced artillery within them, intending to receive the Ottoman advance behind cover and then counterattack with cavalry. The plan was sound and the instrument was wrong. His guns were siege and fortress pieces, heavy, slow to serve, and fixed once emplaced along set lines of fire. The Ottoman army declined to attack the works frontally and manoeuvred around them, so the Mamluk artillery contributed nothing and the cavalry had to fight in the open regardless. Organized resistance collapsed within hours and the road to Cairo lay open.

### Q: Why did the Mamluks lose to the Ottomans?

Chiefly because the Ottoman battlefield system denied the Mamluk method its opening move. Mamluk armies won by wearing down an enemy formation with sustained mounted archery and then charging the weakened point, and an entrenched infantry line behind obstacles with artillery in support does not weaken under arrows. That left only the charge against unbroken firepower, which is the most expensive thing heavy cavalry can attempt. Secondary factors were real and secondary: the Ottoman army was larger and better supplied, defection in Syria accelerated a defeat already under way, and the Mamluk state was fiscally exhausted after a century and a half of plague, the loss of its commercial monopoly to the Portuguese route, and an expensive failed naval campaign. None of those explains why elite cavalry was destroyed within hours on ground of its own choosing.

### Q: How did Ottoman guns defeat the Mamluks?

Through a system rather than through the weapons alone. The Ottomans fielded disciplined firearm infantry that would hold formation while cavalry charged, field artillery that could be moved, emplaced, and re-laid during an engagement, and prepared positions of wagons, stakes, earthworks, or ditches that horsemen could not enter. Combined, these produced a front that shot outward continuously and could not be loosened, which removed the first step of the Mamluk tactical sequence. The Mamluks had guns of their own and had never built the surrounding system: no movable field pieces, no crews trained to displace them, no infantry doctrine to hold ground in front of them, and no practised combination of the three. Ridaniya demonstrated the difference precisely, since emplaced Mamluk artillery pointed at ground the enemy simply walked around.

### Q: What happened to the last Mamluk sultan?

Tuman Bay, elected in Cairo after the death of his predecessor in Syria, did not surrender after Ridaniya and the fall of the capital. He withdrew, gathered what forces remained, sought support among the Bedouin of the Delta and Upper Egypt, and continued armed resistance for roughly three months, seriously enough that suppressing it was treated as a priority rather than as mopping up. He was eventually betrayed, captured, brought back to Cairo, and publicly executed by hanging at one of the city gates in April 1517. The manner and place were chosen for their message. The Egyptian chroniclers treat him sympathetically and there is little reason to discount them: he inherited an impossible position, understood the tactical problem well enough to try to solve it, and fought on when surrender was available.

### Q: Did the Mamluks survive under Ottoman rule?

Yes, and this is one of the most surprising facts about 1517. Ottoman policy retained Mamluk households as part of the provincial military and administrative order, permitted continued recruitment by purchase from the Caucasus, and used Mamluk cavalry for tasks a province needed: escorting the pilgrimage caravan, policing desert routes, and providing a mobile force for Upper Egypt. The reasons were practical, since Egypt is difficult to garrison from a distance, the cavalry remained genuinely useful, and the men who knew how Egyptian revenue actually worked were the men who had been running it. Over the following centuries these households recovered the substance of power within the province while leaving imperial forms intact, which is strong evidence that the Mamluk system was robust rather than fragile.

### Q: Why was the Ottoman conquest a turning point?

Because Egypt stopped being the centre of its own political world and became part of somebody else's. Before 1517 it was the seat of an independent sultanate with its own foreign policy, its own army, tributaries and client relationships, and recognized guardianship of Mecca and Medina. Afterward it was a province: wealthy, important, and administered from a distant capital, with its governor appointed elsewhere, its surplus revenue remitted outward, and its capital reduced from an imperial seat to a provincial one. The guardianship of the holy places passed to the Ottoman sultan, and the monumental patronage that had filled Cairo largely ended with the rulers who had needed to make their mark on it. What did not change was the administration, the economy, the religious institutions, or the exclusion of Egyptians from power.

### Q: What weapons did the Ottoman army bring to Egypt?

Three arms brought together under one command. Its infantry corps carried firearms and, more importantly, was trained to fight in disciplined formation, loading and firing in sequence and holding position while cavalry came at it. Its artillery was an establishment rather than a collection of guns, with foundries producing standardized pieces, powder manufacture, trained crews, transport for moving guns with a marching army, and a command structure for emplacing and serving them in the field. Its cavalry was substantial and is often forgotten, including provincial horsemen supported by revenue assignments and lighter irregulars for screening and pursuit. Behind all of it stood a supply corps, magazines, engineers, and an administrative apparatus capable of planning a winter desert crossing with artillery, which is the least visible and most decisive element.

### Q: How large were the armies that fought at Ridaniya?

No reliable figures exist and the ones in circulation are firmer than the evidence permits. Chronicle numbers for armies of this period are inflated, sometimes formulaic, and often intended to convey enormity rather than report a count. Ottoman administrative records are better than most but describe payrolls and entitlements rather than men present on a given field. Mamluk records suffer acutely from the same problem, since amirs were supposed to maintain a set number of mamluks and inspection complaints show many maintained fewer. What can be said comparatively is that the Ottoman force was substantially larger, drawn from a state with a bigger population and an expanding revenue, and that the Mamluk force after the Syrian defeat was a fraction of what the sultanate could have fielded a generation earlier.

### Q: Who was Tuman Bay?

The last sultan of the Mamluk state, elected in Cairo in late 1516 after his predecessor died in the Syrian defeat. He was a senior officer of that sultan's household and by every account a capable and determined commander, and he inherited a position that would have defeated anyone: the field army destroyed with its best troops dead or scattered, a treasury emptied by the campaign and by an expensive failed naval effort against the Portuguese, and an elite divided by the succession he had just won. He reassembled survivors, armed new recruits, raised money by exactions the chroniclers record with feeling, acquired artillery, and fortified the approach to the capital. After Ridaniya he refused surrender and fought on for three months before being betrayed, captured, and publicly executed in April 1517.

### Q: Did any Mamluks fight with firearms in 1517?

Yes, and this is the qualification that makes the gunpowder argument serious rather than glib. The Mamluk state had cannon and used them in sieges and fortress defence, maintained artillery establishments with personnel to serve them, and had raised firearm-equipped infantry drawn from groups outside the mamluk military elite. It had also purchased guns and technical assistance from the Ottomans themselves a few years earlier for its Red Sea fleet. At Ridaniya, Tuman Bay emplaced artillery in a prepared position specifically to meet the Ottoman advance. So the story is not one side with guns and one without. It is that one side had integrated gunpowder into a battlefield system and the other had acquired it as equipment, and the difference decided the battle.

### Q: How did Egyptians react to the Ottoman conquest?

The immediate experience was violence, and no version of the winter and spring of 1517 has the population of Cairo coming off well: street fighting, sacking, public executions, and heavy exactions imposed by both sides during the crisis. The subsequent reaction was more complicated. Egyptian writers were not partisans of the Mamluk regime, which had taxed them heavily, excluded them from power, and allowed its troops to behave badly toward them, and some commentary carries judgment on the fallen regime rather than mourning. At the same time the loss of status was felt keenly by the articulate classes, since Cairo had been the centre of a world and became a provincial seat, and the careers that depended on an imperial capital narrowed. Those consequences fell hardest on precisely the people who wrote the sources.

### Q: Did treachery help the Ottomans win?

It helped and it did not decide. The defection of a senior Mamluk governor during the Syrian battle is a recurring feature of the accounts and it accelerated a defeat that was already under way, since the tactical situation was not created by it. Divisions within the Mamluk command were real throughout, and Tuman Bay's authority rested on an agreement among households assembled three months earlier after a contested succession, which is brittle in a desperate position. The deeper point is that defection is exactly the failure mode a system organized around personal loyalty to individual patrons should be expected to produce once the patron at the top has been killed. Treachery was therefore a symptom of the political structure rather than an independent cause of the defeat.

### Q: How long did the conquest of Egypt take?

Roughly eight months from the first decisive battle to the execution of the last sultan, which is remarkably fast for a state of this size and wealth. Four factors explain the speed. The Mamluk state concentrated its military capacity in one field army, so a single defeat removed most of it, with no provincial armies or reserve formations of comparable quality to raise. Its political structure amplified defeat, since a coalition of households loses cohesion when the sultan holding it together dies. Its treasury was empty, so the emergency measures a defence required could not be funded. And Egypt has no interior: a country whose population and wealth are concentrated in one river corridor, with the capital at the junction of valley and Delta, offers a conqueror a single target.

### Q: What did the Ottomans take from Egypt after the conquest?

The treasury and the portable wealth of the defeated elite were seized, which was standard practice and expected by everyone, followed by confiscation of the property of amirs who had resisted and levies on a Cairo population and merchant community already squeezed by the outgoing regime. Skilled craftsmen were transferred to the imperial capital, which had a real effect on Egyptian luxury production, since Cairo held extraordinary traditions in stone, marble, wood, metal, and textiles and the removal of masters shifted their character. Objects of symbolic value associated with the guardianship of the holy places were removed. Beyond the one-time seizures lay the permanent change: an annual remittance of surplus revenue to the imperial centre, so money that had been spent in Egypt began leaving it.

### Q: Could the Mamluks have won?

Not tactically in January 1517 and possibly strategically had the previous fifty years gone differently. Tuman Bay's plan at Ridaniya was sound and his instrument was wrong, since immovable siege artillery cannot answer an enemy who manoeuvres around it, and no arrangement of fixed guns changes that. The operational alternative of trading space for time up the Nile, which had worked against Crusader invasions of the Delta, had largely closed against a well-supplied winter campaign that reached Cairo quickly, and a cavalry elite whose identity rested on the decisive engagement was unlikely to choose retreat. The strategic window had been open for decades and closed for structural reasons: status, the genuine limitations of early firearms for cavalry, household organization that resisted reform, and a contracting revenue base that could not fund two armies at once.

### Q: Why did the Mamluk fleet not help?

Because there was effectively no fleet, and the reasons were structural rather than a failure of judgment in 1517. Egypt lacked shipbuilding timber and had to import it, at expense and subject to foreign suppliers who were periodically forbidden to sell. There was no large maritime population from which to crew warships. And the prestige system of the military elite ran entirely through horsemanship, so naval command offered nothing to an ambitious officer. The sultanate had built a Red Sea fleet a few years earlier, with imported timber, imported guns, and Ottoman technical assistance, to contest the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean, and it was destroyed at Diu in 1509. That effort consumed money the treasury could not spare immediately before it needed to fund a land defence.

### Q: What ended and what continued in 1517?

What ended: the sultanate as a state, Egypt's status as the seat of an empire, the guardianship of Mecca and Medina, Cairo's position as an imperial capital, and the retention of Egyptian surplus revenue within Egypt. What continued: the Mamluk households themselves, retained by the new rulers with continued recruitment from the Caucasus; the administrative machinery of land assessment, tax collection, courts, endowments, and guilds; the economy, including a Red Sea trade that recovered substantial volume under Ottoman administration; the religious and intellectual institutions of the capital; and the position of a population governed by an armed elite it had no part in choosing, which was as true before as after. The accurate description is a transfer of sovereignty rather than a social revolution.

### Q: How did the fighting affect Cairo itself?

Severely, and more in human than in physical terms. The capture of the capital was not a formal surrender: Mamluk survivors fought in the streets, and Cairo's dense fabric of narrow lanes, walled quarters, and monumental buildings favoured a defender willing to contest it, so Ottoman troops had to take the city block by block over several days. The chroniclers, several writing from inside the events, describe fighting in named quarters, buildings used as strongpoints, and the resulting destruction. Sacking, killing, and score-settling accompanied it, followed by public executions and the display of heads at the gates. Physical damage to the fabric, while real, was less than the human cost, and order was imposed within weeks because the new rulers wanted a functioning revenue province rather than a ruin.
