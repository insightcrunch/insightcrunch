## What a luxury stay actually buys you at Lollapalooza

Start with the honest objection, because a thoughtful traveler raises it before a salesperson can. The objection runs like this: a festival is the opposite of a hotel experience. You leave at midday, you come back at midnight, you spend the daylight hours in a dusty park sweating through a shirt, and the room is just a place to crash. By that logic, every dollar above a clean bed and working air conditioning is wasted, and the marble lobby and the rooftop pool are decorations you will photograph once and never use. The instinct is reasonable, and for some travelers it is correct. The mistake is treating it as a universal law rather than a personal calculation.

The reason the objection fails for many premium travelers is that it measures the hotel by the hours you spend lounging in it, when the right measure is the quality of the recovery it delivers in the hours you are actually there. A Lollapalooza day is physically demanding in a way a normal vacation day is not. You are on your feet for the better part of eleven hours, often in direct sun, walking miles across the footprint between stages, standing through sets, and pushing through dense crowds at the exits. By the time you reach your room, your feet ache, your ears ring, you are dehydrated, and you have to do it all again tomorrow, and then again. The variable that decides how Saturday and Sunday feel is not how the festival went. It is how completely you recovered overnight. And recovery is precisely what a genuine luxury property is engineered to deliver: a deep, quiet, dark room with serious climate control, a bed built for sleep rather than for a budget spreadsheet, a deep tub or a powerful rain shower for cramped legs, room service that runs late enough to feed you when you finally crawl in, and a staff whose entire job is removing friction from your day.

### Is a luxury hotel worth it for Lollapalooza?

A luxury hotel is worth it for Lollapalooza when you value three things: a high-floor view you will use, service that resets you between days, and a short trip back after a long day. If those matter and the budget is comfortable, the premium pays off in recovery. Otherwise, a smart mid-range room serves you better.

That framing changes the math entirely. You are not paying a premium to enjoy the lobby. You are paying it to arrive at Saturday morning feeling like a person rather than a wreck, which directly determines how much of the festival you can actually enjoy. A traveler who sleeps badly on Thursday night, skips the late afternoon on Friday because their feet gave out, and bails before the Saturday headliner because they are running on fumes has not saved money by booking a cheaper room. They have paid full price for a festival pass and then thrown away a third of it. Seen that way, the recovery premium is not an indulgence layered on top of the festival cost. It is insurance on the much larger sum you already spent to be here. For the traveler who can afford it and who feels the difference good sleep makes, that insurance is worth buying.

There is a second thing the premium buys that has nothing to do with sleep, and it is the removal of small frictions that compound over four days. At a luxury property, the bag you do not want to lug to the park gets stored without a fuss and retrieved instantly. The dinner reservation you could not get yourself appears because the concierge made a call. The car you ordered is waiting under the canopy instead of circling the block while you stand in the sun. The late checkout that lets you sleep past noon on the day you fly out is granted with a smile rather than negotiated. None of these is dramatic on its own. Across a weekend that is already taxing your patience and your body, the cumulative effect of never having to fight the building you are staying in is real, and it is exactly the thing the premium service tier is selling. The question is simply whether you are the kind of traveler who notices and values that, or the kind who would rather pocket the difference and tough out the friction.

## The view premium: skyline, park, and what you actually see

The single largest variable inside the luxury tier, the one that separates two rooms at the same property by a meaningful margin, is the view. Chicago is one of the great skyline cities, and the stretch of downtown around Grant Park stacks the lakefront, the park, the river, and the towers into sightlines you cannot get in most American cities. A high floor facing the right direction is genuinely spectacular, and at a festival where the park itself is the stage, a room that looks down over the green you will spend your days in carries a particular romance. But the view is also where premium travelers most often overpay, because the view premium is real money and not every traveler will use what they bought.

Think about the views in three families, because they cost differently and suit different people. The first is the park-and-lake view, the rooms that face east and south toward Grant Park, Millennium Park, and Lake Michigan. These are the rooms that overlook the festival footprint and the water beyond it, and during the weekend you can watch the crowds move and, from the closest properties, catch the wash of stage light after dark. The second is the skyline view, the rooms that face into the cluster of downtown towers and the river, which give you the postcard Chicago of glass and steel rather than the park. The third is the city view, a polite hotel euphemism for rooms that face a side street, an interior court, or a neighboring building, and which carry no premium because there is nothing to charge for.

### Which Lollapalooza hotels have views of Grant Park?

The hotels with genuine Grant Park views sit where downtown meets the lakefront: the grande dame properties on South Michigan Avenue facing the park, and the high towers in the Lakeshore East and Millennium Park pocket whose upper floors look down over the green and water. For the closest-to-the-gates ranking, see the near-venue guide.

The honest advice on views is to buy the one you will look at and refuse to pay for the one you will not. A park-and-lake room rewards the traveler who lingers in the room in the mornings with coffee, who comes back between the afternoon and the headliner to rest with the curtains open, or who simply finds that looking down over the festival from a quiet high floor is part of why they came. For that person, the view premium is the whole point, and skimping on it to save a fraction would be a strange economy given everything else they are spending. The skyline room rewards the traveler who loves the architecture and the night lights more than the park, and it often costs a little less than the prime park-facing stock at the same address. The plain city room is the right call only for the traveler who is honest with themselves that they will be in the room to sleep and shower and nothing else, in which case paying a park-view premium is paying for a window they will keep curtained.

A few specifics sharpen the decision. View premiums climb with floor height as well as direction, so a low park-facing room and a high park-facing room are not the same purchase, and the difference between them is frequently larger than the difference between a mid-floor park view and a mid-floor skyline view. If the view is your reason, buy the height, not just the direction. Corner rooms and corner suites at the better towers give you two exposures at once, which is the closest thing to having both the park and the skyline, and they are the rooms that book first among travelers who know the building. And remember that the festival changes what a view is worth: a park-facing room is at its most magical exactly on the weekend you are there, and at its most ordinary the rest of the year, which is part of why the prime stock moves so early for the festival dates. If a park view is the experience you want, it is also the experience the most other premium travelers want at the same time, so the decision and the booking both have to happen early.

## The luxury zones: where Chicago's high-end hotels sit relative to Grant Park

Luxury in this city is not concentrated in a single block, and understanding where the high-end clusters sit relative to the festival gates is half the decision. There is no on-site lodging and no campground, because this is an urban festival in a downtown park, so every luxury option is a city hotel that you reach the gates from on foot or by a short ride. The zones differ on how far that trip is, what kind of view they offer, and what flavor of luxury they deliver, and matching the zone to what you value is the move that the generic luxury lists skip. The full zone logic for every budget lives in the [where to stay overview](/2026/05/16/where-to-stay-lollapalooza/); what follows is the luxury slice of that map.

The first cluster is the Magnificent Mile and the Gold Coast, the stretch of North Michigan Avenue and the blocks around it that hold many of the city's flagship five-star names. This is luxury at its most polished and most concentrated, the address most people picture when they think of a grand Chicago hotel, with the deepest bench of marble lobbies, club lounges, spa floors, and brand-name service. The tradeoff is distance. The Mag Mile sits north of the river and the festival gates sit at the south and central edges of Grant Park, so you are looking at a longer walk or a short ride to reach the music, which on a festival weekend means leaning on rideshare, taxi, or transit at the busiest hours. For the traveler whose priority is the service and the view and who does not mind the commute, this zone is the heart of Chicago luxury. For the traveler who wants to walk home from a headliner in ten minutes, it is the wrong cluster, and the next two zones serve them better.

The second cluster is the Loop and the riverfront, the central business district immediately west and northwest of Grant Park. Here the luxury is often newer and more design-forward, the riverfront towers with rooftop bars and floor-to-ceiling glass, and the location splits the difference: closer to the central and western gates than the Mag Mile, with the river and the architecture as the signature view rather than the park. The Loop has a busy weekday-downtown character that quiets on the weekend, and it puts you within a reasonable walk of the gates while keeping you among the towers. The Loop-versus-value question for this zone, including how it compares to the calmer neighborhood just south, is settled in the [Loop versus South Loop comparison](/2026/05/17/loop-vs-south-loop-lollapalooza/), which is the right next read if you are torn between central convenience and a softer base.

The third cluster, and the one that matters most for the view-chaser, is the pocket where downtown meets the lakefront directly: the grande dame hotels along South Michigan Avenue that face Grant Park across the street, and the high towers in the Lakeshore East and Millennium Park area whose upper floors look straight down over the green and the water. This is the zone with the truest park-and-lake views and the shortest civilized trip to the closer gates, and it is where the festival-facing luxury experience is most fully realized. The historic properties here have grand-old-Chicago bones and a sense of occasion, while the modern towers deliver the high-floor glass-walled version of the same view. This pocket is where a luxury stay and the festival overlap most tightly, and it is also, predictably, where the prime rooms vanish first for the weekend.

A practical note ties the zones together. The closer a luxury property sits to the gates, the more its premium is buying you both a view and a short walk home, which is the double benefit at the center of the whole luxury case. The farther north into the Mag Mile you go, the more the premium is buying you service and address rather than proximity, which is the right trade only if those are what you value. None of the zones is wrong. The error is booking the famous northern address for its name and then spending the weekend annoyed at the commute, or booking purely for proximity and missing the grander service you came to Chicago to enjoy. Decide which of the three things, view, service, or short walk, ranks first for you, and the zone chooses itself.

## Festival-weekend packages: what they include and whether they pay off

The luxury tier is where festival-weekend packages are most common and most worth scrutinizing, because a well-built package can turn a steep room rate into a genuinely good value, and a poorly matched one can have you paying for credits you never spend. Premium properties near the park frequently release weekend bundles timed to the festival, and the high-end traveler who books the bare room without checking for the package is often leaving real value on the table. The reverse is also true: a package loaded with perks you will not touch is just a higher price wearing a bow. The skill is reading the bundle against your actual weekend.

### Do luxury hotels offer Lollapalooza packages?

Yes, luxury hotels near Grant Park frequently offer festival-weekend packages, though the contents change every edition and must be confirmed when you book. Typical bundles add dining or bar credits, club-lounge access, valet parking, late checkout, or a view-room upgrade. The value depends on whether you will use the inclusions, so check each against your plans.

Look first at what packages typically bundle, and judge each inclusion by whether it maps to something you would buy anyway. Dining and bar credits are the most common addition, and they pay off if you plan to eat or drink at the hotel, which on a festival weekend you may well do late at night when the park has emptied you out and you do not want to go searching for food. They are dead weight if you intend to eat every meal out or inside the festival. Club-lounge access is a high-value inclusion for the traveler who will use the lounge breakfast on festival mornings and the evening spread before heading out, and a wasted line item for the traveler who will be at the gates before the lounge opens and back after it closes. Valet or self-parking is a meaningful inclusion only if you are driving, and irrelevant if you flew in and will rideshare to the gates. A view-room upgrade folded into a package is the inclusion to chase if the view is your priority, because it is often cheaper bundled than bought separately.

The test for any package is simple and worth applying every time. Add up what you would have spent on the inclusions you will genuinely use, and compare that to the gap between the package price and the bare room rate. If the perks you will actually use cover the gap or come close, the package is a good buy and you should take it. If you would only use a fraction of the inclusions, the bare room is the smarter purchase even though the package looks more generous on paper. The luxury market is built to make the package feel like the obvious choice, and for the traveler whose weekend matches the bundle it genuinely is, but the discipline of pricing the inclusions you will use against the ones you will waste is what separates a good luxury value from an expensive habit. Because the specific contents and prices of these bundles change every edition, the one durable instruction is to ask the property directly what its current festival-weekend package includes and to run the math on your own plans before you commit.

## The perks that matter on a festival weekend, and the ones that do not

Luxury hotels sell a long list of perks, and a festival weekend is a sharp filter for which of them are worth anything to you. The ordinary luxury calculus, where you might value the fitness center or the business amenities, gets rewritten when the reason you are here is to spend four punishing days in a park. Some standard premium perks become genuinely valuable in that context, a few become essential, and others that command a premium the rest of the year are simply irrelevant to a festivalgoer. Knowing which is which keeps you from paying for the wrong list.

### What perks do high-end Lollapalooza hotels offer?

High-end hotels near Lollapalooza offer late checkout, valet parking, concierge service, club-lounge access, late room service, deep tubs and rain showers, spa treatments, and rooftop bars. The perks that matter most on a festival weekend aid recovery and remove friction: late checkout, late dining, a serious shower, and a concierge who handles logistics.

Start with the perks that earn their keep on a festival weekend, because these are the ones to prioritize when comparing properties. Late checkout is near the top of the list, because the day you leave is usually the day after a long final night, and the ability to sleep past the standard checkout and pack at a human pace is worth real money to a wrecked traveler. Late-running room service or in-house dining matters because you will frequently come back too late and too tired to go find food, and a kitchen that can feed you at midnight is a genuine comfort. A deep soaking tub or a powerful rain shower is not a frill after a day of standing and walking; it is direct recovery for cramped legs and aching feet, and the better luxury rooms build serious bathrooms. A concierge who can secure a hard dinner reservation, arrange a car at a sane time, store your bag, and solve the small problems of a festival weekend removes exactly the friction that grinds you down by Sunday. And a spa floor, if you will use it, can turn a rest afternoon between the early sets and the headliner into real physical recovery rather than just lying down.

Then there are the perks that look luxurious and mean little to a festivalgoer, the ones not to pay extra for unless you happen to want them for their own sake. An elaborate fitness center is close to useless to someone walking miles a day in a park; you are not short on exercise. A rooftop pool is lovely and genuinely pleasant if you carve out a midday rest at the hotel, but if your days run gate to gate it is a photograph you take once. Extensive business amenities, executive workspaces, and meeting facilities are irrelevant to a festival trip. A famous in-house restaurant with a difficult reservation is a draw only if you are willing to spend a festival evening at dinner rather than at the music, which most attendees are not. None of these is bad, and a traveler who specifically wants a pool afternoon or a destination dinner should absolutely seek them out, but they should not be the reason you pay one premium over another when the reason you are in Chicago is the park.

The clarifying move is to rank the perks by how directly they touch the festival weekend rather than by how impressive they sound. Recovery and friction-removal sit at the top: sleep quality, the shower or tub, late checkout, late food, bag storage, and a concierge who absorbs logistics. Convenience perks sit in the middle: valet if you are driving, club access if your hours line up with the lounge, a rooftop bar for a late drink without leaving the building. Prestige perks sit at the bottom for this specific trip: the gym, the meeting space, the trophy restaurant, the amenities that signal luxury without aiding a festivalgoer's actual weekend. Build your comparison around the top of that ranking and you will choose the property that helps you survive and enjoy four hard days, rather than the one with the longest list of things you will never touch.

## Recovery value: why premium service earns its price after a long day

The deepest case for the luxury tier, the one that answers the wasted-on-a-festival objection most completely, is recovery value, and it deserves its own treatment because it is the perk that is hardest to see on a booking page and most decisive once you are living the weekend. A Lollapalooza day does not end when you leave the park. It ends when you have eaten, washed off the day, rested your body, and fallen into deep sleep, and the speed and completeness of that wind-down is what determines whether tomorrow starts from strength or from a deficit. Every luxury feature that touches the wind-down is buying you a better tomorrow, which is why recovery is the frame that makes the premium make sense.

Consider the wind-down step by step, because the luxury difference shows up at each one. You come back late, often past midnight, dehydrated and footsore, your ears still humming. At a premium property the bag you stashed is retrieved instantly, the car you ordered dropped you under the canopy rather than three blocks away, and the lobby is calm rather than chaotic. Upstairs, the room is genuinely dark and genuinely cool, because serious climate control and proper blackout are things the luxury tier actually delivers and the budget tier often only approximates. The shower has real pressure and real hot water, or the tub is deep enough to soak the ache out of your legs. If you are hungry, the kitchen is still running. The bed is built for sleep. And in the morning, you can sleep as late as the late checkout allows, eat without rushing, and walk back to the gates from a body that recovered rather than one that merely paused. Each of those steps is a small thing, and the sum of them across four nights is the difference between finishing the festival strong and limping to the end.

This is where the value genuinely compounds, and where the premium stops being indulgence and becomes the smartest line in the budget for the right traveler. The broader logistics of resting and resetting between festival days, including the pacing and the rest-day moves that keep a long weekend from breaking you, are covered in depth in the [guide to recovering between festival days](/2026/06/06/recovering-between-festival-days/), which pairs naturally with this one: that article is the method, and the luxury room is the best place to execute it. A premium property is, in effect, a recovery machine you sleep inside, and the traveler who feels the difference good sleep and real rest make is buying tangible value, not a status symbol. The traveler who can sleep anywhere and bounces back regardless does not need it and should save the money. The recovery frame is what separates those two travelers cleanly, and it is the honest answer to anyone who says luxury is wasted at a festival: it is wasted only on the person who does not need to recover, and most people, after four days in that park, very much do.

## Matching the property to what you value

The luxury tier is not one decision but several, because premium travelers arrive with different priorities, and the property that delights the view-chaser will frustrate the service-first guest who wanted a flagship spa. Rather than crown a single best hotel, the useful move is to match the property archetype to the traveler archetype, so you can find yourself in the list and book accordingly. This is the series wager applied to the high-end stay: curation by what you value, not a generic ranking.

The view-first traveler, the one for whom looking down over the festival from a quiet high floor is part of the point, belongs in the park-facing and lakefront-tower stock, and should buy height as well as direction and book early, because the prime view rooms are exactly the ones every other view-chaser wants on the same weekend. For this traveler the deciding question is grand-old character versus modern glass, and the answer is a matter of taste rather than logistics. The service-first traveler, the one who came to Chicago partly for the polish and the spa and the sense of a flagship address, belongs on the Magnificent Mile and should accept the commute to the gates as the price of that experience, leaning on the concierge and rideshare to make the distance painless. For this traveler the view is secondary and the service floor is the draw.

The short-walk-home traveler, the one who wants to step out of a headliner crowd and be in a luxurious room within minutes, belongs in the park-adjacent pocket where downtown meets the lakefront, and should weigh proximity heavily even against a slightly grander address farther north, because for them the whole value of the premium is not having a long, surge-priced trip at the end of a long day. The couple splurging on a special weekend should think about the view and the dining and the spa together, because their trip is as much about the romance of the room and the city as about the music, and a park-view room with a good in-house restaurant and a rest-afternoon at the spa is a different and richer experience than gate-to-gate festivalgoing. The group or family taking a premium base should reframe the whole search around suites and connecting rooms, where the priority is space and a second bathroom and a common area, and the perfect single view matters less than the floor plan that keeps several people comfortable.

Finally, the points-and-status traveler, the one sitting on hotel loyalty currency or elite status, has a genuine edge in this tier and should use it, because the luxury brands are exactly where status perks like upgrades, lounge access, and late checkout carry the most value, and where a points redemption can make a steep festival-weekend rate far more palatable. For this traveler the deciding factor is often which luxury brand they have status or points with, and the smart play is to let that align the property choice, then apply the view-and-service logic within that brand's portfolio. Across all of these archetypes the underlying rule holds: name your first priority honestly, whether it is the view, the service, the short walk, the romance, the space, or the loyalty value, and let that priority choose the property, rather than reaching for the most expensive name and hoping it fits.

## The mistakes that quietly waste a luxury budget

A premium budget is forgiving of small errors and brutal on a few specific ones, and the travelers who feel cheated by their luxury stay almost always made one of a short list of mistakes rather than simply paying too much. Knowing the failure modes in advance is worth more than any single recommendation, because avoiding them is what turns a steep rate into a stay you are glad you booked. The mistakes cluster around five themes, and each has a clean fix.

The first and most common mistake is paying for a view you will not use. A park-facing high-floor room carries a real premium, and the traveler who books it on instinct because it sounds magical, then spends every waking hour at the gates and every sleeping hour with the curtains drawn, has bought an expensive window. The fix is the honesty test from earlier: if you will genuinely linger in the room with the view, in the mornings or on a rest afternoon, buy it without hesitation, and if you will only be there to sleep, take the skyline or city room and put the difference toward the festival or a better dinner. The view is the single largest discretionary line in a luxury booking, so it is the line where self-honesty saves the most.

The second mistake is missing the package when one exists, or taking the package when it does not fit. Premium properties near the park frequently bundle the room with credits and perks for the festival weekend, and the traveler who books the bare rate without asking can overpay for inclusions they would have used, while the traveler who grabs a generous-looking package they will not touch overpays the other way. The fix is to always ask what the current festival-weekend bundle includes and to price the inclusions you will actually use against the gap to the bare rate, taking the package only when the used inclusions cover the gap. This single habit, asking and then doing the arithmetic, is worth more than any general tip.

The third mistake is treating every property that calls itself luxury as equivalent. The word is unregulated, and a hotel can wear it while delivering thin service, dated rooms, and a recovery experience no better than a good mid-range stay. ## Two flavors of luxury: the historic grande dames and the modern glass towers

When the view is your priority and you have narrowed the search to the park-facing and lakefront stock, the deciding question is one of character rather than logistics: do you want the historic grande dame or the modern glass tower? The two flavors of luxury deliver the park-and-lake outlook in very different packages, and the choice is genuinely a matter of taste, but it helps to understand what each does better so the taste is informed rather than a coin flip.

The historic grande dames, the grand old hotels with decades of Chicago history in their bones, sell a sense of occasion that a new building cannot manufacture. The trade-offs are the trade-offs of old buildings done well: rooms can vary more in size and layout than in a uniform modern tower, some historic floor plans are quirkier, and the ceiling heights and window sizes that read as grand in the public spaces do not always translate to floor-to-ceiling glass in the guest rooms. A great grande dame has updated everything that matters for comfort while keeping the character, and that is the property to seek; a tired one trades on its name while delivering dated rooms, which is the trap to avoid.

The modern glass towers sell the high-floor, floor-to-ceiling version of the same view, and for a certain traveler that is the dream: a wall of glass on a high floor, the park and the lake spread out below, the skyline framed at night, and a clean contemporary room engineered around the outlook. The newer towers tend to deliver the most uniform comfort, the most reliable climate control and soundproofing, and the most dramatic unobstructed sightlines, because they were designed in an era that prizes the view and builds the rooms to maximize it. The trade-off is character: a modern tower is sleek and comfortable but rarely carries the sense of history a grande dame does, and for the traveler who wanted the weekend to feel grand in an old-world way, the glass can feel a little anonymous. For the traveler who simply wants the best possible view from the most reliably comfortable room, the glass tower is often the stronger pick.

The honest way to choose between them is to decide which experience you are buying. If part of why you came to Chicago is the city itself, its history and its grand hotels, the grande dame is the richer choice and the romance is the point. If the view and the room comfort are what you are paying for and the building's history is secondary, the modern tower delivers them more reliably. Neither is better in the abstract; they are different products wearing the same luxury label, and the traveler who knows which one they actually want will be happier than the one who booked the most famous name without asking the question. When in doubt, look at recent guest accounts specifically for whether the historic property has kept its rooms genuinely current and whether the modern tower has any warmth beyond the glass, and let those details break the tie.

## The split strategy: spending the premium where it counts most

For the traveler who wants the luxury experience but feels the full four-night premium straining the budget, there is a middle path worth naming, because the premium does not have to be all or nothing across the whole weekend. The split strategy treats the luxury stay as something you deploy where it earns the most rather than a uniform commitment, and for the right traveler it captures most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost.

The core idea is that the value of premium recovery is not equal across the four nights. The nights that matter most for recovery are the ones before the days you most want to be at full strength, and the night that matters most for comfort is often the final one, when you are most depleted and most want a soft landing and a late checkout before you travel home. A traveler on a budget can therefore consider concentrating the premium on the nights where it pays off most, the back half of the weekend when the cumulative fatigue is heaviest, while staying somewhere more modest for the earlier nights when a fresher body recovers fine in an ordinary room. The result is the luxury experience exactly when the body needs it most, without paying the top rate for the nights when it matters least.

The honest caveats keep this from being a free lunch. Changing hotels mid-weekend has a real cost in friction: you pack, you check out, you move your base, and you lose a little of the settled comfort that is part of why you wanted luxury in the first place. For some travelers that friction cancels the savings, and the cleaner move is to pick one base and commit. The split also requires that both properties have availability for your specific nights on a high-demand weekend, which is not guaranteed and argues again for booking early. And it works best when the two properties are close enough that the move is trivial rather than a cross-city ordeal. So the split is not for everyone; it is a tool for the traveler who genuinely wants luxury, feels the full-weekend rate as a stretch, and is willing to trade one mid-weekend move for a meaningful saving on the nights where premium recovery matters least.

There is a simpler version of the same logic that avoids the move entirely: pick a single property a notch below the absolute top of the market, a genuine luxury room rather than the grandest suite or the highest park-facing floor, and put the saved premium toward the perks that actually aid your weekend, a spa treatment, a great dinner, the package that includes late checkout. This keeps you in one comfortable base for all four nights while spending the luxury budget on the recovery-and-experience pieces that touch the festival directly rather than on the single most expensive room category. For many travelers this is the smarter luxury than chasing the top room: a very good room you stay in the whole weekend, plus the services that genuinely help, beats the grandest room with nothing left over for the spa and the dinner that would have made the weekend. The principle underneath both versions is the same one that runs through this whole guide: spend the premium on what you will use and what aids the festival, not on the label, and the luxury tier rewards you.
