## Romeo and Juliet in Verona: A City That Commemorates by Calendar

Verona's Romeo and Juliet festivals are not decorations hung on a famous story. They are a civic calendar, built in dated layers across three quarters of a century, and they amount to an argument about how a city should remember: not with a single monument, visited once, but with recurrence, a sequence of feasts, seasons and ceremonies that returns the lovers to the streets at fixed points in every year. The calendar has a February movement built around the feast of Saint Valentine, a summer movement of open-air theatre that reaches back to 1948, a September movement gathered around a birthday the city appointed for Juliet, and beneath all three a daily substrate of letters, visits and small rites that never closes. Each layer was made at a documented moment, by named institutions, for stated reasons, and each layer can be read off the record the city itself keeps.

The verdict of the pages that follow can be stated at the outset. Verona's calendar succeeds where the single shrine fails, because recurrence is the one commemorative form the legend itself supplies. The play ends with two fathers promising statues of gold, and a statue is what Verona duly possesses, bronze rather than gold, standing in a courtyard. Yet the statue is the least of the city's commemorations. The festivals are the greater work, because a festival does what a monument cannot: it assembles the city, assigns its citizens parts to perform, and then, by returning, makes the story a habit of the place rather than an exhibit in it. The legend survives in Verona not because Verona built for it, but because Verona scheduled it.

![The festival calendar of Verona, from the February hearts to the summer stage of the Teatro Romano - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The comparison that fixes Verona's singularity is Stratford. The playwright's own town keeps a birthday: David Garrick's Shakespeare Jubilee of 1769 founded the tradition of celebrating the author at his birthplace, and Stratford's April observances descend from that invention. Stratford commemorates a man, on a date, with a parade. Verona commemorates two characters, across a year, with a constitution of feasts. The difference is not scale but kind. An author's birthday belongs to literary history, and its rites are tributes paid by the culture that reads him. The lovers' calendar belongs to the story itself, and its rites are re-enactments performed by the city that hosts it. Verona did not merely decline to copy Stratford. Verona answered a different question: not how a town honours its writer, but how a place behaves when the fiction assigned to it is a love story with a feast at its centre and a cult at its end.

That verdict needs the evidence laid out season by season, and the evidence has a discipline of its own. A calendar is a designed object, and its design choices are datable: a trademark registered in 2005, a theatre season founded in 1948, a birth date reconstructed from a sixteenth-century novella, a film festival folded into the February days after a nine-year sleep. Where the record gives an edition number, a founding year or an organizer's name, the account below holds to it. Where promoters' arithmetic and the calendar of years part company, the gap is shown rather than smoothed. Verona's festivals have attracted a fog of promotional approximation over the decades, and the fog is part of the subject, since a calendar built to be repeated is also a calendar built to be advertised.

The evidence for such a calendar is a peculiar archive, and a word about it belongs at the start. Festivals leave programmes the way plays leave editions: printed, dated, partial, and revised at every recurrence. The February festival's editions can be reconstructed from municipal presentations and concession notices; the summer season's from the festival network's records and the press of its anniversary years; the September feast's from the Club's own published programmes. A seminar that sets the February programme of 2011 beside the concession document of 2025, or the Teatro Romano's founding cast beside its fiftieth edition's, is doing textual criticism on a city, and the habit of laying such documents in parallel, for instance in slides reviewed through [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), is the right scholarly posture toward them: editions first, impressions second. The account below follows that order throughout, preferring the programme to the poster and the count of editions to the claims of brochures.

Three proprietors share the year between them, and the sharing explains the calendar's durability. The municipality owns the summer season outright and holds the February brand as registered property. The Club, a voluntary association, owns the letters, the prizes and the September feast. The market owns the rest: the stalls of the heart-shaped market, the festival menus, the race's sponsors, the platform that sold a night in the shrine. Each proprietor can fail without killing the calendar. A town hall can lose interest, a club can dwindle, a sponsor can withdraw, and the other two strata continue. Verona's year survived a nine-year death of its film festival and a year's silence in its own edition count because no single keeper's failure is fatal. The calendar is less a programme than a constitution, and constitutions outlast governments.

One boundary should be drawn before the seasons begin. This article owns the calendar: the festivals as programming, the editions as evidence, the sequence as design. The meaning of the February feast itself, its Chaucerian invention and its doubtful Roman warrants, belongs to a companion study of the lovers and the feast day, and the money the calendar moves belongs to the economic record examined elsewhere in this series. What remains, and it is plenty, is the year Verona built for two fictional teenagers: how it is assembled, who assembles it, and whether the assembly honours the play or replaces it.

## The Play's Own Feast: Capulet's Ball and the Statue Promised at the End

The calendar's deepest warrant is that the play is already about civic ritual. Shakespeare set the tragedy inside a city that marks its year with a feast, and he ended it with a city promising itself monuments. Verona's festival-makers, whether or not any of them reasoned in these terms, inherited both templates and chose between them. Quotations in this article follow the Folger Shakespeare, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine.

The feast comes first, in every sense. Capulet announces the machinery of the plot as a fixture of the social year: "This night I hold an old accustomed feast, / Whereto I have invited many a guest" (1.2). The ball at which Romeo meets Juliet is not an improvisation. It is an old accustomed feast, a recurring observance with a standing guest list, the kind of event a household holds because the season calls for it. The lovers' whole story is ignited inside a calendrical institution, at the one gathering of the year where the city's young people are licensed to look at one another behind masks. A tragedy that begins at an annual feast has recurrence in its foundations, and the city that adopted the tragedy has, in its February and September gatherings, reproduced the founding situation with documentary fidelity: crowds, costumes, music, and the managed suspension of ordinary rules.

The feast in the play is also an instrument, and its instrumentality matters to the calendar's defence. Capulet's ball is his house's public machinery: the heir displayed, the suitor Paris invited to look and to woo, the alliance market of Verona held in a candlelit hall. The feast is where the city's private arrangements are made in public, under licence of the season, and its licence is real: masks loosen the rules of recognition, dancing loosens the rules of approach, and a Montague can cross the room unchallenged until the dancing ends. Every element of Verona's February, the costumes, the ball at Castelvecchio, the synchronized kiss, the messages pinned in a courtyard, is a descendant of that licensed evening. The festivals did not invent their grammar from the story's romance. They inherited it from the story's sociology, from the one night in the play when the city is gathered, masked and watching its young people pair off. Capulet, the calendar's unwitting founder, would have recognized the arrangements at once.

The play also supplies its lovers with a date, and the date matters because Verona's calendar quietly overrules it. The Nurse fixes Juliet's age by a feast of the agricultural year: "Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3). Lammas Eve is the last night of July, so the play's Juliet approaches her fourteenth birthday at midsummer, in the heat of the season in which the whole action unfolds. The text is precise about its own calendar even while compressing it, and a city building a festival year around the heroine had that precision available to it. Verona kept a different date, in September, drawn from the Italian novella behind the play rather than from the play itself. That substitution, examined in the September section below, is the calendar's most revealing editorial act: where the novella and the play disagree, the festival follows the novella, because the festival's Juliet is Verona's own citizen, and her papers are Italian.

### What promise at the play's end does the calendar keep?

Montague closes the feud with a commission: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold, / That while Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3). Capulet answers in kind, promising Romeo a matching figure.

The calendar keeps that promise in the fathers' spirit and beyond their letter: Verona raised the statues, and then, finding stone too still, raised a year of observances around them. The closing tableau deserves a slower reading, because the Prince frames the statues as an act of civic accounting. The monuments are "poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3), in Capulet's phrase: the feud's cost, converted into public art by the city's two great houses acting, for once, as a municipality. Commemoration in the play is therefore already a civic function, something the community performs to close a wound and to instruct itself. Verona's festivals extend that function along the time axis. A statue instructs whoever happens to pass; a calendar instructs on schedule, gathering the instructed into a crowd, and a crowd that gathers at the same season each year becomes, over decades, a tradition with its own memory of itself. The editions accumulate their own history, until the festival commemorates two things at once: the lovers, and the city's long fidelity to the lovers.

There is a sterner point underneath. The play's Verona is a city whose public rituals failed: the Prince's edicts could not keep the peace, the feast produced a killing within hours, and the only civic ceremony that works is the final one, performed over corpses. A modern city adopting this story for its festival year is adopting a warning as well as a romance. Whether Verona's calendar hears the warning is the spectacle question, and it is taken up at the end of this article, season by season. The short answer is that the calendar is divided against itself exactly as the play is: its February movement stages the feast and stops before the tomb, while its summer movement, the oldest of all, stages the whole tragedy, corpses included, in a Roman theatre across the river.

The contrast of timescales deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the calendar's boldest transformation. The sources gave Shakespeare a story of months: Brooke's poem and the novellas let the love ripen across a season and more. The play crushes that span into less than a week, Sunday to Thursday dawn, and drives its people with timetables: a feast tonight, a marriage on Monday, a wedding to Paris set for Thursday and then dragged forward to Wednesday by a father's impatience (3.4; 4.2). Verona's festival year performs the opposite operation on the same material. Where the play compresses, the calendar dilates, stretching a five-day tragedy across twelve months of observance until the story becomes coextensive with the city's time itself. The lovers who in the text have no time, whose haste is their doom, are given in Verona all the time there is: a year that returns, and returns, and returns. A calendar is the kindest possible revenge on the play's clock.

## February in Verona: Verona in Love and the Invention of a Season

Verona in Love is the calendar's youngest major layer and its most frankly manufactured. The event was founded in 2005, on the initiative of the Comune di Verona and the Consorzio Verona Tuttintorno, with an aim its early chroniclers stated without embarrassment: to draw visitors in the low season, when the city's hotels and museums stood emptiest. The Comune registered the festival's name as a trademark in the same year, joining it to the municipal slogan "Se ami qualcuno portalo a Verona", the instruction that anyone who loves someone should bring that person to Verona. A feast day inherited from the church calendar was thus rebuilt as a branded municipal product, and the brand was fenced with the legal instruments of commerce from the first edition onward. The question of what Saint Valentine's Day itself means, and whether any Roman festival stands behind it, has been tried elsewhere in this series, in [the study of Romeo and Juliet and the feast that adopted them](/2029/09/14/romeo-and-juliet-and-valentines-day/); the February days concern this article as programming, the deliberate filling of a season.

The seventh edition shows the machine at full assembly. Verona in Love ran from 12 to 14 February 2011, presented at the Palazzo Scaligero by the tourism assessor Ruggero Pozzani and organized by the Province, the Comune, the Consorzio Verona Tuttintorno and Provincia di Verona Turismo under the patronage of the Veneto Region. Its geography was a designed itinerary. Two squares anchored the event, Piazza Bra and Piazza dei Signori, joined by a route lit with giant red hearts that led past themed shop windows to the Casa di Giulietta and on through Piazza delle Erbe. In Piazza dei Signori stood the festival's emblem, the market called Un Cuore da Scoprire, a Heart to Discover, its stalls laid out across the square in the shape of a great heart. In the Cortile Mercato Vecchio a board invited lovers to leave written messages under the heading "Il messaggio del cuore", the message of the heart, with prizes promised for the finest. The edition's novelty, reported that February, was an invitation to every couple in the city to climb the staircase of Palazzo Barbieri, beside the Arena, where the steps were lit red, and to seal the festival with a kiss held for one full minute.

The design of that minute repays attention, because it is the festival's whole theory in miniature. A kiss is the least schedulable of acts, private, spontaneous, and over in a moment; the festival schedules it, publicizes it, synchronizes it and stretches it to sixty counted seconds on the steps of the seat of government. What looks like whimsy is engineering. The rite gives the crowd a part to play that requires no ticket, no costume and no talent, and it gives photographers an image that summarizes the brand in a single frame: hundreds of couples, one staircase, one colour of light. The message board in the Cortile Mercato Vecchio performs the complementary operation for those who arrive without a partner or without a public temperament. Its heading, the message of the heart, and its prizes for the finest messages turn the festival's visitors into correspondents, miniature Juliets and Romeos posting their declarations in a courtyard a few steps from the house where the real letters arrive. February's two signature rites are thus a matched pair: the kiss, for couples, performed with the body; the message, for anyone, performed with a pen. Between them they cover the play's two great instruments of love, the embrace and the letter, and they recruit every visitor to one or the other. A festival that can assign its crowd a role in the story has solved the oldest problem of commemoration, the passivity of the commemorators, and Verona solved it with a staircase and a notice board.

The programme around those set pieces mixed the expected with the characteristic. Concerts and street performance filled the route; the Torre dei Lamberti, the city's tallest medieval tower, was lit in red; restaurants served festival menus; a costumed ball in Juliet's name was staged at Castelvecchio, the medieval castle on the river; and the romantic film festival Schermi d'Amore, whose own history is treated below, belonged to the same February constellation. The edition's texture, as the local coverage recorded it day by day, was that of a city amusing itself in character. The Saturday of the 2011 festival opened at the Palazzo della Gran Guardia with "Tango che passione", a dance spectacle followed by an open milonga on the floor. On the Sunday, in Piazza Bra, an astrologer and a sexologist presented a sentimental horoscope under the title "Eros... alle stelle!", Eros among the stars, a piece of festival programming that deserves preservation for its nerve alone: the star-crossed lovers' city consulting the stars, in public, as entertainment. Music and street performance ran along the illuminated route between the squares. The organizers' spokesman reckoned that some five thousand visitors from outside the province had come for the previous edition, a figure that belongs to the festival's own publicity and is quoted here at that grade. What the publicity arithmetic cannot show, the structure can: by 2011 the event had settled into the classic form of the invented civic festival, a fixed date, a fixed route, a fixed emblem, and a programme varied at the edges so that repetition would feel like return rather than routine.

Later editions kept the skeleton and changed the flesh. The official Italian calendar of cultural events listed the 2019 festival from 11 to 14 February, describing red heart lanterns and hundreds of love notes filling the streets, concerts and tastings in the main squares, and Juliet's House open at a special festival price. The 2020 edition ran from the morning of 13 February to the evening of the sixteenth, and its programme carried a detail that complicates any simple account of February as pure courtship: on the evening of 15 February the Teatro Ristori staged Romeo e Giulietta itself, the whole tragedy, indoors, inside the festival's own days. The production matters to the argument of this article. February's public rites, its kisses and hearts and races, do stop at the balcony; but the feast's theatrical margin admits the entire play, ticketed and seated, at the edge of the square. The division of the story among the seasons, drawn in the verdict below, is therefore a division of emphasis rather than an absolute law, and the exception stands in the record where an honest calendar must keep it.

### Who owns the Verona in Love brand, and how is it used?

The Comune di Verona owns it. The municipality registered the mark "Verona in Love, Se ami qualcuno portalo a Verona" in 2005 and remains its sole proprietor, licensing its use to the organizers of the February programme. For the 2025 edition, a municipal concession offered the brand, the squares and a single communication campaign to one operator.

That 2025 notice is the calendar's most candid document. In it, the Comune describes the event it owns: an observance that, from its first edition in 2005, has presented itself as a festival of romantic love in the city known across the world for Juliet and Romeo, built to valorize the myth in the winter season of Saint Valentine and to fix Verona in the visitor's mind as the city of love. The licence covered five days, 12 to 16 February 2025, together with the setting-up and dismantling around them, and it gathered under the brand not only the licensee's own programme but the love-themed proposals of the Comune, of associations and of other public and private bodies active in the same days. The February calendar, in other words, is curated by contract. Independent initiatives in the squares were excluded for the period, with one exception the notice names expressly, and that exception belongs to the next section. First, though, the edition numbers deserve a footnote of their own, because they misbehave in an instructive way. The 2011 edition was billed as the seventh, which counts back cleanly to 2005. The 2020 edition, held from 13 to 16 February of that year, was billed as the fifteenth, which does not count back cleanly: somewhere in the festival's second decade an edition was skipped, merged or miscounted, and the record does not say where. A calendar is a promise of regularity, and Verona's February promise, examined closely, contains one silent gap. The gap does not damage the festival. It does show that even the arithmetic of recurrence is a promotional material, to be handled at the grade the documents earn.

The licensing model is the mechanism by which the calendar reproduces, and it deserves naming as such. A municipality that owns a festival outright must staff it, fund it and answer for its failures. A municipality that owns a brand can do something subtler: grant the mark for five specified days, require one coordinated campaign, gather the season's independent proposals under the umbrella, and exempt the fixtures too established to need shelter. The feast is therefore less an event than a franchise of the city's affections, renewed by notice, hedged by trademark law, and filled each year with other bodies' programming. Students of festival speak of invented tradition; the Verona documents speak the plainer language of concession, and the plain language is the more revealing. The city's love for its lovers is administered like a market hall, by licence, for a term, under a registered name, and the administration works: editions recur under the mark regardless of which operator holds it.

## The Ceremonies Inside February: A Prize for Letters and a Race for Lovers

A festival of stalls and illuminations would be only a market with a theme. What raises Verona's February into ritual is that two of its fixtures demand something of the participants: a letter, judged and crowned, and a race, run through the streets on the feast's own weekend. Both fixtures predate the brand that contains them, and both reach the calendar from the city's older, unbranded strata.

The letter prize is the elder. The [Juliet Club chronicled in this series](/2029/06/29/juliet-club-letters-to-juliet/), the volunteer body that answers the letters addressed to Juliet from across the world, awards its Premio Cara Giulietta, the Dear Juliet prize, to the finest love letter of the year, and the ceremony falls on Valentine's Day, inside the February festival. The institution of the prize belongs to the Club's own history; its placement belongs to the calendar's design, and the placement is a decision worth pausing on. The Club keeps a second great date in September, Juliet's appointed birthday, surrounded by its own celebrations. The letter prize might have crowned that autumn gathering, where the Club stands at the centre of its own feast. Instead the ceremony sits in February, on the one day when the whole city, and not only the Club's devotees, is dressed for love. The effect is a deliberate subordination that is also an elevation: the letters, the most private and least theatrical of all the Verona practices, are brought out once a year and exhibited at the city's most public moment, like a relic carried in procession on the patron's day. February borrows the letters' authenticity; the letters borrow February's crowd.

The ceremony is also the February programme's conscience, the one fixture in the branded week whose content is judged rather than counted. The market is measured in stalls, the race in finishers, the kiss in couples; the prize is measured by a jury reading for quality, and its winner is a text. In a festival assembled by a tourism consortium and licensed as a trademark, the letter prize preserves a standard that commerce did not set: the year's best love letter, chosen from the mail of strangers, most of whom will never visit Verona at all. The ceremony's placement on the feast day turns the whole branded apparatus toward that standard for one hour, exhibiting the mail as the festival's title deed. Without the letters, February would be a market in search of a meaning. With them, crowned in public on the fourteenth, the market can point to the devotion that justifies it, and the calendar's youngest layer is anchored to its oldest practice in front of the crowd.

The race is the brasher fixture. The Giulietta e Romeo Half Marathon sends its field through the city on the Sunday nearest the feast, finishing in Piazza Bra, and it has grown into one of the fixture's documented fixtures: race reports record its winners in February 2012, the Italian athletics federation's rolls number its thirteenth edition in 2020 and its sixteenth in 2023, and the Comune's 2025 concession notice, while excluding every other independent initiative from the February squares, singles the half marathon out by name as the one consolidated appointment allowed to stand outside the licensed programme. That clause is the calendar's own testimony to the race's standing. A half marathon is a strange rite for a love festival until its logic is examined. The runners perform the legend's essential action, which is haste: the play is a story of messages, journeys and arrivals measured in hours, of a friar's letter that travels too slowly and a lover who rides too fast. The February runners cover their twenty-one kilometres through the streets where the fiction is set, in pairs and packs, many in costume, and the city watches its myth enacted as exertion rather than as pageant. Of all the February inventions, the race is the one that asks the body to do what the story does.

The race's own record shows an institution settling into the calendar's grain. The 2020 running went out under a sponsor's name as the Gensan Giulietta e Romeo Half Marathon, and its results sheets preserve a category that reads like a quotation: the Duo Half Marathon, in which two runners divide the course between them as a pair. A relay for two, inside a race named for two, on the feast of lovers, is the calendar's wit at its most structural. The lovers of the play are forever being separated by distance and rejoined by messengers; the duo runners enact the pattern as sport, one waiting where the other must arrive. The race also finishes where the festival's other rites gather, in Piazza Bra under the Arena, so that the February weekend ends with its athletes arriving, spent and paired, at the square where its couples kiss. No planner needed to intend the symmetry for the symmetry to work on the crowd that watches both.

February also holds the calendar's most instructive survival. Schermi d'Amore, Verona's international festival of romantic cinema, was founded in 1996, nine years before Verona in Love existed, as the heir of the city's international film week that Pietro Barzisa had founded in 1969. Its charter, as the festival's own histories state it, was to explore films in which love drives the plot, and its founders justified a festival of melodrama by appeal to Verona's Shakespearean myth of the lovers and to the Arena's operatic tradition: the February film festival was, from birth, a satellite of the legend. It ran until 2010 and then slept for nine years. Its return was staged in two movements, a summer preview at the Teatro Romano in 2018 under the title Il filo ritrovato, the thread found again, and then a fifteenth edition from 12 to 19 February 2019 at the Teatro Ristori, directed by Paolo Romano and organized by the Comune's Verona Film Festival office, with a sixteenth edition following in 2020. The revived festival thus took its place inside the same February days the younger brand had colonized, an elder institution re-entering a calendar that had been built, in part, over its grave.

The revived edition's programme shows what the festival thought it was. Its thirty-five titles were arranged in four strands: a survey of sentimental cinema past and present; a focus on impossible loves in fantastic film, opened by the 1925 Phantom of the Opera; a survey of the screen Carmens of the world, from Chaplin's burlesque to the operatic Carmen of Francesco Rosi; and a section of love in short films. The edition was held under the sign of Claude Lelouch, who received the festival's prize and whose film A Man and a Woman of 1966 closed the proceedings. A festival that programmes Carmen beside the lovers, and impossible love as a genre beside melodrama, is making the same argument as the calendar that hosts it: that Verona's franchise is not one couple but the whole grammar of love stories, with the home couple presiding. Schermi d'Amore is the February cluster's library, the place where the feast's emotion is given a canon.

The choice of melodrama as the festival's province was itself a critical act, and it explains the institution's long hold on the calendar. Melodrama is the lovers' native genre displaced onto film: love obstructed by family, fortune and misunderstanding, virtue recognized too late, feeling stated at a volume polite art finds embarrassing. A city whose patron story is a melodrama before the word existed does not merely tolerate the genre; it holds the genre's founding charter. The festival's programming across its editions, the Carmens beside the Phantom, the impossible loves beside the great romances, amounts to a genealogy, an argument that the Veronese story stands at the head of a family that includes opera's gypsy and the silent screen's masked organist, all of them lovers destroyed by the arrangements of the world. When the festival slept for nine years, the February cluster lost not an ornament but its memory of why the feast's emotion matters, the archive in which swooning is a serious aesthetic category. Its revival restored the calendar's critical faculty, the one institution in the year whose business is judgment of the story's descendants rather than celebration of the story. The February cluster is not one invention but three strata in one week: a film festival from 1996, a letter ceremony from the Club's older practice, and a municipal brand from 2005 spread over both.

The brand, to its credit, kept inventing. On Valentine's Day 2020 the Casa di Giulietta itself became, for one night, a dwelling again. The Comune and the accommodation platform Airbnb offered a single couple, chosen by a competition whose entry requirement was a letter to Juliet, a night inside the museum: dinner cooked in the house by Giancarlo Perbellini, a chef holding two Michelin stars, and a bed made up in Juliet's room with the very bed that stands in the museum as a relic of Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film. The mayor, Federico Sboarina, presented the night as the myth brought to life as never before, and the house was a home, the organizers noted, for the first time since the 1930s. The night's machinery deserves one more documented detail. Admission to the fiction was franchised: alongside the single stay, the platform listed four bookable Valentine experiences for the same February, a Michelin-starred dinner, a day spent as Juliet's secretary among the letters, a romantic photoshoot and a food and wine walk. The calendar's rites, in other words, could be purchased separately, as modules. A devotee with a smaller purse than the competition's winner could still buy a shift inside the story, answering letters in Juliet's name for a day. The episode belongs to the calendar because it shows the festival's deepest instinct at work. Verona's February does not only represent the lovers' story at a distance, in stalls and screenings. It recruits participants to step inside the fiction and occupy it, as letter-writers, as runners, as kissers on a staircase, and, once, as sleepers in Juliet's bed. Participation is the festival's true programme, and the stalls are its scenery.

## Verona's Shakespeare Summer: The Teatro Romano Season Since 1948

If February is the calendar's invention, summer is its inheritance. The Estate Teatrale Veronese, the Veronese theatre summer centred on the Verona Shakespeare Festival, was founded in 1948, and it is the oldest organized stratum in the lovers' year. Its founder was Renato Simoni, a Veronese who stood at the centre of Italian theatrical life: the leading theatre critic of his generation, a playwright in the Venetian tongue, and, with Giuseppe Adami, the librettist of Puccini's Turandot. Simoni's founding purpose, as the city states it, was homage: to honour Shakespeare and to underline the playwright's bond with Verona, the city of the lovers, of The Taming of the Shrew and of The Two Gentlemen of Verona. The season was a municipality's act of cultural possession, a claim that the English dramatist who never saw Verona nevertheless belonged to it, and that the belonging should be renewed on a stage every summer.

### What did Renato Simoni build at the Teatro Romano in 1948?

Simoni built the institution that still frames Verona's summer: a municipal Shakespeare season in the Roman theatre across the Adige, founded as homage to the playwright and his Veronese settings. He opened it with the lovers' own play, directed the production himself, and took as his assistant the young Giorgio Strehler, fresh from co-founding the Piccolo Teatro in Milan.

The founding cast the season's mould: civic ownership, the Roman stones for a stage, and the tragedy of Verona as the first offering.

The opening production deserves its billing. The play was Giulietta e Romeo, in a translation by Salvatore Quasimodo, the Sicilian poet who would receive the Nobel Prize in Literature eleven years later. A Nobel poet's version, the city's foremost critic directing, the coming master of the Italian stage assisting: the first night of 1948 was assembled as a national occasion, and it set the season's pattern of prestige. The festival's own network, the European Shakespeare Festivals Network, repeats the founding facts with pride, and notes that the season has since drawn landmark Italian productions and international directors and stars to the Roman theatre. Playbill's account of the fiftieth edition lists the traffic across five decades: Vittorio Gassman, Carmelo Bene and Giancarlo Giannini among the actors, Franco Zeffirelli among the directors, Vanessa Redgrave among the visiting stars. A season founded as homage became, by accumulation of editions, one of the standing Shakespeare festivals of the world.

The founding year matters to the meaning. Italy in 1948 was a country rebuilding its cities and its institutions out of a lost war, and a municipality that chose to spend its cultural budget on an English playwright's Veronese tragedy was making a statement about the kind of peace it intended: international, literary, and anchored in the deepest stratum of the city's past. The stage itself preached the same sermon. The Roman theatre had spent the middle centuries buried beneath convents and houses, a ruin under the city rather than in it; excavation began in earnest in the nineteenth century, the town received the site in 1904, and the long work of recovery ran on into the twentieth century. To found the season in 1948 was to declare the excavation complete in the only sense that finally matters: the ruin had a use again, and the use was the lovers. Simoni, who had spent a career arguing in print for the renewal of the Italian stage, built his festival where the argument could be made in stone, in a theatre older than the fiction by fifteen centuries, recovered by the same city that the fiction had made famous. The homage ran in both directions, to Shakespeare and to Verona, and each half has been paying the other ever since.

The theatre itself is half the argument. The Teatro Romano is a first-century auditorium cut into the hill of San Pietro across the river from the old town, buried under later building for centuries, excavated from the nineteenth century and given to the town in 1904. To stage the lovers there is to make a claim in stone: that the fiction belongs to the same deep civic past as the Roman cavea and the medieval towers, that Juliet is as much a part of Verona's antiquity as the emperors. The claim is false as history and magnificent as theatre, and the festival has never pretended otherwise. It is a homage, not an excavation. The audience climbs Roman steps to watch a story that a London playwright set in a Verona he assembled from Italian novellas, performed in Italian translation in a city that adopted the result as a birthright. Every layer of that sentence is a construction, and the construction has held for more than seventy editions.

### How many evenings does the summer season fill?

The season runs the length of the summer. One recent documented edition stretched from 20 June to 13 September and filled forty-eight evenings, among them fourteen national premieres and seven co-productions across theatre, dance and music. The Comune, which produces the season, counted its seventy-fourth edition in 2022.

The Comune presents the season as its own production, made with the production partner Arteven and with the support of the Ministry of Culture and the Veneto Region, an administrative spine that has carried the festival across more than seven decades of editions.

Growth, in the season's case, meant dilation beyond its founding charter. Dance entered the programme in 1968 and became a fixture; jazz arrived in 1985, when the Roman theatre took on the Verona Jazz festival as a summer tenant; classical drama beyond Shakespeare joined in later editions, in collaboration with the National Institute of Ancient Drama of Syracuse, so that Greek tragedy shares the stones with the Veronese tragedy. Other city venues were drawn into the season's orbit, among them the Corte Mercato Vecchio, the old market courtyard in the historic centre where the September festival also plays. The season's centre of gravity nevertheless held. The Shakespeare festival kept its central position inside the larger programme, and the lovers' play kept its position inside the Shakespeare festival, returning at the anniversaries that matter. The fiftieth edition, in 1998, opened on the first of July with the Royal Shakespeare Company's Romeo and Juliet, directed by Michael Attenborough and played by Ray Fearon and Zoë Waites, finishing a world tour on the stage of the city where the fiction is set. The homage had come full circle: half a century after Simoni claimed Shakespeare for Verona, the guardian company of Shakespeare's own birthplace brought the play home to Verona as the festival's crown.

The rest of that fiftieth programme, as the contemporary listings preserve it, shows the season's range at its jubilee. An Italian Hamlet, directed by Tato Russo, followed the visitors later in July; Gigi Dall'Aglio's production of The Taming of the Shrew, with Michele Placido and Elisabetta Pozzi, carried the season into August, honouring the city's second Shakespearean title; and in the second half of August the Arena's ballet company mounted Prokofiev's Romeo and Juliet at the Teatro Romano, in Robert North's choreography, so that the lovers closed their own festival's fiftieth summer in dance, wordless, in the theatre where their words had opened the institution fifty years before. The jubilee programme is the season in cross-section: the founding play in an imported landmark production, the canon around it in national productions, and the ballet carrying the story into the one medium that needs no translation at all.

The season also grew a scholarly limb, and the limb matters to the calendar's claim of seriousness. The European festival network's catalogue records the Verona Shakespeare Centre and its summer school on Shakespeare and the Mediterranean as part of the Estate Teatrale Veronese from 2022, under the festival's artistic direction of Carlo Mangolini. A summer school is a festival turned inward: students and scholars gathering in the city to study the texts the season performs. With the school's absorption into the municipal programme, the summer stratum completed a circuit that the other strata cannot match. February recruits the crowd's feelings, September its hands, but the summer recruits its mind as well, housing the study of the play inside the same municipal frame as the staging of it.

The breadth of the modern programme deserves a harder look, because it decides what the summer stratum of the lovers' calendar actually is. A season that began as one man's homage to one playwright has grown to mount dance, jazz, Greek tragedy and contemporary drama beside the canon, and a purist might call the result dilution: Shakespeare reduced to a founding logo on a general arts festival. The record supports a kinder and more accurate reading. The season's expansion follows the logic of the calendar itself. Simoni's founding act gave the city a summer institution with a Shakespearean charter; institutions with charters grow by annexation, drawing neighbouring arts under the authority of the founding name. Dance arrived in 1968, in the decade when the lovers' ballet was conquering the world's stages on Prokofiev's score, and the annexation was thematic before it was commercial: the story itself had migrated into dance, and the season followed its heroine. Jazz settled in 1985 as the city's own musical citizen. Greek tragedy arrived under the Syracusan institute's authority, the elder classical theatre honouring the younger. Each annexation was ratified by recurrence, kept because audiences returned for it, and the Shakespeare core was never displaced from the charter: the festival network still files the season under Shakespeare, and the founding play still opens the argument of every edition. The summer stratum is therefore not a Shakespeare festival diluted but a civic season chartered by Shakespeare, which is what Simoni, a man of the whole theatre and not of one author, would have recognized as his design fulfilled.

Among the directors the season drew in its first five decades, the contemporary record singles out Franco Zeffirelli, and his presence closes a circle inside the calendar. Zeffirelli staged Shakespeare at the Teatro Romano, and in 1968 he filmed Romeo and Juliet with the courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta standing in his Verona, fixing the balcony's image for the world's imagination. One career thus binds the calendar's two great stage sets: the Roman theatre where the play is performed entire each summer, and the courtyard where its single scene is venerated all year. The festivals' Verona and the cinema's Verona are the same construction, and Zeffirelli worked on both halves of it.

There is a question of scale to be answered honestly before the season is praised further. The summer festival is a theatre festival that honours a playwright, and only a fraction of its evenings, across seven decades, can have belonged to the lovers themselves. Hamlet and the Shrew, Goldoni and the Greeks, ballet and jazz all take their turns. The calendar argument of this article does not require the lovers to monopolize the summer. It requires only that the season exist because of them, and that is beyond dispute: no lovers, no homage, no festival. The Teatro Romano season is the calendar's load-bearing wall precisely because it is the least commercial of the strata. It was founded by a critic, not a consortium; it is run by a municipality as a cultural service; and it stages, when its turn comes, the whole tragedy, ending and all, which no other layer of the calendar dares to do.

## The Arena Across the Square: A Festival That Is Not Theirs

Every account of Verona's summer must reckon with the Arena, and the reckoning begins with a distinction. The Arena opera festival, founded in 1913 for the centenary of Verdi's birth, is the city's great summer institution, a season of opera in a Roman amphitheatre that seats its thousands under the stars from June into September. It is not a Romeo and Juliet festival. Its repertory is the grand Italian canon, its emblem Aida, its scale an order beyond anything the lovers command. The sums it moves through the city's economy are recorded in [the profile of Verona's economy built on the two fictional lovers](/2029/07/27/verona-economy-fictional-lovers/), where the Arena's figures dwarf the shrine trade they are usually quoted beside. Its place in this article is structural: the Arena is the calendar's neighbouring giant, and the lovers' year is arranged in its gravitational field.

The Arena festival's own founding is worth a sentence, since it is the calendar's oldest dated act of self-invention. The tenor Giovanni Zenatello, a Veronese by adoption and the festival's first artistic force, promoted an open-air Aida for the centenary of Verdi's birth, and the first performance was given on 10 August 1913. Verona had therefore practised the conversion of a Roman ruin into a summer institution a full generation before Simoni performed the same conversion across the river for Shakespeare. The lovers' summer of 1948 is, among other things, the second application of a civic technique the city had proved in 1913: take a monument, give it a season, and let recurrence do the rest. The Arena's success taught Verona that a festival could be founded, dated and owned, and the Shakespeare season applied the lesson to literature.

Read as geography, the calendar has two poles and a shrine between them, and the festivals distribute themselves along the axis. At the western end stands Piazza Bra, the Arena's square, the city's ground for spectacle: the summer opera crowd gathers there, the February race finishes there, and the Palazzo Barbieri on its edge lends its staircase to the synchronized kiss. At the eastern end stands Piazza dei Signori, the old civic square, Dante's statue presiding, where February pitches its heart market and September posts its hundred painters. The route of red hearts lit in 2011 ran from the one square to the other, past the Casa di Giulietta, which stands between the poles like an altar on a processional way. The year's rites keep redrawing that axis: crowds flow from spectacle to shrine to civic square and back, winter and summer, and the lovers' house sits at the crossing, collecting devotion in both directions. Verona's festival map is a sentence the city writes with its streets, and the sentence reads the same in every season: from the theatre, to the beloved, to the town.

The arrangement shows in small, telling ways. The February festival's signature rite, the minute-long kiss, is staged on the staircase of Palazzo Barbieri, the city hall that stands in Piazza Bra with the Arena at its shoulder, so that the lovers' emblematic embrace is photographed against the opera house's silhouette. The half marathon finishes in the same square. The summer season at the Teatro Romano defines itself across the river, in the Arena's counter-image: stone against stone, a theatre of two thousand seats against an amphitheatre of many thousands, prose against opera, the city's adopted Englishman against its native Italian repertory. Verona in summer is a city of two Roman venues running two festivals at once, and the lovers' claim on the season is the claim of the smaller, older-textured stage. That the claim holds, edition after edition, says something real about the legend's local weight. The Arena sells Verona to the world as a place of spectacle. The Teatro Romano, three hundred metres of river away, sells Verona to itself as a place of memory, and it was the lovers who bought the site its purpose in 1948.

## September in Verona: The Birthday the Play Never Gave Her

The calendar's boldest editorial act is a date. Verona celebrates Juliet's birthday in September, on the sixteenth of the month, with a medieval festival in the old town's courtyards. The play gives her a different birthday altogether: the Nurse's Lammas Eve falls at the end of July, and Juliet's fourteenth year turns at midsummer. A city that staged its heroine's birthday by the play's text would hold the feast in high summer, in the crowd of the theatre season. Verona instead keeps the sixteenth of September, and the reason is a lesson in whose Juliet the festival serves. The September date does not come from Shakespeare. It comes from Luigi da Porto, the Vicentine soldier whose novella of the two noble lovers, written in the early sixteenth century, stands at the head of the story's Italian line. Da Porto's Giulietta was born on the feast of Saint Euphemia, and Saint Euphemia's day is the sixteenth of September. The Veronese scholar Franco Viviani, working from the novella's internal chronology and the years of Bartolomeo della Scala's lordship over Verona, reconstructed the birth year as 1284. The birthday Verona celebrates is therefore a scholarly reconstruction of a novella's implication: a date with footnotes, appointed for a girl who never lived, in honour of a text most of the celebrants have never read. It is the calendar in miniature, erudition and pageantry fused.

Even the chosen day carries a footnote of doubt, and the doubt is worth keeping. In the Veneto and Paduan reckoning, as the Veronese reference accounts note, Saint Euphemia was also kept on 13 April, so the novella's Juliet has two candidate birthdays in the scholarly record, one in spring and one in autumn. The Club keeps September's. The choice was perhaps a calendrical convenience, a September feast standing clear of the August crowds and the February cold, or perhaps simple fidelity to the Roman calendar's better-known date. Either way the pattern holds: where the record offers the calendar a choice, the calendar decides, and the decision hardens into observance. A birthday that began as a scholar's inference has become a date on which painters book easels and companies book courtyards, which is to say it has acquired the only reality a festival date needs.

Viviani's reconstruction carried a second finding that ties September back to February in a way no planner designed. Working from the novella's clues, the reconstruction placed the Capulet feast at which the lovers met on a Friday in Lent, in the season of the late Easter of 1302. Da Porto's lovers, in other words, met in the late winter of the church year, in the weeks when Verona's modern February festival dresses the city in hearts. The February feast, invented in 2005 to fill a low season, thus keeps, by accident, the novella's own season for the meeting, while the September feast keeps the novella's date for the birth. The calendar's two hinges divide the novella's chronology between them, meeting in February and birth in September, without any organizer having consulted the reconstruction that holds both. The legend's Italian original is inscribed in the year more thoroughly than the brand knows.

The Club di Giulietta keeps the day. Around the sixteenth of September the Club stages its Festa Medievale, a medieval fair that turns the centre into a costume of itself: jugglers, musicians, street artists, falconers and poets in the Cortile Mercato Vecchio, the Cortile del Tribunale and the precincts of the Casa di Giulietta, with artisans demonstrating old crafts through the day. The play itself is performed inside its own birthday feast: in the documented programme, the Compagnia del Sipario Medievale staged Romeo e Giulietta in the Cortile del Tribunale on the Saturday evening, while the Lavanteatro company presented a Juliet piece in Piazza Dante earlier in the week. A third fixture spreads the celebration across the two great squares: Cento Pittori per Giulietta, a Hundred Painters for Juliet, a day of open-air painting in Piazza delle Erbe and Piazza dei Signori, with a hundred artists at their easels on the theme of love.

The Club's published programme for one documented edition lets the birthday week be watched entire, and the sequence is worth keeping because it shows the feast's true architecture. The week opened with the Scrivere per Amore ceremony on 10 September, the literary prize presented in the early evening. Two evenings later the Lavanteatro company played its Juliet piece in Piazza Dante. On the Saturday the Compagnia del Sipario Medievale performed the whole of Romeo e Giulietta in the Cortile del Tribunale. The Sunday belonged to the fair itself, running through the courtyards and the Casa, with the hundred painters posted in the two great squares. A prize, a play within the week, the tragedy whole on the Saturday, a city at its easels and stalls on the Sunday: the September feast is not a day but a small festival in its own right, with the heroine's birthday as its still centre and the sixteenth as the date toward which the whole sequence climbs. The September programme is the calendar's most participatory layer after the letters themselves. Its crowds do not watch a spectacle so much as staff one, painting, juggling, processing and performing in the streets their city has lent to the fiction.

### What fills Verona's courtyards during the Festa Medievale?

The Club's medieval fair fills them with the population of a festival from the story's own century: jugglers and musicians, street artists and falconers, poets reciting in the open air, and artisans demonstrating old crafts through the day. The Cortile Mercato Vecchio and the Cortile del Tribunale become fairgrounds for the birthday weekend.

The Casa di Giulietta joins the same circuit, so that the shrine is one stall among many in a city dressed as its own past.

The medievalism of the September fair is the calendar's most literal act of imagination, and its most democratic. February assigns its visitors the roles of lovers; the summer assigns them the role of audience; the Festa Medievale assigns them the role of citizens of the story's own century, extras in the Verona of the Scala lordship through whose streets the fiction moves. Falconry, juggling and period crafts are not decorations on the feast but its thesis made visible: that the birthday belongs to a girl of the early fourteenth century, and that honouring her means furnishing her world, not merely naming her date. The fair also redistributes the cult's honours downward. The painters at their easels, the artisans at their benches, the costumed companies in the courtyards are Veronese practitioners exhibiting Veronese skills under the heroine's patronage, and the feast pays them in the oldest festival currency, a crowd. September is the one stratum of the year in which the city celebrates itself in the act of celebrating Juliet, its crafts and companies displayed as the birthday gifts, which is perhaps why the Club, the calendar's most devoted keeper, guards this feast as its own.

September also carries the calendar's literary ceremony. The Club's second prize, Scrivere per Amore, Writing for Love, which honours published writing on love rather than the private letters crowned in February, holds its award ceremony in the birthday season: in the documented year its eighth edition was presented on 10 September, the prize going to the writer Maurizio Maggiani. The pairing of the two prizes across the year is the calendar's neatest piece of design. February crowns the amateur, the unknown letter-writer whose words arrived in an envelope addressed to a fictional girl. September crowns the professional, the novelist whose book is weighed by a jury. Between the two ceremonies the whole economy of writing about love is gathered into the city's year, the private letter and the published novel each assigned its season, its square and its rite.

One small practice, recorded by an American magazine's account of a visit to the Casa, ties the September feast to the February one with a craftsman's economy. The entrance passage of the Casa di Giulietta, where visitors leave their notes and names, is fitted with replaceable panels, and the panels are renewed twice in the year: once on the sixteenth of September, Juliet's birthday, and once on Valentine's Day. The detail is a calendar at the scale of a wall. Even the graffiti of the shrine is administered seasonally, its two great clearances falling on the two hinges of the lovers' year, the feast of love in February and the heroine's birthday in September. A visitor who writes on the wall writes, without knowing it, inside an edition, on a surface whose term expires with the season. Nothing in Verona's observance is too small to be scheduled.

Why September, though, when the play says midsummer? The honest answer is that the calendar serves two texts and prefers the nearer one. Shakespeare's Juliet is a London construction, fourteen at Lammas Eve, dead within the week. Da Porto's Giulietta is an Italian citizen, eighteen years old, born on Saint Euphemia's day into the Verona of the Scaligeri, and it is her city that keeps the feast. The September birthday belongs to the same order of choice as the Casa and the tomb: Verona consistently commemorates the story in its Italian, local form, treating Shakespeare as the legend's most famous exporter rather than its proprietor. The festival year thus performs a quiet act of repatriation. The summer stage honours the English playwright who made the lovers immortal; the September feast honours the Italian novella that made them Veronese. Between the two, the calendar keeps both allegiances, and assigns each its season.

## Between Festivals: The Calendar's Ordinary Days

A calendar is defined as much by its gaps as by its feasts, and Verona's is unusual in having almost none. Beneath the festivals runs a daily practice that fills the intervals: the letters arrive at the Club in every month, the Casa opens its courtyard in every season, the tomb receives its visitors in the rain of November as dutifully as in the theatre crowds of July. The festivals punctuate a devotion that does not need them. They are best understood as the year's high days in a church whose ordinary offices never close, the feasts of a cult whose daily liturgy is the writing and answering of letters to a dead girl who never lived.

The standing shrines are the substrate's fixed points. [The Casa di Giulietta examined in this series](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/), with its assembled balcony and its bronze lady in the courtyard, receives the daily crowd that the festivals periodically multiply; the tomb across the old town receives the quieter procession that prefers its devotion melancholy. Between the two sites the city's ordinary day is threaded with minor rites that no organizer schedules: the touching of the statue's breast for luck, the note folded into the passage wall, the photograph taken upward at the balcony by a visitor who has come on no festival errand at all. Verona also marries couples at the Casa and in the fresco museum that holds the tomb, on dates chosen by the couples rather than the calendar, a private rite threaded through the public year at whatever season love and paperwork agree on. The calendar's editors did not invent this substrate and do not control it. Their achievement was to recognize it, and to build the year's peaks where the devotion already ran deepest: in February, when the whole Western calendar turns to love; in summer, when the city fills; in September, when the schools return and the light cools, and a birthday could be kept in the courtyards without the August crush. The festivals are less an imposition on the legend than a drainage system for it, channels dug where the water already flowed.

There is also a citizen's year inside the visitor's year, and the calendar reads differently from within. For the resident, the festivals arrive as infrastructure: streets dressed in hearts in the dark month, a race that takes the roads on a February Sunday and returns them by noon, forty-eight evenings of performance across the river through the summer, a medieval fair filling the courtyards on a September weekend. The resident need never buy a ticket or write a letter to live inside the lovers' calendar; the city schedules it around its citizens the way it schedules markets and processions of the saints. This is the deepest sense in which the adjudication of this article runs in Verona's favour. A tourist attraction addresses strangers. A calendar addresses neighbours, marking the shared year into seasons that everyone in the place can feel arriving. The lovers belong, by the end of this long municipal labour, not to the visitors who celebrate them but to the city that keeps their time.

The substrate also keeps the calendar's memory. Answered letters do not evaporate: the Club gathers them into the Archivio di Giulietta, an archive of the correspondence that visitors may consult by appointment, so that the year's devotion is filed as it accumulates. Every observance in the calendar produces paper, programmes, results sheets, concession notices, and the letters out-produce them all. A festival is usually remembered by its posters. Verona's year is remembered by an archive of other people's love letters, tens of thousands of private documents addressed to a heroine and preserved by her secretaries. The archive is the calendar's unconscious, the record of what the feasts are actually for, kept in boxes while the hearts are taken down and the stages struck. When the February lights come off the route and the summer stage goes dark, the archive remains open for consultation, the one institution of the lovers' year that is also a library.

The archive also corrects the calendar's public image in one respect worth recording. The festivals present the cult as spectacle, crowds and hearts and floodlights, but the archive preserves its private majority: the letters are written alone, at desks and kitchen tables across the world, by people most of whom attend no festival and appear in no photograph. The year's loudest months rest on the year's quietest practice, and the Club's boxes hold the evidence. A calendar of the lovers that counted only its public rites would mistake the cult for its celebrations; the archive, growing in the dark between editions, is the proof that the celebrations are the smaller half of the devotion they organize.

## The Calendar's Arithmetic: Editions, Gaps and Returns

A calendar makes one promise above all others: that its events recur. Verona's observances keep the promise imperfectly, and the imperfections are among the most instructive documents the calendar has produced. Edition numbers are claims, and the claims deserve the same grading as any other evidence in this series.

The summer season's count is the gentlest puzzle. A season founded in 1948 and held without interruption would have reached its seventy-fifth edition in 2022. The European Shakespeare Festivals Network, repeating the festival's own reckoning, counts the seventy-fourth in that year. One edition somewhere in the seventy-four is missing from the naive arithmetic: a year uncounted, a season suspended, or a founding counted from the second summer rather than the first. The sources consulted for this article do not say which, and the honest response is to print both numbers and let the gap stand. What the gap establishes, even unexplained, is that the season's continuity is a human continuity, maintained by administrators across wars of budget and changes of regime at the town hall, and not the mechanical ticking the word calendar suggests.

February's arithmetic is looser. The seventh edition fell in 2011, which counts back in a clean line to the founding of 2005. The fifteenth was billed in 2020, which does not: a straight count gives sixteen editions for sixteen years. An edition was skipped, or two were merged, or the count was restarted at some reorganization, and again the record in hand does not identify the missing year. The film festival's numbers wander further. Schermi d'Amore began in 1996 and called its 2019 revival the fifteenth edition, after a run that ended in 2010 and a silence of nine years. Fourteen editions across the fifteen seasons from 1996 to 2010 would itself require a gap, and the revival's numbering inherits the uncertainty. Even the half marathon's tidy sequence, thirteenth in 2020 and sixteenth in 2023, reaches back by implication to a first edition in 2008 that the federation's numbering asserts but that the race reports consulted here do not independently document.

None of this is scandal, and none of it is peculiar to Verona. Festivals everywhere count generously, restart silently and round in their own favour. The point for the calendar's student is methodological, and it strengthens rather than weakens the article's verdict. Recurrence in Verona is real, documented across decades by programmes, results sheets and concession notices. The precise numbering of the recurrences is promotional, and should be read at that grade. A tradition that can survive its own arithmetic, that can skip a year and return, sleep for nine years and be revived under its old name, is not a machine but an institution, kept by people who decide, edition by edition, that the observance is worth another year. The calendar's gaps are the signatures of its keepers.

## Invented Tradition or Living Ritual? The Critics on Verona's Year

Four accounts of what a festival is compete for Verona's calendar, and the calendar, examined closely, votes for each of them in a different season. The dispute is worth staging properly, because the verdict on the festivals depends on which theory of festivals survives contact with the evidence.

The cultural historian Eric Hobsbawm, introducing the 1983 collection The Invention of Tradition, defined his subject as a set of practices, governed by accepted rules and ritual in nature, which seek to inculcate values by repetition and imply continuity with a suitable historic past. Verona in Love fits the definition so exactly that it might have been drafted from the Comune's files: a fixed date, a fixed route, a repertoire of symbolic acts, a registered trademark, and a claimed continuity with a past that is, on inspection, a novella and a play. Hobsbawm's traditions are typically invented from above, by institutions that need loyalty or legitimacy, and the February festival is institutionally immaculate, founded by a municipality and a tourism consortium to fill hotel rooms in the low season. On the February evidence, Hobsbawm wins outright, and any account of the festival that reaches for words like organic or spontaneous is refuted by the trademark register.

Hobsbawm's own history adds a refinement that Verona confirms. His invented traditions cluster in periods of rapid social change, when the patterns that once organized loyalty weaken and institutions manufacture replacements. Verona's great inventions belong to the city's two ages of fastest change: the summer season to the reconstruction after 1945, when a shattered Italy was reimagining itself as a nation of culture, and the February brand to the age of mass tourism and the low-cost flight, when a provincial city had to manufacture reasons to visit in the rain. The calendar's strata are dated by Italy's transformations as much as by the lovers. A tradition invented in an age of rupture is not thereby false; it is a bridge built where the old road washed out, and Verona built two.

The critic of literary tourism Nicola J. Watson, in The Literary Tourist of 2006, describes a different engine. Literary places, in Watson's account, are made by texts and remade by readers who travel to them, and the literary tourist is no dupe: the pilgrim knows the balcony was assembled in the twentieth century and is moved anyway, because the emotion belongs to the reading, not to the masonry. Verona's calendar supports Watson wherever it is participatory. The letter-writers, the hundred painters, the runners and the couples on the staircase are not consuming a spectacle arranged for them; they are producing the observance, each cohort manufacturing the festival it attends. Watson's model explains what Hobsbawm's cannot: why an invented tradition, whose invention is public knowledge documented in the press, loses none of its power by exposure. The crowd is not deceived about the calendar's origins. The crowd is the calendar's origin, renewed at each edition.

The anthropologist Alessandro Falassi, whose 1987 collection Time Out of Time remains the standard morphology of festival, offers the third account: a festival is a periodically recurrent social occasion in which a community suspends ordinary time and enacts its values in rite, at once solemn and licensed. Falassi wins on function. Verona's observances do suspend the ordinary: couples kiss on command on a staircase, adults write letters to a fictional girl without embarrassment, a city closes its squares to commerce in art and fills them with hearts. Yet Verona also refutes Falassi in an interesting direction. His festival is time out of time, an interval carved from the year. Verona's achievement is the opposite: not an interval but an annexation, the legend spread across the whole year so that no month is wholly ordinary. The calendar does not interrupt Verona's time. It colonizes it.

The scholar Dennis Kennedy, writing on festival Shakespeare in Foreign Shakespeare in 1993, supplies the necessary scepticism for the summer stratum. Kennedy argued that the great festival audiences are largely tourists, that festival Shakespeare accordingly trades in spectacle, in the visual and the ceremonial, over the verbal subtleties its spectators may be unable to follow. The Teatro Romano is vulnerable to the charge: a Roman ruin at night is a spectacle before a word is spoken, and the season has always welcomed guest productions and star names. Yet the record limits Kennedy's victory. The Veronese season's spine, across seven decades, has been Italian-language production mounted by and for a municipal public, founded by a critic as an act of national homage; the tourist gaze was a beneficiary of the season, not its author. Kennedy explains the festival's temptations. He does not explain its persistence, which is civic before it is commercial.

One further witness belongs in the record before the verdict, because she judged Verona itself. Watson's later writings on Shakespeare's Verona return to the city as the limiting case of literary tourism: a place where the text commemorated is set, scene by scene, in streets that survive, so that the pilgrim's walk and the play's geography can be laid one over the other. The festivals exploit exactly that overlay. The February route walks the crowd from the Arena to the Casa along the play's own map; the September fair dresses the courtyards the fiction names; the summer stage performs the tragedy within sight of the river the lovers' city stands on. Hobsbawm's invented traditions usually float free of geography, fastening on any suitable past. Verona's are pinned to a map, and the pinning is a large part of their hold. An invented tradition that can be walked is harder to laugh off than one that can only be watched.

The adjudication, then, is seasonal. Hobsbawm describes February's manufacture and cannot describe its uptake. Watson describes the uptake and cannot describe the trademark. Falassi describes the experience and mistakes its duration. Kennedy describes the summer's risk and understates its roots. Verona's calendar is a palimpsest in which each theory governs one stratum: a brand invented from above in 2005, a devotion practised from below since the letters began, a theatre founded between the two in 1948 by a man of letters acting through a municipality. The festivals are an invented tradition that fastened onto living rituals, and the fastening is the whole story. Invention alone produces a promotion, forgotten in a decade. Ritual alone produces a custom, invisible beyond its parish. Verona welded the two together, and the weld is why the calendar holds.

## Verdict: The Calendar Against the Play, Season by Season

The standing charge against the festivals is that they are spectacle set against the play, a civic theme park trading on a tragedy it dare not stage. The charge is half true, and the calendar's own seasons mark exactly where the truth stops. February is guilty as charged, by design. The February movement stages the courtship and suppresses the catastrophe: its emblem is a heart, its rites are kisses, races, letters and a night in a museum bed, and the tomb appears nowhere in its iconography. The February Juliet has no age worth printing, no forced marriage, no corpse. What February celebrates in its public rites is the first two acts of the play, edited into a feast, and the editing is the same amputation the Valentine icon performs everywhere, documented in the companion study of the feast. The one qualification stands in the record and is kept here: the 2020 February programme staged the whole tragedy indoors at the Teatro Ristori, at the feast's margin, ticketed and seated, which shows the amputation to be a choice of the public programme rather than a limit of the festival's nerve.

Summer answers the charge. The Teatro Romano season stages the whole play when the play's turn comes, the feast, the killings, the tomb and the Prince's audit, in a civic theatre maintained at public expense for that purpose among others. The oldest stratum of the calendar is the one that refuses the amputation. September splits the difference in its own way: its Juliet is the novella's, older, Italian, born on a saint's day the play never mentions, and its fair stages the world of the story rather than the story, a city dressed as the fifteenth century through which the tragedy moves as one performance among jugglers and falconers. The calendar, read whole, does not replace the play. It distributes the play across the forms each season can carry: February takes the desire, summer takes the drama, September takes the world. A reader who attended Verona for a full year, and attended properly, would have encountered the entire tragedy by instalments, framed by the two civic acts the play itself prescribes, a feast at the beginning and a commemoration at the end.

That is the final ground for the verdict given at the outset. Verona's festivals are the strongest of the legend's afterlives because they alone commemorate in the play's own grammar. The play opens inside an old accustomed feast and closes with a city commissioning memorials; between those poles its action is a rush of days. The calendar repeats the grammar at civic scale: feasts at the year's hinges, memorials renewed by recurrence, and a story forever being hurried through the streets by letter, by runner, by actor. The statues the fathers promised stand in Verona, in bronze, and are touched threadbare by the faithful. The calendar is the promise kept in the better medium, not stone but time.

The verdict can be tested, and the calendar has already run the experiment. If recurrence were mere habit, an observance dropped for a decade would stay dropped, its slot in the year silently reabsorbed. Schermi d'Amore died in 2010 and the February days went on without it, apparently complete. Its revival in 2019, prepared by a summer preview and received as a restoration rather than a novelty, showed the slot had remained open in the city's memory for nine years, a place kept at the table. Institutions that are only promotions do not leave shaped absences. The film festival's empty chair, and its refilling, is the calendar's own evidence that its recurrences are load-bearing in the minds of its keepers, and that the year Verona built has the property the play's feast had: it is old accustomed, expected, and missed when it fails.

One last formulation gathers the whole argument. A calendar of festivals is a reading of the work it honours, conducted in public, at civic scale, and Verona's year can be read as an interpretation in three movements. February reads the play's first movement, the feast and the meeting, and reads it as the novella's Lent season and the saint's day jointly allow. The summer reads the whole text, in order, on a stage, restoring the deaths that the feast edits out and submitting the romance to the discipline of performance. September reads behind the text, into the novella and the reconstruction, honouring the heroine's Italian birth rather than her English fame. Each movement corrects the others. The feast's sentimentality is answered by the tragedy's corpses; the tragedy's finality is answered by the feast's promise that the meeting happens again next year; the brand's modernity is answered by the birthday's scholarship. No single season tells the truth about Romeo and Juliet. The year, taken whole, comes close, and the closeness is the achievement: the most complete popular interpretation of the play ever mounted is not a production but a calendar.

The festivals also settle, by their mere survival, the question of whether the legend is static. A static legend gets a museum. Verona's legend gets editions, and editions can be argued with, expanded, skipped, revived: a film festival dies for nine years and returns, a race joins the feast, a brand is licensed to new operators, a season adds dance and jazz and Greek tragedy to its Shakespeare. The calendar is the legend's mechanism for remaining negotiable, the form in which each generation of Veronese re-signs the contract its city made with two fictional lovers. On the evidence of the editions, the contract is in good order.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: In which months of the year does Verona hold its Romeo and Juliet events?

The lovers' events fall in three clusters: February, the summer from June to September, and September. February holds Verona in Love, with the letter prize ceremony, the half marathon and the film festival Schermi d'Amore gathered around Valentine's Day. The summer holds the Teatro Romano theatre season, founded in 1948, with the Arena opera festival running in parallel. September holds Juliet's appointed birthday, the medieval fair and the Scrivere per Amore ceremony. Between the clusters the observance never fully stops, since the letters to Juliet arrive in every month and the Casa and the Tomb open in every season. The festivals are the year's high days, set into a devotion that runs all year at a lower flame.

### Q: What route do the red hearts trace through the city during Verona in Love?

The hearts trace a designed itinerary between the festival's two anchor squares. In the documented 2011 programme, Piazza Bra and Piazza dei Signori were joined by a route illuminated with giant red hearts, passing themed shop windows, the Casa di Giulietta and Piazza delle Erbe. The walk is the festival's processional way: it leads the crowd from the Arena's square, through the shrine of the heroine, to the square where the heart-shaped market stands. A visitor following the lights performs the legend as a small pilgrimage, moving from the city's Roman monument to its fictional one and ending at the market laid out in the shape of a heart. The route turns sightseeing into a rite with a beginning, a middle and a destination.

### Q: What happens on the steps of Palazzo Barbieri during the festival?

Couples kiss there for one full minute, on cue, as the festival's collective vow. The rite was introduced as a novelty of the 2011 edition: the staircase of Palazzo Barbieri, the city hall in Piazza Bra beside the Arena, is lit in red, and every couple in the city is invited onto the steps to testify to love with a kiss held for sixty seconds. The gesture converts the crowd from spectators into performers. A kiss that is private by nature is made public, synchronized and witnessed, closer to a civic oath than to a romantic moment, and the staircase of the seat of government becomes, for one minute, the balcony of the entire city.

### Q: Why does the letter prize ceremony fall on Valentine's Day rather than in September?

The placement is a deliberate exchange between the Club and the city. The Premio Cara Giulietta crowns the finest love letter of the year, and its ceremony sits on Valentine's Day, inside Verona in Love, at the moment of the calendar's greatest crowd and brightest publicity. February borrows the letters' authenticity, since the correspondence is the oldest and least manufactured of all the Verona practices. The letters borrow February's audience, exhibited once a year before the whole city rather than only before the Club's devotees. September, the Club's own season, is reserved for the counterpart prize, Scrivere per Amore, which honours professional published writing. The amateur's letter and the novelist's book are thus crowned in different seasons, each in the company that suits it.

### Q: When is the Giulietta e Romeo Half Marathon run, and where does it finish?

The race is run on the Sunday nearest Valentine's Day, inside the February festival's span, and it finishes in Piazza Bra. Race reports record its winners in February 2012, and the Italian athletics federation's rolls number the thirteenth edition in 2020 and the sixteenth in 2023, a sequence that reaches back into the festival's first decade. The Comune's own concession notice for the 2025 February programme singles the half marathon out as the one consolidated appointment permitted to stand outside the licensed brand, an official acknowledgment of the race's independent standing. Thousands of runners cover twenty-one kilometres through the streets of the fiction, and the finish under the Arena gives the feast its most strenuous rite.

### Q: What is the Estate Teatrale Veronese?

The Estate Teatrale Veronese is Verona's municipal summer theatre season, held at the Roman theatre across the Adige and founded in 1948 as an act of homage to Shakespeare. At its core stands the Verona Shakespeare Festival, opened in 1948 with Romeo and Juliet in Salvatore Quasimodo's translation. Across the decades the season dilated beyond its charter: dance entered the programme in 1968, the Verona Jazz festival took up residence in 1985, and Greek tragedy arrived in collaboration with the National Institute of Ancient Drama of Syracuse. The Comune produces the season, in recent editions with the partner Arteven and the support of the Ministry of Culture and the Veneto Region, and the festival network counts its seventy-fourth edition in 2022.

### Q: Which translation of the play opened the Teatro Romano season in 1948?

The season opened with Salvatore Quasimodo's translation. The Sicilian poet, who received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1959, supplied the Italian text for Giulietta e Romeo, the production with which Renato Simoni launched the festival in 1948. Simoni directed the production himself, and his assistant was the young Giorgio Strehler, who had co-founded the Piccolo Teatro in Milan the year before. The choice of translator was a founding statement: the homage to the English playwright would be paid in the highest available Italian, by the nation's foremost lyric poet, in a Roman theatre, under municipal patronage. Prestige was the season's first programme, and the lovers' play was its vehicle.

### Q: How did the summer season grow beyond Shakespeare?

The season grew by annexation, adding art forms at dated intervals while keeping Shakespeare at its centre. Dance entered the programme in 1968 and became a fixture of the Roman theatre's summer. Jazz followed in 1985, when the theatre took on the Verona Jazz festival as a tenant of the same stones. Classical drama beyond Shakespeare arrived in later editions through the collaboration with the National Institute of Ancient Drama of Syracuse, placing Greek tragedy beside the Veronese one. The season also spread beyond its founding venue into other city spaces, among them the Corte Mercato Vecchio. Through every addition the Shakespeare festival retained the central position in the programme, and the lovers' play retained its ceremonial role at the anniversaries that mattered.

### Q: Which festival devoted to the lovers is the oldest in the calendar?

The summer Shakespeare season of 1948 is the oldest festival devoted to the lovers. The Arena opera festival is older, founded in 1913, but it belongs to Verdi and to the Italian repertory, not to Romeo and Juliet, and it stands in the calendar as a neighbour rather than a member. The letters to Juliet are older still as a practice, reaching back to the nineteen-thirties, but a practice is not a festival: the correspondence had no editions, no programme and no organizer until the Club gave it form. The Teatro Romano season was the first observance of the legend to be founded as an institution, with a date, a founder, a venue and a recurring programme, and every later layer of the calendar was built in its long shadow.

### Q: Where is the play itself performed during the September celebrations?

The play is performed in the Cortile del Tribunale, the courtyard of the old law courts, as part of the birthday programme. In the documented programme of the Club's September celebrations, the Compagnia del Sipario Medievale staged Romeo e Giulietta in that courtyard on the Saturday evening of the festival, while the Lavanteatro company presented a Juliet piece in Piazza Dante earlier in the week. The setting is pointed. The tragedy of the lovers is played inside a courtyard of the city's justice, surrounded by a fair of jugglers, falconers and artisans, during the feast of the heroine's appointed birthday. September does not merely commemorate the story at a distance; it performs the story, in costume, in the open air, in the midst of its own celebration.

### Q: When in September is the Scrivere per Amore prize awarded?

The prize is awarded in the birthday season, in the days around the sixteenth of September. In the documented year of its eighth edition, the ceremony of Scrivere per Amore, the Club di Giulietta's prize for published writing on love, was held on 10 September, and the prize went to the writer Maurizio Maggiani. The September placement completes the calendar's pairing of its two literary ceremonies. February crowns the private letter, the finest of the thousands addressed to Juliet by unknown writers. September crowns the public book, weighed by a jury and awarded to a named author. The two ceremonies divide the whole field of writing about love between them, and the division is made by the calendar, assigning each kind of writing its season.

### Q: Why does Verona keep Juliet's birthday in September when the play sets it at Lammas Eve?

Verona keeps the novella's date, not the play's. In the play, the Nurse fixes Juliet's fourteenth birthday at Lammas Eve, the end of July, in high summer. The September feast descends instead from Luigi da Porto's novella, the Italian source at the head of the story's line, in which Giulietta was born on the feast of Saint Euphemia, the sixteenth of September. The Veronese scholar Franco Viviani reconstructed the birth year, 1284, from the novella's chronology and the years of Bartolomeo della Scala's lordship. The choice is consistent with the whole Verona observance, which commemorates the story in its Italian and local form, treating Shakespeare as the legend's great exporter rather than its proprietor. The heroine of the September feast is da Porto's citizen, not the London playwright's invention.

### Q: What does the Arena's opera festival contribute to the lovers' year?

The Arena festival contributes scale, silhouette and a rival gravity. Founded in 1913 for the centenary of Verdi's birth, the opera season fills the Roman amphitheatre from June into September and moves sums through the city that dwarf the shrine trade. It is not a festival of the lovers, and its repertory belongs to the Italian canon. Yet the lovers' calendar is arranged in its field: the February kiss is staged on the city hall steps at the Arena's shoulder, the half marathon finishes in its square, and the summer Shakespeare season defines itself across the river as the Arena's counter-image, prose against opera, memory against spectacle. The lovers' year holds its shape partly by leaning, knowingly, on the giant next door.

### Q: Do the festivals stage the play's ending, or only its courtship?

The answer divides by season. February stages the courtship in its public rites and stops there: its emblem is a heart, its rites are kisses, letters, a race and a night in a museum bed, and the tomb appears nowhere in its imagery, though the 2020 programme did stage the whole play indoors at the Teatro Ristori, at the feast's ticketed margin. September stages the heroine's world, the fair and the city of the story, and performs the play itself in a courtyard as one element of the feast. The summer season stages the whole tragedy when the play's turn comes, feast, killings, tomb and the Prince's closing audit, in a municipal theatre kept for the purpose. Read across the year, the calendar distributes the play among its seasons, desire to February, the world to September, the drama entire to summer, and a devotee who kept the whole year would meet the complete story by instalments.

### Q: What is Schermi d'Amore, and where does it sit in the February days?

Schermi d'Amore is Verona's international festival of romantic cinema, and it sits inside the same February days as Verona in Love. It was founded in 1996, nine years before the municipal brand existed, as the heir of the city's international film week of 1969, and its founders justified a festival devoted to melodrama by appeal to Verona's Shakespearean myth of the lovers and to the Arena's operatic tradition. The festival ran until 2010, slept for nine years, and returned after a summer preview at the Teatro Romano in 2018, holding its fifteenth edition from 12 to 19 February 2019 at the Teatro Ristori, with a sixteenth following in 2020. The February cluster is thus older and deeper than its brand: an elder film institution re-entered the calendar that had been built during its sleep.

### Q: What happened at the Casa di Giulietta on Valentine's Day 2020?

A couple slept in Juliet's bed, as the prize of a letter. For Valentine's Day 2020 the Comune and the platform Airbnb turned the Casa di Giulietta into a dwelling for one night, the first time the house had served as a home since the nineteen-thirties. Entry to the competition required a letter to Juliet, and the winning couple dined inside the museum on a dinner cooked by Giancarlo Perbellini, a chef of two Michelin stars, and slept in Juliet's room in the bed preserved there from Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film. The mayor, Federico Sboarina, presented the night as the myth brought to life as never before. The stunt was a one-off, never repeated, and it remains the festival's purest expression: the fiction not watched, not read, but occupied.

### Q: Who runs the summer season at the Teatro Romano?

The Comune di Verona runs it. The season is a municipal production, made in recent editions in collaboration with the production body Arteven and with the support of the Ministry of Culture and the Veneto Region. Municipal ownership is the season's founding fact as well as its present arrangement: the city council set the observance up in 1948, under Renato Simoni's direction, to pay homage to Shakespeare and to display his bond with Verona. Administration has been the municipality's work across the decades, and the season's venues are the city's own, the Roman theatre first among them. The lovers' summer is therefore a public service before it is a tourist product, a distinction that explains both its prestige and its longevity across more than seventy editions.

### Q: What keeps the legend running between one festival and the next?

The letters keep it running. Correspondence addressed to Juliet arrives in Verona in every month of the year, is read and answered by the Club's volunteers, and accumulates in an archive that no festival interval interrupts. Around that daily office the standing shrines hold their own routine: the Casa opens its courtyard, the statue receives the touch of hands seeking luck, notes accumulate on the passage panels until their seasonal renewal, the tomb receives its quieter procession, and couples marry at the shrine on dates of their own choosing. The festivals are peaks built on this plateau. Remove every festival from the year and the devotion would continue at its daily rate; remove the devotion, and the festivals would be promotions in search of a public.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch festival calendar to argue that Verona commemorates by recurrence rather than by monument?

An essay should set the calendar's rows against the single bronze in the courtyard and count what each form of commemoration does. The monument is visited once and touched in passing; the calendar assembles the city at fixed seasons, assigns its citizens parts as letter-writers, runners, painters, actors and kissers, and renews the assignment at every edition. The calendar's strata then supply the argument's evidence: a theatre founded in 1948, a correspondence practised since the nineteen-thirties, a brand registered in 2005, each layer dated and each still running. The play itself supplies the warrant, since it opens inside an old accustomed feast and ends with memorials promised by a reconciled city. Verona, the essay can conclude, read its adopted tragedy correctly, and chose the feast over the statue as the truer instrument of memory.
