The dialectics of nature is the most argued about text in the Marxist tradition that its author never actually published, never finished, and never arranged for anyone to read. Friedrich Engels worked on the material across roughly a decade, filled notebooks and loose sheets with drafts, plans, fragments, and one nearly finished essay, and then set the whole bundle aside when Karl Marx died and the editing of Capital took precedence over everything else. The bundle stayed in a drawer through Engels's own death in 1895 and waited three more decades before editors in Moscow assembled it into something that looked like a book. That sequence of facts, none of them controversial among scholars who have handled the manuscripts, changes everything about how the text should be read, because most of the famous quarrel about Engels and nature has been conducted as though a finished treatise existed, with chapters in an intended order and conclusions its author had weighed. No such treatise exists. What exists is a workshop, photographed mid-task, with tools laid out, some blades sharpened and some still rough, and the photograph was captioned by other people.

![Engels and the dialectics of nature, the unfinished manuscript, its defenders and its critics - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-116.webp)

This guide treats the manuscript status of the work as the master key, because it is. A reader who knows that the order of the printed text is an editorial construction reads differently from a reader who assumes Engels placed a fragment on electricity beside a fragment on Darwin for reasons of his own. A reader who knows that Marx read the published companion volume, Anti-Duhring, in manuscript and contributed a chapter to it, weighs the charge that Engels betrayed Marx's method differently from a reader who has only heard the charge repeated. And a reader who knows which of Engels's scientific examples survived the following century and which collapsed can separate the durable insight from the period detail without either worshipping the text or sneering at it. The pages that follow work through the composition and publication history, the structure of the material fragment by fragment, the three laws and where Engels found them, the scientific reading he did and what became of it, the labour essay that is the bundle's finished jewel, the published companion through which his philosophy actually reached the world, the critical tradition that begins with a footnote written in 1923, the defence mounted by scientists and by manuscript scholarship, the codification that turned fragments into state doctrine, and the Lysenko catastrophe that stained the whole inheritance. The verdict at the end is a reading instruction rather than a loyalty declaration, which is the only kind of verdict an unfinished text deserves.

## A book that was never a book: what the dialectics of nature actually is

Begin with the physical object. When Engels died, his literary estate contained four bundles of material connected with a projected work on dialectics and natural science, alongside the far larger mass of manuscripts belonging to Capital and to his correspondence. The bundles held material of wildly uneven finish. Some pieces were polished essays that needed only a fair copy. Some were plans, several plans, drawn up at different moments and never reconciled with one another. Some were reading notes, excerpts from scientific works with Engels's comments interleaved. Some were single paragraphs, aphorisms, and reminders to himself. Two substantial essays stood near completion: a historical introduction surveying the development of natural science since the Renaissance, and the fragment on the part played by labour in the transition from ape to man, written in 1876 and later famous far beyond the bundle that contained it. Around these larger pieces lay dozens of shorter fragments on heat, electricity, magnetism, chemistry, biology, mathematics, and the mechanical theory of nature that Engels believed the science of his day was outgrowing.

No instruction accompanied the bundles. Engels left no preface, no final table of contents, no letter saying which plan governed the rest. He had mentioned the project to correspondents over the years, sometimes hopefully and sometimes with the ruefulness of a man who knew his time was being consumed by other duties, and after Marx's death in 1883 the duties became absolute. The second and third volumes of Capital existed as chaotic manuscripts that only Engels could decipher, and he regarded their publication as a debt owed to his dead friend that outranked any work of his own. The dialectics material was not repudiated. It was postponed, repeatedly, until postponement became permanent. When scholars speak of Engels abandoning the project, the word misleads if it suggests a judgement against the material. He abandoned it the way a man abandons a house he is building when a neighbour's house catches fire.

The first consequence for readers is that every printed edition of Dialectics of Nature embodies decisions Engels never made. Which fragments belong together, which plan to privilege, whether a reading note counts as part of the work or as preparation for it, whether the labour essay closes the argument or stands apart from it: all of this was settled by editors, first in Moscow in the 1920s and then in the collected works projects that followed. Editorial work of this kind is honourable and necessary, and the Moscow editors preserved material that might otherwise have been lost. But an edited construction is evidence of a different kind from an authored book. It shows what a thinker was working on, what he read, what problems gripped him, and how his mind moved among them. It cannot show, with the same confidence, what he finally concluded, because the concluding was never done. Arguments built on the sequence of the printed text, on transitions between chapters, or on the relative weight of topics within it are arguments built on scaffolding erected by other hands.

The second consequence concerns the target of the long polemic against Engels. The dominant Western Marxist verdict, that Engels distorted Marx by extending dialectics from society to nature, draws its most quotable evidence from these bundles. Passages that in a finished work would have been qualified, relocated, or deleted survive in the manuscripts in their raw workshop state, and critics have understandably treated the printed volume as a doctrine delivered. The guide will give that critical tradition its full strength later, because it deserves it. But the asymmetry should be registered at the start and never forgotten. The philosophy Engels actually published in his lifetime, in Anti-Duhring in 1878 and in the popular extract drawn from it, was read by Marx before publication and carries a chapter Marx wrote himself. The philosophy Engels never published waited forty years for editors. A trial that prosecutes the drawer and acquits the bookshop has its evidence arranged in a strange order, and naming that strangeness is the work of the namable claim this article develops under the heading of the unfinished-target problem.

## The bundles on the desk: composition across a decade

Engels came to the natural sciences with a preparation no other figure in the early socialist movement could match. His youthful studies in Berlin, where he attended lectures while serving as a military volunteer and sparred with the Young Hegelians in print, gave him Hegel early and deeply. His years in Manchester placed him inside the industrial process he would analyse, and his reading ran constantly to the sciences that industry was transforming: chemistry above all, then physics, geology, and the physiology of his day. By the time he and Marx settled into their mature collaboration, the division of labour between them left the natural sciences largely in Engels's province. Marx read science seriously, especially mathematics, chemistry, and geology in his later years, but the systematic attempt to think the sciences together, and to ask what philosophy their results implied, was Engels's assignment, accepted and pursued with appetite.

The immediate provocations of the 1870s are worth naming, because the manuscripts answer a specific intellectual situation rather than a timeless riddle. German intellectual life in that decade rang with controversies over materialism and the limits of science. Emil du Bois-Reymond had delivered his famous address on the limits of natural knowledge in 1872, declaring permanent ignorance on certain questions and feeding both clerical satisfaction and scientific humility of a theatrical kind. Eugen Duhring was constructing his rival system, a self-proclaimed philosophy of reality that mixed materialist phrases with moralising economics and won converts inside the German socialist movement itself. Popular materialists such as Ludwig Buchner had spread a crude mechanical doctrine in bestselling books, identifying thought with brain secretion in formulations that invited ridicule and associated materialism with vulgarity. Engels watched a movement he had helped build absorb confusions from all three directions, and his projected book was conceived as an intervention: a demonstration that the sciences themselves, read carefully, disclosed a picture of nature as interconnected process, and that this picture needed neither Duhring's system building nor Buchner's crudity nor du Bois-Reymond's staged despair.

His method of work was characteristic. He read enormously and excerpted as he read, filling notebooks with summaries of treatises on thermodynamics, organic chemistry, cell theory, electromagnetism, and the new evolutionary biology. He corresponded with scientifically trained friends, most usefully Carl Schorlemmer, the chemist who held a chair at Manchester and served as Engels's private tutor in the discipline's finer points. He drafted plans. A plan from 1878 sketches a book moving from the historical development of science through the individual sciences in ascending order of complexity, from mechanics through physics and chemistry to biology, with the dialectical laws emerging from the material rather than preceding it. Other plans rearrange the same elements. The plurality of plans matters for the editorial question, because an editor choosing among them is not recovering an intention but manufacturing one, and the recent manuscript scholarship discussed later in this guide has shown how consequential that manufacturing became.

The writing proceeded in bursts between other obligations. The years 1875 and 1876 were the most productive, producing drafts of the introduction and the labour essay alongside Anti-Duhring, which consumed the following two years and may be understood as the dialectics project's public outwork: the polemic against Duhring forced Engels to state his positive philosophy in print, in a form aimed at movement readers, while the larger scientific treatise remained in the drawer. After Anti-Duhring came the popular extract, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, then the death of Marx in 1883 and the immense labour of Capital volumes two and three, published in 1885 and 1894. Engels's letters in his final decade still mention returning to the natural science material, and a few fragments carry late dates, but the return never happened. He died in August 1895, and the bundles passed with his papers into the keeping of the German party's executors, where they slept through the collapse of empires and the founding of an institute in Moscow dedicated to exactly such papers.

### What did Engels actually set out to do?

Engels set out to show that the natural sciences, read in their results, disclosed nature as a web of processes and transitions rather than a warehouse of fixed things, and that the habits of thought adequate to such a nature were the dialectical habits Hegel had developed for history and logic, now to be learned from the sciences themselves.

Hold that answer against the caricature. Engels did not propose to deduce nature from a philosophy, sitting in an armchair and informing physicists what their experiments must find. His notebooks are full of the opposite movement: excerpt, summary, hesitation, questions addressed to Schorlemmer, admissions that a point must wait until he understood the mathematics. The projected book's rhetoric, where drafts survive, presents the laws as findings. Again and again the fragments insist that dialectics is not a lever for constructing a world system but a summary of how the investigated world turns out to behave. Whether the summary succeeds is a fair question, and scientists and philosophers have answered it both ways for a century. But the intention matters for fairness, because the standard accusation describes a thinker imposing a scheme on nature, while the manuscripts show a reader attempting, sometimes clumsily and sometimes brilliantly, to extract a scheme from nature as the sciences were reporting it. The gap between those two portraits is one measure of how much of the polemic has been aimed at the printed construction rather than at the working man.

There is a further element of intention that the plans reveal and the printed editions flatten. Engels conceived the book as a polemic on two fronts at once. Against the metaphysical habit, his word for the style of thought that treats categories as rigid and opposites as absolute, he marshalled the sciences' own discoveries of transitions: species shading into species, chemical elements combining and transforming, energy passing from form to form without loss of quantity. Against the vulgar materialists, he insisted that a nature of processes could not be captured by reducing every level to mechanics, and that thought, life, and society were real novelties in the world's history, not secretions to be embarrassed about. The two fronts explain the text's double reputation. Readers who meet only the first front find a prophet of complexity and interconnection. Readers who meet only the second find a systematiser policing the boundaries of materialism. The manuscripts, read whole, show a thinker fighting both battles with the same weapon, and the weapon's name was dialectics.

## How loose papers became a book: the Moscow construction

The bundles reached print through the Marx-Engels Institute in Moscow, the scholarly establishment founded under David Riazanov to collect, edit, and publish the literary remains of the two founders. The institute's agents had gathered copies and originals of papers scattered by war and exile, and among the acquisitions were Engels's natural science manuscripts, obtained from the German party's archive holdings. In 1925 the institute published Dialektik der Natur in a bilingual scholarly presentation, German text with Russian translation, inside its archival series. Four decades separated the publication from its author's death, and three decades had passed since the material was last touched by its author's hand. The men and women who prepared the edition faced bundles without an authorial order and did what editors must: they arranged. Fragments were grouped under headings, the more finished essays were placed to the front of their topics, the plans were printed as evidence of intention, and a continuous-looking volume emerged from discontinuous remains.

Riazanov's institute worked to standards that commanded respect across the scholarly world, and its great projected complete edition, the first Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe, set new benchmarks for editorial care before Stalinist terror destroyed the institute's independence and Riazanov himself. The Dialectics of Nature edition belongs to that heroic phase of textual scholarship, and nothing in this guide should be read as an accusation against its editors. The point is structural, not personal. An arrangement made in 1925 by scholars working under the pressures of a revolutionary state that already treated Engels as a founding authority cannot transmit the hesitations of a private workshop unchanged. The volume entered the world wearing the uniform of a classic, bound and titled and prefaced, and generations of readers, friendly and hostile alike, received it as a classic is received: as a settled statement of doctrine. The fragments about a creator being superfluous, about heat death, about the eternity of matter in motion, were read as theses Engels had published to the world, rather than as notebook entries written for an audience of one, in a decade when the science itself was still molten.

Later editions compounded the effect. The text passed into the Russian collected works and from there into the great German and English collected works projects of the postwar decades, where it sits in the English edition as a single volume among fifty, its editorial apparatus honest about the manuscript status but its placement implying parity with books Engels finished and published. The scholarly remedy arrived only with the second complete edition, the MEGA2 project, whose presentation of the manuscripts in their bundle structure lets a researcher see what Engels grouped, what he separated, and where the joins in the printed text were made by other hands. For most of the century during which the Engels controversy burned hottest, that remedy was unavailable, and polemicists on both sides quoted the Moscow arrangement as though it were a manuscript. The companion article in this series on [the editorial apparatus of the complete editions](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/) explains how that apparatus works and why it changes what citation means for texts of this kind.

Publication history also governs the text's strange double life in the two halves of the Marxist world. In the Soviet sphere, the 1925 volume was received into a canon under construction, and its fragments supplied proof texts for the doctrine that came to be called dialectical materialism, a term Engels never used as the name of his philosophy and which entered circulation through Joseph Dietzgen and Georgi Plekhanov. In the Western sphere, the volume arrived as evidence in a trial that had already begun: the question whether the Marxism of the Communist International, with its laws of nature and history, represented Marx's method or an Engelsian deformation of it. The same printed object thus served as scripture in one courtroom and as the prosecution's exhibit in another, and in both courtrooms its status as an edited construction of unfinished papers was the fact least often entered into evidence. Restoring that fact to the centre of the record is not a trick for acquitting Engels. Several of his positions fail on their merits, as the sections on the scientific examples will show in detail. Restoring it is a condition of trying the right case.

### Why does the printed order of the text mislead its readers?

The printed order misleads because editors, not Engels, decided which fragment follows which, so transitions that look like steps in an argument are joins made in Moscow decades after his death, and readers who reason from the sequence, or from the space a topic occupies in the volume, are reasoning from an arrangement its author never saw.

Consider what follows from that single caution. A critic who argues that Engels subordinated biology to mechanics because mechanical fragments stand early in the volume is describing the filing system of 1925. A defender who finds a rising arc from matter in motion to human labour is admiring the same filing system from the other side. Both should be sent back to the bundles, to the dated fragments and the successive plans, where the genuine questions live: what did Engels write when, what had he just been reading, which plan was he following in that season, and what did he cross out. The manuscript scholarship of the last generation, culminating in the study by Kaan Kangal published in 2020 under the title Friedrich Engels and the Dialectics of Nature, has done exactly this work, reconstructing the composition bundle by bundle and showing that the standard picture of the project, a systematic treatise applying three laws to the sciences one by one, owes more to the edition than to the papers. Kangal's Engels is a thinker whose plans kept shifting because his reading kept teaching him things, and whose project was closer to a critical survey of the sciences' philosophical confusions than to a catechism. Readers need not accept every element of that reconstruction to profit from its method, which is simply to cite the manuscripts as manuscripts.

## The three laws and where Engels found them

No element of the bundle has travelled further or attracted more derision than the three laws of dialectics. Engels named them in a fragment that the editors placed prominently, and the naming did the damage and the work at once. The laws, as he stated them, are the transformation of quantity into quality and the reverse, the interpenetration or mutual penetration of opposites, and the negation of the negation. He credited Hegel with having formulated them for thought and the world together, and his own claim was that they were not inventions to be applied to nature but abstractions drawn from nature and history alike, valid because the investigated material kept exhibiting them. In Anti-Duhring, where the scheme met the public, the examples were chosen for vividness: water passing to steam and ice at definite temperatures as quantity turning into quality, the barley seed negated by the plant and the plant negated in turn by the many seeds of its death as the negation of the negation, magnetic poles and chemical combination as opposites interpenetrating. Generations of textbook writers converted these illustrations into a universal key, and generations of critics converted the textbooks into a refutation of Engels.

Read in the manuscripts rather than in the textbooks, the laws sit differently. They appear as one strand among several in the bundles, prominent in some plans and subordinate in others, and the fragments around them are occupied as much with criticising the mechanical worldview, expounding the conservation and transformation of energy, and reviewing the history of science as with legislating for nature. The laws function in the drafts less as premises from which Engels deduces than as headings under which he collects: here a phase transition, there a chemical synthesis, there a mathematical puzzle about infinity, each filed as an instance of a pattern he believed the sciences themselves were stumbling upon. That is why the same writer who announced the laws could also insist, repeatedly, that dialectics amounts to little more than the science of interconnections, a formulation far looser than any threefold code. The codifiers of the next century kept the code and dropped the looseness. What they built from it belongs to the history of dialectical materialism as a doctrine, mapped in the companion guide to [dialectical materialism as a complete system](/2012/07/01/dialectical-materialism-complete-guide/), and should not be billed to the account of the manuscripts without a hearing.

The honest evaluation of the laws has to proceed law by law and register by register. As a contribution to physics or chemistry, the laws add nothing a scientist can use: no experiment was ever designed from the negation of the negation, and the examples Engels collected were discovered by researchers who had never heard of his scheme. As a philosophical gloss on the sciences, the laws are uneven. The quantity and quality formula captures something real about thresholds and phase transitions, though modern treatments of emergence and critical phenomena do the work with a precision the formula cannot approach. The interpenetration of opposites ranges from the genuinely suggestive, in its insistence that polarities in nature are often internally related rather than externally juxtaposed, to the merely verbal, when any two contrasted terms are declared opposites and their unity celebrated. The negation of the negation is the weakest of the three as a law of nature, surviving chiefly as a description of certain developmental sequences in biology and history where later stages preserve and cancel earlier ones, and failing wherever it is promoted into a universal rhythm. As rhetoric in the movement's education, finally, the laws had a career their author neither foresaw nor controlled, and the section on codification below takes up that career, including its darkest chapter.

A distinction Engels himself drew deserves rescue from the wreckage, because it disciplines everything he wrote on science. He separated the dialectics found in nature, which he called objective, from the dialectical thinking of human beings, which he called subjective, and treated the second as a reflection of the first rather than its source. Critics from Lukacs onward seized on the separation as the original sin, the moment where a method born in human practice was projected onto a mindless world. Defenders reply that the separation is ordinary realism: motions and transitions occur whether or not anyone thinks them, and a method that tracks them is answerable to them. The quarrel is genuine philosophy and cannot be dissolved by editorial facts. What the editorial facts do dissolve is the cruder charge that Engels spun the laws out of Hegel's Logic and imposed them on an unwilling science. The notebooks refute that biography of the ideas. Whatever the laws are worth, they were quarried from treatises on thermodynamics and chemistry by a reader who took notes like a student, not pronounced from a lectern by a man who took himself for nature's legislator.

## The scientific reading behind the fragments

To judge the examples, a reader first needs the library. Engels educated himself in the science of the middle nineteenth century with a thoroughness that still startles specialists who open the notebooks. Three great unifications dominated that science, and he built his philosophical hopes on all three. The cell theory, established by Schleiden and Schwann in the late 1830s, had shown every living organism to be composed of the same elementary unit, developing and differentiating according to intelligible patterns, and Engels seized on the cell as proof that nature's kingdoms were connected by real transitions rather than walled off by essences. The conservation and transformation of energy, established in the 1840s by Mayer, Joule, and Helmholtz among others, had shown heat, motion, electricity, magnetism, and chemical affinity to be convertible forms of a single indestructible something, and Engels took this as the scientific death warrant of the old metaphysics of separate forces and fixed domains. Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection, published in 1859, had shown species themselves to be historical products, shading into ancestors and descendants, and Engels extended to the organic world the same lesson: boundaries in nature are real but they are drawn by processes, and processes cross them.

Around these three pillars clustered his other reading. In chemistry he followed the revolution of atomic weights and the periodic ordering of the elements, corresponded with Schorlemmer about the hydrocarbons, and delighted in the prediction and discovery of new elements as evidence that chemical kinds were a system, not a chaos. In physics he wrestled with the mechanical theory of heat, the kinetic picture of gases, and the puzzles of electricity, where his notes show him struggling honourably with material at the edge of the period's understanding. In mathematics he pondered infinity, the calculus, and the status of imaginary quantities, with more enthusiasm than rigour. In astronomy he absorbed the nebular hypothesis of Kant and Laplace, the Earth's slow cooling, and the tidal friction that lengthens the day, and he fought a private war against the heat death conclusion that Clausius had drawn from the second law of thermodynamics. The war against heat death produced some of his most characteristic and least successful pages, examined in the audit of examples below. Throughout, the pattern of the reading is what matters for the guide's purpose. Engels read the strongest science available to him, read it in bulk, and read it for its philosophical yield, asking at every stage what picture of nature the results compelled. His errors, which were real, were the errors of an exceptionally well informed amateur working before relativity, quanta, genetics, and the expansion of the universe rearranged every science he knew.

His historical introduction, the most finished of the longer drafts, shows the same reading turned toward the past. It surveys the Renaissance liberation of inquiry, the age of collection and classification that he associated with a metaphysical style of thought, and the breaches torn in that style by geology, by the nebular hypothesis, by cell theory, energy, and evolution. The survey is Whiggish in places and gets individual scientists wrong in detail, but its governing idea, that styles of thought in science have a history connected to what the sciences have actually found, anticipated a discipline that barely existed in his lifetime. Historians of science who have no sympathy for the three laws still mine the introduction for its early grasp that the categories of natural knowledge are themselves historical products. That grasp is the respectable core of the whole project, and it survives the failure of particular examples the way a good question survives bad answers.

### What happened to Engels's scientific examples?

The examples divide by fate. Energy transformation, the cell, and Darwinian descent remain foundations of science, and the labour essay's sequence, upright posture freeing the hand before the brain enlarged, fits the fossil record well. Other examples collapsed: life defined through protein, the denial of any heat death, and several chemical and mathematical illustrations belong to their century alone.

That division, stated plainly, is the answer a fair reader carries into the detailed audit later in this guide. Engels bet on the three strongest unifications of his age and won those bets. He lost where he pushed beyond the evidence into cosmology, where he defined life by the chemistry his century privileged, and where his philosophical needs, particularly the need for an eternal recycling universe that required no creator and no final state, steered his reading of thermodynamics. The mixture is exactly what one would predict for a brilliant autodidact working at the frontier of a science in motion, and it is neither the record of a prophet nor the record of a charlatan. Polemic has usually demanded one of those two portraits, because polemic is easier than bookkeeping. The bookkeeping is done in full below, example by example, with the survivors and the casualties each given their evidence.

One methodological habit of Engels deserves separate praise before the audit begins, because it explains the survivors. He distinguished, in fragment after fragment, between the results of science and the philosophical costumes in which scientists dressed them. A physicist's measurements could be impeccable while his pronouncements about matter, force, or the limits of knowledge were muddled, derivative, or borrowed from theology in disguise. Engels felt licensed, indeed obliged, to accept the measurements and refuse the costume. The licence is the same one every philosophy of science since has claimed, and he exercised it with a polemical vigour that made enemies among the costume makers. Du Bois-Reymond's declaration of permanent ignorance on the nature of matter and motion and on the origin of sensation drew Engels's particular scorn, not because the questions were easy but because declaring them eternally unanswerable converted a frontier into a fence, and fences around inquiry served interests Engels named without flinching. Readers who find the three laws empty may still find this habit, the refusal to let a laboratory result be confiscated by a worldview, worth the price of the volume.

## The labour essay: the bundle's finished jewel

Among the bundles lay one piece that Engels brought close to completion and that later generations published, translated, and argued over as a freestanding work: the fragment usually titled The Part Played by Labour in the Transition from Ape to Man, drafted in 1876 as part of the introduction to a larger projected study. Engels never published it. It appeared in print in 1896, the year after his death, in the journal Die Neue Zeit, and its afterlife has been stranger than that of any other dozen pages he wrote. For decades it served as the authorised Marxist account of human origins. Anthropologists mostly ignored it. Then, in the second half of the twentieth century, as the fossil record filled in, readers began to notice that its central sequence had aged remarkably well, and scientists of the standing of Stephen Jay Gould treated it with a respect they granted few nineteenth century speculations on human evolution.

The argument runs as follows. Our ancestors were a species of ape living in trees, whose descent to the ground and adoption of an upright gait freed the hand from locomotion. The free hand took up labour: first the fashioning of tools, then cooperative work of increasing complexity. Labour, in Engels's telling, did not merely use the hand but made it, refining its sensitivity and strength across generations, and the hand's development reacted back upon the whole organism. Cooperative labour created the need for communication, and the larynx developed toward speech. Labour and speech together were the stimuli under which the brain enlarged and sharpened, and with the brain came the senses it coordinated, the diet it made possible through fire and cooking and eventually agriculture, and the society that labour had required from the start. His summary sentence declares labour the prime basic condition for all human existence, to the point that in a sense labour created man himself. The sentence is quoted wherever the essay is discussed, usually without the careful scaffolding of posture, hand, cooperation, speech, and brain that the essay builds beneath it.

Grade the argument against the record its author could not see. The sequence is the triumph. Engels placed upright walking and the free hand before the enlargement of the brain, at a time when the expectation, shared by much of the scientific world, was that a large brain led the process and the rest followed. The fossil record of the twentieth century reversed the expectation in Engels's favour: the australopithecines walked upright with brains little larger than a chimpanzee's, and substantial brain expansion came later, in the lineage of Homo, alongside stone tools whose earliest known examples now reach back beyond three million years. His insistence that tool making and cooperative labour were not decorations on human evolution but engines of it placed him closer to the eventual consensus than most of his contemporaries, and his stress on the hand, the organ Darwin had also noticed, reads like a draft of themes that palaeoanthropology would later develop with evidence he never handled. Gould's admiration fastened on exactly this: an amateur in 1876 had sketched the feedback structure, behaviour changing anatomy changing behaviour, that the mature science would confirm.

The mechanism is the casualty, and honesty requires its name. Engels wrote before the rediscovery of Mendel's work, before the gene, before the modern synthesis welded selection to genetics. His account of how labour's effects accumulated across generations leans on the inheritance of acquired characteristics, the use and disuse inheritance that Darwin himself also entertained but that twentieth century biology discarded as a general mechanism. The hand was not refined because each generation's practice was somehow inscribed into the germ line. It was refined because variation, selection, and the new niches that behaviour opened did the work across immense time. Replace the mechanism with selection operating on a labouring, tool using, communicating animal, and the essay's architecture stands; keep the mechanism, and the essay fails with the rest of nineteenth century Lamarckianism. A related weakness touches his treatment of speech and society, where the essay moves swiftly and speculatively, and his picture of ape society owes more to the travellers' tales available to him than to the primatology that would come. The balanced verdict, which the audit section formalises, is that the labour essay is a brilliant conjecture with a superseded engine, and that Engels's contemporaries who dismissed it whole were worse scientists, on this question, than the philosopher they dismissed.

Notice, too, what the essay implies for the larger quarrel. Its method is not the imposition of three laws on the apes. It is a natural historical reconstruction, attentive to anatomy, sequence, and feedback, written by a man thinking with Darwin rather than over him. Whatever may be said of the cosmological fragments, the labour essay shows Engels practising the dialectics of nature as patient attention to transitions in the real world, and it is no accident that this, his most empirical piece, is the one the following century treated most kindly. Teachers choosing a single fragment to put before a class choose this one, and the persona sections below return to the choice.

## Anti-Duhring: the philosophy Engels actually published

If the bundles are the workshop, Anti-Duhring is the shopfront, and the difference between them is the difference between a man's notes and his testimony. Eugen Duhring, a blind lecturer at Berlin whose system building had won a following among German socialists, published doctrines on philosophy, economics, and socialism that Engels judged both wrong and dangerous, dangerous because the young party was absorbing them uncritically. The reply, serialised in the party paper Vorwarts between 1877 and 1878 and issued as a book in 1878 under the mocking title Herr Eugen Duhring's Revolution in Science, marched through Duhring's system field by field and stated, in the gaps of the demolition, the positive views of its author on philosophy, political economy, and socialism. It became, by an accident of polemic, the first systematic presentation of the Marxist worldview between two covers, and for decades it was the book through which the movement's members actually learned what they thought. Lenin's generation cut its teeth on it. The extract of its third part, published separately as Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, became perhaps the most widely read socialist pamphlet of the age.

The philosophy chapters state in public everything the drawer manuscripts were exploring in private. Nature is a connected whole in process; its motions exhibit the transformation of quantity into quality, the interplay of opposites, and developmental sequences Engels glossed as the negation of the negation; human thought, arising from nature and society, can know the world because practice, experiment, and industry test our conceptions against it. The barley seed, the steam point of water, the calculus, the chemical series: the examples that would fill the textbooks all appear here first, in a published book, in 1878, five years before Marx's death. This chronology is the rock on which the simplest form of the distortion thesis breaks. The extension of dialectics beyond society, the laws, the materialism of nature: none of it was a posthumous invention smuggled into Marxism by a grieving executor in the 1880s and 1890s. It was in print, in the movement's own paper, while Marx lived a short train ride away, read the socialist press as a matter of daily habit, and corresponded with the author constantly.

Marx's involvement goes further than silent proximity, and the details matter enough to state them exactly. Engels, by his own account in the preface, read the whole manuscript aloud to Marx before publication, a practice the two men used for work of consequence. Marx did not merely listen. He contributed a chapter to the book's economic part, the section of the historical sketch of political economy dealing with the theory of value's prehistory, which Engels printed as part of his own text and acknowledged in correspondence. The contributed chapter is not a marginal squib; it sits inside the book's constructive core, treating the economists whose doctrines the positive theory inherited. Add the surrounding record: Marx and Engels discussed the natural science material in letters across the 1870s, Marx sent Engels his own mathematical and scientific enthusiasms for comment, and no letter, note, or reported utterance from Marx registers dissent from the philosophical direction of Anti-Duhring. Silence from a correspondent of Marx's ferocity, on a book he helped write, serialised in a paper he read, is evidence of a kind that historians in other fields accept without blinking.

### What does Marx's contributed chapter actually prove?

It proves that Marx knew the philosophy Anti-Duhring published, heard the whole manuscript, and placed his own writing inside the book without recorded objection, which destroys the claim that the extension of dialectics to nature was a posthumous surprise. It does not prove Marx would have endorsed every notebook fragment Engels left unpublished in his drawer.

That calibration, between what the evidence destroys and what it leaves standing, is the mature form of the guide's central claim. The unfinished-target problem, named and developed in its own section below, holds that the indictment of Engels rests principally on the unpublished bundles, while the philosophy Engels did publish passed under Marx's eye and partly through Marx's pen. A critic may still argue that Marx, had he lived to read the cosmological fragments on heat death or the definition of life, would have objected, and the argument is not absurd. But it is an argument from a counterfactual, and it must compete with the documented behaviour of the real Marx, who read the dialectics of nature in its published form and reached for his pen to add a chapter rather than to protest. Burdens of proof matter in scholarship as in law. After Anti-Duhring, the burden sits with the prosecution.

The companion question, whether Marx's own writings contain a dialectics of nature independent of Engels, can be answered briefly and honestly. Marx's published and unpublished work applies dialectical analysis overwhelmingly to society, history, and the critique of political economy, and his scattered remarks on natural science, in letters and in the footnotes of Capital, show sympathy for Darwin and a materialist respect for the sciences without constructing a philosophy of nature of his own. Scholars who stress the difference between the partners are right about the division of labour and wrong only when they convert a division of labour into a doctrinal border, with Marx patrolling one side and Engels smuggling goods across it. The partnership, documented across forty years of letters that this series treats in its guide to [the wider authorship question between Marx and Engels](/2013/06/01/marx-engels-authorship-question/), was a working collaboration in which each man read, corrected, and bankrolled the other. Borders inside it should be drawn where the evidence draws them, and on natural philosophy the evidence draws a workshop with two names on the door and one man's handwriting on most of the pages. For the thinker behind that handwriting, his formation, his journalism, his military writings, and his role as the movement's organiser, the series provides a full [intellectual profile of Friedrich Engels](/2013/05/15/friedrich-engels-intellectual-profile/), to which this text guide is the companion.

## A footnote that founded a tradition: Lukacs and the critics

The case against Engels acquired its classic form not from an enemy of Marxism but from one of its most brilliant defenders. Georg Lukacs published History and Class Consciousness in 1923, a collection of essays that rebuilt Marxist philosophy around the categories of reification and totality and founded what later scholars named Western Marxism. In a footnote to the essay on reification, Lukacs paused over Engels's examples of dialectics in nature and objected that they lacked the decisive feature of the dialectic as he understood it: the interaction of subject and object, the self-knowledge of a humanity that makes its own world and can therefore comprehend it in the making. Nature, having no subject, could exhibit movement and change, but not dialectics in the strict sense, which belonged to history alone. The footnote ran to a few lines. Its consequences ran to a century.

Understand first why the objection landed with such force. Lukacs was not carping at details of chemistry. He was defending a conception of Marxism as the self-consciousness of a revolutionary class, against a conception of Marxism as a general science of laws, natural and social, administered by a party that monopolised the science. The political charge inside the philosophical point was plain to every reader of 1923: if dialectics governs nature independently of human practice, then a priesthood of dialecticians can govern human beings in nature's name, and the proletariat becomes the object of its own doctrine rather than its subject. Western Marxism, in the hands of Karl Korsch, the Frankfurt School, Jean-Paul Sartre in his critical mode, Alfred Schmidt, Lucio Colletti, and many others, elaborated the footnote into a settled verdict. Engels had taken a method whose home was the critique of society and projected it onto the cosmos, converting Marx's historical materialism into a metaphysical system, and the Soviet doctrine of dialectical materialism was the legitimate child of that conversion. The full route of that verdict, from the footnote through the reification essay's own later troubles, is traced in the series guide to [Lukacs and the theory of reification](/2015/11/15/lukacs-reification-guide/), which this article presupposes and does not repeat.

The tradition's strongest scholars added careful qualifications that its popularisers dropped. Lukacs himself, in later self-criticism and in his late ontology, moved toward granting nature a dialectical structure of its own, and his 1923 position softened across a long life, though the footnote outran its author's second thoughts. Schmidt's study of Marx's concept of nature argued the subtler thesis that Marx's nature is always socially mediated, a claim about Marx rather than a simple condemnation of Engels. Colletti, the most rigorous of the critics, aimed much of his fire at the Hegelian residue in Marx himself and treated the Engels question as one front in a larger war. None of this nuance survived into the slogan that a generation of students absorbed, in which Engels the vulgariser ruined Marx the subtle dialectician sometime around 1878. Slogans are how traditions travel, and this one travelled into textbooks, party schools of the rival camp, and eventually into the reference works from which journalists take their bearings. Correcting it does not require acquitting Engels of every charge. It requires noticing that the charge was framed by a footnote, aimed originally at examples rather than at the whole project, and hardened by institutions that found a simple Engels useful, in Moscow as the founder of a doctrine and in the West as the scapegoat for the doctrine's career.

### Why did a single footnote matter so much?

The footnote mattered because it supplied Western Marxism with its founding distinction, a dialectic of history grounded in human practice against a dialectic of nature governed by laws, and because it let a generation honour Marx while blaming Engels, and through Engels the Soviet doctrine, for everything in the tradition they needed to disown.

There is an irony in the footnote's target selection that manuscript scholarship has sharpened. Lukacs wrote in 1923, two years before the Moscow edition made the bundles generally available, and his knowledge of Engels on nature came chiefly from Anti-Duhring and from the Feuerbach book, the published work. His objection, in other words, was aimed at the shopfront, not the drawer, and aimed on philosophical grounds that no editorial discovery can dissolve. Later critics inherited the objection and re-aimed it at the drawer, quoting cosmological fragments Lukacs never saw. The migration of the target is a small history of the controversy in itself: a philosophical argument about published texts became, without new argument, a judgement on unpublished notebooks, and the notebooks' unfinished state, which should have complicated the judgement, was treated as irrelevant to it. Keeping the two trials separate, the trial of Anti-Duhring on philosophical grounds and the trial of the bundles on textual grounds, is the minimum condition of a fair hearing, and this guide conducts them separately throughout.

## The strongest objection, stated at full strength

Fairness now requires the objection in its most formidable form, shorn of slogan and stated as its best defenders would state it. It runs as follows. Dialectics, in Marx's hands, is not a pattern stamped on the world but the logic of a specific subject matter: human beings producing their lives under inherited conditions, in relations that are simultaneously material and conscious, so that the categories of the analysis, commodity, value, capital, are at once objective structures and forms of human practice. The method works because its object is self-made and therefore self-knowable. Transfer the method to nature, where no practice mediates and no subject confronts itself in its object, and the method degenerates. Contradiction, which in society names real opposed interests and tendencies within a unity, becomes in nature a loose metaphor for any polarity or resistance. Negation, which in history names the determinate overcoming of a social form by forces the form itself generates, becomes in nature a label for any succession, seed to plant to seed, in which nothing is overcome in the relevant sense and everything is preserved by definition. The three laws, harmless as headings for a notebook, become pernicious the moment they are taught as the skeleton key of reality, because a key that opens every lock opens none, and a philosophy that can find its pattern in steam, barley, magnetism, and revolution with equal ease has explained the peculiarity of none of them.

The objection then turns from logic to history, and here it grows teeth. A dialectics of nature is precisely the instrument a ruling party needs. If history obeys laws as nature does, knowable in advance and independent of what the governed think, then the party that possesses the science possesses the future, and dissent becomes not error but a kind of natural illiteracy. The codification of dialectical materialism in the Soviet Union was therefore not an accident that befell an innocent text. It was the activation of a possibility the text contained from the moment dialectics was detached from practice and promulgated as cosmology. The line from the barley seed to the Short Course, on this account, is not straight, but it is a line, and Engels stands at its head because he wrote the sentences from which the catechism was quarried. The critics concede the biographical defences, that Engels was a subtler man than his codifiers, that Marx read Anti-Duhring without protest, and they answer that texts are responsible for their strong readings as well as their subtle ones, and that the strong reading of the dialectics of nature was available in the text from the start, in the claim to have found the most general laws of motion of nature, society, and thought in a single formula. A philosophy that announces itself as the science of the general laws of everything has already built the cathedral in which a priesthood will later install itself.

No honest guide may soften that case, and this guide does not. Parts of it are, in the verdict offered below, simply right. The three laws as a finished system do degenerate when they leave the notebook. The codification did happen, and its consequences included the policing of sciences, most catastrophically genetics, by philosophical decree. Engels's formulations, especially the most sweeping ones about laws governing nature, society, and thought at once, handed the codifiers their raw material pre-cut. Where the objection overreaches is in its bookkeeping and its biography. Its bookkeeping convicts the published philosophy on evidence drawn disproportionately from unpublished fragments, while discounting the one piece of biographical evidence that bears directly on the published philosophy, namely Marx's participation in it. Its biography requires an Engels who invented the extension of dialectics to nature against the grain of Marx's thinking, when the letters show two men exchanging enthusiasm for Darwin, for chemistry, for the mathematics of the infinite, across the very years of composition, with the dialectical reading of the sciences a shared enthusiasm rather than a private heresy. And its history, tracing the catechism to the text, underrates the catechism's other parents: the pedagogical needs of mass parties, the Russian tradition of Plekhanov, the institutional demand for a teachable worldview, and the choices of men who needed doctrine the way armies need drill. The reply does not acquit. It redistributes responsibility across the text, its editors, its codifiers, and its century, which is where responsibility for intellectual catastrophes usually belongs.

## The defence: scientists, readers, and the slow rehabilitation

Against the critical tradition stands a counter-tradition that is smaller in the philosophy faculties and larger in the laboratories, and its membership surprises readers who expect the verdict of science on Engels to be unanimous scorn. The English edition of Dialectics of Nature published in 1940 carried a preface by J. B. S. Haldane, one of the founders of population genetics and a scientist of unimpeachable standing, who found in Engels a thinker grappling seriously with the philosophy his science implied, and who defended several of the fragments against the charge of obsolescence while discarding others with a practitioner's bluntness. Joseph Needham, the great historian of Chinese science and a biochemist of distinction, treated Engels as an honoured ancestor of the integrative, levels aware thinking he himself practised. Christopher Caudwell, dead in Spain in 1937 with his manuscripts unpublished, built a whole philosophy of science on the dialectical inheritance. In a later generation, the palaeontologist Stephen Jay Gould praised the labour essay in terms quoted earlier in this guide, and the biologist Richard Lewontin and the geneticist Richard Levins wrote as self-declared dialectical biologists, arguing that the habits Engels recommended, attention to interconnection, to levels, to the transformation of quantity into quality in developmental systems, described how they actually worked at the bench and in the field. John Bellamy Foster's reconstruction of Marx's ecology drew the labour essay and the nature fragments into an account of the metabolic relation between society and nature that a new environmental readership has taken up with energy.

Handle this defence with the same care as the prosecution. Eminent scientists have been wrong about philosophy before, and a preface is not a proof. Haldane wrote as a party member as well as a geneticist, and the exhibit cuts both ways for anyone determined to discount motives. What the defence actually establishes is narrower and more valuable than acquittal. It establishes that the fragments, read as a working scientist's prompts rather than as a legislator's code, have repeatedly struck working scientists as fertile: not true in every proposition, but fertile in the questions they train a reader to ask about levels of organisation, about the conversion of quantitative accumulation into qualitative novelty, about the refusal to treat the organism and its environment as separable billiard balls. Gould's praise is the model of the genre, since Gould praised the labour essay's structure while rejecting its mechanism and much else in the tradition that carried it. Fertility without orthodoxy is the counter-tradition's real claim, and it is a claim the text, being unfinished and aphoristic, is peculiarly built to sustain. Notebooks fertilise. Catechisms sterilise. The tragedy of the text's career is that institutions insisted on reading a notebook as a catechism, first in Moscow and then, in mirror image, in the Western seminar, where dismissing the catechism passed for dismissing the notebook.

The scholarly wing of the defence works on the text itself. Editors of the complete editions reconstructed the bundles and their chronology, dissolving the illusion of a single treatise. Historians of the partnership re-read the correspondence and recovered the extent of the scientific collaboration between the two men. Kangal's study of 2020, Friedrich Engels and the Dialectics of Nature, represents the current state of this wing: a reconstruction of the project's composition from the manuscripts, arguing that Engels's plans were exploratory, that the famous laws occupy less of the project than the editions suggest, and that the standard image of the book, Engels marching through the sciences stamping each with a triad, is an artifact of editorial arrangement and doctrinal reception. A reader may contest Kangal's conclusions at several points, and reviewers have. What cannot be contested is the method's demand, that claims about this text cite its manuscripts with their dates and bundle positions, the way claims about Marx's early writings learned to cite the Paris manuscripts after their own editorial history was understood. The guide's researcher layer below turns that demand into practical instruction.

## Codification: how fragments became a state metaphysics

Between the notebook and the catastrophe stands the codifier, and no account of this text is honest that leaps directly from Engels to Lysenko. The term dialectical materialism itself postdates both founders as a system name: Joseph Dietzgen used kindred language in the 1880s, and Georgi Plekhanov, the father of Russian Marxism, gave the term currency in the 1890s as the label for the philosophy he expounded against populists and Machists alike. Lenin's Materialism and Empirio-criticism of 1909 hardened the philosophy into a doctrine of reflection, matter independent of consciousness and knowledge as its copy, written in polemic and admitting no loose ends. After the revolution, the young Soviet state required a teachable worldview for its schools, its party academies, and its censors, and the raw materials were systematised in the 1930s into the doctrine generations of students across half the world learned by rote: matter in eternal motion, the three laws, the spiral of development, the criterion of practice, all arranged as a catechism with correct answers. The codification received its canonical short form in 1938, in the chapter on dialectical and historical materialism inside the Short Course history of the Communist Party, published under Stalin's authority and bearing his stamp in every sense that matters.

Grade the codification against the manuscripts and the differences are the story. The manuscripts hesitate; the catechism asserts. The manuscripts collect examples and worry them; the catechism teaches the laws first and assigns the examples as exercises. The manuscripts treat the sciences as the teacher of philosophy; the catechism appoints philosophy as the supervisor of the sciences, entitled to certify results and to condemn deviations as idealist. Engels bears a real but limited responsibility for this inversion. His sweeping formulations, the claim that dialectics is the science of the general laws of motion of the external world and of human thought alike, offered the codifiers their charter sentence. His prestige as the surviving founder made his name the natural masthead. But the inversion itself, from a reader's notebook to a supervisor's rulebook, was the work of institutions with needs Engels never faced, executed by men who had never watched a thinker change his mind in a margin. The distinction matters practically as well as morally, because the remedy differs. If the poison were in the propositions, the text would have to be discarded. If the poison entered through the codifying use, the remedy is to break the use: to read the fragments as fragments, to restore their dates and hesitations, and to refuse any philosophy, of whatever party, the office of certifying laboratory results.

### How did codification change what Engels wrote?

Codification changed a reader's working notebook into a supervisor's rulebook by fixing the three laws as a catechism with correct answers, appointing philosophy the judge of scientific results, and erasing the hesitations, dates, and revisions that fill the manuscripts, so that generations met as settled doctrine what its author had left as open inquiry.

The Lysenko affair is where the rulebook drew blood, and this guide treats it as the tradition's standing warning rather than as an anecdote. Trofim Lysenko, an agronomist of peasant origin with a gift for promising abundance, rose through the Soviet agricultural establishment in the 1930s on the strength of vernalisation claims and a rhetoric that cast Mendelian genetics as bourgeois, idealist, and alien. His doctrine, dressed in the vocabulary of dialectics and of the malleability of nature under practice, promised that heredity could be reshaped by environment in ways that flattered the regime's transformist hopes. In August 1948, at a session of the Lenin Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Lysenko's report was declared approved by the Central Committee, genetics was proscribed as a science, its textbooks were withdrawn, its laboratories were broken up, and its practitioners were dismissed, exiled, or worse. Nikolai Vavilov, one of the great plant geneticists of the century, had already died in a Saratov prison in 1943 after arrest in 1940. Soviet biology lost a generation. Lysenko's ascendancy outlasted Stalin and collapsed only in the middle 1960s, leaving a wound in Russian science whose scars remain legible.

Responsibility for Lysenkoism is a question on which this guide insists on precision, because both available slogans are false. The slogan that dialectical materialism caused Lysenkoism ignores the affair's other engines: a regime demanding quick agricultural miracles amid famine's memory, a careerist's mastery of institutional politics, the destruction of independent scientific bodies that might have resisted, and Stalin's personal patronage, without which the 1948 session is unintelligible. The slogan that philosophy had nothing to do with it ignores the record: Lysenko's camp argued in dialectical vocabulary, claimed the founders' authority, denounced genetics as metaphysical in the precise sense the catechism taught, and operated inside a system where a philosophy office could indeed certify science, the very inversion the codification had built. Engels's manuscripts contain none of the affair's specific poisons; they contain a respect for the sciences' autonomy of result that the codification inverted. But the name dialectics, detached from the manuscripts and attached to a state doctrine, was the flag under which the wreckers marched, and any defence of the text that refuses to pronounce the name Lysenko has not understood what a reputation is. The FAQ below returns to the affair, because readers rightly refuse to let the subject go.

## The audit of examples: what survived and what failed

The fairest tribunal for a philosopher of science is the subsequent history of the science, and this section convenes it, example by example, with verdicts recorded without loyalty. Engels asked to be judged this way. His whole method staked philosophy's credit on the sciences' results, and the credit has now had well over a century to be audited.

Energy stands first among the survivors. The discovery that heat, mechanical motion, electricity, magnetism, light, and chemical affinity are transformable forms of a single conserved quantity was the load-bearing fact of Engels's natural philosophy, and the twentieth century only enlarged it, adding mass itself to the ledger of conversions. His philosophical moral, that the sciences were dissolving the old fixed domains into one interconnected process, reads in retrospect like the plain truth about where physics was travelling. His associated critique of the phrase force, and of the muddles it bred, anticipated clarifications the physicists themselves later performed. On the cell, likewise, the verdict is survival with honours: the cellular constitution of life, the development of the organism from a single cell, and the continuity of cell from cell became the unshaken ground of biology, and his philosophical use of the cell as a unity that differentiates internally is a fair gloss on what embryology found. On Darwin, the verdict is survival with corrections. Descent with modification by natural selection remains the spine of biology; Engels's grasp of it was strong, his extension of struggle language was sometimes cruder than Darwin's own, and his anthropology of the labour essay, audited already, shows the theory's best philosophical pupil at work. His enthusiasm for the nebular hypothesis and for a universe of becoming rather than a finished clockwork belongs with the survivors too, since the cosmos of the twentieth century turned out historical to its roots: stars are born and die, elements are cooked in stellar furnaces, and the universe itself has a past. A philosopher who bet on process against fixity, in 1876, bet on the winning horse in science after science.

The casualties begin with the definition of life. Engels, following the chemistry of his day, defined life as the mode of existence of albuminous or protein bodies, a continuous exchange of matter with the environment. The definition was respectable in 1876 and is unusable now, not because metabolism dropped out of the picture, it remains central, but because the protein monopoly collapsed: nucleic acids carry heredity, viruses straddle every boundary the definition draws, and the origin of life is researched through chemistries in which proteins are latecomers rather than sovereigns. The casualty list continues with the heat death war. Clausius had drawn from the second law of thermodynamics the conclusion that the universe tends toward a final equilibrium, a heat death in which nothing further can happen. Engels, whose philosophy required nature's motion to be eternal and whose materialism recoiled from a state that seemed to demand a creator to wind the clock, argued that radiated heat must somehow be reconcentrated and motion restored, and he predicted that science would find the mechanism. Science did not find it. The second law survived every assault of the following century, cosmology rebuilt the question on expanding spacetime and horizons Engels could not have imagined, and his specific confidence stands refuted even by those who share his distaste for smuggling theology into physics. His pages on the subject show the project's characteristic vice in its purest form: a philosophical requirement, eternity of process, dictating in advance what physics must eventually find. When the vice governs, the method that elsewhere learned from science begins lecturing it, and the lecture is the moment the critics' caricature comes true.

Smaller casualties deserve their sentences. His chemistry, sound in its structure thanks to Schorlemmer, contains period errors of detail. His mathematics, the dialectics of infinity and of the differential calculus, mixes genuine philosophical puzzlement about infinitesimals, which the rigorous analysis of the following century partly vindicated as puzzlement, with arguments no mathematician can now accept. His remarks on electricity and on the ether bear the date of a science about to be revolutionised by the electron and by relativity. His biology of inheritance, as the labour essay showed, relied on mechanisms the gene concept dissolved. None of this is scandalous. The same audit applied to any philosopher of science of the 1870s, including the scientists' own philosophical asides, yields a comparable wreckage list. What distinguishes Engels is that he asked for the audit, in writing, as the method's own criterion, and that the survivors' side of his ledger is heavier than that of any comparable system builder of his generation. The Spencerian evolutionism that outsold him in his lifetime is read now only by historians. The bundles are still argued over by scientists. Longevity is not truth, but in the philosophy of science it is evidence of a kind, and the evidence here favours the notebook over its rivals.

### How should a reader weigh a failed example against a surviving one?

A reader should weigh examples the way Engels's own method demands, by asking whether the failure came from period ignorance, which every science outgrows, or from the philosophical requirement overruling the evidence, which is the project's real vice, and by noticing that the survivors cluster where he followed the sciences and the failures where he instructed them.

That rule of weighing also settles the standing of the three laws in a final, defensible position. Where a law summarises what the sciences independently found, thresholds, transitions, internal relations, it is a respectable piece of philosophical bookkeeping, neither needed by the scientist nor refuted by the science. Where a law instructs the sciences in advance, dictating that nature must negate and negate again or must recycle its heat eternally, it oversteps the licence the manuscripts elsewhere claim, and the subsequent record has punished the overstep every time. A reader who applies the rule loses the thrill of a universal key and gains something better: a way of reading the whole bundle that explains its unevenness from inside its own principles, rather than by the loyalties of the reader.

## The unfinished-target problem

The evidence is now assembled, and the guide's namable claim can be stated in its full form. The unfinished-target problem is this: the case that Engels distorted Marx rests principally on a manuscript Engels never completed, never ordered, and never authorised for publication, assembled into book form by editors four decades after his death, while the philosophy Engels actually published on the same subject was read in full by Marx before publication and contains a chapter from Marx's own pen. The asymmetry reverses the burden of proof that casual discussion assumes. It is not the defender of Engels who must explain away an anomaly. It is the prosecutor who must explain why the drawer should outweigh the bookshop, why fragments a man withheld should define him against the volumes he issued, and why the testimony of the one witness whose opinion settles the biographical question, Marx himself, should be discounted in favour of inference from texts their alleged victim never treated as crimes.

State the claim's limits as carefully as its force, since a claim that survives is one whose borders are marked. The problem does not touch the philosophical objection to a dialectics of nature as such. Lukacs aimed that objection at the published work, and it must be answered philosophically, by argument about subject and object, practice and law, as the steelman section of this guide attempted. The problem does not canonise the fragments. Several fail their audit, and the cosmological pages fail it badly. The problem does not even settle what Marx thought of natural philosophy in his private judgement, beyond what his conduct documents. What the problem governs is the distribution of scholarly labour. Once it is stated, no responsible account of the Engels question may proceed as though Dialectics of Nature were a book Marx's partner published behind his back, and no syllabus, encyclopedia entry, or documentary may present the Moscow arrangement as Engels's architecture. Accounts that continue to do so are not taking the other side of an argument. They are declining to have the argument on the available evidence.

### Did Engels distort Marx's dialectic?

The standard Western Marxist view, beginning with Lukacs in 1923, says he did by extending dialectics to nature. Against this, Marx read and contributed a chapter to Anti-Duhring, where that extension is published, and the principal evidence for the charge is an unfinished manuscript assembled by later editors.

The question survives its reframing, and should. Strip the editorial illusions away and a real dispute remains, about whether the concept of dialectics survives its journey from the critique of society to the philosophy of nature without changing its meaning, and about whether Engels's own published formulations already show the change Lukacs feared. That dispute has serious people on both sides and cannot be closed by an archivist. What the reframing changes is the dispute's temperature and its evidence base. It becomes a dispute about Anti-Duhring and the Feuerbach book, conducted in the open, on texts their author defended in his lifetime, with Marx's documented proximity admitted as a fact to be interpreted rather than an embarrassment to be ignored. Scholars will go on disagreeing about whether Marx's silence was consent, indifference, or the loyalty of a friend who had outsourced natural philosophy to his partner and trusted the outsourcing. Each reading is arguable. None is compatible with the melodrama of the secret betrayal, and retiring the melodrama is this guide's chief service to the subject.

A word belongs here on the wider stakes, because the Engels question has never been only about Engels. It functions in the culture of the left and in the academy as a sorting device: traditions that wish to keep Marx and discard the Soviet inheritance use Engels as the border checkpoint, while traditions that defend the Soviet inheritance canonise him as co-founder of its philosophy. The unfinished-target problem refuses both uses. It denies the border checkpoint its forged passport evidence, and it denies the canon its finished book. What remains is a historical Engels, more interesting than either effigy: a formidably read Victorian amateur of science, working out a philosophy of process in notebooks he could not finish because his dead friend's masterpiece consumed his remaining strength, publishing the defensible core of it in a polemic his friend helped write, and leaving the rest to a posterity that proceeded to fight over the leavings with more passion than philology. The series situates this text within the partners' whole shared output in its treatment of [how dialectics fares as a method in the social sciences](/2012/11/01/dialectics-in-social-science/), where the afterlife of these arguments in working disciplines is followed in detail.

## Hegel behind the curtain, and the Feuerbach book

No reader can price the manuscripts without understanding the inheritance Engels believed he was administering. Hegel had built the grandest philosophy of process in the tradition: reality as self-developing, categories passing into their opposites, logic and history and nature as one movement contemplated from different stations. Marx's generation inherited the system along with Ludwig Feuerbach's demolition of its theology, and the young Engels, who had defended Hegel in print as a student volunteer, spent his life negotiating the estate. His settled position, stated most cleanly in the finished book of 1886, Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy, was that Hegel's method was revolutionary and his system conservative, that the method had to be extracted from the system's idealist shell and set on its feet in a materialist world, and that the dialectical laws belonged to that extraction, having been in Hegel a doctrine of the self-movement of the concept and becoming, in the materialist version, a doctrine of the movement of things themselves, which thought reflects.

The Feuerbach book matters to this guide for three reasons. It is finished, published by Engels in his lifetime, and written with the lucidity of his maturity, so it shows what the dialectics of nature looks like when its author completes a presentation rather than leaving notes. It contains his canonical account of the philosophy's pedigree, the basic question of philosophy, and the relation to Hegel, in a form no editor arranged. And it confirms, from the author's own hand in a polished text, the elements the critics most dislike: the reflection language about thought and being, the confidence that the sciences vindicate the dialectical picture, the laws themselves in summary form. A trial of Engels conducted on the Feuerbach book and Anti-Duhring is a trial of the real defendant. The same trial conducted on notebook fragments about heat death is a trial of a man for his marginalia, and the sentence, whatever it is, will be appealed forever by anyone who reads the file. Scholars who wish to convict should therefore welcome this guide's insistence on the published texts, since a conviction secured there is worth ten secured in the drawer.

The Hegel inheritance also explains a feature of the manuscripts that baffles newcomers, their constant traffic between the driest scientific detail and the largest philosophical generality. Engels believed, following Hegel and reversing him, that the categories of thought are not private furniture but the distilled forms of real relations, so that a puzzle about the calculus or about chemical combination could be, at the same time, a puzzle about the category of quantity or of identity. Modern philosophy of science has largely abandoned that traffic, preferring its questions sorted by department. Readers who share the modern preference will find stretches of the bundles alien in a way that has nothing to do with their politics. Readers who suspect that the departmental sorting has costs, that a science unable to think its own categories historically is poorer for the inability, will recognise in Engels an early, rough practitioner of the history and philosophy of science they wish existed. Both readers are reading the same pages. The guide's neutrality consists in showing them the pages accurately, Hegel's shadow included, and letting the audit of examples, rather than the shadow, carry the verdict.

## What to read before and after, and which editions to trust

Practical counsel belongs in a close reading guide, and this text needs more of it than most, because the wrong door into the bundle has ruined many first readings. Read Anti-Duhring's philosophy chapters first, or the Feuerbach book entire, to meet the doctrine as its author published it. Read the labour essay next, entire, since it is short, finished in all but name, and shows the method earning its keep. Then take the historical introduction for the sweep of the sciences' development, and only then browse the fragments by scientific appetite, chemistry for those who love the periodic system, the mathematical fragments for those who enjoy watching a strong mind wrestle the infinite without the proper tools. Save the cosmological fragments for last and read them as biography, the record of what a thoroughgoing materialist of the 1870s felt he had to believe about eternity, rather than as physics. A reader who follows this order meets Engels at his strongest before meeting him at his most speculative, which is the order any fair editor would have chosen and the reverse of the order in which controversy usually serves him.

Editions require the same care. The text circulates freely online in the translation carried by the great archive of Marxist texts, which derives from the early English edition and is serviceable for the argument, though its editorial notes reflect the doctrinal period of its making. The English collected works volume prints the Moscow arrangement with fuller apparatus. The scholarly horizon is the complete historical critical edition, whose presentation of the manuscripts in their bundles is the basis of the newer research and the only safe foundation for claims about what Engels grouped, dated, or revised. For the labour essay, seek a printing that notes its 1896 first publication and its status as a fragment of a larger introduction. For Anti-Duhring, any modern edition will serve, but the prefaces Engels added to later editions repay attention, since in them he reflects on the book's reception and on the natural science project behind it, and the preface to the second edition contains his own rueful summary of how the Duhring affair consumed the years the treatise needed. Translations of the bundle's German vary in their handling of his scientific vocabulary, which was the technical German of the 1870s; a reader dependent on one translation should treat any single startling sentence as a translation problem before treating it as a doctrine.

Set beside this text, in a reader's programme, the works that argue with it. Lukacs's History and Class Consciousness supplies the objection in its birthplace, though its author later complicated it. Alfred Schmidt's study of the concept of nature in Marx supplies the mediation thesis in its strongest scholarly dress. Kangal's monograph supplies the manuscript reconstruction. Haldane's preface and Gould's essays supply the laboratory's answer. A shelf holding those five responses around the bundle and its published companions is a complete education in one of the Marxist tradition's longest arguments, and it fits in a single box, which cannot be said of many educations.

## Misreadings that follow this text around

Five misreadings recur so reliably that a guide can set them out as a checklist for the reader's protection. The first is the book illusion itself, the assumption that Dialectics of Nature is a treatise Engels wrote, whose chapters argue in sequence. Its correction has organised this entire guide: the work is an edited construction from bundles, and every interpretive move that depends on the printed sequence is unsound until checked against the manuscripts. The second misreading holds that Engels claimed nature obeys dialectical laws imposed on it from outside, a cosmic police force of triads. His stated position was the reverse: the laws are abstractions from nature's own investigated behaviour, and his polemic against bringing ready-made schemas to the material, aimed at Duhring and at Hegel's own lapses, is among the bundles' most consistent notes. A reader may judge the abstraction unsuccessful. Judging it an imposition mistakes the direction of the argument.

The third misreading attributes the three laws to Marx, or speaks of Marx's dialectics of nature as a doctrine awaiting only exposition. Marx published no such doctrine, formulated no three laws, and left the philosophy of nature to his partner in the division of labour that both men acknowledged. Attributing the laws to Marx flatters neither thinker: it saddles Marx with formulations he never made and robs Engels of the authorship, creditable or not, that the manuscripts document. The fourth misreading treats the Lukacs footnote as a settlement, as though the Engels question were closed in 1923 by an authority all parties accept. Lukacs himself revised his position across his career, the manuscript evidence that transformed the question was unavailable to him, and the scientists' counter-tradition postdates his verdict. A footnote, even a great one, opens an argument; it does not close one. The fifth misreading demotes Anti-Duhring to a minor popularisation, a pamphlet beneath the notice of serious readers. Whatever its literary faults, and they include the tedium of following Duhring through every thicket, the book was the principal vehicle by which several generations learned the philosophy this guide discusses, the text Marx contributed to, and the source from which the codifiers and the critics alike drew their quotations. Minor books do not have major trials.

A sixth misreading, newer than the others and growing with the ecological readership, deserves a sentence of its own. It conscripts the fragments wholesale into environmental philosophy, finding in Engels a prophet of ecological crisis complete with warnings about humanity's revenge taking nature. The famous passage on the deforestation of Mesopotamia and the Alps, which does exist and does warn that nature answers human shortsightedness in kind, comes from the labour essay's orbit and deserves its fame. But a prophet needs a doctrine, and the bundles offer fragments, some profound on the interconnection of human labour with natural process and some silent or dated on everything a modern ecological politics must decide. The ecological reading is strongest when it claims the metabolic insight, society and nature as one process with real feedbacks, and weakest when it claims the whole manuscript as an ancestor portrait. Ancestor portraits, like effigies at a checkpoint, serve the living more than they describe the dead.

## Two laboratories reading one notebook

The extra question this guide is often asked, how scientists of opposite temperaments extract opposite verdicts from identical fragments, has a concrete answer worth a section. Imagine the pages on the transformation of quantity into quality placed before two readers of equal eminence. The sympathetic reader, formed by developmental biology or by the physics of phase transitions, sees a nineteenth century mind groping toward the concept of emergence: the recognition that accumulation along one dimension issues, at thresholds, in behaviour that the lower level's vocabulary cannot state, and that a philosophy honouring this pattern will resist the reduction of life to chemistry and of chemistry to mechanics without denying their material continuity. That reader forgives the barley seed, treats the laws as scaffolding, and keeps the habit of asking, at every level of organisation, what is qualitatively new here and what quantitative history produced it. The hostile reader, formed by the standards of testable theory, sees the same pages and asks what observation could possibly count against the law. Water freezing fits it, water not freezing would fit some other law, and a scheme compatible with every outcome forbids none, which is the definition of an empty scheme in the philosophy of science that Karl Popper would later formalise and aim at this very tradition. That reader keeps the fragments as a museum of the prehistory of emergence talk and declines the habits.

Both readings are honest, and the guide declines to declare one of them illiterate. Their divergence is not about Engels's sentences but about what a philosophy of nature is for. If its office is to orient inquiry, to train attention toward interconnection, levels, and thresholds, the fragments have repeatedly done that office for serious practitioners, and fertility is the right measure. If its office is to state truths about nature that stand beside the sciences' truths and answer to the same tests, the fragments fail in the form in which the codifiers cast them, and emptiness is the right charge. Engels himself, at his best, held the first conception: dialectics as the science of interconnections is an orientation, a discipline of attention, learned from the sciences and repaid to them as questions. At his worst, in the cosmological pages, he grasped the second conception and dictated to physics. The whole text is the record of a mind oscillating between those two offices, which is one more reason its unfinished state suits it. A finished book would have had to choose. The notebook shows the choosing, page by page, and lets every reader repeat it with fuller knowledge.

There is a lesson in that oscillation for the sciences' other philosophical suitors. Every generation produces its would-be universal gloss on nature, some from within the laboratories and some from the armchairs, and the gloss always oscillates between orienting its readers and instructing them. Engels's bundles are the most instructive failure and success of the genre in the Marxist tradition precisely because both poles survive in his hand, undated by an editor's smoothing into a false consistency. Students of the philosophy of science who read nothing else of his should read the heat death fragments beside the energy fragments and watch, within a few pages, the same intelligence learn from thermodynamics and then lecture it. The spectacle teaches the difference between the two offices better than any treatise on method, and it is available only because nobody finished the book.

## The ladder of motions: how Engels organised the sciences

Beneath the famous laws, the bundles contain a quieter construction that specialists regard as the project's most durable piece of architecture: a classification of the sciences by their forms of motion. Engels proposed that each science studies a characteristic form in which matter moves. Mechanics treats the displacement of masses. Physics treats the motions of molecules, heat, light, electricity, magnetism. Chemistry treats the motions of atoms, the combination and decomposition of substances. Biology treats the motion of living bodies, for which he used the period word protein as the material seat. Above the natural series, in the projected continuation he never wrote, stood the sciences of society and thought. The classification did philosophical work in two directions at once. Downward, it honoured reduction as far as reduction honestly goes: chemistry really does rest on the motions physics studies, and life really is chemistry organised. Upward, it blocked the imperialism of the lower sciences, since each higher form of motion obeys laws of its own that the lower vocabulary cannot state, so that a living cell is not merely a chemical event wearing a costume, and a society is not merely biology with flags.

The ladder deserves its reputation because the problem it addresses never went away. Twentieth century philosophy of science rebuilt the same structure under other names, in the doctrines of levels of organisation, of emergent properties, of the relative autonomy of the special sciences. Physicists who announced that everything was ultimately their subject, and biologists who announced that everything social was ultimately theirs, kept re-enacting the imperialism Engels's ladder was built to refuse, and the refusal reads freshly in every generation that has to have the argument again. The ladder also explains the bundles' organisation better than the three laws do. Engels's fragments march up the rungs, mechanics to physics to chemistry to life, collecting at each rung the transitions that embarrassed the rigid categories of the older thought: the border where physics becomes chemistry in the molecule's behaviour, the border where chemistry becomes life in the self-maintaining exchange of matter. A projected book built on the ladder would have been a survey of the sciences' frontiers rather than a liturgy of triads, and Kangal's reconstruction suggests the ladder, more than the laws, was the spine of the mature plan. Editors who front-loaded the laws and scattered the ladder through the fragments thus inverted the project's centre of gravity, a distortion for which the printed sequence, once again, bears the responsibility that polemic assigns to the author.

Judge the ladder by the same audit as the examples and it scores well with local damage. Its physics rung is the physics of the 1870s, molecules and ether, and quantum theory redrew the relation between the physical and the chemical more strangely than his scheme imagines, making the lower rung less a foundation than a neighbouring country with heavy trade. Its biology rung carries the protein definition that failed. But the architecture, distinct levels with distinct laws joined by real transitions, survived the century better than the monisms that competed with it, whether the monism of energy, of the gene, or of information. A reader building a philosophy of science from scratch could do worse than start where the ladder starts, which is more than can be said for the catechism that claimed to summarise it.

## The partnership at work: two men and the sciences

The bundles were written inside a partnership, and the partnership's daily traffic in science is recoverable from the correspondence in a detail that should embarrass any theory of a private Engelsian heresy. When Darwin's Origin of Species appeared in 1859, it was Engels who wrote to Marx within weeks calling its author the man who had dealt teleology its death blow in one regard while its empirical basis still wanted stating, and Marx who replied in 1860 that the book contained the basis in natural history for their view, and returned to the compliment for years, telling correspondents that Darwin's work supplied a basis for the class struggle in history by analogy with the struggle in nature. The two men swapped scientific enthusiasms the way other friends swap novels. Marx sent his geological and agricultural chemistry reading; Engels sent his physiology and his military science. Marx's mathematical manuscripts, his private wrestling with the calculus in the 1870s and early 1880s, went to Engels for judgement, and Engels's own fragments on the mathematics of infinity answer the same puzzles from the other desk. In Capital itself, the footnotes salute Darwin, the chemists, the geologists, with a respect that is never ironical, and the chapter on machinery thinks with the natural historians about the differentiation of tools and organs in a vocabulary the labour essay shares.

None of this proves Marx held a worked out dialectics of nature; he did not, and pretending otherwise would repeat the attribution error this guide corrects elsewhere. What the traffic proves is the atmosphere. Natural philosophy in the partnership was a shared climate, not a smuggled cargo. When Engels drafted the fragments, he was extending conversations the two men had conducted for twenty years, in letters any interested scholar may read, and when he published the philosophy in Anti-Duhring the extension appeared in the movement's press under his partner's eye. Biographers who need a border between the partners on this subject must draw it through a correspondence that keeps crossing it, and the honest cartography shows a frontier zone worked by both men, farmed mostly by one. That zone, not a wall, is what the documentary record contains.

## Party classrooms before the canon: the first reception

Between the drawer and the decade of the footnote, the dialectics of nature lived a first public life through Anti-Duhring alone, and that life shaped the readership that later received the bundles. The German party of the 1880s and 1890s, illegal or harassed for much of the period, educated its cadres in reading circles where Engels's polemic served as the philosophy course. Karl Kautsky, the party's chief theorist and editor of Die Neue Zeit, absorbed the natural philosophy whole and transmitted it as part of the Marxist common sense, while treating its author's authority on science as second only to his authority on everything else. Edward Bernstein, who would later found revisionism, knew the material intimately and aimed several of his doubts precisely at the dialectical machinery, an early sign that the machinery, not the economics, was where the tradition's internal critics felt the strain. August Bebel's popular works carried a simplified Engelsian nature philosophy to a mass readership. Through these channels, a generation of socialists learned to speak of nature dialectically without ever seeing a line of the manuscripts, which still slept in the archive.

The significance of this first reception for the guide's argument is that the doctrine preceded the text by half a century. When the Moscow edition appeared in 1925, it did not introduce the dialectics of nature to its world. It supplied scriptural warrant, retroactively, for a philosophy the movement had already absorbed through the published companion. Codifiers could therefore present the catechism as the mere transcription of a classic, and critics could present the classic as the source of the catechism, while both parties overstated the bundles' causal role. The bundles were less an origin than a reliquary: the movement brought its beliefs to them and found, or placed, the relics it required. Reliquaries matter, and this one mattered enormously once states began certifying doctrine, but an account of the tradition that begins the causal story in 1925 mistakes the reliquary for the saint's life. The life was lived in the party classrooms, out of a polemic against a forgotten Berlin lecturer, while the relics waited in their bundles for a century that would fight over them.

## Moscow argues with itself: mechanists and dialecticians

The Soviet 1920s, the decade of the edition, also hosted the one debate in the tradition's history where the dialectics of nature was contested from inside a revolutionary state before the state settled the contest by decree. The mechanists, strong among natural scientists and their philosophical allies, held that the sciences needed no dialectical philosophy above them, that the future belonged to a unified scientific materialism in which philosophy as a separate discipline would dissolve into results, and that talk of irreducible levels and qualitative leaps obstructed research. The dialecticians, gathered around Abram Deborin and his school, defended a Hegelianised Engels: philosophy as the science of the general laws, the levels defended, the leaps insisted on, with the manuscripts and Anti-Duhring as their charter texts. The debates filled journals and conferences for most of the decade and were conducted, by the standards of what followed, with genuine openness. Scientists of the calibre of the geneticists and physicists of that remarkable generation took sides, and the mechanist case, which in substance anticipated much of the later Western critique of a supervisory philosophy, was argued by men who considered themselves Marxists to the bone.

The settlement is the cautionary part. Neither side won the argument. The party's philosophical interventions of 1930 and 1931 condemned the Deborinites as Menshevising idealists, condemned the mechanists in the same breath, and installed a line by administrative act, after which debate meant exegesis and deviation meant danger. Within seven years the Short Course chapter had frozen the settlement into catechism form. Reading the 1920s debates now is a melancholy exercise, because they show the tradition's last moment at which the status of Engels's project was a live question argued in public by competent people, before the question became a test of loyalty with a published answer key. The guide draws one conclusion from the episode that transcends its setting. The danger in the dialectics of nature was never located in any single proposition about seeds or steam. It was located in the institutional arrangement that made one reading of the propositions compulsory. The same fragments, argued over in journals, harmed no one. Certified by committees, they helped license the machinery that later certified Lysenko. Texts do not appoint their own certifiers, and the distinction between a text and its enforcement apparatus is the distinction on which any fair judgement of this one finally rests.

## Haeckel, Spencer, and the Victorian crowd

Engels wrote inside a crowded market for philosophies of nature, and placing him among his competitors sharpens the audit's verdicts. Herbert Spencer had built the era's bestselling system, an evolutionism of everything, from nebulae to societies to ethics, deduced from the persistence of force and marketed with immense success on both sides of the Atlantic. Engels read Spencer with contempt for the system's elasticity, its ability to redescribe any fact as integration or differentiation, and the contempt is instructive because the same elasticity is what hostile critics later charged against the three laws; Engels, whatever his own lapses, knew the vice when a rival committed it and said so in the fragments with precision. Ernst Haeckel, Darwin's German champion, built monism, a nature philosophy of one substance wearing scientific dress, and carried it to a mass public through bestselling volumes whose confidence Engels admired as propaganda and distrusted as thought, since Haeckel's monism kept sliding into the mysticism it claimed to replace. The popular materialists, Buchner and his school, offered the market a third product, matter and force without dialectics, in which thought figured as a secretion of the brain as bile is of the liver, a formula Engels treated as the reductio of mechanical materialism, crude enough to discredit the materialism it advertised.

Measured against that shelf, the bundles' distinctiveness comes into focus. Engels was the only one of the Victorian systematisers of nature whose method required him to excerpt the primary scientific literature in bulk, to correspond with a professional chemist about his drafts, and to stake his philosophy's credit on results that could refute it. Spencer's system could absorb any discovery; Haeckel's could bless any; Buchner's could ignore most. Engels's could lose, and in the heat death pages did lose, on evidence. A philosophy of nature that can be wrong in detail is already a different animal from the rhetorical systems surrounding it, closer in spirit to the sciences it read than to the metaphysics it fought. Posterity's shelf space ratified the difference with rough justice. Spencer is a historian's subject, Haeckel a cautionary tale with a complicated scientific legacy, Buchner a footnote, while the bundles still draw working scientists into argument. The market of the 1870s would have found that outcome incredible. The audit of the following century delivered it anyway.

## The ecological afterlife and the metabolic reading

One readership arrived late and changed the text's centre of gravity: the environmental one. Scholars reconstructing Marx's account of the metabolic relation between human production and the natural world, the exchange of matter in which labour mediates between society and its conditions, found in Engels's fragments a partner text rather than an embarrassing uncle. The labour essay's picture of humanity emerging through its own transformative activity, and its warnings about the revenge nature takes on shortsighted conquest, the forests stripped from Mesopotamia and the Alps until whole regions dried, supplied the ecological reading with its most quotable pages. John Bellamy Foster's work, and the research programme that followed it, treated the nature manuscripts as evidence that the founders' materialism was ecological in structure from the start: one process, two moments, with real feedbacks and real limits. Whatever verdict a reader reaches on the larger claim, the ecological scholarship performed a service this guide acknowledges: it read the bundles closely, in their manuscript context, for what they say about labour and nature, and it thereby modelled the post-catechetical reading the text had always needed.

The limits of the conscription were marked in the misreadings section, and they bear repeating in shorter form here. An ecological politics needs positions on energy systems, on growth, on the governance of commons, on which the fragments are silent or Victorian. Treating silence as ancestry flatters the living and falsifies the dead. The sound ecological claim is the structural one: that a materialism which begins from labour as metabolism, which treats nature neither as warehouse nor as temple but as the medium of human self-production with its own recalcitrant order, has resources for environmental thought that the mechanical materialisms lack. Engels's bundles state that structure repeatedly and sometimes beautifully. They also contain a Victorian confidence in the conquest of nature that the ecological reader must argue with rather than inherit, and the argument, conducted honestly, is more valuable than the inheritance. A tradition that can argue with its founders' pages on nature is finally treating those pages as the founders treated Darwin: as material to think with, under correction, in the open.

## The enemy called metaphysics: what Engels meant by the word

No term in the bundles misleads modern readers more reliably than metaphysics, because Engels uses it in a sense almost opposite to the bookshop sense. For him, metaphysical thinking names a habit, not a subject: the habit of treating things and concepts as isolated, finished, and governed by fixed opposites, yes or no, cause or effect, identity or difference, with no passage between the poles. He blamed the habit on the sciences' necessary youth. A science that must first collect, name, and classify its objects, as natural history did magnificently in the centuries of Linnaeus and Cuvier, trains its practitioners to see boundaries because boundaries are its working tools, and the training outlives the stage that required it. The great discoveries of his century then embarrassed the habit one after another: species that shaded into ancestors, elements that combined in series, forces that turned out to be one energy in costume, a cosmos with a biography. Dialectical thinking, in his vocabulary, is simply the habit reformed by the embarrassment: things taken in their motion, their interconnection, their arising and passing away, with opposites understood as poles of a process rather than as walls between compartments.

Stated in that deflated form, the doctrine startles by its modesty, and the modesty is worth dwelling on because it recalibrates the whole controversy. Much of what Engels demands under the banner of dialectics, a modern scientist demands under the banner of good practice: do not reify your categories, remember that classifications are instruments, expect thresholds and feedbacks, distrust any analysis that isolates a system from the environment that constitutes it. Systems biology, ecology, and the earth sciences speak this language natively, without the banner. Where Engels exceeds the modesty is in the codifying temptation, his own as much as his followers': the wish to promote a reformed habit into a set of most general laws, valid for nature, society, and thought at once, which can then be taught as results rather than practised as discipline. The habit and the laws are the two souls of the project, and they have had opposite fortunes. The habit emigrated into the sciences and flourishes there incognito. The laws were embalmed in the catechism and perished with it. A reader who understands the distinction between the souls understands why the same book can be quoted with profit by a working biologist and with horror by a philosopher of science, each accurately reporting a different stratum of it.

## Close reading: the historical introduction

The introduction rewards the close attention this framework promises, since it is the longest stretch of connected prose in the bundles and the place where Engels's historical intelligence works at full stretch. It opens in the Renaissance, with the liberation of inquiry from theology's tutelage figured as a revolution in its own right, the age when the sciences learned to see nature as a process to be investigated rather than a text to be glossed. Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, and Newton pass in review, honoured for the breach they made and criticised, in Newton's case, for the first-cause theology that walled the breach behind them. The narrative then turns to the long age of collection, when the sciences amassed their materials under the sign of fixity: species immutable, continents permanent, the heavens a finished mechanism, each discipline guarding its objects behind definitions. Engels's judgement of that age is fairer than his reputation suggests. He treats the classificatory style as a necessary stage, the indispensable gathering without which no later synthesis could have known what it was synthesising, and reserves his scorn for the moment when a stage's tools hardened into a philosophy of nature as such.

The breaches in the fixed picture are then mustered in sequence, and the sequence is the introduction's argument in narrative form. Kant's nebular hypothesis gives the heavens a history. Geology, in Lyell's hands, gives the Earth one. The cell gives life a unit that develops. Mayer, Joule, and Helmholtz give the physical world a single convertible currency of motion. Darwin gives species a genealogy and man a place inside nature's history rather than above it. Each breach is presented the same way, as a case where the facts of a science compelled its practitioners, often against their own philosophy, to think in processes and transitions. The rhetoric is cumulative and, within the limits of its century's knowledge, the history is broadly just. Its weakness is the Whig temptation, the arrangement of the past as a procession toward the author's standpoint, and a reader should notice how the procession tidies figures, Herschel, Lamarck, Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, whose actual positions were knottier than their roles in the march. Even with the tidying marked, the introduction stands as an early essay in the history of scientific thought by a participant observer, and historians who dismiss the bundles wholesale usually exempt it in practice by quietly agreeing with its periodisation.

One passage of the introduction deserves rescue from summary, since it contains the project's moral in a sentence's compass. Reflecting on the sciences' progress, Engels remarks in substance that nature is the test of dialectics, and that the modern sciences have furnished a rich harvest of facts for the test, a harvest growing daily. The sentence reverses the direction that the caricature assumes. Nature examines the philosophy; the philosophy does not examine nature and return a verdict from above. Where the manuscripts live up to the sentence, in the energy fragments, in the labour essay, in the reading notes that ask questions of Schorlemmer, the project succeeds on its own terms. Where they violate it, dictating eternity to thermodynamics, the project fails on its own terms too, without any assistance from Lukacs. A text that supplies the criterion of its own judgement has given its critics their best weapon and its defenders their best discipline in the same sentence, and the introduction's harvest metaphor, modest, empirical, corrigible, is the version of Engels on nature that this guide finally recommends to the reader's memory.

## The mathematical fragments: infinity and the calculus

A short section belongs to the strangest pages of the bundles, the notes on mathematics, because they display the project's full range in miniature, from penetration to embarrassment within a single subject. Engels was fascinated by the infinite and the infinitesimal, by the scandal that the calculus worked magnificently while its foundations, quantities smaller than any assignable quantity yet not nothing, offended every logic brought to inspect them. He treated the scandal as a case study in his central theme: mathematics itself, the supposed refuge of fixed categories, had been compelled by its own practice to think in motions, limits, and transitions, and the differential was his Exhibit A of a concept that is and is not, vanishing and effective at once. Marx, wrestling the same material in his own manuscripts, reached kindred conclusions by a different road, and the parallel struggle of the two men over the calculus is among the best documented cases of their shared natural philosophy in action.

The audit here must be double. On the side of embarrassment stand Engels's attempts to settle by dialectical reasoning questions that analysis was settling by rigour: the limit concept, rebuilt in the nineteenth century by Cauchy and Weierstrass and in the twentieth by the rehabilitation of infinitesimals inside nonstandard analysis, dissolved most of the mystery he pondered, and his pronouncements on particular points, on the nature of imaginary quantities, on axioms, have not survived contact with the discipline's development. On the side of penetration stands the instinct that the history of mathematics is itself historical, that its concepts are made and remade under the pressure of problems, and that a philosophy treating them as eternal furniture misunderstands its subject. That instinct, heretical in an age of Platonist common sense, became a research programme in the following century's history and sociology of mathematics. The mathematical fragments thus repeat the bundle's master pattern at higher magnification: the philosophy of process vindicated as orientation, the particular decrees refuted as doctrine, and the whole performance conducted with an amateur's courage that professional mathematicians have alternately mocked and, in their historical moods, envied.

## After the archive opened: scholarship's second century

The last transformation of the text's fortunes belongs to scholarship itself, and a guide written for researchers should close the historical account with it. For most of the twentieth century, the argument about Dialectics of Nature was conducted over a small stock of quotations endlessly recycled: the barley seed, the protein definition, the heat death denial, the labour essay's summary sentence. The stock circulated because the text was hard to study as a manuscript object; editions printed the arrangement, libraries held the arrangement, and the bundles themselves sat in archives whose politics governed access. The complete historical critical edition changed the material basis of the argument by publishing the manuscripts in their own order, with their dates, cancellations, and bundle boundaries visible, and a generation of scholarship followed the evidence into the archive. The composition could be reconstructed season by season. The plans could be compared as plans rather than conflated into a design. Engels's reading could be checked against his excerpts. Claims that had floated free for decades acquired weights and measures.

Kangal's monograph of 2020 is the fullest fruit of that archival turn, and its reception marks the state of the question better than any single verdict could. Sympathetic reviewers praised the reconstruction for dissolving the systematic treatise into a shifting, exploratory project. Critical reviewers questioned whether the exploratory Engels of the manuscripts can be separated so cleanly from the systematic Engels of Anti-Duhring, where the laws are stated with a public confidence no bundle reconstruction can soften. Both responses concede the new ground: the argument now runs through the archive, and a contribution that ignores the manuscripts' structure is recognisable at sight as a contribution to the older, quotation based controversy. That is what progress looks like in a humanities discipline, not agreement but a better class of disagreement, conducted over evidence all parties can inspect. For a text whose misfortune was to be canonised and prosecuted for a century on the strength of an arrangement, arrival at a better class of disagreement is a kind of vindication, the only kind unfinished works can honestly receive.

The practical consequence for readers of this guide can be stated as a rule of the house. On this subject, date every claim about the text's contents by the scholarship it rests on: pre-archival claims inherit the Moscow arrangement's distortions whether their authors were commissars or professors, and post-archival claims must show their bundle and dating or be read as belonging to the older stratum. The rule sounds severe. Applied consistently, it retires most of the internet's stock of Engels verdicts at a stroke, which is precisely its recommendation.

## Darwin in the bundles: enthusiasm and its limits

Darwin deserves a section of his own in any account of these manuscripts, because the relation between Engels's project and Darwin's theory is the point at which the philosophy of nature touches the science that defined the century. The enthusiasm was immediate, shared, and never withdrawn. Engels hailed the Origin of Species within weeks of its appearance, Marx followed with his own tributes, and the two men treated the destruction of fixed species as the biological form of everything their historical thought asserted about society: kinds that look eternal turn out to have biographies, and the biographies explain the kinds. In the bundles, Darwin functions as both evidence and method. As evidence, descent with modification is the organic world's certificate that nature makes transitions the old categories forbade. As method, Darwin's patient accumulation of variations, his refusal of essences, and his explanation of adapted complexity without a designer supplied Engels with the model of what a materialist science of process looks like when it succeeds, against which the speculative systems of the age could be measured and found weightless.

The limits of the enthusiasm are equally instructive, and Engels stated several of them himself. He objected to the importation of Malthus into nature, the projection of bourgeois competition onto organisms as the struggle for existence, noting that Darwin had taken a doctrine from society, dressed nature in it, and that socialists then reimported the dressed nature as a justification of the society it was borrowed from, a circular traffic he anatomised with real wit. He distinguished, as Darwin himself did in careful moments, between the struggle metaphor's wide sense and its literal one. He complained, in the letters, that Darwin's first edition underplayed the interdependence and cooperation of organisms, the sides of organic life that a later biology of symbiosis would restore to the centre. And he never absorbed the full severity of natural selection as an undirected mechanism, since his Lamarckian inheritance kept a directed, effortful element in evolution that the mature theory excludes. The pattern is the familiar one in a new key: the reading is strong where it follows the science and criticises its costumes, weaker where the philosophy's preferences, for direction, for progress, for labour's dignity, lean on the scales. Darwin survives the encounter entirely. Engels survives it as Darwin's most philosophically ambitious Victorian reader, which in that company is no small estate.

## The creator fragments and the theology question

A cluster of fragments on God and cosmology has done disproportionate work in the text's reputation, and a guide should handle it directly. Engels, like his partner, treated theology as a social fact to be explained rather than a partner in argument, and the bundles repeatedly return to the points where the science of the day was said to require a creator: the first impulse of Newton's heavens, the origin of motion, the heat death's implication of a beginning in which the world's clock was wound. His counter was uniform. Every such requirement marked a frontier of knowledge dressed as a boundary of nature, and the history of science was the history of frontiers retreating. The eternity of matter and motion, neither creatable nor destructible, was for him the materialist axiom that the retreats collectively underwrote. As polemic against the clerical exploitation of scientific humility, the fragments retain their bite, and their target, the conversion of an unsolved problem into a proof of theology, remains a live genre that scientists of many convictions still resist in Engels's own terms.

As cosmology, the audit has already returned its verdict, and the two judgements must be kept from contaminating each other. The twentieth century gave the universe a history with a hot early state, gave thermodynamics its expanding stage, and retired the eternal recycling cosmos the fragments required, while also retiring, just as thoroughly, the claim that any of this licensed theological conclusions in a laboratory. Engels lost the cosmology and won the demarcation dispute, and a reader who cannot hold both halves of that sentence will mishandle every fragment in the cluster. The deeper lesson concerns the vice diagnosed throughout this guide: the moment a philosophy of nature treats its axioms as guarantees about what cosmology must find, it has changed offices from orientation to instruction, and the record will punish it. Engels the reader of frontiers knew this. Engels the defender of eternity forgot it, in pages he fortunately never published. The drawer, once more, contains both men, and scholarship's task is to keep their names attached to the right pages.

## A reading itinerary for one season

Guides earn their keep in itineraries, so this one ends its practical counsel with a route a serious reader can complete in a season of evenings, built from the manuscript and companion map and sequenced by the argument of the whole article. Give the first evenings to Anti-Duhring's philosophy chapters, reading slowly enough to mark every place where a law is announced and every place where an example is asked to carry it, since the friction between announcement and example is the text's true subject. Follow with the Feuerbach book entire, one evening's work, for the philosophy in its finished dress. Then take the labour essay with a modern introduction to human evolution open beside it, scoring Engels's sequence against the fossil timetable as you go, an exercise that converts the audit of examples from a verdict received into a skill acquired. The historical introduction comes next, read as history of science, with its procession of breaches checked against any standard modern history of the period. Only then enter the fragments proper, by the ladder of motions: the energy and heat fragments, the chemistry, the biology, the mathematics for those equipped, the cosmology last and fastest. Close the season with the critics and the archive: the Lukacs footnote in its essay, a chapter of Schmidt, Kangal's reconstruction of the bundles, and Haldane's preface as the laboratory's dissent. A reader who completes the route owns the question rather than borrowing a verdict on it, and owns it in the only form the evidence permits, as a set of discriminations among texts of different status, sciences of different fates, and arguments of different tribunals. That set of discriminations is the article's whole content in one sentence, and the itinerary is its delivery mechanism.

## The fragment as a form: reading a notebook honestly

A final discipline of reading remains, the most general and the one this guide has practised throughout without naming: how to read a notebook. Notebooks, drafts, and fragments obey a rhetoric of their own, and misreading the rhetoric is the root error from which the century's quarrels grew. A notebook entry is a thinker talking to himself at a particular date, with a particular book open beside him, trying a formulation to see if it bears weight. It may be a breakthrough, a reminder, a provocation, or a mistake he would have caught at the next sitting, and the page itself rarely says which. The honest reader therefore assigns notebook claims a provisional status that print never asks for, weighting a formulation by its recurrence across the bundles, by its survival into the published work, and by its consonance with the dated reading around it. A sentence that appears once, in a cosmological aside of the middle 1870s, and never again in a decade of related writing is evidence about a mood. A distinction that recurs across plans, fragments, the published polemic, and the finished Feuerbach book is evidence about a doctrine. Much of the prosecution's case rests on mood sentences promoted to doctrines, and much of the defence's case rests on doctrines quietly substituted for the mood sentences the prosecution quoted. Both promotions are the same fallacy wearing different rosettes.

The discipline generalises beyond this text, which is why the guide insists on it as the series' transferable lesson for every edited construction in the canon. The Paris manuscripts, The German Ideology, the Grundrisse, the prison notebooks of Gramsci: the tradition's library is unusually rich in works their authors never issued, and each has suffered the same promotion of fragments into systems by friends and enemies who needed a system to bless or to burn. The reader trained on Engels's bundles, where the editorial facts are now unusually well documented, carries the training to the whole shelf: ask first what kind of object this is, who arranged it, what its author finished elsewhere on the same subject, and which tribunal, philosophical, scientific, or historical, each claim belongs to. Those four questions, asked in that order, would have prevented most of the errors catalogued in this article, and they cost nothing but the willingness to let a notebook be a notebook. Engels, who spent his last strength editing another man's unfinished masterpiece into publishable shape, and who wrestled publicly with the ethics of that editing, would have recognised the discipline. It is, in the end, the discipline he practised on Marx's drawer. The least his own drawer is owed is the same courtesy, extended now by readers who know what he never had the chance to decide.

## Engels in the classroom of nations: the text as a schoolbook

One habitat of the text remains unexamined, and it governed more readers' first encounters than all the others combined: the schoolbook. Across the socialist states of the twentieth century, and through the communist parties' education systems beyond them, generations met Engels on nature not in the bundles or even in Anti-Duhring but in manuals that digested the codification into lessons, with the three laws as headings, the barley seed and the steam point as illustrations, and examination questions whose answers were known before they were asked. For millions of students, that manual version simply was the dialectics of nature, and the habits it trained, reverence in the East and reflexive derision among those who later emigrated or defected, entered the wider culture's bloodstream. Memoir literature from the socialist world returns to the lessons with a mixture of boredom and retrospective anger that no reading of the manuscripts could have produced, because the manuscripts were never the object of the experience. The object was the catechism, wearing the founder's name.

The schoolbook habitat explains a feature of the controversy that purely philosophical accounts miss: its emotional register. Arguments about propositions are cool. Arguments about what was drilled into fourteen year olds, by teachers who were themselves drilled, under regimes that punished the wrong answer, run hot on both sides, and the heat attaches to Engels regardless of what his pages contain. The Western seminar's scorn and the Eastern lecture hall's piety were both, in part, memories of classrooms. A guide that aspires to settle the reader's relation to the text must therefore perform one disentangling act explicitly: the schoolbook is a reception artifact, evidence about institutions, and it cannot be used as evidence about the manuscripts in either direction. The student who was bored by the catechism has not refuted the fragments. The student who was inspired by it has not vindicated them. Both students are owed the chance to meet the actual object, a Victorian reader's unfinished notebook and its published companions, at an age when they can grade its examples for themselves. Supplying that chance is the social function of guides like this one, and it is why the itinerary above ends not with a verdict but with a reading route.

## For students and exam candidates

The Engels question is a gift to the prepared candidate, because it is one of the few standard topics where a single documented fact rearranges the whole answer. Structure the essay in three movements. Open with the charge in its classic form, Lukacs's footnote and the Western Marxist verdict that the extension of dialectics to nature converted a method of historical self-knowledge into a cosmology. Then introduce the evidence the charge usually omits: Anti-Duhring's publication in Marx's lifetime, the reading aloud of the manuscript, and the chapter Marx contributed, followed by the manuscript status of Dialectics of Nature itself and the editorial construction of 1925. Close with a calibrated verdict that distinguishes the philosophical objection, which survives, from the biographical melodrama, which does not, and name the unfinished-target problem as the distinction's label. The trap to avoid is the balanced shrug that gives each side a paragraph and no fact the force of decision. Markers reward the candidate who knows that the principal text was unfinished and that the companion text was co-signed in effect by Marx. Keep quotations few and short, date everything, and never attribute the three laws to Marx, the single error that most reliably exposes secondhand knowledge of the topic.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The dominant misconception in a class meeting this material is that the seminar must choose a team, Engels the betrayer or Engels the co-founder, before it may proceed. Design the session to make that choice harder rather than easier. Put three short exhibits before the students in an order that scrambles loyalties: the Lukacs footnote, so the objection is met in its birthplace and its brevity surprises them; a page of the heat death fragments, so the defence must swallow Engels at his most speculative; and the record of Marx's contribution to Anti-Duhring, so the prosecution must swallow the partnership at its closest. Then set the question that organises real discussion: what kind of evidence should settle a question of this sort, manuscript evidence, philosophical argument, or the later record of the sciences, and what verdict does each kind support. Students discover that their disagreements are usually about the tribunal, not the facts, and that discovery is the lesson. Guard the session's balance by insisting that the codification and Lysenko be discussed as history, with the distinctions this guide draws, rather than as ammunition. A class that leaves able to say which claims about Engels are manuscript claims, which are philosophical claims, and which are reception claims has learned the transferable skill, whatever its members conclude about the man.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Citation practice for this text must change to match its status, and this layer, expanded as the brief for the article directs, sets out the working rules. Never cite Dialectics of Nature by the printed volume's chapter order as though it were authorial; cite the fragment by its title or incipit, its approximate date of composition where scholarship assigns one, and the edition used, and where a claim depends on grouping or sequence, check the bundle structure in the historical critical edition before making it. Never quote the three laws from a textbook paraphrase; the locus is a specific fragment and a specific chapter of Anti-Duhring, and the two differ in ways arguments have turned on. Verify every scientific example against the subsequent record before repeating it, since the protein definition of life and the denial of heat death circulate in secondary literature as though they were Engels's insights rather than his casualties. For the Marx question, the safe formulations are documentary: Marx read the Anti-Duhring manuscript, Marx contributed a chapter to its economic part, no recorded dissent from its philosophy exists. Anything stronger, in either direction, is interpretation and should be flagged as such. Journalists on deadline should treat the phrase Engels distorted Marx as a claim with a 1923 birthday and a footnote for a birth certificate, not as a datum, and should treat any Soviet era doctrinal formula attributed to Engels as a product of the codification until the manuscript locus is produced. Researchers building on the newer scholarship should start from the bundle reconstruction and from Kangal's monograph of 2020, Friedrich Engels and the Dialectics of Nature, and should store their reading notes, dated fragment by fragment with bundle positions attached, in a system that keeps the citation attached to the note; the study companion at [VaultBook's Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is built for exactly that discipline of source linked excerpting, and on a text whose order is editorial, keeping the provenance attached to every excerpt is not pedantry but the whole method.

## Verdict: how to approach the text

The verdict of this guide can be given in the form the text itself earns, as instructions for reading rather than as a sentence on its author. Read the published philosophy first and judge it as philosophy, in Anti-Duhring and the Feuerbach book, where Engels answered for his words in his lifetime and where Marx's documented participation fixes the biographical frame. Read the labour essay as the project's empirical masterpiece and its introduction as its historical one. Read the fragments as a great reader's notebook, fertile where it follows the sciences, fallible where it instructs them, and never as a code of laws. Refuse the codifiers' use of it, in any century, and refuse equally the melodrama that makes its author the secret assassin of his friend's method. What survives those refusals is considerable: a picture of nature as interconnected process that the sciences of the following century ratified in outline while correcting in detail, a method of reading science for its philosophical yield that founded a discipline's habits, and a standing lesson in how traditions mishandle unfinished books, first by canonising them and then by prosecuting them, when the scholarly duty was always the humbler one of reading them as what they are. The drawer in Moscow has been open for a century. It is time the argument caught up with the archive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Dialectics of Nature about?

Dialectics of Nature is the name given to the bundles of manuscripts Friedrich Engels left on the philosophy of the natural sciences. Their argument is that the sciences of his century, cell theory, the transformation of energy, and Darwinian evolution above all, disclose a nature of interconnected processes and transitions rather than fixed kinds, and that the habits of thought adequate to such a nature are dialectical: attentive to thresholds where quantity passes into quality, to polarities that define each other, and to development through stages that preserve what they supersede. Around that argument the bundles gather a historical survey of science since the Renaissance, the famous essay on labour and human origins, and dozens of fragments on heat, electricity, chemistry, mathematics, and biology. Because Engels never finished or arranged the material, the subject is inseparable from the manuscript's own history, which is why this guide treats the two together throughout.

### Q: Did Engels finish Dialectics of Nature?

No. Engels worked on the material through the 1870s and into the early 1880s, drafted several plans that were never reconciled, brought a historical introduction and the essay on labour close to completion, and left the rest as fragments, drafts, and reading notes. After Marx died in 1883, the editing of the second and third volumes of Capital consumed the years that might have finished the project, and Engels's later letters mention returning to the natural science material without the return ever happening. He died in 1895 with the bundles unpublished and unordered. The volume readers know is an editorial construction, first assembled in Moscow in the 1920s, and its sequence of fragments reflects the editors' decisions rather than any final design of its author. That unfinished state is the single most important fact about the text, and arguments about it that ignore the fact should be discounted accordingly.

### Q: When was Dialectics of Nature published?

The manuscripts were first published in Moscow in the middle of the 1920s, in a bilingual scholarly edition prepared by the Marx-Engels Institute under David Riazanov, with the German text and a Russian translation presented together. The date deserves emphasis: 1925 stands four decades after Engels set the bundles aside and thirty years after his death, so the text entered public debate in a world its author never saw, amid a revolution that was already treating him as a founding authority. Individual pieces had appeared earlier; the essay on the part played by labour was printed in the journal Die Neue Zeit in 1896, the year after Engels died. Later editions carried the assembled volume into the Russian, German, and English collected works, where its manuscript status is acknowledged in the apparatus but easily missed by readers who meet the text as one bound classic among others.

### Q: What is the part played by labour essay about?

The essay, drafted in 1876 and titled The Part Played by Labour in the Transition from Ape to Man, argues that labour made humanity rather than merely employing it. A species of ape descended from the trees, adopted an upright gait, and freed its hands for work; the work refined the hand, and the hand's development reacted on the whole organism. Cooperative labour then created the need for speech, the organs of speech and the brain developed together under the pressure of work and communication, and diet, fire, and society followed. Later palaeontology vindicated the essay's boldest sequence, upright posture and the free hand preceding the enlargement of the brain, which the fossil record of small brained bipeds confirmed. Its mechanism, the inheritance of acquired characteristics, was superseded by genetics, so the essay survives as a brilliant conjecture with a dated engine, respected by scientists such as Stephen Jay Gould precisely for its structure.

### Q: Should you read Anti-Duhring instead?

For most readers, Anti-Duhring should come first, and for many it can stand in place of the bundles altogether. It is the philosophy Engels finished and published in 1878, stating in public the connected picture of nature, the three laws with their famous examples, and the materialist theory of knowledge that the manuscripts explore in private. It carries a further authority no notebook can match: Engels read the whole manuscript to Marx, and Marx contributed a chapter to its economic part. Its faults are real, since the polemic against Duhring forces long marches through an opponent's thickets, and the extract published as Socialism: Utopian and Scientific offers a shorter road into the same worldview. The sound sequence is Anti-Duhring or its extract first, then the labour essay and the historical introduction from the bundles, then the fragments by appetite, with the cosmological pages reserved for readers who already know where they fail.

### Q: Did Engels distort Marx's dialectic?

That is the Engels question, and the honest answer separates its parts. The Western Marxist tradition founded by Lukacs's footnote of 1923 holds that Engels distorted the dialectic by extending it from history, where human practice supplies the mediating subject, to nature, where it does not, thereby converting a method of self-knowledge into a cosmology of laws. The philosophical objection is serious and survives every documentary discovery. The biographical version, in which a loyal but limited executor smuggled foreign doctrine into Marxism after Marx's death, does not survive the documents: the extension was published in Anti-Duhring in 1878, Marx heard the manuscript and contributed a chapter, and the chief exhibit for the prosecution, Dialectics of Nature, is an unfinished bundle assembled by editors in 1925. Readers of this guide are given the full case on both sides under the name of the unfinished-target problem, and the verdict there is that the burden of proof has moved.

### Q: Did Marx approve of Engels's philosophical writing?

The documentary record supports a careful yes for the work Marx actually saw. Engels read the complete manuscript of Anti-Duhring to him before its serial publication began, Marx contributed the chapter on the prehistory of value theory to the book's economic part, and the two men corresponded across the 1870s about the natural sciences, Darwin, chemistry, and mathematics, with Marx sending his own scientific studies to Engels and receiving comments in return. No letter or recorded remark of Marx's dissents from the philosophy of Anti-Duhring, and silence from so combative a correspondent, about a book he helped write for a paper he read, is strong evidence. What the record cannot show is Marx's judgement of the unpublished bundles, including the cosmological fragments, which he may never have read in draft. The precise formulation is therefore that Marx approved, and partly authored, the philosophy Engels published, while his verdict on the drawer remains unrecorded.

### Q: Are Engels's scientific examples still valid?

They divide, and the division follows a rule a reader can apply independently. The examples drawn from the great unifications of his century survived: the transformation and conservation of energy, the cellular constitution of life, Darwinian descent with modification, and the historical character of the cosmos itself all remain foundations, and his philosophical moral about interconnection aged well. The examples where he pushed beyond the evidence failed: life defined as the mode of existence of protein bodies collapsed with the discovery of nucleic acids and viruses, his rejection of any final heat death of the universe was not borne out by later thermodynamics and cosmology, his inheritance mechanism was dissolved by genetics, and his chemical and mathematical illustrations contain period errors throughout. The pattern is instructive. Where Engels followed the sciences he was usually right in outline; where a philosophical requirement instructed the sciences in advance, he was wrong in the event.

### Q: Which parts of Dialectics of Nature are worth reading?

Begin with the two pieces Engels brought close to finish, since they show the project at full strength. The essay on the part played by labour is the essential fragment: short, argued, and vindicated in its central sequence by the fossil record, provided its inheritance mechanism is read as period machinery. The historical introduction, surveying the sciences from the Renaissance to Darwin and energy, rewards readers interested in how styles of scientific thought arise and collapse. Among the shorter fragments, those on the transformation of energy, on the cell, and on Darwin repay attention as records of a strong mind reading strong science. The plans are worth a skim as evidence of intention, since they show the project changing shape. The mathematical fragments interest specialists, and the cosmological arguments against heat death should be read last, as biography rather than physics, because there the method instructs science instead of learning from it.

### Q: Why did Lukacs criticise the dialectics of nature?

Georg Lukacs, in a celebrated footnote to History and Class Consciousness in 1923, objected that Engels's examples of dialectics in nature omitted the element that makes dialectics dialectical in his account: the interaction of subject and object in human practice. History can be dialectical, for Lukacs, because humanity makes its world and can know it in the making; nature, having no subject, exhibits change and motion but not the self-mediation the concept requires. Behind the philosophy stood a politics. A dialectics resident in nature independently of practice could be administered as a finished science by a party claiming to possess it, turning the proletariat from the subject of its doctrine into its object. The footnote founded the Western Marxist case against Engels, though Lukacs himself later softened the position, and though he wrote before the Moscow edition of the manuscripts existed, aiming at the published Anti-Duhring rather than at the bundles later critics would prosecute.

### Q: Why did the unfinished state of Dialectics of Nature shape its reception?

Because an unfinished text cannot correct its readers. A finished book pushes back: its author has ordered the arguments, qualified the claims, and deleted the speculations that failed, so misuse must fight the text itself. The bundles offered no such resistance. Editors arranged them into a doctrine shaped volume in 1925, codifiers quarried the arrangement for a catechism, and critics prosecuted the same arrangement as a system, each party treating editorial joins as authorial argument and notebook entries as published theses. The hesitations, dates, and revisions that would have complicated every use were invisible until the manuscript scholarship of the complete editions restored them. Reception history in this case is therefore not a garnish on the text but a large part of its effective content: what the twentieth century argued about, in Moscow lecture halls and Western seminars alike, was substantially a book that editors and institutions had made out of a drawer, and recovering the drawer changes the argument's terms.

### Q: What should a researcher verify before citing Engels on a scientific question?

Four checks, each cheap and each capable of saving an argument. Verify the locus: whether the sentence comes from a work Engels published, from the near finished labour essay or introduction, or from a rough fragment, since the three carry different authority and are routinely confused. Verify the date and bundle position in the historical critical edition, because claims about the project's shape depend on them and the printed order is editorial. Verify the science: whether the example Engels used survived the following century, since the protein definition of life and the heat death denial circulate as insights in secondary literature when they are casualties, while the energy and cell examples survive and can be used with confidence. Verify the attribution: the three laws are Engels's formulation drawn from Hegel, not a doctrine of Marx's, and any sentence beginning Marx held that nature shows should be traced to an actual page before it is repeated.

### Q: How did the Lysenko affair damage the dialectics of nature tradition?

The affair of 1948, in which Trofim Lysenko's doctrines were declared official in the Soviet Union and Mendelian genetics was proscribed as bourgeois idealism, attached the vocabulary of dialectics to the destruction of a science. Lysenko's camp argued in the codified language of dialectical materialism, denounced genes as metaphysical fictions, and claimed the founders' authority, while the machinery that let a philosophy office certify biology had been built by the codification this guide describes. Geneticists were dismissed and imprisoned, Vavilov died in prison, and Soviet biology lost a generation. The damage to the tradition was twofold and lasting. Inside the sciences, dialectics of nature became a warning label, associated with the policing of research by doctrine. Inside Marxism, every later defence of Engels on nature has had to begin by answering for 1948, although the manuscripts themselves respect the autonomy of scientific results that the codification inverted. The affair stands as the historical proof of what happens when fragments become a rulebook.

### Q: What separates Engels's dialectics from the laws later codified by others?

The difference is one of use, and it runs through every page. In Engels's manuscripts the laws are findings and filing headings: patterns he believed the sciences themselves disclosed, collected from thermodynamics, chemistry, and biology, held loosely enough that he could define dialectics in one breath as the science of interconnections and worry an example for pages in the next. In the codification of the 1930s, canonised in the Short Course chapter of 1938, the same laws became a catechism: stated first, illustrated by assigned examples, and enforced as the test of correctness in philosophy and eventually in the sciences themselves. Engels never used the system name dialectical materialism that the codifiers placed over his work, a term carried into circulation by Dietzgen and Plekhanov. Where his pages hesitate, date themselves, and argue with the science of a particular decade, the catechism asserts, timelessly, and grades the answers. Confusing the two is the master error of the subject.

### Q: How do sympathetic scientists and hostile scientists read the same fragments?

They read for different things, and the fragments, being aphoristic and unfinished, reward both readings. Sympathetic scientists, Haldane in his preface to the English edition, Needham, Gould on the labour essay, and in a later generation Levins and Lewontin as self-described dialectical biologists, read for orientation: habits of attention toward interconnection, thresholds where quantity becomes quality, levels of organisation that cannot be reduced away without loss. They treat the laws as scaffolding and judge the text by its fertility for working inquiry. Hostile scientists read for testable content and find the laws compatible with every outcome and therefore empty as theory, a scheme that explains steam and barley and magnetism with the same formula and so explains none of them. The divergence is finally about what a philosophy of nature is for, orientation or assertion, and Engels oscillates between the two conceptions, which is why eminent readers have extracted opposite verdicts from identical pages without either side misquoting him.
